QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Does a State deprive a person of property without due 
process of law if it allegedly engages in false advertising in 
violation of section 43(a) of the Lanham Act, such that 
Congress, acting pursuant to section 5 of the Fourteenth 
Amendment, may abrogate Eleventh Amendment immunity 
and make the States subject to suit in federal court for Lanham 
Act violations? 


2. Does Parden v. Terminal Railway of Alabama Docks 
Department, 377 U.S. 184 (1964), which held that Congress 
may employ its authority under the Commerce Clause to 
compel States to “waive” their Eleventh Amendment 
immunity if they participate in commerce regulated by 
Congress, survive this Court’s decision in Seminole Tribe of 
Florida v. Florida, 517 U.S. 44 (1996), and apply to claims 
against a state agency arising out of the State’s administration 
and promotion of a college tuition-funding program?

COUNTERSTATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Respondent Florida Prepaid is an agency of the State of 
Florida that was created to administer a prepaid college tuition 
and dormitory-expense program. See Fla. Stat. § 240.551, 
J.A. 45. The principal purpose of the Florida Prepaid program 
is to enable and encourage the people of Florida to obtain the 
benefits of higher education at Florida institutions by providing 
a reliable mechanism for paying college expenses. Participants 
pay a contract price today that entitles program beneficiaries to 
college credit hours or dormitory services at a future date. The 
contract 1s for guaranteed college services; it is not an investment 
contract and the beneficiaries do not receive funds. Participation 
is generally open to Florida residents or the children of Florida 
residents. See Fla. Stat. § 240.551(2)(e)(1)-(3), J.A. 46. The 
program is a top Florida priority: 


The State considers this an essential governmental 
operation to assist its citizens to access higher 
education. All legal and beneficial interests in the 
assets held by the trust fund are vested in the State 
for its exclusive benefit and the exclusive berefit of 
the colleges and universities; therefore, payments are 
guaranteed to be made on the beneficiary’s behalf to 
the State college or university. Exercise of full 
benefits under the contract guarantees the beneficiary 
receipt of services and the beneficiary will not receive 
any funds. 


Florida Prepaid Master Covenant, § 5.08 (Exhibit A to Florida 
Prepaid Answer to Complaint; see J.A. 29, 4 16). 


The District Court found that Florida Prepaid is an “arm of 
the State” of Florida and thereby entitled to assert Florida’s 
Eleventh Amendment immunity as a bar to jurisdiction in federal 
court. College Savings Bank v. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary 


2 


Education Expense Board, 948 F. Supp. 400 (D.N.J. 1996); 
see Pet. App. at 46a-58a. Neither College Savings Bank nor the 
United States sought review of that finding before the Court of 
Appeals. 


Petitioner College Savings Bank (“CSB” or “Petitioner’’) 
is acommercial financial institution that markets a certificate of 
deposit product designed to assist customers with investing to 
cover the costs of a college education. CSB owns a United States 
patent relating to a calculation methodology used with its 
certificate of deposit product. In 1994, CSB sued Florida Prepaid 
for patent infringement in the United States District Court for 
the District of New Jersey. In 1995, CSB brought a second action 
against Florida Prepaid in the same court, this time alleging 
violations of section 43(a) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. 
§ 1125(a). The Lanham Act claims relate exclusively to alleged 
false advertising by Florida Prepaid in its descriptions of its own 
tuition funding program. There are no allegations of false 
statements regarding CSB’s certificate of deposit product, nor 
any Claims for trademark infringement, trade dress infringement, 
or other matters covered by the Lanham Act. 


After this Court announced its decision in Seminole Tribe 
of Florida v. Florida, 517 U.S. 44 (1996), Florida Prepaid moved 
to dismiss both the Lanham Act action and the patent 
infringement action as barred by the Eleventh Amendment. The 
United States intervened to defend the constitutionality of the 
Trademark Remedy Clarification Act of 1992, Pub. L. No. 102- 
542, 106 Stat. 3567 (1992) (“the TRCA”), which made the States 
amenable to suit in federal court for violations of the Lanham 
Act, and the Patent and Plant Variety Protection Remedy 
Clarification Act, Pub. L. No. 102-560, 106 Stat. 4230 (1992), 
which made the States amenable to suit in federal court for patent 
infringement. With respect to the TRCA, the United States 
argued only that Florida Prepaid had lost its Eleventh 
Amendment immunity to Lanham Act suits via the “constructive 


3 


waiver” doctrine of Parden v. Terminal Railway of Alabama 
Docks Department, 377 U.S. 184 (1964). CSB argued both that 
the Parden doctrine applied and that the TRCA was 
constitutional because Congress possessed authority under 
section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment to abrogate Eleventh 
Amendment immunity and permit Lanham Act false advertising 
suits against the States in federal court. 


In a single decision, the District Court granted the motion 
to dismiss the Lanham Act case but denied the motion to dismiss 
the patent infringement action. College Savings Bank v. Florida 
Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board, 948 F. Supp. 
400 (D.N.J. 1996), Pet. App. B. In the instant case, both CSB 
and the United States appealed the dismissal of the Lanham Act 
suit to the Third Circuit, which affirmed. College Savings Bank 
v. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board, 
131 F.3d 353 (3d Cir. 1997), cert. granted, 67 U.S.L.W. 3433 
(1999), Pet. App. A. In the patent infringement case, Florida 
Prepaid appealed the decision to the United States Court of 
Appeals for the Federal Circuit, which affirmed. College Savings 
Bank v. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense 
Board, 148 F.3d 1355 (Fed. Cir. 1998), cert. granted, 67 
U.S.L.W. 3433 (1999), Pet. App. D. 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


This Court’s decision in Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida 
established that Congress has no power under Article I of the 
Constitution to abrogate the States’ Eleventh Amendment 
immunity. That holding means that Congress cannot, when 
legislating pursuant to the Commerce Clause, make the States 
amenable to suit in federal court for alleged violations of federal 
law. Because the Lanham Act, as amended by the TRCA, is 
unquestionably an exercise of congressional authority under the 
Commerce Clause, the Court of Appeals properly affirmed the 


4 


District Court’s dismissal of CSB’s Lanham Act-based false 
advertising claim against Florida Prepaid. 


Although Congress does have power under section 5 of the 
Fourteenth Amendment to abrogate Eleventh Amendment 
immunity in appropriate cases, not every legislative act under 
Article I can be justified as an exercise of Fourteenth Amendment 
enforcement authority. As this Court held in City of Boerne v. 
Flores, 521 U.S. 507 (1997), abrogation pursuant to the 
Fourteenth Amendment must be restricted to legitimate 
congressional efforts to enforce the substantive guarantees of 
that amendment — there must be a constitutional wrong 
Congress seeks to remedy or prevent. In particular, the concept 
of “property” under the Fourteenth Amendment cannot be made 
so elastic that it becomes the proxy for every statutory right or 
interest created by Congress under the Commerce Clause, such 
as a false advertising claim brought under the Lanham Act. 
Finding Fourteenth Amendment abrogation authority to be 
coextensive with congressional power under Article I, as CSB 
demands, would nullify Seminole Tribe, defy City of Boerne, 
and virtually eliminate the Eleventh Amendment. 


This Court has never suggested that a business’s statutory 
right under the Lanham Act to be free of a competitor's false 
advertising about the competitor’s own product constitutes 
“property” for purposes of the Fourteenth Amendment. 
Recognizing such a statutory interest as a constitutionally 
protected property right would essentially federalize much of 
the common law of tort and contract, and would subject States 
to litigation in federal court every time a governmental action is 
alleged to affect a privately owned business. Florida Prepaid 
agrees with the United States that the concept of property 
embodied in the Fourteenth Amendment simply does not extend 
to attenuated interests of the type asserted by CSB. 


5 


Even assuming that the interest in being free of false 
advertising could constitute a legitimate property right for 
purposes of the Fourteenth Amendment, CSB has failed to 
demonstrate that the State of Florida has deprived it of such 
property without due process of law. The denial of due process, 
not the mere deprivation of property, is necessary to justify a 
remedial scheme that includes the abrogation of Eleventh 
Amendment immunity. States already provide common law 
remedies for unfair competition, and state courts also have 
concurrent jurisdiction to adjudicate claims arising under the 
Lanham Act. Florida, like many States, permits “takings” claims 
to be brought against the State in state court, and also has enacted 
a limited statutory waiver of its sovereign immunity for tort 
claims brought against the State in its own courts. Thus, Florida 
provides adequate legal process to redress any deprivation of 
property that may have occurred in CSB’s case or in similar 
unfair competition cases. The TRCA is therefore unconstitutional 
under the rule of City of Boerne, because the TRCA purports to 
provide a remedy — suits against the States in federal court — 
for a nonexistent constitutional harm — the supposed denial of 
due process by States that allegedly have deprived persons of 
property through false advertising. 

Accordingly, congressional abrogation of Florida Prepaid’s 
Eleventh Amendment immunity cannot be justified as an 
exercise of Fourteenth Amendment enforcement power. 

Nor may jurisdiction over Florida Prepaid be maintained 
through application of the constructive waiver doctrine of Parden 
v. Terminal Railway of Alabama Docks Department, which 
provided that Congress could require a State to “waive” Eleventh 
Amendment immunity as the price of engaging in commercial 
activity subject to federal legislative control. The Pa: den doctrine 
cannot be applied here because Parden itself directly conflicts 
with Seminole Tribe, and therefore should be formally overruled. 
Congress should not be permitted to do indirectly, by means of 


6 


a compulsory waiver doctrine, what it no longer can do directly 
after Seminole Tribe. Parden was squarely based on 
congressional abrogation authority assumed to exist under the 
Commerce Clause. Now that the Seminole Tribe has made clear 
that congressional authority under Article I does not include the 
power to curtail Eleventh Amendnient immunity, Parden is 
bereft of support, and cannot justify CSB’s suit against Florida 
Prepaid. 

Even if Parden somehow remains good law, it does not 
apply to the activities of Florida Prepaid here. As the Court of 
Appeals correctly found, Florida Prepaid engages in the core 
governmental function of furthering the education of its citizens 
by providing a service that enables state residents to attend 
college or university. Mischaracterizing this activity as a money- 
making or commercial endeavor turns a blind eye to the realities 
of this non-profit education program as well as to the increasingly 
creative strategies that state governments must employ in order 
to discharge their primary responsibility of securing the well- 
being of their citizens. Florida Prepaid’s education-financing 
activities are essentially governmental, not commercial, and 
therefore Parden is inapplicable. 


Finally, resort to Parden also fails because the TRCA does 
not regulate prepaid tuition programs and is therefore incapable 
of triggering a Parden waiver of immunity. The Lanham Act, 
as amended by the TRICA, regulates a// interstate commerce, 
and therefore does not aliert States to the loss of their Elewenth 
Amendment immunity as a consequence of operating prepaid 
tuition programs. The TRCA notifies States only that they have 
no immunity if they are allleged to have violated the Lamiam 
Act — a warming that is far too broad and vague to satisfy Parden.

ARGUMENT 


I. THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY APPLIED 
SEMINOLE TRIBE v. FLORIDA AND CITY OF 
BOERNE v. FLORES IN HOLDING THAT 
CONGRESSIONAL AUTHORIZATION OF SUITS 
AGAINST THE STATES IN FEDERAL COURT ON 
FALSE ADVERTISING CLAIMS CANNOT BE 
JUSTIFIED AS AN EXERCISE OF FOURTEENTH 
AMENDMENT ENFORCEMENT POWER. 


The decision of the Court of Appeals is manifestly correct. 
The tightly confined holding is consistent with this Court's 
precedents regarding the Eleventh and Fourteenth Amendments. 
In contrast, CSB asks this Court to adopt a sweeping theory of 
property rights and Fourteenth Amendment enforcement power 
that the Court of Appeals properly rejected, and that even the 
United States, which intervened in this litigation in order to 
defend the TRCA, has declared unfounded. 

A. The Statutory Right To Be Free Of A Competitor’s False 
Advertising Is Not “Property” That Congress May 
Protect Under Section 5 Of The Fourteenth Amendment. 
(1) In Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida, 517 U.S. 44 

(1996), this Court held that Congress has no power urider the 

Indian Commerce Clause or the Interstate Commerce Ciause to 

abrogate the Eleventh Amendment immunity enjoyed by the 

States. As the Court of Appeals recognized, the holding of 

Seminole Tribe is squarely applicable here. The Lanham Act 

represents an exercise of congressional power under the Interstate 

Commerce Clause. See, e.z., Trade-Mark Cases, 100 U.S. 82, 

94-96 (1879) (stating that congressional power to provide 

trademark protection can arise only from the power to regulate: 

Act against 2 State or state agency in federal court pursuant to 

the TRCA miust be dismissed as barred by the Eleventh 

Amendment. 


To overcome this limitation on congressional power and 
rescue the TRCA from unconstitutionality, CSB argues that the 
TRCA can be treated as an exercise of Congress’s enforcement 
power under section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment. This 
argument requires that a person’s interest in being free from the 
type of false advertising alleged here be deemed a “property” 
nght, so that section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment may be 
triggered. Indeed, according to CSB, because a “business” is a 
form of property, any state action that affects that business in 
any way Is a potential impingement upon property that Congress 
may remedy or deter under section 5. See Brief for Petitioner at 
19-20. The Eleventh Amendment, it would appear, simply has 
no force where a “business” is concerned. 


After Seminole Tribe, however, close scrutiny must be 
applied to any contention that a particular congressional attempt 
to abrogate Eleventh Amendment immunity legitimately 
exercises Fourteenth Amendment power. Seminole Tribe makes 
it perfectly plain that Congress enjoys no power to abrogate 
Eleventh Amendment immunity under any provision of Article 
I of the Constitution. Congressional enforcement power under 
section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment, which applies only to 
the substantive provisions of that Amendment, should not be 
improperly converted into a replacement for the entirety of 
Article |. Congress is not free to transform exercises of Article | 
power into applications of Fourteenth Amendment enforcement 
authority simply because the Fourteenth Amendment is 
Congress’s only remaining source of power to abrogate the 
Eleventh Amendment. Judicial recognition of such pretextual 
invocations of Fourteenth Amendment power would flout the 
rule of Seminole Tribe and would eviscerate the Eleventh 
Amendment. “The need to promote the supremacy of federal 
law must be accommodated to the constitutional immunity of 
the States.” Idaho v. Coeur d'Alene Tribe of Idaho, 521 U.S. 
261, 278 (1997) (quoting Pennhurst State School & Hospital v. 
Halderman, 465 U.S. 89, 105 (1984)). 


9 


A notable law review commentary has explained the 
jeopardy to the Eleventh Amendment created by unjustified 
congressional reliance on Fourteenth Amendment authority to 


protect congressionally created rights: 


In Seminole Tribe, the Court held that Congress 
has the power to abrogate Eleventh Amendment 
immunity pursuant to its power to enforce the 
Fourteenth Amendment, but not under its Article | 
legislative power. The Fourteenth Amendment, 
however, includes a clause prohibiting states from 
depriving persons of property without due process 
of law. Can Congress pass a statute giving individuals 
a property right vis-a-vis the states and then, to 
“enforce” the Due Process Clause, abrogate the 
states’ Eleventh Amendment immunity with respect 
to suits to enforce that right? An affirmative answer 
to this question threatens to reduce Seminole Tribe’s 
abrogation holding to nothing. . . . If this reasoning 
were to hold, then Congress’s power to abrogate 
Eleventh Amendment immunity would appear to be 
coextensive with its power to legislate under Article 
I, and, if so, Seminole Tribe’s holding that Congress 
lacks the power to abrogate Eleventh Amendment 
immunity under Article I would be eviscerated. 


Carlos Manuel Vazquez, What Js Eleventh Amendment 
Immunity? , 106 Yale L.J. 1683, 1744-46 (1997); see also Henry 
Paul Monaghan, Comment: The Sovereign Immunity 
“Exception,” 110 Harv. L. Rev. 102, 126 n.162 (1996) 
(“Whether the Seminole Tribe majority would permit so 
transparent an evisceration of its holdings is unclear.”). Here, 
the Court of Appeals properly declined CSB’s invitation to 
“eviscerate” the Eleventh Amendment. In so doing, the Third 
Circuit followed the teachings of this Court on both Eleventh 
Amendment and Fourteenth Amendment issues. 


10 


There is no historical tradition linking the Lanham Act and 
the Fourteenth Amendment. The Lanham Act always has been 
regarded as an exercise of the power to regulate commerce. ' 
The legislative history of the TRCA, which amended the Lanham 
Act to permit suits against the States in federal court, contains 
only a passing reference to the Fourteenth Amendment as a 
source of authority for congressional abrogation of immunity 
for trademark infringement suits, see S. Rep. No. 102-280 (May 
12, 1992), at 7-8, and is completely silent with respect to the 
false advertising prong of the Lanham Act, which is all that is 
invelved here. Thus, there is no direct support for the view that 
Congress intended to use its Fourteenth Amendment power to

ANNOUNCED IN PARDEN v. TERMINAL 


RAILWAY OF ALABAMA DOCKS DEPARTMENT 
CANNOT BE APPLIED HERE. 


After Seminole Tribe, Congress no longer has authority 
under Article I of the Constitution to abrogate Eleventh 
Amendment immunity. Petitioner, and the United States, 
therefore resort to the “constructive waiver” doctrine of Parden 
v. Terminal Railway of Alabama Docks Department, 377 U.S. 
184 (1964), as a basis for abrogation. Parden held that a state 
which engages in commercial activities subject to congressional 
regulation relinquishes its Eleventh Amendment immunity and 
subjects itself to suit in federal court for claims relating to those 
activities. See 377 U.S. at 192. This doctrine cannot support 
jurisdiction over Florida Prepaid, however, because Parden, 
which is grounded on the Commerce Clause, cannot stand after 
Seminole Tribe. The Court should therefore overrule whatever 
may be left of Parden. Moreover, as the Court of Appeals 
correctly held, even assuming that Parden remains good law, 
the Parden doctrine does not apply here.

(1) The District Court below held that Parden did not

that Parden was inapplicable on its own terms. Given the 
uncertainty that exists among the lower courts about Parden’s 
survival, this Court should take this opportunity to overrule 
Parden and make explicit what is implicit in Seminole Tribe: 
the Commerce Clause-based doctrine of “constructive waiver”

If the teaching of Seminole Tribe is followed, a State’s mere 
participation in an area subject to federal legislation that includes 
a congressional attempt to curtail sovereign immunity cannot 
create a “waiver” of immunity. It would be directly contrary to 
Seminole Tribe to permit a congressional attempt to abrogate

positing a “waiver” that arises out of nothing more than a State’s

achieve indirectly, through “waiver,” what it cannot do directly, 
through express abrogation. 


Parden effectively permits Congress to require States to 
cede their immunity. Accordingly, a Parden waiver can be 
implemented only where Congress retains authority to curtail 
the Eleventh Amendment. No such authority remains, however,

the Fourteenth Amendment. The decision in Parden that States

C. The TRCA Is Insufficient To Trigger A Waiver Of 
Immunity Under Parden Because The Statute Does Not 
Provide Clear Notice To States That Engaging In A 
Specifically Defined Type Of Commerce Will Result In 
The Loss Of Eleventh Amendment Protection. 


Even assuming that, theoretically, a State’s operation of a 
prepaid tuition program could be considered the type of 
commercial activity that falls within the narrow scope of 
Parden’s constructive waiver doctrine, the TRCA is plainly 
insufficient to trigger such a waiver. The statute simply cannot 
be read as an adequate warning from Congress that the States 
are deemed to forfeit their Eleventh Amendment immunity if 
they decide to operate prepaid tuition programs. The TRCA 
does not regulate state prepaid tuition programs or any related 
financial programs or products. Like the Lanham Acct itself, the 
TRCA simply applies to commerce in general, conceived in the 
broadest possible terms. Because the TRCA does not set forth a 
congressional plan with respect to a particular type of commercial 
activity, it cannot reasonably be interpreted to alert States to the 
possibility of the loss of sovereign immunity resulting from 
participation in a defined type of commerce. 


Thus, the Court of Appeals was mistaken in stating that 
Parden could have been triggered here because 


the [TRCA] declares that if a state or its 
instrumentality chooses to engage in a proprietary 
activity that potentially falls under the Lanham Act, 
it can be held liable in federal court for violations of 
that Act. 


Pet. App. at 21a. There are few types of proprietary activity that 
do not “potentially fall” within the scope of the Lanham Act, 
which forbids false advertising, trademark infringement, and 
other offenses in interstate commerce generally. 


42 


Parden itself sets forth the requirement of an explicit 
connection between the regulation of a specific type of commerce 
and a State’s decision to “waive” its immunity by participating 
in that type of commerce: 


By adopting and ratifying the Commerce Clause, the 
States empowered Congress to create such a right of 
action against interstate railroads; by enacting the 
FELA in the exercise of this power, Congress 
conditioned the right to operate a railroad in 
interstate commerce upon amenability to suit in 
federal court as provided by the Act; by thereafter 
operating a railroad in interstate commerce, Alabama 
must be taken to have accepted that condition and 
thus to have consented to suit. 


377 U.S. at 192 (emphasis added). In contrast, through the TRCA 
Congress has not conditioned the operation of a prepaid tuition 
program in interstate commerce on anything, let alone the 
forfeiture of Eleventh Amendment immunity. Congress has 
simply declared that States which violate the Lanham Act may 
be sued in federal court. 


The Brief for the United States correctly describes the

Clearly, however, Congress has not unambiguously provided 
that the operation of a prepaid tuition program will subject a 
State to suit in federal court; the TRCA nowhere mentions 
prepaid tuition programs. The only “conduct” described in the 
statute as the basis for the loss of immunity is the conduct of 
violating the Lanham Act. 


A statute that applies to a// interstate commerce, and merely 
warns States of the loss of sovereign immunity if they violate 
the statute in any way, cannot be sufficient to trigger Parden. 
The contrary result would tear the Parden doctrine loose from 
its already fragile moorings in the law of waiver and make it a 
free-floating abrogation engine just as powerful as, and little 
different from, the Commerce Clause abrogation doctrine this 
Court rejected in Seminole Tribe.
