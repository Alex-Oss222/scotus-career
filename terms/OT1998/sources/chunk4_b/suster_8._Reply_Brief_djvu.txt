REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


Respondents, as well as the court below, have ignored 
one of the fundamental principles of American liberty: 
judicial impartiality. Without the assurance of neutrality and 
impartiality of judicial officers, the law as we know it would 
cease to exist. Interpretation of the law would go to the 
highest bidder, and the judiciary will have succumbed to the 
political pressures of the day. 


A compelling, and indeed paramount state interest in 
the impartiality of the judiciary must be recognized if state 
laws are to be administered according to consistent, neutral 
principles. Otherwise, elected judges will not be valued by 
state citizens as impartial arbiters of the law, but viewed as 
demeaning the rule of law in exchange for political power. 


This case presents a unique opportunity for the Court 
to assure the impartiality and the perception of impartiality of 
the elected judiciary in Ohio, and around the country. The 
Court must take this case to recognize the compelling state 
interest in a neutral and unbiased state judiciary, and to hold 
that such an interest can justify limits on expenditures in 
judicial campaigns. 


L. THE SIXTH CIRCUIT’S FIRST ERROR 
IS IN THE SIMULTANEOUS REVIEW 
OF THE FORMER CANON AND 
REMAND OF THE AMENDED CANON 


The court below made a fundamental error in 
remanding the amendment to Canon VII(C)(6)(a) for a “full 
review” and simultaneously reviewing and rejecting the 
former version of that Canon. Respondents’ briefs in 
opposition seek to separately justify the two actions of the 


2 


Sixth Circuit. See, Brief for Respondents Boyko, Kane and 
Saferin, (“Boyko brief’) p. 9-13, 13-14; Brief for 
Respondents Suster & Cleary (“Suster brief’) p 7-9. By 
handling the issues separately, Respondents attempt to make 
the remand and review each seem reasonable. Indeed, either 
action alone, given the proper circumstances, might have 
been reasonable; the lower court's simultaneous review and 
remand is what renders its holding fundamentally erroneous. 


If, during the course of litigation involving a facial 
challenge to a law, the law undergoes an amendment, the 
federal courts are enjoined to review only the new law. 
Diffenderfer v. Central Baptist Church, 404 U.S. 412, 414 


~~ (1972). As explained in the Petition, the courts have 


consistently exercised one of three options: (1) if the 
amendment moots the appeal, the lower court decision is 
vacated and the case dismissed; (2) if the amendment requires 
additional findings of fact or legal analysis, the case is 
sometimes remanded for further review; (3) if the amendment 
does not make substantial changes, the appellate court will 
review the law as it now stands. See, Petition, p. 9 Review 
of the former law is not an option. 


The Sixth Circuit, in its attempt to “split the baby” has 
instead created a split in the circuits that must be resolved. 
The holding below contradicts a long line of cases in the 
circuit courts, holding that the court must review the new 
law, and exercise one of the options outlined above. See, 
Petition, p. 10-11. This line of cases is best represented by 
Rosenstiel v. Rodriguez, 101 F.3d 1544 (8" Cir 1996). 
Respondent’s main argument against review of the new law is 
that the amendment came after the main briefing was 


2 exe 


3 


complete.’ However, in Rosenstiel, a case virtually identical 
to this one, the Eighth Circuit reviewed the new law, even 
though the law was amended after both briefing and oral 
argument. Respondents can distinguish Rosenstiel only on 
the basis that the parties there did not want remand. Boyko 
brief, p. 11. In this case, no party requested remand; the 
court remanded sua sponte. If the court below wished to 
“protect” the Respondents from potential discipline, see 
Suster brief, p. 8, it had two options: to review the new law, 
and the injunction, or remand both the injunction and the new 
law for review. 


Above all, a simultaneous review of the former law 
and remand of the amended law results in an untenable 
procedural posture. Respondents argue that the remand is 
necessary for a “full development of the factual record and the 
legal arguments of the parties.” Boyko brief, p. 10-11. 
However, such a “full development” has been forever 
precluded by the court’s simultaneous determination that the 
former Canon is unconstitutional. Having determined that 
Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1 (1976) precludes any caps on 
campaign spending, the Sixth Circuit has already decided any 
legal arguments with regard to compelling state interest for 
either Canon. Factual findings with regard to the amended 
Canon would be a vain act, because under the Sixth Circuit’s 
analysis, the district court will never reach the issue most 
affected by a factual development: narrow tailoring. 


' Contrary to assertions by Respondents Boyko, Kane and 
Saferin, the amendment was brought to their attention and to 
the attention of the Sixth Circuit in Petitioner’s reply brief 
before that court. In addition, the motion dealing with this 
issue came months before oral argument, and therefore 
afforded Respondents more than adequate time to respond. 


4 


In addition, as argued by Amici States, the actions of 
the Sixth Circuit put all States in the Circuit in a difficult 
position. The States are strongly discouraged from improving 
a law through amendment when there is pending litigation 
involving that law, for fear of a procedural mess similar to the 
one here. 


Simply put, the Sixth Circuit has created an impossible 
situation for the district court, and produced a split in the 
circuits on the issue of the review of an amended law and the 
procedural options available when a law has been amended. 
The Court must take this case in order to resolve the split, 
and review the law as it now stands. At the least, the Court 
should vacate the Sixth Circuit's opinion and allow the district 
court a clean slate upon which to review the new Canon. 


Il. THE SIXTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN 
HOLDING THAT LIMITS ON 
CAMPAIGN SPENDING ARE FOREVER 
PRECLUDED BY BUCALEY 


Contrary to the assertions of al! Respondents, Buckley 
held only that campaign spending limits for federal /egislative 
and executive campaigns were not narrowly tailored to serve 
the only governmental interest put forward in that case: the 
prevention of corruption and the appearance of corruption. 
See, Boyko brief, p. 15-16; Suster brief, p. 10-11. This Court 
has always left open the possibility that other compelling 
interests might exist that would justify limits on campaign 
speech, perhaps including limits on spending. FEC v. NC- 
PAC 470 U.S. 480, 496-497 (1985). 


Respondents stridently insist that there is no case from 
this Court or the Sixth Circuit which expands the list of 
compelling state interests justifying limits on political speech. 


5 


See, Suster brief p. 11. That is the very reason Petitioners are 
before the Court today. If there were a case dealing with 
judicial elections, or otherwise expanding the list of 
compelling state interests, the need for a grant of certiorari in 
this case would be far less compelling. 


Respondents also argue that the Court need not 
examine the proposed compelling state interest because limits 
on campaign spending are not the least restrictive means of 
achieving that interest. Boyko brief, p. 16-17. Respondents 
have the analysis exactly backwards. Before arguments can 
be made about least restrictive means, the exact nature of the 
compelling state interest must be determined. 


Respondents quote extensively from Buckley to 
emphasize their point that judicial elections are amenable to 
the same restrictions as those for legislative or executive 
office. See, Suster brief p. 14-16. However, these quotes 
merely serve to illustrate the stark differences. In a legislative 
or executive election, a restriction on spending may “restrict 
the number of issues discussed” and “the depth of their 
exploration.” In a judicial campaign, the number of issues is 
already severely restricted. The judicial candidate cannot 
discuss how he would vote on a particular case, or even his 
views On a subject that may come before him. 


Similarly, an assurance of the “unfettered interchange 
of ideas for the bringing about of political and social changes 
desired by the people,” is exactly contrary to the nature of a 
judicial campaign. Political and social changes desired by the 
people are the province of the legislature and executive, 
decidedly not the province of the judiciary. Restricting such 
an “unfettered exchange” in a judicial election is not only 
appropriate, but desirable. The public has a right to an 
elected judiciary unfettered by the pre-election partiality 


6 


which is the natural result of unlimited exchange and debate 
of ideas regarding social policy. We should not permit a 
judge to be torn between a decision based on the law and 
facts and a decision made in fulfillment of a campaign 
promise. 


In short, Respondents have pointed out the two most 
significant reasons for the Court to take this case: (1) to 
examine judicial elections for the first time, and determine 
whether the list of compelling state interests justifying limits 
on political speech should be expanded; and (2) to determine 
whether the compelling state interest of the preservation of 
impartiality and the appearance of impartiality justifies limits 
on campaign spending in judicial elections. 


CONCLUSION 


For the foregoing reasons, the petitioners respectfully 
request the Court to grant the writ.
