Pursuant to Rule 15.6 of the Rules of this Court, 
Petitioner Irene L. Wright respectfully submits this brief 
in reply to the Brief for the Respondent in Opposition 
filed with this Court on December 14, 1998 (“Res. Br.”). 


INTRODUCTION 


First, in opposing petitioner’s writ, respondent Ernst 
& Young LL? (“E&Y”) argues that under any of the 
disparate tests applied by the various circuits for deter- 
mining liability for secondary actors under the Securities 
and Exchange Act of 1934 (the “Exchange Act”) interpret- 
ing Central Bank of Denver v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, 
511 U.S. 164 (1994), Ms. Wright’s claim would be dis- 
missed. Not only is this argument factually inaccurate (as 
discussed below), but respondent, in analyzing three of 
the different tests currently being applied respectively by 
three of the circuits to have considered this issue, has 
unwittingly highlighted the need for review by this 
Court. 

Second, given respondent’s acknowledgment of this 
clear conflict among the circuits, E&Y’s assertion, made 
without any legal authority, that it is somehow too early 
for the Court to consider the issue because there is not yet 
sufficient confusion among the circuits is without merit. 
As several of this Court’s prior decisions make clear, 
there is no established threshold for the number of cir- 
cuits that must disagree on an issue before this Court will 
address conflicting interpretations of a question of law 
among the circuits. Indeed, the varying interpretations by 
the Second Circuit on the issue presented in this petition 
alone demonstrate that the time is ripe for clarification of 
this Court’s decision in Central Bank. 

Finally, conspicuous in its absence is any reference by 
respondent to the split among the circuits on the key 
issue of attribution. Indeed, respondent's failure to even 
attempt to reconcile the holdings of Wright v. Ernst & 
Young LLP, 152 F.3d 169, 175 (2d Cir. 1998) (“a secondary 
actor cannot incur primary liability under the Act for a 


2 


statement not attributed to that actor”), and Klein v. Boyd, 
[Current Transfer Binder] Fed. Sec. L. Rep. (CCH) 
{ 90,136, at 90,324 (3d Cir. Feb. 12, 1998). vacated, Klein v. 
Boyd |Current Transfer Binder] Fed. Sec. L. Rep. (CCH) 
{ 90,165 (3d Cir. Mar. 9, 1998) (liability may attach “even 
when the secondary actors are not identified to the inves- 
tor”), undermines respondent's hypothesis that insuffi- - 
cient disagreement exists among the circuits for this 
Court to grant petitioner’s writ. 


ARGUMENT 


I. Respondent Has Mischaracterized Petitioner’s 
Factual Claims 


As an initial matter, respondent's recitation of the 
facts on which it bases its argument that plaintiff cannot 
state a claim under any circuit's interpretation of Central 
Bank is inaccurate. Respondent asserts that petitioner’s 
allegations concerned E&Y’s recklessness in not discover- 
ing that “rogue employees of [BT Office Products Interna- 
tional, Inc. (“BT”)] had engaged in theft and fraud.” Res. 
Br. at 3. This is not the gravamen of petitioner’s claim nor 
is it the question reviewed by the Second Circuit below. 
Rather, petitioner claims that E&Y is culpable under Sec- 
tion 10(b) of the Exchange Act because it represented to 
management of BT that BT’s 1995 year-end financial 
results were sufficiently accurate to disseminate to the 
public despite E&Y’s knowledge of numerous unex- 
plained “red flags” discovered in the course of its audit of 
BT, including a $3.9 million under-accrual in accounts 
payable at BT’s New York Division. Although E&Y dis- 
covered this under-accrual in January of 1996, it failed to 
investigate further, concluding, without reasonable basis, 
that the under-accrual was not material because there had 
been a similar under-accrual the previous year. E&Y’s 
conclusion, reported to management of BT, that the 
under-accruals somehow offset each other, was reckless 
in the extreme because, inter alia, E&Y had not even 


| 


audited BT’s New York Division in 1994. Thus, E&Y had 
no idea whether the 1995 under-accrual had any relation 
to that of 1994. 

Nevertheless, on January 22, 1996, having completed 
its audit field work for the 1995 fiscal year, the audit 
engagement partners from E&Y gave the Chief Financial 
Officer of BT, John McKiernan, their assurance that BT’s 

~ 1995 financial results were accurate. E&Y knew at the 
time of the meeting that the purpose of BT’s obtaining 
E&Y’s assurance on the accuracy of the 1995 financial 
results was to immediately release them to the public. 
Based on E&Y’s assurances, without which, according to 
Mr. McKiernan, the disclosure of the 1995 financial 
results would not have occurred, BT issued a press 
release on January 30, 1996, (the “January Press Release”) 
announcing what were undisputedly materially inflated 
and false financial results. As a result, BT’s share price 
rose nearly eight dollars during the following two 
months. When the inaccuracies of those financial results 
were revealed on March 28, 1996, the share price dropped 
nearly 25% in one day. 


II. The Question Presented For Review Is Properly 
Before This Court 


Resting on its mischaracterization of the facts, 
respondent argues that because the January Press Release 
does not recite E&Y’s assurances to BT as to the accuracy 
of the 1995 financial results or BT’s complete reliance on 
those assurances, E&Y’s misstatements were not commu- 
nicated to investors, and, thus, the question petitioner has 
presented for review is not raised in this case. Res. Br. at 
7. A review of the January Press Release itself belies this 
assertion. 

The January Press Release contains two parts: (1) a 
narrative description of BT’s 1995 operations and the 
reasons for its 1995 financial results; and (2) the numeri- 
cal financial results themselves, contained both in the 
body of the January Press Release and in an annexed 


4 


chart. It is the numerical results alone that petitioner 
contends are false and actionable, not the accompanying 
narrative. It is these numerical results that E&Y knew 
would be made public if, and only if, E&Y assured BT 
management that the results were sufficiently accurate 
such that any subsequent change in the year-end 
numbers would be immaterial. These figures and their 
dissemination were, thus, virtually the sole province of 
E&Y, with BT acting as a mere conduit to the investing 
public. 

Respondent simply glosses over these allegations by 
asserting that the public never knew about E&Y’s “pri- 
vate advice to its client.” Res. Br. at 4. However, that is 
precisely the question raised by this petition - whether 
the investing public’s lack of knowledge of the pivotal 
role of a secondary actor, like E&Y here, in the prepara- 
tion and dissemination of false and misleading financial 
results that such secondary actor knew would be commu- 
nicated to the public, shields that secondary actor from 
liability. This narrow attribution question is one over 
which the circuits have split, and is, therefore, properly 
before the Court at this time. 


III. The Klein Decision Is Squarely At Odds With The 
Second Circuit 


By ignoring the actual question presented by this 
petition, respondent avoids discussing the reasoning 
which has led to the split between the Second and Third 
Circuits regarding the need for attribution. Instead, it 
simply insists that the Third Circuit’s ruling in Klein has 
no precedential value because it was vacated pending en 
banc review. In Klein, the Third Circuit sets forth a test 
that explicitly disagrees with the Second Circuit’s holding 
in Wright. The Klein court found that attribution was 
irrelevant as long as the secondary actor “significantly 
participate|d] in the creation of their client’s misrepresen- 
tations, to such a degree that they may fairly be deemed 
authors or co-authors. ... ” (CCH) ¥ 90,136 at 90,324. In 


5 


Klein, a law firm was subject to liability for its role in 
disseminating disclosure documents even though the 
firm was not identified in the documents. Clearly, given 
the undisputed facts here, E&Y could be deemed a co- 
author of the statement under the Klein test, since without 
its participation, no year-end financial results or com- 
mentary about those results would have been issued by 
BT. 


The ultimate conclusion of the en banc panel in Klein 
will never be known, because the case has now been 
settled for an undisclosed amount.! However, this Court 
may take note of a vacated decision in its evaluation of a 
split among the circuit courts. In Bateman Eichler, Hill 
Richards, Inc. v. Berner, 472 U.S. 299, 305 (1985), this Court 
granted certiorari because “[t]he lower courts have 
divided over the proper scope of the in pari delicto 
defense in securities litigation.” In coming to this conclu- 
sion, this Court cited a vacated case from the Fifth Cir- 
cuit, Woolf v. $.D. Cohn & Co., 515 F.2d 591 (5th Cir. 1975), 
vacated and remanded on other groun 4s, 426 U.S. 944 (1976). 
See Bateman, 472 U.S. at 305 n.10. See also Basic, Inc. v. 
Levinson, 485 U.S. 224, 247 n. 25 (1988) (noting a vacated 
Second Circuit decision in demonstrating consensus 
among circuit courts). Therefore, that the original Third 
Circuit panel’s decision in Klein was vacated does not 
diminish in the least the evidence it provides of confusion 
among the circuits on the key attribution question. This 
Court may certainly consider that evidence in evaluating 
whether to grant a writ of certiorari. 


Indeed, the district court for the Northern District of 
Georgia, foreshadowing events in the Eleventh Circuit, 
recently issued a decision which closely follows the rea- 
soning in Klein. See Carley Capital Group v. Deloitte & 
Touche, L.L.P., No. 1:97-CV-3183-TWT, slip op., (N.D. Ga.

IV. Respondent’s Conduct Is Actionable Under The 
Ninth And Tenth Circuit Tests 


Respondent also argues that this case should not be 
reviewed because under the facts presented here, E&Y 
could not be held liable under any of the different tests 
utilized by the other respective circuits that have consid- 
ered the issue. However, respondent would not only be 
liable under the Third Circuit’s Klein test, but would 
likely have been held liable under the other different tests 
interpreting Central Bank devised by the Ninth and Tenth 
Circuits. Importantly, all of these standards contradict at 
least one version of the Second Circuit’s various tests.2 


The Ninth Circuit in In re Software Toolworks Inc., 50 
F.3d 615, 628 n.3 (9th Cir. 1994), stated that a secondary 
actor could incur primary liability if that actor had 
played a “significant role” in the dissemination of mis- 
leading statements (the “substantial participation” test). 
In Software Toolworks, the accounting firm defendant was 
subject to liability where it had helped its client prepare 
two misleading letters to the Securities and Exchange 
Commission. Respondent asserts that the accounting 
firm’s conduct in Software Toolworks went far beyond that 
of E&Y’s alleged conduct here. Res. Br. at 10-11. That 


* For a discussion of the various Second Circuit rulings on 
this issue, see Petition for a Writ of Certiorari (“Petition”) at 8-9. 


7 


assertion ignores petitioner’s key allegations. Quite sim- 
ply, but for E&Y’s representations to BT, no public misrep- 
resentation would have occurred at all, because the 
| January Press Release would never have been issued. In 
fact, E&Y’s participation in the dissemination of the false 
financial results to the public was not merely “substan- 
tial” or “significant,” it was absolutely definitive. Differing 
sharply from the Second Circuit’s decision here, Software 
Toolworks did not require direct attribution; the focus was 
on the conduct of the secondary actor, not its identifica- 
tion. Thus, E&Y, according to its own rendition of the 
facts, would be liable under the Ninth Circuit’s test. 


Respondent also asserts that the Tenth Circuit's deci- 
sion in Anixter v. Home-Stake Production Co., 77 F.3d 1215 
(10th Cir. 1996), applies the same test as did the Second 
Circuit in this case (Res. Br. at 12), and therefore that 
petitioner’s claims would fail under the Tenth Circuit's 
test. Respondent is wrong on both counts. For primary 
liability of a secondary actor to exist, both the Second 
Circuit in Wright and the Tenth Circuit in Anixter have 
held that there is no requirement that an alleged violator 
directly communicate with investors so long as the sec- 
ondary actor knew or should have known that its repre- 
sentation would be communicated to investors. However, 
the Second Circuit’s decision in Wright injected the addi- 
tional condition that “a secondary actor cannot incur 
primary liability under the Act for a statement not attrib- 
uted to that actor at the time of its dissemination.” Peti- 
tion at 12a. It is the addition of this attribution element in 
the Second Circuit’s interpretation of Central Bank (which 
is not found in Anixter and is, in fact, contradicted by 
Klein) which gives rise to this petition. 


Respondent seizes on the phrase “his misrepresenta- 
tion” in Anixter to support its conclusion that the Second 
Circuit and Anixter actually applied the same test, despite 
that the Second Circuit requires attribution and Anixter 
does not. Respondent essentially argues that the above 


8 


language in Anixter means that the exact misrepresenta- 
tion of the secondary actor must be repeated by the 
primary actor. However, the Anixter court did not so 
hold. Rather, the court set forth a flexible standard, sig- 
nificantly different than that used by the Wright court 
(and also different from that in Software Toolworks) and 
one under which E&Y would likely have incurred pri- 
mary liability here: 

Reading the language of § 10(b) and 10b-5 

through the lens of Central Bank of Denver, we 

conclude that in order for accountants to “use or 
employ” a “deception” actionable under the 

antifraud law, they must themselves make a 

false or misleading statement (or omission) that 

they know or should know will reach potential 
investors. In addition to being consistent with 

the language of the statute, this rule, though far 

from a bright line, provides more guidance to 

litigants than a rule allowing liability to attach 

to an accountant or other outside professional 

who provided “significant” or “substantial” 

assistance to the representations of others. 
77 F.3d at 1226-27 (emphasis added). 

The Tenth Circuit’s use of the word “reach,” and 
comment that this is not a bright line test, counsel against 
respondent's restrictive reading of Anixter. Since plaintiff 
has properly alleged that E&Y knew that its representa- 
tions as to the accuracy of the false financial results 
would be communicated to the market through the public 
dissemination of these figures, E&Y would be liable 
under Anixter. 


V. The Issue Raised In This Petition Is Ripe For 
Consideration By This Court 


Respondent urges this Court to wait for lower courts 
te continue to consider the issue raised in this petition 
since “only” three circuit courts (respondent fails to 
include the vacated Klein decision, which would bring the 


ee 


9 


total to four) have done so thus far (Res. Br. at 16).3 
Petitioner disagrees. This Court has not made a practice 
of waiting for all circuits to weigh in ona question before 
clarifying its own decisions. Rather, it has been the prac- 
tice of this Court to grant certiorari when important issues 
require clarification, without adhering to a rigid rule 
concerning the number of lower courts to address a par- 
ticular issue. 


In one leading securities case, Basic, Inc. v. Levinson, 
485 U.S. 224, 230 (1988), this Court granted certiorari to 
resOive an issue, that had been addressed by only three 
circuit courts, concerning the proper materiality standard 
to be applied in securities cases. See also Aaron v. Securities 
and Exchange Commission, 446 U.S. 680 (1980) (split among 
two circuit courts and a district court on one issue and 
three circuit courts on another). In fact, this Court has 
granted certiorari in securities cases in the absence of a 
circuit split. See, e.g., Dirks v. Securities and Exchange Com- 
mission, 463 U.S. 646, 652 (1983) (in clarifying an earlier 
case, this Court stated that “[i]n view of the importance 
to the SEC and to the securities industry of the question 
presented by this case, we granted a writ of certiorari.”). 
Further, respondent offers no countervailing authority 
illustrating a practice of this Court to await a specific 
number of circuit court decisions or a particular level of 
chaos or legal uncertainty within the federal judiciary 
before granting certiorari.
