BRIEF FOR THE RESPONDENT IN OPPOSITION 


Petitioner seeks review f a decision of the United States 
Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit affirming the 
dismissal of her complaint under Section 10(b) of the 
Securities Exchange Act, 15 U.S.C. § 78j, and Rule 10b-5 
thereunder, 17 C.F.R. § 240.10b-5, against respondent Ernst 
& Young LLP (“Emst & Young”) in connection with her 
purchase of common stock of BT Office Products 
International (“BT” or “the Company”). The district court 
dismissed the action because petitioner sought to base liability 
not on any misstatement made by Ernst & Young, but rather 
on a BT press release announcing the Company’s unaudited 
year-end financial results that did not mention Ernst & Young 
and that explicitly described the financial results as 
“unaudited.” In the district court’s view, petitioner’s claim 
was an impermissible attempt to end-run this Court’s decision 
in Central Bank of Denver v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, 
511 U.S. 164 (1994), which held that there can be no liability 


2 


in private damage actions for aiding and abetting alleged 
violations of Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5. The Second 
Circuit affirmed, holding that petitioner’s claim is foreclosed 
by Central Bank and by its earlier decision in Shapiro v. 
Cantor, 123 F.3d 717 (2d Cir. 1997), which adopted a 
“bright line” test requiring a material misstatement or 
omission by the defendant, as opposed to mere review and 
approval of another party’s misstatement or omission, as a 
prerequisite to liability. The Second Circuit also concluded 
that petitioner’s claim would be defective even under the 
“substantial participation” test adopted by the Ninth Circuit 
because there is no allegation that Ernst & Young participated 
in the creation of the BT press release. 


In attempting to persuade this Court that the decision 
below is worthy of review, petitioner argues that there is 
serious circuit conflict and confusion on the scope of liability 
for Section 10(b) violations following Central Bank. As 
shown below, however, the particular question presented by 
the Petition — whether an auditor can be held liable for 
communicating false information to a company that it knows 
the company will disclose — is not even raised in this case, 
for there is no allegation that Ernst & Young’s private advice 
to its client BT ever became public. Moreover, a ruling by 
this Court on the appropriate standard for liability would be 
premature, as that issue is now just beginning to percolate at 
the circuit level. Thus far, one circuit, the Tenth, has joined 
the court below in applying a “bright line” test that requires, 
as a prerequisite for liability, a material misstatement (or 
omission) that is made by the defendant and relied on by the 
plaintiff. Another circuit, the Ninth, has adopted an 
apparently more liberal “substantial participation” test that 
imposes liability on those who play a key role in creating a 
material misstatement that is in fact made by someone else. 
Petitioner fails to acknowledge, however, that even under the 
Ninth Circuit test, there could be no liability here because 
there is no allegation that Ernst & Young contributed to the 


3 


creation of BT’s press release. Petitioner’s inability to cite a 
single decision applying any legal standard that would sustain 
her allegations demonstrates the unworthiness of her 
complaint and this Petition. 


STATEMENT 


1. The Complaint’s Allegations. On January 30, 
1996, BT issued a press release announcing its unaudited 
1995 financial results. The press release expressly described 
the Company’s financial results as “unaudited” and made no 
mention of Ernst & Young, BT’s independent outside auditor. 
Pet. Sa. 


Three months later, on March 28, 1996, BT announced 
that it had discovered accounting and financial reporting 
irregularities, as well as inventory theft and embezzlement, at 
one of its subsidiaries and that, as a result, it was reducing its 
previously reported (unaudited) net income for the year. Pet. 
Sa. Approximately two weeks after that announcement, 
petitioner filed a class action lawsuit in the Southern District 
of New York, accusing the Company and a number of its 
officers of securities fraud. Pet. 3a-4a. In March 1997, 
those defendants agreed to pay $1,480,000 to the class in 
settlement of these claims. Pet. 4a. 


On the last day before the statute of limitations would 
otherwise have expired, petitioner filed a second class action 
complaint against Ernst & Young. The only actionable 
misstatements alleged in the complaint were statements made 
by BT in its January 30 press release announcing the 
Company’s unaudited 1995 financial results. Petitioner 
alleged that Ernst & Young was responsible for the press 
release because it was supposedly reckless in not discovering, 
before the release was made, that rogue employees of the 
Company had engaged in theft and fraud. Petitioner alleged 
that before BT issued the January 30 press release, Ermst & 
Young had provided “oral assurances” to the Company that 
its 1995 financial statements were accurate. She further 


4 


claimed that it was based on these assurances that BT made 
its earnings announcement. Although the complaint alleged 
that “the market” believed that the release of BT’s 1995 
financial results must have been approved by Ernst & Young 
because companies do not issue such press releases without 
their outside auditors’ approval, the press release itself did 
not substantiate that allegation. Instead, it described BT’s 
1995 financial results as “unaudited.” Pet. 3a-Sa. 


2. The Decisions Below. The district court granted 
Ernst & Young’s motion to dismiss, holding that petitioner’s 
allegations would be insufficient under any standard of 
primary liability adopted by the federal courts in the 
aftermath of Central Bank. Pet. 23a-25a. The court 
correctly observed (see n.3 infra) that “[v]irtually all cases 
. . . have required a greater degree of participation in the 
fraudulent conduct than the mere review and approval of 
unaudited figures.” Pet. 24a. The court rejected petitioner’s 
claim that Ernst & Young had “endorsed” the Company’s 
January 30 announcement; given the press release’s 
characterization of the Company’s financial results as 
unaudited, “E&Y’s only ‘representation’ as to the accuracy 
of those figures was that it made no representation at all.” 
Pet. 23a-24a. The court concluded that sustaining the 
complaint in light of Ernst & Young’s “clearly tangential role 
in the alleged fraud would effectively revive aiding and 
abetting liability under a different name, and would therefore 
run afoul of the Supreme Court’s holding in Central Bank.” 
Pet. 25a. 


The Second Circuit affirmed, relying on Central Bank 
and its own recent decision in Shapiro. In Shapiro, the court 
noted two differing standards in determining liability for 
Section 10(b) violations and elected to follow a “bright line” 
test under which the defendant cannot be held liable unless it 
actually made a material misstatement or omission on which 
the plaintiff relied. 123 F.3d at 720. Anything short of such 
conduct, the court concluded, was mere aiding and abetting, 


5 


which is insufficient to trigger liability under Central Bank. 
Id. 


In the instant case, the Second Circuit held that the 
requisite standard was not satisfied because: 


BT’s press release did not attribute any assurances to 
Ernst & Young and, in fact, did not mention Ernst & 
Young at all. Thus, Ernst & Young neither directly nor 
indirectly communicated misrepresentations to investors. 
Therefore, the amended complaint failed to allege that 
Ernst & Young made “a material misrepresentation (or 
Omission) on which a purchaser or seller of securities 
relie[d].” Central Bank, 511 U.S. at 191. 


Pet. 12a-13a. 


Nor would the result likely be any different, the court 
reasoned, under the Ninth Circuit’s “substantial participation” 
test. See Pet. 14a. Although declining to adopt that 
standard, the Second Circuit concluded that under it, “we 
would be hard pressed to conclude that the amended 
complaint alleged an actionable misrepresentation within the 
meaning of § 10(b).” Pet. 14a. 


REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 


The Petition advances four principal reasons for review. 
First, petitioner argues that the Second Circuit standard is at 
odds with the Ninth Circuit’s “substantial participation” test 
that was announced in a footnote in In re Software Toolworks 
Inc., 50 F.3d 615, 628 n.3 (9th Cir. 1994). Second, 
petitioner asserts that the decision below conflicts with the 
Tenth Circuit’s ruling in Anixter v. Home-State Product Co., 
77 F.3d 1215 (10th Cir. 1996), a leading decision addressing 
the scope of liability in the wake of Central Bank. Third, 
petitioner claims to have found yet a third test for primary 
liability in the Third Circuit’s vacated decision in Klein y. 
Boyd, {Current Transfer Binder] Fed. Sec. L. Rep. (CCH) 4 
90,136 (3d Cir. Feb. 12, 1998), vacated [Current Transfer 


6 


Binder] ¢ 90,165 (3d Cir. Mar. 9, 1998). Finally, petitioner 
urges that review by this Court is necessary because of 
internal confusion within the Second Circuit on the 
appropriate liability standard. 


As explained below, review should be denied because the 
question presented by petitioner is not even raised by the 
allegations in this case. Further, although the “substantial 
participation” test appears to differ from the “bright line” 
rule followed by the Second and Tenth Circuits, the Ninth 
Circuit standard has thus far gained no sizable following and, « 
as the courts below recognized, would not save petitioner’s 
claim in any event. These factors make this case a 
particularly poor vehicle for addressing the primary liability 
issue. 


In addition, there is no conflict between the Tenth and 
Second Circuits. The Anixter standard is the same as the 
Second Circuit test - unsurprising given that the court below 
cited Anixter favorably in this case and in Shapiro. That 
leaves petitioner’s contentions that this Court should exercise 
its discretionary jurisdiction because of an allegedly 
conflicting - but vacated - panel decision in Klein and 
because of supposed intra-circuit confusion. Neither ground 
would be good reason for granting review, and neither ground 
is meritorious even if it were certworthy. 


Finally, the time is not yet ripe for this Court to address 
the precise scope of primary liability under Section 10(b) 
following Central Bank. That issue is only beginning to be 
considered in the courts of appeals. If this Court’s 
intervention is ultimately needed, the Court will benefit from 
consideration of the issue in a number of different factual 
contexts by a number of different courts.

NOT RAISED IN THIS CASE s

Can a secondary actor not identified in a public statement

BT’s press release did not attribute any assurances to 
Ernst & Young and, in fact, did not mention Ernst & 
Young at all. Thus, Ernst & Young neither directly nor 
indirectly communicated misrepresentations to investors. 


Pet. 12a. As the court below aptly observed, “BT issued the

auditor is the audit opinion itself. See 1 [CCH] AICPA,

Accordingly, if this Court were inclined to address the

Ii. ANIXTER AND THE DECISION BELOW 
APPLY THE SAME TEST 


Ever in search of a conflict, petitioner argues that the 
decision below is incompatible with the Tenth Circuit’s ruling 
in Anixter. Pet. 4. Specifically, petitioner insists that Anixter 
properly recognizes that a defendant can be liable for making 
an “indirect” material misstatement to investors, while the 
Second Circuit supposedly rejected that principle. Pet. 4, 7. 


Petitioner is forced to concede, however (see Pet. 9-10), 
that this argument does not survive a fair reading of the 
Second Circuit’s opinion. The court below explicitly 
recognized that “‘[t]here is no requirement that the alleged 
violator directly communicate misrepresentations to 
[investors] for primary liability to attach.’” Pet. 12a (quoting 
Anixter, 77 F.3d at 1226). 


Petitioner nonetheless persists in urging that the Second 
Circuit has “effectively” eliminated liability for “indirect” 
misrepresentations by requiring that any alleged misstatement 
be attributed to the defendant. Pet. 4, 10. Anixter, however, 
enforced the same requirement in holding, like the court 
below, that an alleged material misstatement must have been 
made by the defendant, and not someone else, in order for 
liability to attach. 


In Anixter, the court cautioned that “for an accountant’s 
misrepresentation to be actionable as a primary violation, 
there must be a showing that he knew or should have known 
that his representation would be communicated to investors 
because § 10(b)-and Rule 10b-5 focus on fraud made ‘in 
connection with the sale or purchase’ of a security.” 77 F.3d 
at 1226 (emphasis added). The Tenth Circuit explained that 
this requirement is essential after Central Bank and expressly 
rejected the view that the “‘substantial assistance’ element of 
aiding and abetting liability” can be “reformulate[d]” by the 
simple expedient of making an accountant liable for a client’s 


13 


Statement. 77 F.3d at 1226 n.10. In the Tenth Circuit’s 
view: 


The critical element separating primary from aiding and 
abetting violations is the existence of a representation 
either by statement or omission, made by the defendant, 
that is relied upon by the plaintiff. Reliance only on 
representations made by others cannot itself form the 
basis of liability. See Central Bank of Denver, 511 U.S. 
at 176-178, 114 S. Ct. at 1448. 


Anixter, T7 F.3d at 1225. This is the very same “bright line” 
tule adopted by the Second Circuit in Shapiro and applied by 
the court below. See Pet. 11a. 


Significantly, in applying that standard, the Tenth Circuit 
differentiated between primary and secondary claims in the 
same way that the Second Circuit distinguished between them 
in this case. The accountant in Anixter, Cross, had issued 
audit opinions on the financial statements of his client, Home- 
Stake, and had also assisted Home-Stake in preparing limited 
partnership offering materials. The court concluded that 
Cross could be held liable for his own public statements in 
the form of opinions - which, of course, were publicly 
attributed to him - but could nor be held liable for his client’s 
statements even though he may have contributed substantially 
to creating them. The court reversed a forty million dollar 
judgment in a twenty-year-old case because it was unclear 
from the verdict whether the jury had found Cross liable 
because of his role in Home-Stake’s misrepresentations “or 
whether his liability rested on actual representations he made 
that reached investors.” Jd. at 1230-1231. 


The court below likewise concluded that one cannot 
transform secondary liability claims prohibited by Central 
Bank into legitimate primary claims by the simple expedient 
of tagging the secondary actor, in this case the accountant, 
with the client’s statement. Accordingly, there is no 
substance to thi: supposed conflict - or to petitioner’s dark 


14 


prediction that the Second Circuit has “effectively eliminated 
liability for any indirect misstatements by a party no matter 
the degree of culpability of that party in perpetrating a fraud 
on the public.” Pet. 10. 


IV. THE VACATED KLEIN DECISION OFFERS NO 
REASON TO REVIEW THIS CASE 


The Petition relies heavily on the panel decision in Klein 
v. Boyd, {Current Transfer Binder] Fed. Sec. L. Rep. (CCH) 
¢ 90,136 (3d Cir. Feb. 12, 1998), vacated [Current Transfer 
Binder] ¢ 90,165 (3d Cir. Mar. 9, 1998), arguing that Klein 
is “remarkably similar” to this case (Pet. 5), and that the 
Third Circuit adopted a liability standard that differs from 
both the Second and Ninth Circuit tests. See Pet. 12-15. 
Even if this characterization of the decision were accurate, 
the fact that Klein was vacated wipes out any conflict. A 
recent Third Circuit order dismissing the Klein appeal also 
ensures that the panel decision will not be reinstated on en 
banc review. Klein v. Boyd, Nos. 97-1143 and 97-1261, 
Order dated Nov. 12, 1998. 


In any case, petitioner's characterization of Klein is 
inaccurate. The liability standard adopted by the panel 
decision is essentially the same as the Ninth Circuit test, as 
it would have imposed liability “when the person's 
participation in the creation of a statement containing a 
misrepresentation or omission of material fact is sufficiently 
significant that the statement can properly be attributed to that 
person as its author or co-author.” Klein, [Current Transfer 
Binder] Fed. Sec. L. Rep. (CCH) at 90,2525. 


Equally important, the factual allegations in Klein differ 
markedly from the allegations here. The lawyer-defendants in 
Klein were the primary authors of the allegedly misleading 
partnership disclosure packages (id. at 90,320). In this case, 
by contrast, there is no allegation that Emst & Young 
contributed at all to drafting the BT press release. Thus, 
even if Klein were valid precedent (which it is not), there is

Vv. ALLEGED SECOND CIRCUIT CONFUSION 
SHOULD NOT PROMPT THIS COURT’S REVIEW 


Petitioner argues that the Second Circuit has issued 
inconsistent decisions on the scope of liability under Section 
10(b), “indicating that the time is ripe for this Court to clarify 
its decision in Central Bank.” Pet. 8. It is well established, 
however, that conflicting decisions within a circuit raise a 
problem to be resolved by that circuit rather than by review 
in this Court. See, e.g., Wisniewski v. United States, 353 
U.S. 901, 902 (1957). 


Further, as the court below recognized (Pet. 13a-1i4a), 
there is no conflict between the decision below or Shapiro 
and the court’s prior decision in SEC v. First Jersey Securities 
Inc., 101 F.3d 1450 (2d Cir. 1996). In First Jersey 
Securities, a fraud action brought by the SEC, the Second 
Circuit held that where the sole owner, officer, and director 
of a company - Brennan - directed the employees of his 
company - First Jersey - to make false and misleading 
statements to customers as part of an elaborate scheme to 
defraud, Brennan was primarily liable for the actions of his 
employees, and responsible also as a “controlling person” 
under the Securities Exchange Act. 101 F.3d at 1471-1474. 
First Jersey Securities thus stands for the proposition that the 
owner and chief executive officer of a business cannot escape 
responsibility for securities frauds committed by his 
employees when the fraud “could only have occurred at the 
direction of upper level management,” and when the 
defendant “was engaged in the purposeful planning” of the 
fraud. 103 F.3d at 1471-1472. : 


Similarly, petitioner’s contention (Pet. 9), that the Second 
Circuit recently retreated from its “bright line” rule in SEC 
v. U.S. Environmental, Inc., 155 F.3d 107 (2d Cir. 1998), 


16 


reflects a misreading of that case. The court acknowledged 
that Section 10(b) prohibits market manipulation (id. at 108), 
and that a defendant who effects “the very buy and sell orders 
that manipulated [the company’s] stock upward” is primarily 
liable for the violation. 155 F.3d at 112. As the court 
observed, “if the trader who executes manipulative buy and 
sell orders is not a primary violator, it is difficult to imagine 
who would remain liable after Central Bank.” Id. 


Finally, we disagree that the time is ripe for this Court 
to clarify the scope of liability in the wake of Central Bank. 
Only three federal circuits have addressed the issue, which is 
in a state of flux and would undoubtedly benefit from further 
consideration by the lower courts.
