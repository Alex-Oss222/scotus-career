STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This case is about the failure of the Sixth Circuit to 
recognize, as a compelling state interest, a fundamental 
foundation of our democratic system -- the impartiality of the 
judicial system. In a country based upon the rule of law, there 
can be no more compelling state interest than the establishment 
and maintenance of a judiciary free from the appearance of 
partiality. The courts are, after all, the last resort for Americans 
to correct injustice caused by unfair bias in other branches of 
government. This is a particularly important issue to the 
country and the legal community at this time, as the American 
Bar Association prepares to consider the Report and 
Recommendation of its Task Force on Lawyer’s Political 
Contributions (“ABA Report”), which includes extensive 
sections discussing the elected judiciary, but specifically reserves 
comment on limits on campaign expenditures pending the 
outcome of this case. ABA Report, p. 23, n. 37. The Ohio 
Supreme Court adopted a Code of Judicial Conduct, including 
Canon VII(C)(6)(a) at issue here, in order to enact a narrowly 
tailored means to preserve public confidence in the integrity and 
impartiality of the State's elected judiciary. 


In 1992, the Board of Commissioners on Grievances and 
Discipline of the Ohio Supreme Court created a subcommittee 
to undertake a comprehensive review of the Ohio Code of 
Judicial Conduct. The next year, an Ad Hoc Committee was 
created by the Ohio State Bar Association to review campaign 
finances of the Ohio Supreme Court. Both committees 
recommended, among a number of suggestions, that campaign 
spending limits be placed on judicial candidates. Reports of 
both committees were submitted by Petitioner Chief Justice 
Moyer to a Special Committee for review. The Special 
Committee, in addition to reviewing the reports, authorized a 
public opinion poll regarding the perception of influence and 
partiality ‘n the State judiciary. That poll indicated that limits on 


5 


campaign expenditures would help improve public respect for 
judicial elections and judges in Ohio, and help eliminate the 
appearance of bias in the public’s eye. 


After this extensive examination of the issue of public 
confidence in the judicial system, the Ohio Supreme Court made 
a number of changes to the Code of Judicial Conduct. Among 
other things, a limit on campaign spending was considered a 
remedy that would enhance impartiality in the Ohio justice 
system, as well as foster the appearance of judicial neutrality 
throughout the State. To this end, the Ohio Supreme Court 
promulgated, among many other reforms, Canon VII(C)(6), 
which limits the amount a judicial candidate can spend on his 
campaign for an elected judgeship in Ohio. 


On August 9, 1996, Respondents Ronald Suster 
(“Suster”) and Patricia Cleary (“Cleary”), judges on the 
Cuyahoga County Common Pleas Court, filed suit seeking 
declaratory and injunctive relief from enforcement of Canon 
VII(C)(6)(a). Suster was seeking election in November 1996 
while Cleary's term is scheduled to end on December 31, 2000. 
Respondents filed suit against the Secretary and the Chair of the 
Board of Commissioners on Grievances and Discipline of the 
Ohio Supreme Court (“Board”) and against all the individual 
Justices of the Ohio Supreme Court. 


Respondents sought to have Canon VII(C)(6)(a) 
declared unconstitutional and to enjoin its enforcement. At the 
time of the original lawsuit, Canon VII(C)(6)(a) prohibited a 
candidate running for Ohio Common Pleas Judge from spending 
more than seventy five thousand dollars ($75,000) during the 
campaign. 


Respondents contend that Canon VII(C)(6)(a) violated 
their free speech rights because Suster wished to spend more 
than the cap amount in his campaign and both Suster and Cleary 


Se ee 


6 


wished to hear as much speech as possible from candidates for 
the Cuyahoga County Common Pleas bench. 


The district court denied Respondents’ request for a 
temporary restraining order. It conducted a hearing and issued 
a partial preliminary injunction. The court found that there was 
a strong likelihood that Respondents would succeed in 
establishing that Canon VII(C)(6)(a) was unconstitutional, and 
enjoined enforcement against all judicial candidates. (A.-70) 
Petitioners appealed to the Sixth Circuit. 


After receiving the September 10 order, Respondents 
filed a motion to stay the order pending an appeal to the Court 
of Appeals. The trial court instead modified its original order. 
(A-40) Under the modified injunction, the Ohio Supreme Court 
was prohibited from enforcing Canon VII(C)(6)(a) against 
Judges Suster and Cleary only. Suster and Cleary filed a Notice 
of Cross-Appeal. 


In October and November of 1996, additional individuals 
filed motions to intervene as plaintiffs and for preliminary 
injunction. The court granted the motions to intervene and 
granted preliminary injunctions in favor of the intervenors. (A- 
30) Because of the long series of orders issued by the district 
court, Petitioners filed five notices of appeal to the Sixth Circuit. 
All of these appeals were consolidated. 


Before the Sixth Circuit heard the case, and indeed 
before briefing was complete, the Supreme Court of Ohio 
amended Canon VII (C)(6). The Canon continues to place 
limitations on expenditures for judicial candidates. but those 
limitations for common pleas judges are now population-based. 


On appeal, the Sixth Circuit first correctly held that it 
could not review Canon VII(C)(6)(a) as it existed at the time the 
suit was filed, but must review the law as it stands now. But, 


7 


having made that acknowledgment, the court remanded the 
amended Canon VII(C)(6)(a) to the district court for 
consideration. Curiously, the Sixth Circuit then proceeded to 
analyze the original Canon, the one which the court itself had 
said was no longer subject to review. 


The court below analyzed the original Canon under the 
“strict scrutiny” standard of review used in Buckley v. Valeo, 
424 U.S. 1 (1976) and progeny. The Sixth Circuit disregarded 
Petitioner’s arguments that judicial officers are different from 
political officers, and in performing the Buckley analysis 
incorrectly stated that the prevention of corruption is the only 
“compelling state interest” permitted when analyzing restrictions 
on campaign finance. Having incorrectly determined that the 
prevention of corruption or the appearance of corruption is the 
only compelling state interest possible for the analysis, the Sixth 
Circuit went on to hold that Cannon VII (C)(6)(a) is not 
narrowly tailored to serve that interest, and is therefore 
unconstitutional and may be enjoined. 


REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


The lower courts in this case neglected a fundamental 
principle of the American political structure: judges are 
different. For all other elected offices, impartiality is 
unnecessary, and indeed discouraged. Voters have a right to 
elect representatives biased toward their viewpoints. But with 
judges, political issue bias is unwelcome and contrary to 
fundamental due process. In declining to recognize the State’s 
compelling interest in preserving judicial impartiality and the 
appearance thereof, the Sixth Circuit cited decisions from this 
Court which considered only non-judicial elections. To allow 
the Sixth Circuit decision to stand is to blur historic and 
important distinctions between the judiciary and every other 
elected office in our constitutional system. 


8 


This issue is vital to the entire legal community, and 
particularly timely; the American Bar Association’s Task Force 
on Lawyers’ Political Contributions, in its Report and 
Recommendations has specifically refrained from dealing with 
the issue of spending limitations in judicial races pending the 
outcome of this particular litigation. ABA Report, p. 23, n. 37. 


In addition, the court below correctly stated that it 
should review the law as it stood at that time, amended Canon 
VII (C\(6\(a). However, it then remanded the amended Canon, 
and proceeded to review and reject the original, unamended 
version of the Canon in such a way as to preclude a “full 
review” of the new Canon by the lower court, by making 
erroneous fundamental constitutional pronouncements which 
preclude any real analysis by the district court. Because the 
Sixth Circuit’s failure to review the current law diverges from 
numerous decisions of this Court and is in conflict with holdings 
from other courts, it warrants prompt review. 


L. THE SIXTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN 
REMANDING THE AMENDED CANON 
FOR “A FULL REVIEW," WHILE 
SIMULTANEOUSLY RESTRAINING 
SUCH A REVIEW BY ISSUING AN 
ERRONEOUS CONSTITUTIONAL 
OPINION ON THE ORIGINAL CANON. 


The court below remanded the amended Canon for a 
“full review” by the district court, while simultaneously issuing 
an opinion regarding the former Canon that prevents the district 
court from exercising any real discretion in its analysis. The 
Court should take this case to establish that such actions are 
incompatible with well-established precedent and judicial 
economy. The Court should review the merits of the case 
under the law as it stands today, or at least vacate the Sixth 


9 


Circuit opinion, in order to allow the district court genuine 
discretion in its review of the amended Canon. 


When a law is amended after entry of judgment in the 
trial court, but before the decision of the appellate court, the 
appellate court, in considering a facial challenge to a state law, 
must “review the judgment of the districi court in light of [the] 
law as it now stands, not as it stood when the judgment below 
was entered.” Diffenderfer v. Central Baptist Church, 404 U.S. 
412, 414 (1972). Ifthe amendment so changes the nature of the 
dispute as to render the appeal moot, the established practice is 
to vacate or reverse the judgment below and remand with a 
direction to dismiss. Arizonans for Official English v. Arizona, 
$20 U.S. ___, 137 L.Ed. 2d 170 (1997); Diffenderfer, 404 U.S. 
at 414; United States v. Munsingwear, 340 U.S. 36, 39, (1950). 
If the change in law requires additional factual findings or legal 
analysis, the case is sometimes remanded for further review. 
Fusari v. Steinberg, 419 U.S. 379, 387 (1975); Thorpe v. 
Housing Authority, 386 U.S 670 (1967); 393 U.S. 268, 272-783 
(1969). But if the amendment does not make substantial 
changes, the appellate court will review the law as it now stands. 

Florida General Contractors v. Jacksonville, 508 U.S. 656, 
661-63, n. 3. (1993), City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, 455 
U.S. 283, 288-89 (1982). In no case has this Court 
simultaneously reviewed a former law and remanded the new 
one for review, nor has it permitted a circuit court to do so. 


Here, the amended Canon has the essential feature 
relevant to the Sixth Circuit’s holdings on the original Canon: a 
cap on campaign spending. Indeed, the only difference between 
the former Canon and the amended Canon is a population-based 
graduated scale in the amounts of spending allowed for 
candidates for the common pleas and municipal courts. The 
change requires no new findings of fact or legal arguments; 
indeed, the parties’ arguments would be essentially unchanged 


10 


on remand. The Sixth Circuit correctly determined that this 
small change in the law did not moot the controversy. 


\_ The proper course of action for the Sixth Circuit should 
have been to review the Canon as it appears now. However, 
after correctly stating the law, the Sixth Circuit ignored it. The 
bizarre splitting of a facial constitutional challenge into the “old” 
and “new” versions of the law goes against numerous holdings 
of this Court, and is contrary to virtually all the circuit law on 
the issue. 


The case most illustrative of the split in the circuits on 
this issue is Rosenstiel v. Rodriguez, 101 F.3d 1544 (8th Cir. 
1996). Ina situation virtually identical to this one, the Eighth 
Circuit was faced with a statute establishing a voluntary state 
campaign financing scheme. After the case was submitted to the 
Court of Appeals, the Minnesota legislature amended the 
statute, altering the operation of the waiver provision in the 
statute. The court correctly determined that it should review the 
law as it stood at the time, not as it was when the lower court 
reviewed it. The Eighth Circuit determined that the case had 
not been mooted, and reviewed the new statute, despite the fact 
that the amendment took place after briefing and oral argument. 
Here, there was an opportunity to brief the new law and address 
its constitutionality during oral argument. See, also, Sanjour v. 
EPA, 56 F.3d 85 (D.C. Cir. 1994)(new law reviewed); Naturist 
Society v. Fillyaw, 958 F.2d 1515, 1519-20 (11th 
Cir.1992)(case not moot, new law reviewed); Coral 
Construction Co. v. King County, 941 F. 2d 910 (9th Cir. 
1991)(one issue reversed and remanded for further review, one 
issue reviewed under new law) . 


In addition to contradicting the law in other circuits, the - 
Sixth Circuit's decision contradicts a long line of decisions in this 
Court. Arizonans for Official English; Florida General 
Contractors; Diffenderfer v. Central Baptist Church, 404 U.S. 


11 


412, 414 (1972), Goodman v. Lukens Steel, 482 U.S. 656, 662 
(1987); Hall v. Beals, 396 U.S. 45, 48 (1969); Thorpe v. 
Housing Authority, 393 U.S. 268, 281-282 (1969), United 
States v. Alabama, 362 U.S. 602, 604 (1960); Hines v. 
Davidowtiz, 312 U.S. 52, 60 (1941); Carpenter v. Wabash R. 
Co., 309 U.S. 23, 26-27 (1940); United States v. Schooner 
Peggy, | Cranch 103, 110 (1801). 


Nor is this a mere procedural problem. There are 
important, principled and practical reasons for the rule. In this 
case every meaningful legal issue regarding the analysis of the 
amended Canon has been determined in the Sixth Circuit’s 
analysis of the former Canon. Unless the Sixth Circuit's 
judgment is an impermissible advisory opinion, Muskrat v. 
United States, 219 U.S. 346 (1911), the district court is bound 
by it as precedent, or at least the law of the case. The district 
court therefore has no meaningful discretion: it must find the 
amended Canon unconstitutional, without being able to address 
the real issues: (1) did the new Canon permissibly protect a 
compelling state interest in the impartiality and appearance of 
impartiality of the state’s judiciary, and (2) is the new Canon 
sufficiently narrowly tailored to protect that compelling state 
interest? 


It cannot be said that the Sixth Circuit’s split of the 
constitutional analysis was necessary in order to protect the 
original plaintiffs and intervenors from potential discipline under 
the old Canon. The court below, consistent with precedent, 
could have retained and reviewed the rights of plaintiffs and 
intervenors by analyzing the new Canon, or could have 
remanded the entire case to the district court for analysis 
unfettered by the Sixth Circuit’s conclusions. 


Even worse, the litigants are unnecessarily frustrated in 
their desire to have the constitutionality of the Canon 
determined. The parties in this case are forced to litigate (and 


12 


pay for) an “analysis” by the district court that is no analysis at 
all. In order to have full review of the constitutionality of the 
Canon, the litigants must once again appeal to the Sixth Circuit, 
also constrained by precedent and the “law of the case” to find 
the amended Canon unconstitutional. Only then can the litigants 
reach review by this Court. Such a result is a waste of state, 
private and judicial resources, as well as being contrary to the 
case law. This approach, contrary to both law and logic, locks 
the Ohio judicial election process into a limbo for which no 
higher court review of the crucial First Amendment questions 
can be achieved for years. The Sixth Circuit should have 
reviewed the new Canon. 


The Court should take this case to overrule the Sixth 
Circuit’s actions, and review the law as it stands now. In the 
alternative this Court should vacate the Sixth Circuit’s review of 
the former Canon as an advisory opinion, and allow the district 
court complete discretion in its analysis of the amended Canon.

INTERESTS IN THE IMPARTIALITY OF 
AND THE APPEARANCE OF 
IMPARTIALITY OF THEIR ELECTED 
JUDICIARY. 


When considering whether a campaign finance 
law unconstitutionally infringes freedom of 
speech, this Court’s task is to decide whether 
the provision in question actually burdens the 
exercise of political speech and, if it does, 
whether it is narrowly tailored to serve a 
compelling state interest. 


Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce, 494 U.S. 652, 657 
(1990)(citing Buckley, 424 U.S. at 25).

The “compelling state interest” put forward by the 
government in Buckley was the prevention of corruption or the 
appearance of corruption in the election of federal executive and

interest is the only one available to a government when it

A. The Decision Below Ignores the 
Possibility of a Compelling State 
Interest other than that Considered in 
Buckley. 


The Court should take this case to eradicate the 
misconception by the Sixth Circuit that the only “compelling 
state interest” possible in the analysis of restrictions on 
campaign expenditures is the one recognized in Buckley. The 
Sixth Circuit ignored the language of the Canon itself, as well-as 
arguments by counsel for the Ohio Supreme Court for a 
compelling state interest in the impartiality of the States’ elected 
judiciary. The court below presented no reasoning at all for its 
holding on this issue, which is the very heart of the case. 


The Code of Judicial Conduct of the Supreme Court of 
Ohio has as its primary purpose the integrity, independence, and 
impartiality of the judiciary of Ohio. All of the Canons are 
aimed at preserving the integrity, independence and impartiality 
of the judiciary. See, Canon I (“A Judge Shall Uphold the 
Integrity and Independence of the Judiciary,”); Canon II (“A 
Judge Shall Respect and Comply With the Law and Shall Act at 
all Times in a Manner That Promotes Public Confidence in the 
Integrity and Impartiality of the Judiciary”), Canon III (“A 
Judge Shall Perform the Duties of Judicial Office Impartially and 


14 


Diligently”), Canon I'V (“A Judge Shall Avoid Impropriety and 
the Appearance of Impropriety in all of the Judge’s Activities’’). 
The provisions of Canon VII dealing with judicial elections were 
therefore promulgated for that purpose. The Sixth Circuit 
should have analyzed Canon VII (C)(6) using the purpose put 
forward by the Canons themselves, and not using the purpose 
put forward in Buckley. 


In addition to ignoring the stated purpose of the Canons, 
the Sixth Circuit misstated the Court’s holdings with regard to 
“compelling state interests” by holding that the interest put 
forward in Buckley is “the only interest compelling enough to 
infringe upon the First Amendment rights of a candidate.” A-19 
(citing FEC v. NC-PAC, 470 U.S. 480, 496-497(1985))

preventing corruption or the appearance of 
corruption are the only legitimate and 
compelling government interests thus far 
identified for restricting campaign finances. 


NC-PAC, 470 U.S. at 496-497(emphasis added). Thus, this 
Court’s cases leave open the possibility that other state interests, 
heretofore unidentified, may be “compelling” for purposes of 
restricting campaign finances. The obvious reason that the 
compelling state interests in the impartiality and appearance of 
impartiality of the judiciary are not interests “thus far identified,” 
is that no case evaluating campaign expenditure limitations in 
Judicial races has yet been heard by this Court.

B. The Preservation of the Impartiality 
and the Appearance of Impartiality of 
the State Judiciary are Compelling 
State Interests. 


We the people expect candidates for political office to 
advocate strong, partisan positions, and indeed vote for those 
candidates who most closely reflect our own biases, viewpoints 
and positions. On the other hand, we expect our judges to be 
impartial and neutral. When judges are elected, as they are in 
Ohio and some other States, we expect them to display a neutral 
and unbiased attitude, and we vote for those candidates who 
most closely reflect this attitude. Such a neutral attitude goes 
beyond the lack of corruption. While corruption is part of the 
category of improper judicial bias, a judge can be biased without 
being corrupt. Because of the legitimate expectation that judges 
will be unbiased, the States have a compelling interest in the 
preservation of judicial impartiality and the appearance of 
judicial impartiality. 


Of course, a candidate for any elective office, including 
for the judiciary, is in some sense “political.” But a judicial 
candidate does not participate in the same robust political debate 
engaged in by a candidate for an executive or legislative 
position. The same public issues discussed in open political 
debate can and often do eventually come before the judiciary, 
and, for the judicial system to work, the parties must not feel 
that the judiciary is prejudiced. That is why judges and judicial 
candidates are not expected to engage in, and indeed are 
forbidden from, the “uninhibited, robust and wide open” (New 
York Times v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254, 270 (1964)) debate of 
political issues: 


Although the political elective process for the 
judiciary makes judicial candidates political party 
candidates, they are not as others. ... They 


16 


are, in short, to be as non-partisan as the 
selection of Judges by election permits. 


Rosenthal v. Harwood, 35 N.Y. 2d 469, 473; 323 N.E.24 179, 
182 (1974). See, also, Buckley v. Judicial Inquiry Board, 997 
F.2d 224, 228 (7th Cir.1993)(Posner, J.)(“[j]udges remain 
different from legislators and executive officials, even when all 
are elected, in ways that bear on the strength of the state’s 
interest in restricting their freedom of speech.”); Ackerson v. 
Kentucky Jud. Ret. & Removal Comm'n., 776 F. Supp. 309, 313 
(W.D. Ky. 1991)("Because the judicial office is different in key 
respects from other offices, the state may regulate its judges 
with the differences in mind."). 


Indeed, the American Bar Association itself recognizes 
the unique qualities of an elected judiciary. In its Report and 
Recommendations, the Task Force on Lawyers’ Political 
Contributions has a lengthy discussion about the differences 
between judges and other elected officials. ABA Report, 9. 4 & 
ff’ The lengthy discussion begins with the statement: 


Any aspect of judicial elections begins with one 
over-riding fact: these elections are uniquely 
different from all other elections. 


ABA Report, p. 4. See, also, Hansen, A Run for the Bench, 
ABA Journal, October 1998, p. 68-72. Although recognizing 
this important difference, the ABA has specifically withheld 
consideration of specific limitations on campaign spending 
pending the outcome of this case. ABA Report, p. 23, n. 37. 


Pennsylvania has not been so reticent. The Supreme 
Court of Pennsylvania appointed a Special Commission to Limit 
Campaign Expenditures. In its Report (“Pa. Report”), issued 
this past March, the Commission recommends limits on 


17 


campaign spending for the elected judiciary, in spite of “the 
apparent conflict with” Buckley. Pa. Report, p. 19. 


In Ohio, as elsewhere, judges and judicial candidates are 
expressly forbidden from engaging in political speech and 
association. Ohio’s Canons III and VII (B), based on canons in 
the Model Code of Judicial Conduct, specifically and properly 
forbid judicial candidates from engaging in a wide range of 
associational and speech activities. The Canons indicate a 
deeply-held conviction by the public and the judiciary itself that 
candidates for judicial office should be and are held to standards 
quite different from those for candidates for political office. 


Nor is a prohibition on certain types of political speech 
and activity for certain government officers unique. This Court 
has held that classified employees of the federal government may 
be prohibited from a variety of political speech and activity. 
United States Civil Service Commission v. National Association 
of Letter Carriers, AFL-CIO, 413 U.S. 548 (1973). Members 
of the Armed Forces may be prohibited from expressing 
religious beliefs in certain ways, Goldman v. Weinberger, 475 
U.S. 503 (1986). Indeed, attorneys and physiciatis, as well as 
many other professionals must accept some restrictions on their 
First Amendment freedoms that are different from restrictions 
on other American citizens. 


The prohibition on certain types of judicial speech is 
vitally necessary to preserve impartiality and the appearance of 
impartiality in the judiciary. If a judge or judicial candidate were 
allowed unrestricted speech, he or she would be free to 
comment on cases and issues on the current docket, or that 
might come before the judge in the near future. Such comments 
would indicate that the judge’s mind is made up before the 
evidence is in. Rather than being an independent arbiter, the 
judge will have become a partisan participant in the dispute. 


18 


For these reasons, the State’s interests involved in the 
regulation of campaign speech for political office and judicial 
office are distinctly different. In contrast to the governmental 
interest outlined in Buckley, the preservation of the impartiality 
and the appearance of impartiality of the judiciary is a broader 
requirement and commands a more rigorous curtailment of 
speech and association on the part of judges and judicial 
candidates.

Canon VII (C)(6“a) is narrowly tailored to serve the 
compelling state interests of the preservation of the impartiality 
and the appearance of impartiality of the judiciary for a number

advertising a candidate wishes to engage in. It also does not 
limit the viewpoint of any judicial candidate. In fact, the 
challenged Canon does not limit the type of speech in any 
manner, although other Canons, not at issue here, do so. 


The reasonable limit on spending is graduated according 
to the office sought and the population served by the office 
being sought. For common pleas, municipal court and county 
courts, the amended Canon allows a candidate to spend more in 
jurisdictions with larger populations.
