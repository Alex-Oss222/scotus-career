CONSTITUTIONAL PROVISIONS INVOLVED 


The First Amendment to the United States Constitution 
provides in pertinent part as follows: “Congress shall 
make no law . . . abridging the freedom of speech.” 


The Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Con- 
stitution provides in pertinent part as follows: “No State 

. Shall . . . deprive any person of life, liberty, or prop- 
erty without due process of law.” 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. Factual Background 


Petitioner Margaret Boring was a teacher at the Charles 
D. Owen High School in the Buncombe County, North 
Carolina School District. App. 8la. In the fall of 1991, 
she selected the play “Independence” for four students in 
her advanced drama class to present in the annual state- 
wide high school drama competition. App. 81la-82a. The 
critically acclaimed play, by noted playwright Lee 
Blessing, depicts the dynamics within a dysfunctional 
single-parent family—-a divorced mother and her three 
daughters, one of whom is a lesbian and another of whom 
is pregnant with an illegitimate child. /d. 


At the time Boring selected “Independence,” Owen 
High School had a “controversial materials” policy, which 
generally required advance parental approval of contro- 
versial materials. Id. at 85a. The policy did not cover 
dramatic presentations; but Boring nevertheless notified 
the parents of the four student actresses of the play’s 
contents, and those parents approved their daughters’ par- 
ticipation in the play. /d. Boring also notified the prin- 
cipal of Owen High School, Fred Ivey, that she had se- 
lected “Independence” as the play to be performed in the 
competition, and he did not object. /d. at 82a. Boring 
therefore proceeded to produce the play. 


After the play had been presented at the regional level 
of the statewide competition, where it won seventeen of 


ee 


3 


the twenty-one possible regional awards, Donna Wyles, 
another teacher at Owen High School, asked Boring to 
present the play for one of Wyiles’ English classes. App. 
82a. Boring agreed to the request, but she advised Wyles 
that the play contained mature themes and asked Wyles 
to obtain consents from the parents of her students prior 
to the staging of the play. Jd. at 82a-83a. Wyles there- 
upon distributed consent forms to her students, and the 
play then was performed. /d. at 83a. 


A controversy developed after one student in Wyles’ 
class described the play to a parent, who in turn called 
Principal Ivey to complain. App. 83a. After receiving 
this complaint and reading the script of the play, Ivey 
told Boring that the students could not present the play 
in the next and final round of the statewide competition. 
!d. When the parents of Boring’s students objected to 
that decision, Ivey agreed to allow the play to be pre- 
sented if certain portions were deleted. Jd. The play, as 
so modified, was then performed at the state finals, and 
it won second place. Id. 


At the end of the 1991-92 school year, Ivey asked the 
Superintendent of Schools to transfer Boring from Owen 
High School, where she had taught advanced drama for 
thirteen years and had built a nationally-recognized drama 
program, to a junior high school, where she no longer 
would be able to teach advanced drama. App. 85a. The 
Superintendent granted the request, stating in purported 
justification of the transfer that Boring had failed to fol- 
low the school system’s controversial materials policy in 
producing “Independence.” /d. 


Boring appealed the proposed transfer to the Board of 
Education. Prior to the Board hearing, there was con- 
siderable public discussion of the transfer, including ac- 
cusations that Boring was “immoral” for having produced 
the play. App. 86a. The Board rejected Boring’s appeal, 
id. at 87a, and this lawsuit followed.

4 


In addition to setting forth the facts described above 
indicating that Boring had obtained consent from both the 
Principal and the students’ parents before producing “In- 
dependence,” the complaint specifically alleged that Boring 
did not violate the controversial materials policy and that 
the school authorities did not transfer her based on a 
genuine belief that she had violated that policy. App. 85a. 
Rather, the complaint alleged, they transferred her “in bad 
faith and with malice toward Plaintiff over the ideas ex- 
pressed in the play.” /d. at 85a, 87a. 


B. Proceedings Below 


The district court dismissed the complaint pursuant to 
Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) for failure to 
state a claim. A panel of the Fourth Circuit reversed in 
a 2-1 decision. The court of appeals, sitting en banc, re- 
versed the panel by a 7-6 majority and affirmed the dis- 
trict court’s decision to dismiss the complaint. 


1. The District Court Opinion 


The district court held that, because Boring’s produc- 
tion of “Independence” took place in the context of cur- 
ricular activities, defendants could discipline her if their 
action was “reasonably related to legitimate pedagogical 
concerns.” App. 74a (quoting Hazelwood School District 
v. Kuhlmeier, 484 U.S. 260, 273 (1988)). The court 
then declared that the defendants passed that test, without 
explaining how the court could make such a determina- 
tion from the face of the complaint. App. 75a.’ 


2. The Panel Opinion 


A panel of the Fourth Circuit reversed the district court 
by a 2-1 majority in an opinion authored by Judge Motz. 


1 The district court also held that Boring’s selection and produc- 
tion of “Independence” did not constitute “speech” within the

Noting that there is not “a single instance in which any 
court has applied Hazelwood to dismiss a teacher’s com- 
plaint for failure to state a claim,” and that, instead, 
“courts have required school administrators to establish 
their legitimate pedagogical concerns either by affidavit or 
at trial,” App. 42a, the panel held that the district court 
had erred in dismissing the complaint in this case. App. 
43a. “Without any basis for determining the defendants’ 
intent in transferring Boring, other than Boring’s allega- 
tion that it was done ‘in bad faith and with malice toward 
[her] over the ideas expressed in the play,’ the district 
court should not have dismissed the complaint on the 
theory that the asserted restriction necessarily related to 
legitimate pedagogical concerns.” Id. 


The panel went on to reject defendants’ argument that 
the district court’s decision could be sustained on an alter- 
native ground, viz, that, where curricular matters are in- 
volved, a teacher’s speech never can Satisfy the “public 
concern” requirement of Connick v. Myers, 461 U.S. 138 
(1983). The panel explained that Connick’s holding 
denies First Amendment protection to speech on matters 
that are solely of personal interest to a government em- 
ployee, but that speech about matters of public concern 
does not become unprotected merely because it is under- 
taken in the performance of an employee’s job duties. 
App. 45a. The panel added that, “in view of the impor- 
tance of the free exchange of ideas in educating our soci- 
ety,” the interpretation of Connick advanced by the de- 
fendants “would be particularly ill-advised when the em- 
ployee is a teacher.” Id. Canvassing this Court’s decisions 
on the subject, the panel concluded: 


A fair reading of the relevant case law leads us to 
conclude that the question-is not, and never has been, 
whether teachers have First Amendment rights in the 
classroom, but how much school authorities can 
legitimately restrict those rights. Striking a balance 
between the school’s role as ultimate arbiter of the 
school curriculum and the teacher’s limited in-class

speech rights obviously presents a challenge. We 

believe the approach set forth in Hazelwood, of re- 

quiring school authorities to provide a legitimate 

pedagogical basis for in-class speech restriction, pro- 

vides the best means of navigating this challenge. 
App. 49a. 


Because the panel held that the complaint had been 
improperly dismissed for the reasons discussed, the panel 
did not find it necessary to reach the merits of Boring’s 
contention that the complaint also stated a claim on the 
alternative theory that the school authorities had failed to 
provide her with proper notice that she could be disci- 
plined for producing “Independence”. App. 52a. The 
court did, however, observe that “some notice would ap- 
pear necessary to ensure that secondary school teachers’ 
limited First Amendment rights have breathing space to 
survive.” /d. 


3. The En Bane Opinion 


By a 7-6 majority, the Fourth Circuit, sitting en banc, 
vacated the panel decision and affirmed the district court. 
The opinion for the court, written by Judge Widener, held 
that it was appropriate for the district court to have 
granted the motion to dismiss, because all that the court 
needed to know to decide this case was that Boring’s 
speech was communicated as part of her curricular duties. 
App. 9a, lla. As Judge Widener saw it, both Connick 
and Hazelwood dictated the conclusion that such speech 
was not entitled to any First Amendment protection. 


Connick, according to Judge Widener, stands for the 
proposition that speech by a teacher that involves part 
of the curriculum “does not present a matter of public 
concern” under any set of circumstances, but presents 
“nothing more than an ordinary employment dispute.” 
App. 7a. And Hazelwood, according to Judge Widener, 
stands for the proposition that any decision by school offi- 
cials that relates to the curriculum “is by definition a

7 


legitimate pedagogical concern.” App. lla (emphasis 
added ). 


Judge Widener then turned to Boring’s contention that, 
at a minimum, the First Amendment requires that a 
teacher not be subjected to discipline because of opposi- 
tion to ideas expressed in curricular materials she has 
selected if the teacher had no notice that her use of such 
materials might be considered impermissible. His reason- 
ing on that point consisted solely of the following sen- 
tence: “Plaintiff's contention that she was not given notice 
as to what was being proscribed is, of course, without 
merit, the plaintiff having no First Amendment right to 
participate in the makeup of the curriculum.” App. 13a 
n.2. 


Judge Luttig joined the majority opinion, but also filed 
a separate concurring opinion in which he took a some- 
what different route to arrive at the same conclusion, viz, 
that teachers have no First Amendment rights with respect 
to their curricular speech. Noting that Hazelwood in- 
volved student speech, Judge Luttig rejected the view that 
the Hazelwood test should apply to speech by teachers. 
App. 17a-18a. Stating that “the analogy would seem to 
be pure between a teacher who, qua teacher, attempts to 
assert her own views through the curriculum itself, and 
any other public employee who, qua employee, attempts 
to assert her personal views through the official policies 
and decisions of her office,” Judge Luttig concluded that 
curricular speech enjoys no protection under Connick. 
App. | 6a. 


Chief Judge Wilkinson, in a brief concurrence, stated 
that he, too, was opposed to the use of the Hazelwood 
test, because he found the phrase “legitimate pedagogical 
concern” to be too “loose, slippery, [and] litigious” to 
“consign . . . to the mercies of the federal courts.” App. 
14a. 


Judge Motz dissented in an opinion joined by five other 
judges, in which she restated and expanded on the views 


8 


set forth in her origina! panel majority opinion. App. 22a- 
33a. Judge Motz pointed out that, although Hazelwood 
involved speech by students rather than by teachers, Judge 
Widener’s conclusion that all decisions relating to cur- 
riculum are by definition “reasonably related to legitimate 
pedagogical concerns” is inconsistent with Hazelwood. As 
Judge Motz observed, this Court recognized that the stu- 
dent newspaper at issue in Hazelwood was “part of the 
educational curriculum,” see 484 U.S. at 268, 271, and 
this Court nevertheless held that the actions of school 
authorities in restricting the content of the newspaper 
could be upheld only “so long as their actions are reason- 
ably related to legitimate pedagogical concerns.” Id. at 
273 (emphasis added). See App. 25a. 


As to the relevance and interpretation of Connick, the 
dissent noted that, “[a]lthough Boring’s in-class speech 
does not itself constitute pure public debate, obviously it 
does ‘relate to’ matters of overwhelming public concern— 
family life, divorce, motherhood, and illegitimacy, among 
others.” App. 29a. 


Judge Hamilton joined Judge Motz’s dissenting opinion, 
but also wrote a separate dissent, in which Judge Murna- 
ghan joined. Judge Hamilton emphasized that the com- 
plaint, fairly read, alleges that Boring complied fully with 
what she reasonably understood to be the standards, pol- 
icies and curriculum of the school, and that she was made 
a scapegoat by the school authorities after some members 
of the community raised an outcry against ideas expressed 
in the play she had produced. App. 20a. In response to 
the concurring opinions of Chief Judge Wilkinson and 
Judge Luttig, Judge Hamilton argued that the application 
of the “legitimate pedagogical interest” standard to restric- 
tions on teacher speech involving curricular matters can- 
not fairly be characterized as an undue intrusion on the 
right of school authorities to control the curriculum, be- 
cause this Court held in Hazelwood that school authorities’ 
control over students’ input into the curriculum must 
satisfy that very standard. App. 21a.

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


The holding of the court below is in conflict with the 
decisions of other circuits and state courts of last resort. 
Furthermore, this case presents an important constitu- 
tional question as to which this Court has not directly 
spoken, and the Fourth Circuit decided that question in a 
manner that is at odds with bedrock principles of First 
Amendment law that this Court has applied in the con- 
text of public education. 


A. The Fourth Circuit’s Holding Creates a Conflict With the 
Decisions of Other Circuits and State Courts of Last 
Resort 


The Fourth Circuit’s decision has created a conflict 
with the decisions of several circuits and state courts of 
last resort. 


In Ward v. Hickey, 996 F.2d 448 (lst Cir. 1993), 
which involved a teacher’s speech in connection with 
“part of the curriculum,” id. at 453, the First Circuit held 
that the proper “test of . . . regulation” of such speech 
is the Hazelwood standard. Id. In stark contrast to the 
Fourth Circuit’s holding that all restrictions on teachers’ 
curricular speech automatically satisfy that standard, the 
First Circuit held that the outcome of the Hazelwood test 
in this context “depend[s] on, among other things, the 
age and sophistication of the students, the relationship 
between teaching method and valid educational objective, 
and the context and manner of the presentation.” Jd. 
The court held as well that, “[e]ven if . . . a school may 
prohibit a teacher’s statements before she makes them, it 
is not entitled to retaliate against speech that it never pro- 
hibited.” Id. The court explained that teachers have a 
“First Amendment right . . . to know that conduct is 
proscribed,” id. at 454, because “few subjects lack con- 
troversy. If teachers must fear retaliation for every utter- 
ance, they will fear teaching.” Id. at 453 


Also in conflict with the decision below is the Sixth 
Circuit’s decision in Stachura v. Truszkowski, 763 F.2d 


10 


211 (6th Cir. 1985), reversed and remanded on other 
issues, 477 U.S. 299 (1986). In that case, the Sixth 
Circuit held that a secondary school teacher’s First 
Amendment rights were infringed when the school board’s 
decision to suspend him for his use of a biology textbook 
was based not on established school standards but on 
after-the-fact hostile community reaction. Jd. at 213. 


The Fourth Circuit’s decision in this case also conflicts 
with the Second Circuit’s decision in Silano v. Sag Harbor 
School District, 42 F.3d 719 (2d Cir. 1994). In Silano 
a guest lecturer challenged a school board resolution cen- 
suring him for having used an inappropriate film clip in a 
class presentation. Although the court upheld the board’s 
action, it did so only after determining from the summary 
judgment record that the disputed film clip was salacious 
and that the clip had nothing to do with the subject mat- 
ter of Silano’s lecture. Jd. at 723. Thus, far from hold- 
ing that the Hazelwood test was satisfied simply because 
curriculum was involved, the Second Circuit held that, 
precisely because “the lectures were analogous to cur- 
riculum,” id., it was necessary to determine whether, on 
the specific facts of the case, the school authorities had 
shown that the particular action at issue was “reasonably 
related to legitimate pedagogical concerns.” Id. 


The Fourth Circuit’s view that all decisions relating to 
the curriculum are “by definition” based on legitimate 
pedagogical concerns, App. lla, has been squarely rejected 
by the Eleventh Circuit as well. In Virgil v. School Board 
of Columbia County, 862 F.2d 1517 (11th Cir. 1989), 
the court held that, in determining whether a school 
board decision to prohibit the use of a book in the cur- 
riculum is permissible under Hazelwood, the finder of 
fact must “look beyond the stated reasons for school board 
action” to the concerns that actually motivated the board, 
862 F.2d at 1522 n.6, and then must determine both 
whether those concerns were legitimate and whether the 
particular action taken was reasonably related to those 
concerns. Jd. at 1522-23. The court expressly rejected

the proposition that, because “the Board decisions at issue 
were curricular decisions,” the Hazelwood test could be 
met without a need to inquire further. Jd. at 1522. 


Decisions from state courts of last resort also are in 
conflict with the decision below. In Hosford v. School 
Committee of Sandwich, 659 N.E.2d 1178 (Mass. 
1996), the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court, in an

violated the First Amendment when they disciplined a 
teacher for curricular speech without affording reasonable 
notice that the speech might subject her to discipline. And 
in Fisher v. Fairbanks North Star Borough Sch. Dist.,

classroom materials,” though broad, is subject to First 
Amendment limitations, including the requirement that 
advance notice be provided before punishment may be 
imposed. See also State Board for Community Colleges y. 
Olson, 687 P.2d 429, 437 & n.6 (Colo. 1984) (stating 
that teachers have a First Amendment interest in choice 
of curricular materials, and rejecting the proposition that 
teachers’ status as employees deprives them of First

B. This Case Presents an Important Question of Constitu- 
tional Law That Has Not Been Directly Addressed by 
This Court, and the Fourth Circuit Decided That Question 
in a Manner That Departs From Basic First Amendment 
Principles Articulated by This Court in the Context of 
Public Education 


1. Because it is the purpose of the public schools to 
“inculcat{e] fundamental values necessary to the mainte- 
nance of a democratic political system,” Ambach v. Nor- 
wick, 441 U.S. 68, 77 (1979), elected school boards and 
their appointed officials have “important, delicate, and 
highly discretionary functions.” Board of Education v. 
Barnette, 319 U.S. 624, 637 (1943). Yet the very fact 
“[t]hat they are educating the young for citizenship is rea- 
son for scrupulous protection of Constitutional freedoms of 
the individual.” 7d. And, “in view of the nature of the 
teacher’s relation to the effective exercise of the rights 
which are safeguarded by the Bill of Rights and by the 
Fourteenth Amendment, inhibition of freedom of thought, 
and of action upon thought, in the case of teachers brings 
the safeguards of those amendments vividly into opera- 
tion.” Shelton v. Tucker, 364 U.S. 479, 487 (1960) 
(emphasis added). As the Court put it in Keyishian y. 
Board of Regents, 385 U.S. 589, 603 (1967), “[o]ur 
Nation is deeply committed to safeguarding academic 
freedom, which is of transcendent valuc to all of us and 
not merely to the teachers concerned.” 


2. Although these basic principles are clear enough, 
their application in a case where a teacher has been dis- 
ciplined because of opposition to ideas expressed in her

13 


teaching or in curricular materials she has selected is not. 
As we have noted, prior to the decision below, the courts 
of appeal and the state courts had uniformly acknowl- 
edged that teachers have at least some measure of First 
Amendment protection in this context. But this Court 
has never addressed that important question.

resolving the question presented here, but is not definitive

In Hazelwood the Court held that the authority of school 
Officials over the content of materials produced by stu- 
dents in curricular activities is not absolute, but must be 
exercised in a manner that is “reasonably related to legiti- 
mate pedagogical concerns.” 484 U.S. at 273. The Court 
expressly stated that the student speech at issue—articles 
in a school newspaper produced by a journalism class— 
“may fairly be characterized as part of the school curricu- 
lum.” Jd. at 271. See also id. at 268 (stating that pro- 
duction of the newspaper was “part of the educational 
curriculum and a ‘regular classroom activit[yj’”). Far 
from suggesting that the curricular nature of the student 
materials should give school officials unfettered control 
over their content, this Court held that the officials’ action 
in removing items from the newspaper would be permis- 
sible only if it were shown to have been “reasonably related 
to legitimate pedagogical concerns”; and the Court re- 
viewed the record evidence in detail to determine whether, 
on the specific facts of the case, the school authorities’ 
action satisfied that test. See id. at 274-76. 


Hazelwood makes clear—if there ever was any doubt— 
that even in matters of curriculum, the power of school 
authorities must be exercised “within the limits of the Bill 
of Rights.” Barnette, 319 U.S. at 637. See also Meyer v. 
Nebraska, 262 U.S. 390 (1923); Epperson v. Arkansas, 
393 U.S. 97 (1968). And, given this Court’s recognition 
that restraints on speech by teachers raise particularly 
vital First Amendment concerns, see Shelton, supra; Ke- 
yishian, supra; Wieman v. Updegraff, 344 U.S. 183, 195 
(1952) (Frankfurter, J., concurring), it would seem 
apparent that if, as Hazelwood holds, the authority of 
school officials over curriculum cannot, under the First 
Amendment, give those authorities unbridled discretion 
to restrain student speech, the same must be true with 
respect to teacher speech.

c. But the question is complicated by the fact, empha- 
sized in Judge Luttig’s concurrence as well as in Judge 
Widener’s opinion for the court below, that curricular 
speech (including, as here, the selection of curricular ma- 
terials) is speech in which a teacher engages as part of 
her duties as a public employee. Consideration therefore 
must be given to the cases that stand for the proposition 
that, when a speaker’s speech is funded by the Govern- 
ment, the Government generally is free to determine what 
the message shall be, and to take action to ensure that the 
message is delivered as intended. See Rust v. Sullivan, 500 
U.S. 173 (1991); Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors, 515 
U.S. 819 (1995). Cf. National Endowment for the Arts 
v. Finley, No. 97-371 (pending). 


The application of that principle to public employment 
was at issue in Arizonans for Official English v. Arizona, 
520 U.S. 43 (1997), but was not addressed by this 
Court because the case ultimately was determined not 
to be justiciable. Just last month, the Arizona Supreme 
Court considered the question that had been before this 
Court in Arizonans for Official English, and held that, 
by “prohibit[ing] all public . . . employees in Arizona 
from acting in a language other than English while per- 
forming governmental functions,” the Arizona Constitu-

employees.” Ruiz v. Hull, No. 96-0493, 1998 WL 203081 
*13 (Ariz. April 28, 1998). The case at bar presents 
an opportunity for this Court to address for the first 
time the important question of the applicability of the 
First Amendment to speech by public employees in the 
performance of their governmental functions—although 
we hasten to add that the question is presented here in 
a context (public education) which the Court has sug- 
gested should constitute an exception to the unfettered 
power that otherwise might inhere in the Government to 
dictate the content of speech for which it pays. See Rust, 
500 U.S. at 200 (suggesting that the notice principle of

Keyishian constitutes an exception); Rosenberger, 515 
U.S. at 834 (suggesting that Hazelwood constitutes an

U.S. 503, 511 (1969) (“In our system, students may not 
be regarded as closed-circuit recipients of only that which 
the state chooses to communicate.” ) 


That teachers engage in speech as part of their jobs 
also raises an unsettled issue under the “public concern” 
test of Connick v. Myers, supra. Connick drew a distinc- 
tion between speech by a public employee “as a citizen 
upon matters of public concern,” and speech “as an em- 
ployee upon matters only of personal interest.” Con- 
nick, 461 U.S. at 147 (emphasis added). This Court 
held that speech in the former category generally is en- 
titled to First Amendment protection as against employ- 
ment sanctions, while speech in the latter category gen- 
erally is not. But a teacher’s classroom speech concern- 
ing controversial public issues, or a teacher’s selection 
of curricular materials addressing such issues, does not 
fit either side of the Connick dichotomy: it constitutes 
speech “as an employee,” but “upon matters of public 
concern.” In our view, Connick, properly read, denies 
protection only to speech that constitutes a mere “em- 
ployee grievance,” 461 U.S. at 154, or that otherwise 
relates solely to the personal interests of the speaker. 
But, as the opinions below by both Judge Widener and 
Judge Luttig attest, this is a matter as to which the Court 
has not yet clearly spoken. 


Thus, the question whether the First Amendment places 
any limits on the power of Government to discipline a 
teacher for the ideas expressed in her curricular speech 
involves a matter of great public importance which this 
Court has never directly addressed, and which is made 
all the more uncertain by unresolved issues arising out 
of both the Rust and Connick lines of cases.

The only issue in this case is whether a public high 
school teacher has a First Amendment right to participate 
in the makeup of the school curriculum through the selec-

the complaint.

The plaintiff also contends that the district court erred

Pedagogical is defined as “2: of or relating to teaching

definition ‘pedagogical’... .” Searcey v. Harris, 888 F.2d 
1314, 1319 (llth Cir. 1989). Kirkland, 890 F.2d at

If the performance of a play under the auspices of a

is not by definition a legitimate pedagogical concern, we

School administrators must and do have final authority 
over curriculum decisions. But that authority is not wholly 
unfettered. Like all other state officials, they must obey 
the Constitution. The Supreme Court has long recognized 
that the Constitution, specifically the First Amendment,

A. 


Connick held that a court generally determines whether 
a public employee’s speech is constitutionally protected by 
balancing the employee’s interest, “‘as a citizen, in com- 
menting upon matters of public concern and the interest 
of the State, as an employer, in promoting the efficiency 
of the public services it performs through its employees.’ ” 
Id. at 142 (quoting Pickering v. Board of Educ., 391 
U.S. 563, 568 (1968)). Under Connick, Boring must 
first establish that her speech related to “a matter of pub- 
lic concern.” See Connick, 461 U.S. at 146. If she does 
so, school authorities may still prevail, provided they can 
show that their interest in workplace efficiency and har- 
mony outweighs Boring’s interest in commenting upon the 
matters of public concern. See Board of County Comm'rs 
v. Umbehr, USS. , 116 S.Ct. 2342, 2347-48 
(1996); Connick, 461 U.S. at 1467-47; Pickering, 391 
US. at 568. 


In my view, the Connick framework does not provide 
a workable formula for analyzing whether the First 
Amendment protects a teacher’s in-class speech. Neither 
element of the Connick balancing test provides much as- 
sistance in assessing whether this speech is entitled to 
constitutional protection. 


The public concern element articulated in Connick fails 
to account adequately for the unique character of a teach- 
er’s in-class speech. When a teacher steps into the class- 
room she assumes a position of extraordinary public trust 
and confidence: she is charged with educating our youth. 
Her speech is neither ordinary employee workplace speech 
nor common public debate. Any attempt to force it into 
either of these categories ignores the essence of teaching— 
to educate, to enlighten, to inspire—and the importance

Moreover, the governmental interest element as set forth

simpler and more rigorous Hazelwood analysis should

However, even if the Connick test applied to a teacher’s 
in-class speech, we would be required to conclude that 
the district court erred in dismissing Boring’s complaint. 
Although Boring’s in-class speech does not itself constitute 
pure public debate, obviously it does “relate to” matters 
of overwhelming public concern—family life, divorce, 
motherhood, and illegitimacy, among others. Thus, if the 
Connick analysis did apply to in-class speech, then Bor- 
ing’s choice and production of a play that raises a number 
of important social issues obviously falls within the Su- 
preme Court’s broad definition of “public concern,” which 
includes speech “relating to any matter of political, social, 
or other concern to the community.” Connick, 461 U‘S. 
at 146 (emphasis added). See also id. at 149 (speech 
need only “touch” on a matter of public concern in order

contrary holding that Boring’s selection and production

public concern under Connick. Ante at 9. (The majority

Conceivably, the majority’s holding is grounded in 
misreading Connick to make the role in which a public 
employee speaks determinaitve of whether her speech 
merits First Amendment protection. Connick does dis- 
tinguish between an employee speaking “as a citizen on 
matters of public concern” and an employee speaking “as 
an employee upon matters of personal interest.” Connick, 
461 U.S. at 147. But Connick never hold that a public 
employee automatically loses all First Amendment protec- 
tion whenever she speaks in her role as employee on a 
matter of public concern. Indeed, the Connick Court im- 
plicitly repudiates such a conclusion, by directing that 
factors other than the role of the speaker are critical to 
determining when an employee speakes on a matter of 
public concern—‘“[w)hether an employee’s speech ad- 
dresses a matter of public concern must be determined by 
the content, form, and context of a given statement.” 
Id. at 147-48. Moreover, Connick itself held that one of

an employee in her role as an employee, did “touch[] upon

Furthermore, to read Connick as holding that any 
speech by a public employee in her role as an employee 
fails to merit First Amendment protection would contra- 
vene other controlling precedent. Both the Supreme Court 
and this court have held that a teacher’s in-class speech, 
which by definition involves speech by a teacher in her 
role as an employee, warrants protection. See, e.g., Key-

Amendment); Piver v. Pender County Bd. of Educ., 835

1206 (1988). Because the majority does not explicitly 
hold that the role in which an employee speaks is deter- 
minative or attempt to distinguish Keyishian or overrule 
Piver, this reasoning must not be the basis for its conclu-

public concern. 


The only possible basis that I can see for the majority’s 
holding is a mistake as to the nature of Boring’s claim. 
The speech for which Boring seek First Amendment pro- 
tection does not constitute a private personnel grievance. 
Boring does not allege that she selected and produced 
Independence after being instructed not to choose that 
play, nor does she allege that school administrators dis- 
ciplined her because she publicized her dissatisfaction 
with their treatment of her. Such a claim, much like 
Kirkland’s insistence on using a disapproved reading list, 
would constitute an “ordinary employment dispute” rather 
than speech relating to a matter of public concern. Boring 
instead alleges that the school administration disciplined 
her for the selection and production of the play itself. 
This is not an employment dispute. Rather, it is a chal- 
lenge to a restriction on classroom speech, which involves 
matters of public concern.

Thus, if the Connick analysis applied here, a court 
could only conclude that Boring has alleged that she was 
disciplined for speech “relating to a matter of public con- 
cern.” We would then have to proceed to the other half 
of the Connick balancing test and examine the school 
administration’s evidence as to the necessity of its restric- 
tion of her speech. But, on this record, we could hardly 
conclude that school administrators had demonstrated that 
they reasonably believed Boring’s speech would disrupt the 
workplace. They have not even asserted, let alone offered 
evidence cf, disruption. Cf. Connick, 461 U.S. at 151-54. 
(Supreme Court relies on trial testimony of employer that 
employee’s speech caused office disruption). Accordingly, 
even under Connick, we would be required to hold that 
the district court erred in dismissing Boring’s complaint.

Shelton v. Tucker, 364 U.S. 479, 487 (1960) (internal

Rather than “vigilant[{ly] protecti[ng] . . . constitutional

OPINION 
DIANA GRIBBON MOTZ, Circuit Judge: 


In this case, a high school drama teacher appeals the 
dismissal of her complaint for failure to state a claim. 
The district court determined from the face of the teacher’s 
complaint that the First Amendment did not protect her 
conduct in selecting, producing, and directing a play that 
her drama students performed. Although the First Amend- 
ment affords a teacher only limited refuge in this context, 
we conclude that it does not leave a teacher so completely 
without protection that her complaint failed to state a 
claim. Accordingly, we reverse and remand for further 
proceedings. 

I. 


Because this case was dismissed for failure to state a 
claim, we must accept the following facts, as alleged in 
the plaintiff's complaint, as true. See Hishon v. King & 
Spalding, 467 U.S. 69, 73 (1984); Advanced Health- 
Care Servs., Inc. v. Radford Community Hosp., 910 F.2d 
139, 143 (4th Cir. 1990). 


During the 1991-92 school year, Margaret Boring was 
employed as a teacher of English and Drama at the 
Charles D. Owen High School in Buncombe County, 
North Carolina. The County had employed Boring as a 
teacher since 1979 and, in that time, she had “built a 
national reputation for excellence in teaching drama and 
directing and producing therater.” Plays she produced 
won numerous awards and many of her students received 
theater-related scholarships to college, including $260,000 
in awards to 1992 graduates. 


In the fall of 1991, Boring chose “Independence” as the 
play for four student-actresses in her advanced acting 
class to perform. “Independence” is a drama that “power- 
fully depicts the dynamics within a dysfunctional, single-

one a lesbian, another pregnant with an_ illegitimate

Boring’s direction, in a state competition. After selecting

every year, of her choice. The principal, Fred Ivey, did 
not comment or react. 


Before rehearsals began, Boring sent the four student-

Org., 991 F.2d 94, 97 (4th Cir. 1992). Only if it “ap-

A. 


First, the district court agreed with the magistrate judge 
that Boring’s complaint did not state a cause of action 
because she had not alleged that the defendants sanctioned 
her for ideas she expressed, but rather for ideas the play 
expressed. The court thus held that Boring’s mere “im- 
plicit approval” of the ideas in the play “was not expres- 
sive conduct entitled to constitutional protection.” 


In reaching its conclusion, the district court relied pri- 
marily on Judge Milburn’s concurring opinion in Fowler

Judge Milburn’s analysis in Fowler. The legal principles

Films, plays, and even “crude street skits,” constitute in-

warrant First Amendment protection even if the speaker 
cannot establish an intent to convey a particularized mes-

and television, and live entertainment, such as musical and 
drama works, fall within the First Amendment guaran- 
tee”). Thus, to state a claim Boring need not establish 
that she “explicitly approved” the ideas in the play or that 
she intended to convey a particularized message through 
her choice of “Independence.”

only select programming originally produced by

compilation of speech generated by other persons is 
a staple of most newspapers’ opinion pages, which, 
of course, fall squarely within the core of First 
Amendment security, as does even the simple selec- 
tion of a paid noncommercial advertisement for in- 
clusion in a daily paper. The selection of contingents

B. 


The district court alternatively concluded that even if 
selection of a play constituted a form of expression that 
could be protected, Boring’s choice of “Independence” did 
not merit protection. The court reached this alternative 
holding in reliance on the standard articulated in Hazel- 
wood School District v. Kuhlmeier, 484 U.S. 260 (1988). 
In Hazelwood, the Supreme Court held that a school could 
restrict student speech if the restriction was “reasonably 
related to legitimate pedagogical concerns.” Jd. at 273. 
Purportedly applying that standard to Boring’s complaint, 
the district court ruled, without elaboration, that defend- 
ants had a “legitimate interest in curbing” Boring’s con- 
duct in selecting “Independence.” 


Boring does not challenge the district court’s determina- 
tion that Hazelwood supplies the appropriate legal stand- 
ard in this case; she challenges only the court’s application 
of that standard. Accordingly, for the moment (although 
we will discuss this issue in detail later), we will assume 
that Hazelwood, which examined student speech, also 
States the appropriate standard for analyzing restrictions 
on teacher classroom speech. The question then becomes 
whether simply by examining Boring’s complaint a court 
can determine that the defendants’ asserted restriction on 
her speech was “reasonably related to legitimate pedagogi- 
cal concerns.” 


The answer is clear: Boring’s complaint tells us nothing 
about the defendants’ “legitimate pedagogical concerns” 
for restricting her speech. A court, therefore, has no basis 
for determining whether the restriction reasonably related 


to such concerns; and Boring specifically alleges that it 
did not.

At present, all we know about the play must be inferred 
from the limited description contained in the complaint, 
i.e., “it powerfully depicts the dynamics within a dysfunc- 
tional single-parent family—a divorced mother and three 
daughters, one a lesbian, another pregnant with an illegiti- 
mate child.” Such a brief description, in and of itslef, can- 
not establish the play’s unsuitability for high school stu- 
dents, or establish that the school’s speech restriction nec- 
essarily related to legitimate pedagogical concerns. After 
all, from similar brief descriptions, many of the classics, 
and even parts of the Bible, might not appear suitable for 
high school students to study or perform. See, e.g., Judges 
16:17-19 (story of Samson and Delilah); 1 Kings 21:8-14 
(Jezebel); 2 Samuel 11:1 - 12.24 (David and Bathsheba) ; 
Giovanni Boccaccio, Tales from the Decameron (R. 
Adlington trans., 1930); Thomas Bullfinch, Bullfinch’s 
Mythology (Crowell 1834, reprinted, Harper & Row 
1970); Homer, The Iliad (Alston H. Chase et al. eds., 
Little, Brown & Co. 1950); William Shakespeare, Hamlet 
(G.R. Hibbard ed., The Oxford Shakespeare 1987); 
Sophocles, Oedipus Rex (David Grene trans., 1954). 


In assessing whether Boring’s complaint states a claim 
we note that a “motion to dismiss for failure to state a 
claim is viewed with disfavor and is rarely granted.” See 
5A Charles A. Wright & Arthur R. Miller, Federal Prac- 
tice & Procedure: Civil 2d § 1357 (1990). It is “only 
in the unusual case where the complaint on its face reveals 
some insuperable bar to relief that dismissal under Rule 
12(b) (6) is warranted.” Fusco v. Xerox Corp., 676 F.2d 
332, 334 (8th Cir. 1984) (quotation omitted). This is 
hardly that “unusual case.” Indeed, defendants have not 
cited, and we have not found, a single instance in which 
any court has applied Hazelwood to dismiss a teacher’s 
complaint for failure to state a claim. Uniformly, courts 
have required school administrators to establish their legit- 
imate pedagogical concerns either by affidavit or at trial. 
See, e.g., Silano v. Sag Harbor Union Free Sch. Dist. Bd. 


43a 


of Educ., 42 F.3d 719 (2d Cir. 1994) (case resolved at 
summary judgment stage), cert. denied, 115 S. Ct. 2612 
(1995); Ward v. Hickey, 996 F.2d 448 (1st Cir. 1993) 
(jury verdict); Miles v. Denver Public Schs., 944 F.2d 
773 (10th Cir. 1991) (summary judgment): Bishop vy. 
Aronov, 926 F.2d 1066 (11th Cir. 1991) (same), cert. 
denied, 505 U.S. 218 (1992); Borger v. Bisciglia, 888 
F. Supp. 97 (E.D. Wis. 1995) (same). 


Although defendants may have had legitimate peda- 
gogical reasons for restricting Boring’s speech, none has 
been established on this record. Without any basis for 
determining the defendants’ intent in transferring Boring, 
other than Boring’s allegation that it was done “in bad 
faith and with malice toward [her] over the ideas ex- 
pressed in the play,” the district court should not have dis- 
missed the complaint on the theory that the asserted re- 
striction necessarily related to legitimate pedagogical 
concerns.

A fair reading of the relevant case law leads us to con- 
clude that the question is not, and never has been, whether 
teachers have First Amendment rights in the classroom, 
but how much school authorities can legitimately restrict 
those rights. Striking a balance between the school’s role 
as ultimate arbiter of the school curriculum and the teach- 
er’s limited in-class speech rights obviously presents a chal- 
lenge. We believe the approach set forth in Hazelwood, 
of requiring school authorities to provide a legitimate 
pedagogical basis for in-class speech restriction, provides 
the best means of navigating this challenge. 


We recognize, of course, that Hazelwood directly ad- 
dressed the free speech rights of students, not teachers. 
Although the Supreme Court has never held that the 
Hazelwood analysis who applies to teachers, certain dicta 
in Hazelwod support this conclusion. When the Court 
discussed whether the school newspaper at issue in the

Hazelwood analysis should apply to teacher speech in the 
classroom as well as to student speech. The First Circuit 
adopted the Hazelwood approach to assess restrictions on 
teacher speech after concluding that a teacher’s speech in 
class, like the student newspaper in Hazelwood, constitutes 
part of the school-sponsored curriculum. Ward, 996 F.2d

proper, “reasonable” limit on teacher’s speech in that set-

a university professor’s classroom comments about reli- 
gion). This limit on teacher speech, like the limit on stu- 
dent speech, ensures that students “learn whatever lessons 
[an] activity is designed to teach, that readers or listeners 
are not exposed to material that may be inappropriate for 
their level of maturity, and that the views of the individ-

Ward, 996 F.2d at 452 (quoting Hazelwood, 484 U.S. at 
271).

Webb, 394 F. Supp. at 799 (“state interest in limiting the 
discretion of teachers grows stronger . . . as the age of the 
students decreases”).

for relief on any possible theory. 


Shepard’s, Motions in Federal Court, § 5.123 (2d ed.

Plaintiff does not ask the Court to find that a unilateral

The selection and production of the play was not ex-

United States Supreme Court has recognized that some

Even if it is assumed that Plaintiff's conduct was “ex- 
pression,” the allegations of the amended complaint fail to 
state a claim. The Supreme Court in Hazelwood School 
Dist. v. Kuhlmeir, 484 U.S. 260 (1988), and Mt. Healthy

In Hazelwood, the Supreme Court found the publication 
of a school-sponsored newspaper which was part of the 
curriculum of a Journalism II class was done in a non- 
public forum; thus, the school could limit the students’ 
first amendment rights of expression in that paper as long 
as it was reasonably related to legitimate “pedagogical” 
concerns. Hazelwood, supra. In Mt. Healthy, the Su- 
preme Court ruled that when a teacher has engaged in 
protected speech, and that speech was a motivating factor 
in terminating the teacher, the school may rebut the show- - 
ing by bringing forth evidence that it would have dis- 
charged the teacher in any event. Mt. Healthy, supra. 


Here the teacher’s speech occurred in a non-public 
forum, like the teachers in Hazelwood and Mt. Healthy. 
The determination of “whether the employee is speaking 
out as a citizen, upon matters of public concern, or as an 
employee, upon matters only of personal interest” is in- 
applicable on these facts. 7d. at 192. The Fifth Circuit 
recently addressed a case where the teacher substituted his 
own supplemental reading list, without prior approval, for 
that which had been supplied by the school district as part 
of its curriculum. Kirkland v. Northside Independent 
School Dist., 890 F.2d 794, 795, 801 (Sth Cir. 1989), 
cert. denied, 496 U.S. 926 (1990). Indeed, the court in 
Kirkland cited the Hazelwood analysis, stating “[s]ince 
schools are typically not public forums, restrictions upon 
speech can be imposed in public schools as long as the 
constraints are ‘reasonably related to legitimate pedagogi- 
cal concerns’”’. Jd. at 801 (citations omitted).

C. Plaintiff's federal liberty interest cause of action. 


The right to liberty guaranteed in the Fourteenth 
Amendment extends to an individual’s right to engage in 
an occupation. Board of Regents v. Roth, 408 U.S. 564, 
572 (1972). “[W]here a person’s good name, reputation, 
honor, or integrity is at stake because of what the govern-

heard are essential.” /d. Under the standard set forth in 
Bishop v. Wood, 426 U.S. 341 (1976), overruled on 
other grounds, Cleveland Bd. of Educ. v. Loudermill, 
470 U.S. 532 (1985), a private communication with an 
employee concerning his termination does not state a 
claim because his reputation is not impaired in a public 
manner. Here, Defendant Yeager stated the reason for 
Plaintiff's transfer was her failure to comply with the con- 
troversial materials policy. Apparently this reason was 
the subject of her hearing before the Board. Plaintiff has 
alleged that the involuntary transfer occurred amid public 
speculation about her own morality and as a result of a 
hearing at which such speculation was not excluded. The 
result, she claims, is that she has been deprived of certain 
occupational opportunities and her public as well as pri- 
vate reputation has been injured. The Court does not 
agree that consideration of such speculation at Plaintiff's 
hearing before the school board constitutes impairment of 
her reputation in a public manner. The hearing itself 
granted Plaintiff notice and an opportunity to be heard. 


Nor does Plaintiff's transfer state a liberty interest. 
“The internal transfer of an employee, unless it constitutes 
such a change of status as to be regarded essentially as a 
loss of employment, does not provide the additional loss 
of a tangible interest necessary to give rise to a liberty 
interest meriting protection under the due process clause 
of the fourteenth amendment.” Thomas v. Smith, 897 


77a 


F.2d 154, 156 (Sth Cir. 1989) (citation omitted). Plain- 
tiff claims that the transfer to a position as an introductory 
drama teacher has interfered with her professional options 
and damaged her reputation. However, she has not sus- 
tained a reduction in pay. The transfer is not tantamount 
to a loss of employment. 


D. Plaintiff’s state cause of action for violation of free 
speech. 


The Court’s ruling supra that Plaintiff has not engaged 
in protected expression applies to the state cause of action 
as well. The Court adopts the Magistrate Judge’s alterna- 
tive Recommendation on this issue. Thus, those claims 
will also be dismissed. 


E. Plaintiff's state cause of action for deprivation of due 
process. 


The Court determined above that Plaintiff does not 
have a federal cause of action for deprivation of due 
process because she does not have a property interest 
stated by virtue of a transfer, as opposed to job termina- 
tion. For the reasons stated in the Memorandum and 
Recommendation, Plaintiff does not have a state cause 
of action based on deprivation of property. 


Moreover, Plaintiff failed to exhaust statutorily pre- 
scribed administrative and state court remedies prior to 
initiating court action. Thus, redress in the federal court 
is not appropriate at this time. Williams v. New Hanover 
County Bd. of Education, 104 N.C. App. 425, 409 S.E.2d 
753 (1991). 


F. Plaintiff's state cause of action for deprivation of 
liberty. 


For the reasons stated in the Memorandum and Recom- 
mendation and this Court’s discussion supra of the federal 
cause of action based on deprivation of liberty, the Court 


78a 


finds that Plaintiff has not stated a separate state cause 
of action for deprivation of liberty. The Court adopts 
this portion of the Memorandum and Recommendation. 


IV. ORDER 


IT IS, THEREFORE, ORDERED that the Defendants’ 
motions to dismiss Plaintiff's causes of action for failure 
to state claims upon which relief may be granted are 
hereby GRANTED. 


A Judgment dismissing this matter in its entirety will be 
filed herewith.

13. In the fall of 1991, Plaintiff produced and di-

the annual competition; Ivey did not comment or react. 


16. Before rehearsals began, Plaintiff sent the four stu-

the play did the parents of the student actresses complain 
to Ms. Boring’s about her conduct or the content or style

17. The play won a regional section of the NCTC 
competition, taking seventeen of twenty-one awards, in- 
cluding Best Actress, Best Ensemble Performance and 
Best Director. But before the play went to the state finals, 
controversy erupted. 


18. During the course of practicing and performing the 
play, one of the actresses spoke enthusiastically about it in

actress responded to the complaining students that plays

21. Defendant Ivey asked Ms. Boring for a copy of

that certain portions of the play be deleted. 


24. The students performed the play in the state com- 
petition in Greenboro and won second place. 


25. After their success there, Plaintiff invited Defend- 
ants Ivey and Yeager to come to a special performance 
of the play, where it would be screened for a national
