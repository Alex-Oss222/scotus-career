RESPONDENT'S BRIEF IN OPPOSITION 


The respondent, Dan A. Gwadosky, Maine’s Secre- 
tary of State (“Secretary”), respectfully requests that the 
Court deny the petition for a writ of certiorari seeking 
review of the decision and judgment of the Maine 
Supreme Judicial Court, sitting as the Law Court (“Law 
Court”), issued on July 28, 1998, reproduced in the 
Appendix to the Petition for a Writ of Certiorari (“Pet. 
App.”) at Pet. App. 1a-15a, and reported in Hart v. Secre- 
tary of State, 715 A.2d 169 (Me. 1998). 


* 


CONSTITUTIONAL AND STATUTORY 
PROVISIONS INVOLVED 


In addition to the constitutional and statutory provi- 
sions cited by the petitioners, the following constitutional 
and statutory provisions are implicated by this lawsuit 
and are reproduced in the attached Respondent’s Appen- 
dix (“Resp. App.”): Me. Const. art. IV, pt. 3, § 18 (Resp. 
App. la-2a); Me. Const. art. IV, pt. 3, § 22 (Resp. App. 3a); 
and Me. Rev. Stat. Ann. tit. 21-A, § 905 (West 1993 & 
Supp. 1998) (Resp. App. 3a-5a). 


¢ 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


The petitioners, Stephanie Hart and Mainers for 
Medical Rights, seek review of a decision that became 
moot as a result of the November 1998 election, concern- 
ing issues that the Law Court had not addressed previ- 
ously in the 90 years that Maine has had citizen’s 
initiatives. Because the conflict alleged by the petitioners 


between the Law Court's decision and Buckley v. American 
Constitutional Law Foundation, No. 97-930, is illusory, the 
Court should not even hold this case pending the out- 
come of Buckley to issue a decision that will have no 
practical consequence to the parties. 


In 1909, Maine amended its Constitution to provide 
for a citizens’ initiative, whereby its citizens may initiate 
and pass legislation. See 1907 Me. Const. Resolves, ch. 121 
(effective January 6, 1909) (codified as amended at Me. 
Const. art. IV, pt. 3, § 18; Resp. App. la-2a). The citizens’ 
initiative has become an ingrained feature of Maine’s 
political landscape, with citizens’ initiatives appearing on 
virtually every statewide ballot in the past 25 years. Last 
year, the Law Court addressed for the first time a consti- 
tutional challenge concerning the twin requirements that 
petition circulators be Maine residents and be Maine 
registered voters. 


The petitioners are seeking to enact by citizens’ ini- 
tiative a measure to legalize the use of marijuana for 
medical purposes in Maine. On November 24, 1997, the 
Secretary approved the form of the petition, which, under 
then existing law, gave the petitioners three years, i.e., 
until November 24, 2000, to circulate their petitions and 
submit the required number of signatures. See Me. Rev. 
Stat. Ann. tit. 21-A, § 903-A (West Supp. 1998) (amended 
by 1997 Me. Laws ch. 637 (reducing circulation period to 
one year for subsequent citizens’ initiatives)). 


By February 24, 1998, i.e., three months after the 
Secretary approved their petition for circulation, the peti- 
tioners submitted 68,330 signatures for the Secretary’s 
review. Under Maine’s Constitution, in order to qualify a 


sneha 


citizens’ initiative for the ballot, the proponents must 
submit valid signatures equivalent to “10% of the total 
vote for Governor cast in the last gubernatorial election 
preceding the filing of such petition.” Me. Const. art. IV, 
pt. 3, § 18(2); Resp. App. la. If the measure is not adopted 
without change by the Legislature, it is submitted to the 
people as a ballot question at the next statewide election. 
Me. Const. art. IV, pt. 3, § 18(3); Resp. App. 2a. 


At the time the petitioners filed their petition, based 
on the November 1994 gubernatorial election, the mini- 
mum number of valid signatures necessary to qualify a 
citizens’ initiative measure for the ballot was 51,131. The 
Secretary found that 21,821 of the signatures submitted to 
him were invalid, and, since the remaining 46,509 signa- 
tures fell below the threshold of 51,131 valid signatures, 
he declined to qualify the measure for the November 1998 
ballot. 


The petitioners then filed suit in Maine Superior 
Court pursuant to Me. Rev. Stat. Ann. tit. 21-A, § 905 
(West 1993 & Sunp. 1998); Resp. App. 3a-5a, challenging 
both the factual and the legal bases of the Secretary’s 
decision. Because the Law Court had not yet issued its 
decision in Palesky v. Secretary of State, 711 A.2d 129 (Me. 
1998), establishing the procedure for resolving factual 
disputes concerning the validity of signatures in citizens’ 
initiatives, the Superior Court and the Secretary, on 
remand, conducted further proceedings to resolve the 
petitioners’ factual challenges. Pet. App. 4a-5a, 35a-39a. 
Although these proceedings delayed the outcome of this 
matter, they ultimately were inconsequential since the 
petitioners abandoned their factual challenges on appeal 
to the Law Court. See Appellants’ Brief at 5 n.1. 


The petitioners challenged the constitutionality of the 
requirement that the petition circulators be Maine resi- 
dents, which had resulted in the invalidation of 347 sig- 
natures, and the requirement that the petition circulators 
be Maine registered voters, which had resulted in the 
invalidation of 4,347 signatures. The Superior Court 
rejected both challenges, Pet. App. 18a-35a, and, on 
appeal to the Law Court, the petitioners conceded that 
“Petitioners must strike down both the residency and the 
voter registration requirements to qualify for the Novem- 
ber 1998 ballot.” Appellants’ Brief at 5 n.1 (emphasis 
added). 


The Law Court reached only the issue of residency, 
holding that this requirement did not offend the first 
amendment. Pet. App. 5a-14a. The Court specifically 
declined to reach petitioners’ challenge to the registration 
requirement, noting that “[b]ecause the number of signa- 
tures invalidated on the basis of the circulator’s residence 
alone would prevent the initiative from being placed on 
the November 1998 ballot, we affirm the judgment with- 
out ruling on the voter registration requirement.” Pet. 
App. 2a. 


Less than two weeks after the petitioners filed their 
Petition for a Writ of Certiorari (“Petition”), Maine held 
its November 1998 gubernatorial election. Fewer persons 
cast votes in that election than in the previous guber- 
natorial election held in 1994. Accordingly, the minimum 
number of petition signatures necessary to qualify an 
initiative measure for the ballot is now 42,101, i.e., 9,030 
less than the number of valid signatures previously 
required, and, more importantly, 4,408 less than the 


———————————E 


number of valid signatures previously submitted by the 
petitioners. Resp. App. 9a. 


On November 24, 1998, the petitioners again submit- 
ted their petition for the Secretary’s review. Resp. App. 
6a-8a. The petitioners resubmitted the 46,509 signatures 
that the Secretary had previously found to be valid, along 
with new petitions containing an additional 1,024 signa- 
tures. Resp. App. 9a. On December 23, 1998, the Secretary 
issued his determination that, in light of the newer lower 
threshold of 42,101 valid signatures, without even consid- 
ering the signatures invalidated on the grounds of resi- 
dency or registration, the petitioners had submitted 
sufficient valid signatures to have the petitioners’ initia- 
tive placed on the November 1999 ballot. Resp. App. 
9a-1la. The petitioners, therefore, seek review of two 
legal issues of no consequence to them, one of which was 
never addressed by Maine’s highest court.

REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 


I. THE PETITIONERS’ CHALLENGE IS MOOT AND 
IS OF NO RECURRING LEGAL SIGNIFICANCE 
TO THE PETITIONERS. 


Regardless of the outcome of the petitioners’ consti- 
tutional challenges, the petitioners have now succeeded 
in their stated aim to place their initiative on either the 
November 1998 or the November 1999 ballot. See Appel- 
lants’ Brief at 5; Petition at 11-12 n.1, 15 n.2. The peti- 
tioners submitted 46,509 valid signatures by February 24, 
1998, which was less than 10% of the ballots cast in the 
November 1994 election, but more than 10% of the ballots 


cast in the November 1998 election. The petitioners’ ini- 
tiative therefore qualifies to be on the November 1999 
ballot. The petitioners thus seek an advisory opinion from 
the Court on the constitutionality of Maine’s require- 
ments that circulators of citizens’ initiatives be Maine 
residents aid be Maine registered voters. 


The petitioners assert in passing that this case is not 
moot for two reasons. Petition at 11-12 n.1. First, the 
petitioners contend that if they prevail on either or both 
of their constitutional challenges, it “would either qualify 
Petitioners’ citizens’ initiative for the November 1999 bal- 
lot or reduce the number of additional signatures that 
they would need to collect to qualify for that ballot.” 
Petition at 12 n.1. This argument, however, only under- 
scores the mootness of this challenge since the peti- 
tioners’ citizens’ initiative will now be on the November 
1999 ballot without regard to the petitioners’ constitu- 
tional challenges. 


Second, the petitioners contend that this case “falls 
squarely” within the “capable of repetition yet evading 
review” exception to the mootness doctrine articulated in 
Murphy v. Hunt, 455 U.S. 478 (1982). See Petition at 12 n.1. 
The petitioners misquote the standard, which provides 
that, to fall within the exception, “there must be a ‘rea- 
sonable expectation’ or a ‘demonstrated probability’ that 
the same controversy will recur involving the same com- 
plaining party.” Murphy v. Hunt, 455 U.S. at 482 (empnasis 
added to show portion excised by the petitioners). Since 
the petitioners’ citizens’ initiative will be on the Novem- 
ber 1999 ballot in light of the Secretary’s recent decision 
on their resubmitted signatures, the petitioners are cer- 
tainly incorrect when they argue that “[l]acking action by 


———————— 


the Court, Petitioners will face the same First Amend- 
ment harm when they attempt to place their petition on 
the November 1999 ballot.” Petition at 12 n.1 (citation 
omitted). 


This also is not the sort of a dispute that is so evanes- 
cent that it necessarily evades review by this Court. 
Under both the Maine Constitution and Maine statutes, 
the Secretary is required to review all petitions on an 
expedited basis, and the courts are required to resoive all 
judicial challenges on an expedited basis. See Me. Const. 
art. IV, pt. 3, § 22 (Resp. App. 3a); Me. Rev. Stat. Ann. tit. 
21-A, § 905 (West 1993 & Supp. 1998) (Resp. App. 3a-5a). 
The petitioners filed their challenge on April 7, 1998, and, 
even with the additional proceedings necessitated by 
their ultimately abandoned factual challenges (and now 
unnecessary in light of the Law Court’s decision in Pal- 
esky v. Secretary of State, 711 A.2d 129 (Me. 1998)), 
obtained a decision from Maine’s highest court on July 
28, 1998. If these constitutional questions ever arise again, 
it certainly is reasonable to believe that in light of these 
expedited procedures, the complaining party could seek 
and obtain review from the Court well in advance of an 
election. 


Furthermore, a citizens’ initiative can appear on the 
ballot in any November statewide election. Me. Const. art 
IV, pt. 3, § 18(3); Resp. App. 2a. This contrasts with a 
candidate’s need to appear on the ballot in a particular 
election for a public office that is filled only periodically. 
Thus, if and when the Court reviews a live controversy 
concerning the constitutionality of Maine’s residence and 
voter registration requirements, it can provide effective 
relief to the complaining party who seeks to place a 


citizens’ initiative on the ballot. The Court need not 
resolve these constitutional issues in the abstract at the 
urging of a party for whom this controversy is of no 
continuing consequence. 


Il. THE LAW COURT CORRECTLY REJECTED THE 
PETITIONERS’ CHALLENGE ON GROUNDS 
THAT DO NOT CONFLICT WITH OTHER 
COURTS. 


The petitioners’ primary argument is that the Court 
should grant review because the decision below conflicts 
with decisions from the Eighth Circuit and the Tenth 
Circuit invalidating voter registration requirements for 
circulators of citizens’ initiatives. See Petition at 14-19 
(citing Bernbeck v. Moore, 126 F.3d 1114 (8th Cir. 1997); 
American Constitutional Law Foundation, Inc. v. Meyer, 120 
F.3d 1092 (10th Cir. 1997), cert. granted sub nom. Buckley v. 
American Constitutional Law Foundation, Inc., 118 S. Ct. 
1033 (1998), and cert. denied, 118 S. Ct. 1045 (1998)). This 
conflict is entirely illusory since the Law Court made 
plain it was not ruling on the petitioners’ constitutional 
challenge to the voter registration requirement: “Because 
the number of signatures invalidated on the basis of the 
circulator’s residence alone would prevent the initiative 
from being placed on the November 1998 ballot, we 
affirm the judgment without ruling on the voter registra- 
tion requirement.” Pet. App. 2a. 


The petitioners attempt to surmount this obstacle by 
claiming in passing that although the Law Court 
expressly stated it was not ruling on the voter registration 
requirement, it nevertheless did so because it affirmed 


the Superior Court’s “order and decision * * * in all 
respects.” Petition at 15 n.2. This argument is belied by 
the unequivocal language of the Law Court’s decision 
quoted above. 


Additionally, the Law Court did only what it was 
asked to do by the petitioners. In the conclusion to their 
brief in the Law Court, the petitioners only sought rever- 
sal of the Secretary’s decision (not the Superior Court's 
“order and decision”) declining to place their initiative 
on the November 1998 ballot. See Appellants’ Brief at 21. 
Furthermore, the petitioners virtually invited the Law 
Court to consider only one of their constitutional chal- 
lenges with their concession that “Petitioners must strike 
down both the residency and the voter registration 
requirements to qualify for the November 1998 ballot.” 
Appellants’ Brief at 5 n.1 (emphasis added). The peti- 
tioners should not be heard to complain about the Law 
Court's refusal to reach their constitutional challenge to 
the voter registration requirement, and the Law Court 
should be taken at its word that it was unnecessary to do 
so. There is no conflict between the decision below and 
the Eighth ard Tenth Circuit decisions on the voter regis- 
tration requirement. 


The Law Court did determine that it was constitu- 
tional to require that circulators of citizens’ initiatives be, 
in fact, citizens of the State of Maine. Pet. App. 5a-14a. 
The petitioners argue that this decision conflicts with a 
district court’s implicit rejection of a residency require- 
ment for circulators. See Petition at 28 (citing Term Limits 
Leadership, Inc. v. Clark, 984 F. Supp. 470, 475 (S.D. Miss. 
1997)). Suffice it to say, this Court does not review alleged 
conflicts between a decision of the highest court of a State 


10 


and the dicta of a lower federal court. See R. Stern, E. 
Gressman, S. Shapiro & K. Geller, Supreme Court Practice, 
§ 4.8 (7th ed. 1993). Indeed, although numerous states 
have citizens’ initiative procedures, the petitioners do not 
cite - and we are not aware — of any cases concerning the 
constitutionality of a residency requirement, which fur- 
ther militates against Court review of the decision below. 
Cf. American Constitutional Law Foundation, Inc. v. Meyer, 
120 F.3d at 1100 (assuming, but not deciding, that “the 
state’s potentially compelling interest in preserving the 
integrity of its elections requires ali circulators to be 
residents,” although concluding that the registration 
requirement was not narrowly tailored to achieve this 
interest). 


The petitioners not only seek review of a constitu- 
tional issue upon which no other court has yet opined, 
but they seek review of a decision that is manifestly 
correct. In reserving for themselves the sovereign power 
to enact legislation by initiative, the citizens of Maine 
were careful to reserve for themselves the right to partici- 
pate in this unique legislative process: 


In short, the sovereign which is the people, has 
taken back, subject to the terms and limitations 
of the amendment, a power vested in the Legis- 
lature when Maine became a state. 


Farris v. Goss, 143 Me. 227, 231, 60 A.2d 908, 910 (1948). 
Thus, only residents of the State may propose measures 
by initiative, circulate and sign petitions in an effort to 


place them on the ballot, and vote to enact them as law. 
Me. Const. art IV, pt. 3, § 20.

Pet say Lemme

The Law Court correctly held that Maine’s interest in 
preserving, for members of its own political community, 
the power to engage in this retained sovereign power to 
legislate is compelling, and that the residency require- 
ment is narrowly tailored to advance this interest. Even 
the petitioners recognize the importance of local citizen 
participation (or the appearance of such participation) in 
the citizen’s initiative process by calling their group 
“Mainers for Medical Rights.” The Law Court’s conclu- 
sion that the residency requirement also advances the 
States’s compelling interest in ensuring the integrity of 
the initiative process was also correct. There simply is no 
pressing national need for the Court to review this deci- 
sion, or even to hold this case pending the outcome of 
buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation, No. 
97-930.

The Court should deny the petition for a writ of

Section 18. Direct initiative of legislation. 


1. Petition procedure. The electors may propose to 
the Legislature for its consideration any bill, resolve or 
resolution, including bills to amend or repeal emergency 
legislation but not an amendment of the State Constitu- 
tion, by written petition addressed to the Legislature or 
to either branch thereof and filed in the office of the 
Secretary of State by the hour of 5:00 p.m., on or before 
the 50th day after the date of convening of the Legislature 
in first regular session or on or before the 25th day after 
the date of convening of the Legislature in second regular 
session. If the 50th or 25th day, whichever applies, is a 
Saturday, Sunday, or legal holiday, the period runs until 
the hour of 5:00 p.m., of the next day which is not a 
Saturday, Sunday, or legal holiday. 


2. Referral to electors unless enacted by the Legis- 
lature without change; number of signatures necessary 
on direct initiative petitions; dating signatures on peti- 
tions; competing measures. For any measure thus pro- 
posed by electors, the number of signatures shall not be 
less than 10% of the total vote for Governor cast in the last 
gubernatorial election preceding the filing of such petition. 
The date each signature was made shall be written next to 
the signature on the petition, and no signature older than 
one year from the written date on the petition shall be 
valid. The measure thus proposed, unless enacted without 
change by the Legislature at the session at which it is 
presented, shall be submitted to the electors together with 


2a 


any amended form, substitute, or recommendation of the 
Legislature, and in such manner that the people can 
choose between the competing measures or reject both. 
When there are competing bills and neither receives a 
majority of the votes given for omagainst both, the one 
receiving the most votes shall at the next statewide elec- 
tion to be held not less than 60 days after the first vote 
thereon be submitted by itself if it receives more than '/s of 
the votes given for and against both. If the measure initi- 
ated is enacted by the Legislature without change, it shall 
not go to a referendum vote unless in pursuance of a 
demand made in accordance with the preceding section. 
The Legislature may order a special election on any mea- 
sure that is subject to a vote of the people. 


3. Timing of elections; proclamation by Governor. 
The Governor shall, by proclamation, order any measure 
proposed to the Legislature as herein provided, and not 
enacted by the Legislature without change, referred to the 
people at an election to be held in November of the year 
in which the petition is filed. If the Governor fails to 
order a measure proposed to the Legislature and not 
enacted without change to be submitted to the people at 
such an election by proclamation within 10 days after the 
recess of the Legislature to which the measure was pro- 
posed, the Secretary of State shall, by proclamation, order 
such measure to be submitted to the people at an election 
as requested, and such order shall be sufficient to enable 
the people to vote.

or for a direct initiative under the Constitution of Maine,

DETERMINATION OF THE VALIDITY 
OF A PETITION FOR INITIATED 
LEGISLATION ENTITLED: 


“An Act to Permit the Medical Use of Marijuana” 


1. On November 24, 1998, petitioners resubmitted to 
the Secretary of State for review, pursuant to the Consti- 
tution of Maine, Article IV, Part Third, Section 18, peti- 
tions containing 68,330 signatures which were already on 
file, plus new petitions containing an additional 1,024 
signatures, for a combined total of 69,354 signatures, on 
behalf of the initiated legislation entitled, “An Act to 
Permit the Medical Use of Marijuana.” 


2. The number of valid signatures required for an 
initiative petition to qualify for submission to the Legisla- 
ture pursuant to the Constitution of Maine, Article IV, 
Part Third, Section 18, is 10% of the total vote cast for 
Governor in the most recent gubernatorial election pre- 
ceding this filing. That election occurred on November 3, 
1998, and the qualifying number is now 42,101 signatures. 
Although, as petitioners requested, I have based this 
determination of validity on a review of all of the signa- 
tures submitted (both newly submitted and previously 
filed), it is clear that petitioners already had in excess of 
the minimum number of signatures to qualify under the 
new threshold, without submitting any additional signa- 
tures for review. 


3. Based upon a consolidated review of the petitions 
which were previously submitted, and the additional 


petitions submitted for the first time on November 24,
