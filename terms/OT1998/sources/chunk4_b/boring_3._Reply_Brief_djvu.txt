ARGUMENT 


Like school districts throughout the country, respondent 
Buncombe County Board of Education sets the general 
curriculum for its schools and prescribes certain imple- 
menting curricular materials. At the same time—and 
again like districts throughout the country—the Board 
calls upon its teachers, through the exercise of their 
professional judgment, to select additional curricular 
materials. 


In the case of plaintiff Margaret Boring, the Board 
called on Ms. Boring to select a play to be presented by 
her advanced drama class in a statewide competition held 
each year. School officials did not tell Boring what play 
to select; it was her responsibility to choose a play, and 
then to notify her principal of the selection she had made. 
App. 81a-82a. 


| 


2 


The question presented by this case is whether, having 
called upon Boring to determine what play would be pro- 
duced by her students, school officials had absolute author- 
ity to discipline her after the fact because of objections to 
controversial ideas expressed in the play, regardless of the 
reasons for the objections and despite the fact that Boring 
had been given no notice whatsoever that her selection of 
the play might subject her to discipline. 


1. The question so presented is an important one— 
not only for teachers, but for students and all who have 
a stake in the public schools. 


If, as the Fourth Circuit held by a 7-6 vote, teachers 
may be disciplined on ideological grounds for their selec- 
tion of instructional materials, without notice and regard- 
less of the reasons motivating the school officials, the 
result will be “a highly efficient in terrorem mechanism.” 
Keyishian v. Board of Regents, 385 U.S. 589, 601 
(1967). 


Far from effectuating legitimate pedagogical decisions 
of school officials, this will cause teachers to “steer far 
wider,” id. at 604, of anything that might be deemed 
controversial by any segment of the community. The 
result will be a stifling of that “ ‘free play of the spirit 
all teachers ought especially to cultivate and practice,’” 
id. at 601 (quoting Wieman v. Updegraff, 344 U.S. 183, 
195 (1952) (Frankfurter, J., concurring) ), and a con- 
comitant dilution of the content of the education that 
public school students will receive. 


Unable to mount any convincing argument that the ques- 
tion presented by the decision below does not warrant 
review by this Court, respondents seek to reduce the issue 
to a trivial proposition that we agree would admit only 
of the answer respondents propose. To that end, respond- 
ents attempt to reframe the issue in a manner that simply 
ignores the crucial points that divided the court below. 


3 


Seizing on language from Judge Widener’s opinion that 
obscures the critical aspects of the Fourth Circuit’s ruling, 
respondents assert that the case turns on whether the First 
Amendment gives a teacher the “right to- participate in 
the makeup of the school curriculum.” Brief in Opposi- 
tion (“Opp.”) i, 3, 4. But that is not what this case is 
about: the six dissenting members of the en banc court 
agreed that “school administrators must ‘be permitted to 
have the final say in setting the appropriate curriculum 
so that students are not exposed to material that detracts 
from or impedes the school’s pedagogical mission.’” App. 
32a (Motz, J., dissenting from the decision en banc) (quot- 
ing panel opinion). As they quite rightly recognized, 
Boring’s claims are entirely consistent with that proposition. 


This case was decided on a motion to dismiss, and the 
allegations of the complaint are not that Boring sought to 
supplant the authority of school officials to determine 
curriculum. Rather, the complaint alleges (i) that Boring 
carried out her responsibility to select a play and to inform 
her principal of the play she selected, and then proceeded 
to produce the play only after the prineipal voiced no 
objection; ! (ii) that Boring complied with both the letter 
and spirit of the school’s policy regarding the use of con- 
troversial materials in the curriculum;? and (iii) that the 
school officials, “actfing] . . . with malice toward plaintiff 
over the ideas expressed in the play,” subsequently de- 
cided to “punish and retaliate against her for expressing 
controversial ideas through the production of the play,” 
amidst unfounded accusations from some members of the 
community “that the play was obscene and that Boring 
was immoral.” 3

3. Contrary to respondents’ contentions, this Court has 
never directly addressed the question presented here, but 
the decisions of this Court that are most closely on point 
suggest strongly that the Court below erred. 


a. This Court has recognized that school officials are 
not free to act “simply because they dislike the ideas con- 
tained in [a] book[ ]”; rather, their actions must be mo- 
tivated by concerns for “educational suitability.” Board of 
Education v. Pico, 457 U.S. 853, 871-73 (1982) (opinion 
of Brennan, J.). See Pet. 13 and n.3 (summarizing the 
other opinions in Pico). The Court also has held that, 
even as to matters that “may fairly be characterized as 
part of the school curriculum,” actions of school officials 
that restrict speech violate the First Amendment if they 
are not “reasonably related to legitimate pedagogical con- 
cerns.” Hazelwood School District v. Kuhlmeier, 484 U.S. 
260, 271, 273 (1988). And, the Court has declared that 
“(t]he danger of a chilling effect upon the exercise of 
vital First Amendment rights must be guarded against by 
sensitive tools which clearly inform teachers what is being 
proscribed.” Keyishian, 385 U.S. at 604. 


Although we have been frank to acknowledge that nei- 
ther these nor other decisions of this Court settle the ques- 
tion presented here, see Pet. 12-18, they point strongly 
against the notion that school officials’ authority on ques- 
tions relating to the curriculum is so unlimited as to leave 
teachers without any First Amendment protection when 
they are disciplined, without notice and without any le- 
gitimate reason, for their selection of teaching materials.

this Court decided in those cages. 


Connick did not hold that an individual’s speech “as an 
employee” is unprotected in all circumstances: the Court’s 
holding was confined to speech involving “matters only 
of personal interest,” such as a mere “employee grievance.” 
461 U.S. at 147, 154 (emphasis added). This is not such 
a case. See App. 29a-32a (Motz, J., dissenting from the 
decision en banc). 


As for Rust and Rosenberger, this Court’s decision in 
Pico shows how problematic it is to Suggest that the 
government's role as the provider of public educational 
programs (in Pico, school libraries) is one that may be 
exercised without regard to First Amendment interests. 


In all events, this Court has not yet considered how 
the principles adumbrated in Rust and Rosenberger 
should be given proper effect in a case where the govern- 
ment has disciplined an employee for expressive activities 
that are undertaken as part of the employee’s job. Re- 
spondents’ contention that Rust and Rosenberger dictate 
the conclusion that teachers and other public employees 
have no First Amendment rights in this setting cannot 
be reconciled with the numerous decisions we have cited 
that are in conflict with the Fourth Circuit's holding. See 
Pet. 9-10. See also Moore y. City of Wynnewood, 57 F.3d 
924, 933 (10th Cir. 1995) (“An employee’s speech is not 
exempted from First Amendment protection just be- 


cause it occurred on the job or as part of an employee's 
official functions.”).6 There simply is nothing in Rust 
or Rosenberger to suggest that the government’s general 
authority to determine the content of speech it funds 
gives the government carte blanche to discipline a teacher 
—or, for that matter, any employee—for having engaged 
in speech that the employee could not reasonably have 
thought was prohibited. In fact, Rust suggests the oppo- 
site. See Rust, 500 U.S. at 200 (discussing Keyishian). 


The uncertainties that attend the application of Connick, 
Rust and Rosenberger in this context are a compelling 
reason to grant certiorari, not to deny it.

is even more tenuous. Respondents make much of lan- 
guage that appears in Edwards v. Aguillard, 482 U.S. 
578, 584 n.6 (1987). See Opp. 8. But, in the passages to 
which respondents refer, the Court merely noted in dictum 
that “‘[a]cademic freedom,’ at least as it is commonly 
understood,” does not give a teacher a right “to teach 
courses different from what is required.” We fully accept 
that proposition, as did the six dissenters below, but it 
does not begin to decide this case.
