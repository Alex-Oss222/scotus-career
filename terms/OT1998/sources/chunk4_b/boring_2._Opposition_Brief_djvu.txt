STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


For the most part, the Respondents agree that the Petitioner’s 
“Statement of the Case” provides an accurate synopsis of the 
allegations contained in the Amended Complaint under the caption 
“Factual Background” and an accurate summary of the decisions 
of the courts below under the caption “Proceedings Below.” 
Rather than recast each element of the Petitioner’s Statement of 
the Case, the Respondents will instead note the following areas 
of dissatisfaction with that portion of her Petition: 


A. Factual Background 


The Petitioner attempts to recast the nature of her claims 
rather than simply pointing the Court to the specific 
allegations which are at issue in this Petition. More 
specifically, the Petitioner alleged in her Amended 
Complaint only that the Respondents’ actions “served to 
punish and retaliate against her for expressing controversial 
ideas through the production of the play.” App. 87a, 
q 48. Fs 


The statement that the Petitioner “notified the principal 
... that she had selected Independence as the play to be 
performed in the competition, and he did not object” 
could be interpreted to mean that she showed the principal 
the play or told him about its content. App. 2a. The 
Amended Complaint, however, alleges only that the 
Petitioner notified her principal as to the name of the play 
she had chosen. App. 82a. 


The Petitioner fails to mention that in the spring of 1992, 
the maple stage floor in the school’s new auditorium was 
damaged in the course of preparing the stage fora musical 
which the Petitioner was producing. App. 3a; 84a-85a, 
{9 27-33.

* The Petitioner asserts that the play was performed in the

REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 
The Petition overstates the scope of the Fourth Circuit’s 


decision and would have this Court review the Fourth Circuit’s 
decision based on unfounded exceptions to well-established First 


4 


Amendment principles. Contrary to the assertions in the Petition, 
the Fourth Circuit’s limited holding is consistent with relevant 
First Amendment jurisprudence as well as sound principles of 
common sense and public policy. 


I. 


THE SCOPE OF THE FOURTH CIRCUIT’S 
DECISION IS APPROPRIATELY LIMITED. 


The Petitioner implies that the Fourth Circuit’s decision has 
far reaching implications and limits a wide range of rights under 
the First Amendment. As noted above, however, the majority 
opinion clearly limits its application to the specific circumstances 
addressed in this case, holding that, 


The case does not concern any right a teacher might 
have to participate in the makeup of the curriculum of 
a public high school other than the right claimed here 
under the First Amendment. 


App. 13a, n.2. The extent of the Fourth Circuit’s decision is 
expressly limited to the proposition that a public secondary school 
teacher does not have a First Amendment right to “participate in 
the makeup of the school curriculum through the selection and 
production ofa play.” App. 2a. 


The Petitioner contends that the Fourth Circuit’s decision is 
inconsistent with her rights under the First Amendment. The First 
Amendment, however, provides only that, “Congress shall make 
no law. . . abridging the freedom of speech.” U.S. Const., Amdt. 
1 (emphasis added). The limited holding of the Fourth Circuit 
does not “abridge” the rights of teachers to speak out as private 
citizens. Whether the Petitioner’s concern in the present case 
was with a curriculum which she viewed as overly restrictive or 


5 


with the issues of lesbianism and illegitimacy raised in 
Independence, she was as free as any other citizen of the 
community to express her views, for example in a statement or 
presentation to the Superintendant or Board of Education about 
what materials she should be permitted to use. 


In this sense, the Fourth Circuit’s restriction on a public 
school teacher’s classroom speech is no different from the 
restrictions on Title X employees established in Rust v. Sullivan, 
500 U.S. 173 (1991): 


Individuals who are voluntarily employed for a Title 
X project must perform their duties in accordance 
with the regulation’s restrictions on abortion 
counseling and referral. The employees remain free, 
however, to pursue abortion-related activities when 
they are not acting under the auspices of the Title X 
project. The regulations, which govern solely the 
scope of the Title X project’s activities, do not in any 
way restrict the activities of those persons acting as 
private individuals. The employees’ freedom of 
expression is limited during the time that they actually 
work for the project; but this limitation is a 
consequence of their decision to accept employment 
in a project, the scope of which is permissibly 
restricted by the funding authority. 


500 U.S. at 198-199.

THE FOURTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION IS 
CONSISTENT WITH BASIC PRINCIPLES GOVERNING 
A PUBLIC EMPLOYEE’S RIGHTS UNDER THE FIRST 
AMENDMENT. 


1. The majority opinion of the en banc court below, as well 
as Judges Luttig and Wilkinson in their respective concurrences, 
relied heavily on this Court’s decision in Connick v. Myers, 461 
U.S. 138 (1983), which established a threshold test for determining 
whether a public employee’s speech is entitled to the protection 
of the First Amendment. Under Connick, a public employee’s 
speech is entitled to First Amendment protection only where the 
employee speaks as a private citizen on a matter of public concern. 


As Judge Luttig explained in his concurrence, the Connick 
model provides a useful framework for determining whether the 
allegations contained in the Petitioner’s Amended Complaint even 
give rise to a claim under the First Amendment, noting that, 


the analogy would seem to be pure between a teacher 
who, qua teacher, attempts to assert her own views 
through the curriculum itself, and any other public 
employee who, gua employee, attempts to assert her 
personal views through the official policies and 
decision of her office. 


App. 16a. The Petitioner’s alleged expression of unpopular views 
through curricular materials of her own choice therefore did not 
satisfy the threshold test under Connick and was not entitled to 
First Amendment protection. 


2. The Petitioner’s position, like that of the dissenters in the 
Fourth Circuit, rests on the erroneous assumption — contrary to 


7 


Connick — that anything she said in the context of a classroom 
was entitled to some degree of First Amendment protection merely 
because it satisfied a colloquial definition of being a matter of 
“public concern.” This Court has never recognized such an 
exception to the threshold requirement of Connick based solely 
on the nature of the employee’s position. If this assumption were 
accurate, then every public employee whose position necessarily 
entails matters of general interest to the public would be entitled 
to First Amendment protection for all speech in the context of 
her job, an outcome which would fly directly in the face of 
Connick’'s threshold test. 


The Petitioner mistakenly relies on the broad language of 
dicta from Keyishian v. Board of Regents, 385 U.S. 589 (1967) 
as support for the proposition implicit in her Petition that teachers 
are entitled to greater First Amendment protection than other 
public employees. In Bishop v. Aronov, 926 F.2d 1066 (1 1th Cir. 
1991), the Eleventh Circuit put Keyishian’s dicta in perspective: 


Keyishian dealt with that brand of regulation most 
offensive to a free society: loyalty oaths. The 
pronouncemient about academic freedom in that 
context, however, cannot be extrapolated to deny 
schools command of their own courses. . . . Though 
we are mindful of the invaluable role academic freedom 
plays in our public schools, particularly at the post- 
secondary level, we do not find support to conclude 
that academic freedom is an independent First 
Amendment right. And, in any event, we cannot 
supplant our discretion for that of the University. 
Federal Judges should not be ersatz deans or 
educators. 


926 F.2d at 1075. 


8 


Even Justice Brennan, who authored the oft-cited dicta in 
Keyishian, later acknowledged the significance of the distinction 
between the university context (as in Keyishian) and public 
secondary schools, noting that: 


The State exerts great authority and coercive power 
through mandatory attendance requirements, and 
because of the students’ emulation of teachers as role 
models and the children’s susceptibility to peer 
pressure.° 


* The potential for undue influence is far less 
significant with regard to college students who 
voluntarily enroll in courses. 


Edwards v. Aguillard, 482 U.S. 578, 584; 584, n.5 (1987). 


Justice Brennan further indicated in Edwards that his 
sweeping rhetoric regarding “academic freedom” would not apply 
in the secondary school context, explaining that: 


[I}n the State of Louisiana, courses in public schools 
are prescribed by the State Board of Education and 
teachers are not free, absent permission, to teach 
courses different from what is required. [citation to 
record omitted] “Academic freedom,” at least as it is 
commonly understood, is not a relevant concept in 
this context. 


Id. at 586, n.6. 


3. If a distinction may be drawn between the First 
Amendment nights of public secondary school teachers and other 
public employees at all, secondary school teachers are subject to 
greater restrictions. For example, in Bethel School District v. 
Fraser, 478 U.S. 675 (1986), this Court held:

Surely it is a highly appropriate function of public 
school education to prohibit the use of vulgar and 
offensive terms in public discourse. . . . Nothing in 
the Constitution prohibits the states from insisting that 
certain modes of expression are inappropriate and 
subject to sanctions. The inculcation of these values 
is truly the “work of the schools.” [citations omitted] 
The determination of what manner of speech in the 
classroom or in school assembly is inappropriate 
properly rests with the school board. 


Id. at 682-683. 


Similarly, in Hazelwood School District v. Kuhlmeier, 484 
U.S. 260 (1988), this Court held: 


Educators are entitled to exercise greater control. . . 
over. . . expressive activities that students, parents, 
and members of the public might reasonably perceive 
to bear the imprimatur of the school . . . to assure 
that participants learn whatever lessons the activity is 
designed to teach, that readers or listeners are not 
exposed to material that may be inappropriate for their 
level of maturity, and that the views of the individual 
speaker are not erroneously attributed to the school. 


Id. at 273. 


In subjecting the Petitioner to at least the same limitations on 
First Amendment protection as other public employees, the Fourth 
Circuit’ decision is therefore consistent with established law. 


4. The Petitioner contends that this case is similar in some 
respects to Board of Education v. Pico, 457 U.S. 853 (1982). 
The plurality decision in Pico, however, by its own terms does 


10 


not apply to the question before the Court in this case. The 
plurality in Pico specifically held that: 


[A]s this case is presented to us, it does not involve 
textbooks, or indeed any books that Island Tree 
students would be required to read. [footnote omitted] 
Respondents do not seek in this Court to impose 
limitations upon their school Board’s discretion to 
prescribe the curricula of the Island Trees schools. 
On the contrary, the only books at issue in this case 
are library books, books that by their nature are 
optional rather than required reading. Our adjudication 
of the present case thus does not intrude into the 
classroom, or into the compulsory courses taught 
there. 


457 U.S. at 861-862 (emphasis in original.) 


To the extent the Pico decision even addresses the issue of 
a school board’s discretion to control the curriculum in a public 
secondary school, it is consistent with the Fourth Circuit’s 
decision in this case. Contrasting the board’s discretion to control 
the curriculum with the discretion to remove books from the 
library, this Court held: 


Petitioners might well defend their claim of absolute 
discretion in matters of curriculum by reliance upon 
their duty to inculcate community values. But we think 
that petitioners’ reliance upon that duty is misplaced 
where, as here, they attempt to extend their claim of 
absolute discretion beyond the compulsory 
environment of the classroom. .. . 


Id. at 869 (emphasis in original.)

5. The Petitioner acknowledges that recent decisions of this 
Court establish that where the government itself is the speaker, it 
may determine what the message shall or shall not be. See Rust; 
Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors, 515 U.S. 819 (1995); National 
Endowment of the Arts v. Finley, _ U.S. __ (June 25, 1998). 
Pet.15. The Petitioner suggests that this principle may not apply 
to the context of public education, but with regard to a public 
university this Court specifically concluded in Rosenberger that: 


When the University determines the content of the 
education it provides, it is the University speaking, 
and we have permitted the government to regulate the 
content of what is or is not expressed when it is the 
speaker or when it enlists private entities to convey 
its Own message. 


515 U.S. at 833. Similarly, when a public school teacher presents 
“the content of the education” which the school provides, it is 
the school speaking, and the local board of education may regulate 
the content of what is or is not expressed. The limited holding of 
the Fourth Circuit that the First Amendment did not protect the 
Petitioner’s nght to select the curriculum was merely another way 
of stating the principle articulated in Rust and reaffirmed in 
Rosenberger. 


The principles of Rust aiid Rosenberger work hand-in-hand 
with the threshold test in Connick as set forth above. More 
specifically, where a public employee’s speech represents the 
speech of the governmental employer, it necessarily follows that 
it is not speech in her capacity as a private citizen on a matter of 
public concern. It therefore does not satisfy the threshold 
requirement of Connick and is not protected by the First 
Amendment.

THE FOURTH CIRCUIT PROPERLY DECLINED TO 
ADDRESS THE PETITIONER’S CONTENTION THAT 
THE FIRST AMENDMENT GUARANTEED “NOTICE” 
THAT HER SPEECH WAS PROSCRIBED. 


The petitioner submits that the decision of the court below 
rules out any requirement of fair notice where what she contends 
is “speech” involving the curriculum is concerned, calling into 
question the application of Keyishian. This position overstates 
both the holding of the Fourth Circuit, and, as noted above, the 
significance of Keyishian. The Fourth Circuit did not even 
address the merits of the Petitioner’s contention that the First 
Amendment, at least as it pertains to teachers, includes a 
requirement of notice as to precisely what speech is prohibited. 
Instead, it merely noted that, 


Plaintiff's contention that she was not given notice as 
to what was being proscribed is, of course, without 
merit, the plaintiff having no First Amendment right 
to participate in the makeup of the curriculum. 


App. 13a. This logical conclusion hardly calls into doubt any 
established principles of constitutional jurisprudence. The 
Petitioner’s contention puts the proverbial cart before the horse, 
seeking to impose a requirement of notice under the First 
Amendment to protect a public employee from the potential 
consequences of expression which the First Amendment does 
not even protect.'
