STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


The Ohio State Bar Association, in its Amicus Brief in the 
Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals filed in support of the 
Respondents, cogently set forth the rationale for striking down 
judicial campaign spending limits as unconstitutional: 

6 


If we are to continue to elect judges in Ohio, then a 
truly informed electorate is critical to maintaining 
public confidence in the judiciary. 


OSBA Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals Brief, pp. 2-3. In order 
for a candidate to inform an electorate, money is required: 
money to mail flyers to voters, money for television and radio 
advertisements, money to print campaign literature. 


Spending limits on judicial campaigns affect a candidate’s 
ability to speak to voters. They limit the quantity of political 
speech. Such quantitative limits on political speech are 
unconstitutional. This is the simple holding of Buckley v. Valeo, 
424 U.S. 1 (1976). An analogy suggested by this Court in 
Buckley, 424 U.S. 1 at n.18, tersely explains why spending limits 
fail to pass constitutional muster: 


Being free to engage in unlimited political 
expression subject to a ceiling on expenditures is 
like being free to drive an automobile as far and as 
often as one desires on a single tank of gasoline. 


The simplicity of this analysis was not lost on the District Court. 
it was not lost on the Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals; however, 
it certainly is lost on the Petitioners. 


2 


In order to justify their currently politically popular view 
that spending limits do not fall within the ambit of Buckley, 
the Petitioners must convince this Court that a person who runs 
for judge and speaks to voters about his or her qualifications 
for office somehow is not engaging in a political act worthy of 
First Amendment protection. The District Court in granting 
Appellees’ a preliminary injunction did not buy this argument, 
the Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals did not by this argument 
and neither should this Court.

REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 


Ohio citizens deserve the opportunity to hear whatever 
quantity of speech is desired by a particular candidate in their 
effort to inform the electorate. It is only through a campaign of 
free speech, unrestricted in quantity, that a voter may truly vote 
thoughtfully, intelligently, and with confidence for an effective 
judiciary. Restrictions which suppress the quantity of speech a 
judicial candidate can disseminate will only serve to reinforce 
the public’s misperception of the Ohio judiciary. 


Restrictions on the quantity of speech made by judicial 
candidates should be analyzed in the same manner as restrictions 
on the quantity of speech for candidates to other offices. 
Whether a person is running for judge or dogcatcher, the 
Constitution protects the quantity of all political speech. It is 


7 


under this analysis that the District Court correctly determined 
the expenditure limits of Canon VII(C)(6)(d) should be 
enjoined. 


Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), clearly addresses 
Canon VII’s campaign spending limitations. Under Buckley, 
campaign expenditure limits contained in the Federal Election 
Campaign Act were found to “necessarily reduce the quantity 
of expression by restricting the number of issues discussed, 
the depth of their exploration, and the size of the audience 
reached.” Buckley, 424 U.S. at 19. Despite Petitioners’ 
suggestions that Buckley does not apply to judicial campaign 
expenditures, they make no coherent, or supported, arguments 
that Buckley should not apply to the quantity of protected 
political speech uttered by judicial candidates. The District 
Court applied the correct standard of review, the same standard 
of review applied in Buckley as well as other decisions, in 
determining the constitutionality of Canon VII(C)(6) and (8). 
Simply stated, an infringement upon free speech must be 
narrowly tailored to serve a compelling interest. Buckley, 424 
U.S at 45. The infringement caused by Ohio’s Canon VII(C)(6) 
and (8) are not narrowly tailored.

THE SIXTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY REVIEWED THE 
LAW AS IT EXISTED AT THE TIME THE LAWSUIT 
WAS FILED AND DECIDED. 


Contrary to Petitioners’ arguments, the Sixth Circuit Court 
of Appeals did not err when it properly reviewed the questions 
before it — did the District Court abuse its discretion when it 
enjoined Petitioners from enforcing former Canon VII(C)(6)(a) 
which -vas the law when the suit was filed and decided. 


8 


The Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals properly held that the 
question of whether the district court abused its discretion in 
granting the preliminary injunction was still ripe. The Court 
found that if it were to determine that the district court erred in 
issuing the preliminary injunction, then the legal interests and 
positions of Respondents would be compromised as they have 
received no assurances that grievances will not be pursued and 
penalties not imposed under the old Canon. See Edgar v. Mite 
Corp., 457 U.S. 624, 647-54, 102 S. Ct. 2629, 2643-47, 73 L. 
Ed. 2d 269 (1982) (Stevens, J., concurring); McPherson v. 
Michigan High School Athletic Ass'n, Inc., 119 F.3d 453, 458 
(6" Cir. 1997) (“The test for mootness ‘is whether the relief 
sought would, if granted, make a difference to the legal interest 
of the parties.’ ”) (Citation omitted). Hence, the appellate 
court’s review of the district court’s grant and denial of 
preliminary injunction could not be said to be moot. 


The Circuit Court properly concluded that as to the 
substantive challenge to the constitutional merits of Canon 
VII(C)(6) and Canon VII(C)(8), it could no longer consider 
the former version of Canon VII(C)(6) as it has recently been 
amended citing Diffenderfer v. Central Baptist Church, 404 
U.S. 412, 414, 92 S. Ct. 574, 575, 30 L. Ed. 2d 567 (1972). 
However, the appellate court held that because such expenditure 
limitations for judicial candidates continues to remain in the 
revised Canon, there was still a case and controversy between 
the parties the Circuit Court cited this Court, when it found 
where a new Canon “is sufficiently similar to the [amended 
Canon] it is permissible to say that the challenged conduct 
continues,” the controversy is not mooted by the change, and a 
federal court continues to have jurisdiction. Northeastern Fla. 
Chapter v. City of Jacksonville, 508 U.S. 656, 662 n.3, 113 S. 
Ct. 2297, 2301 n.3, 124 L. Ed. 2d 586 (1993). Hence, the Sixth 
Circuit Court of Appeals correctly concluded there was 
jurisdiction over the revised Canon VII(C)(6), as well as Canon 
VII(C)(8). 


9 


The Sixth Circuit went on to hold that the fact that the 
Canon has been revised is of no import to its decision to refrain 
from reviewing the amended Canon because, it found where a 
change in law does not extinguish the controversy, the preferred 
procedure is for the court of appeals to remand the case to the 
district court for reconsideration of the case under the amended 
law. See Concerned Citizens of Vicksburg v. Sills, 567 F.2d 
646, 650 (5" Cir. 1978); Korn v. Franchard Corp., 456 F.2d 
1206, 1208 (2d Cir. 1972). 


The Appellate Court properly concluded that as the district 
court never had the opportunity to fully address the merits of 
Respondents’ challenge prior to the amendment during the 
appeal, nor did it have the opportunity to fully consider the 
constitutionality of the amended Canon, it would refrain from 
considering the merits of amended Canon VII(C)(6); limit its 
view to the consideration of the appropriateness of preliminary 
injunction under the old Canon VII(C)(6) and Canon VII(C)(8); 
and remand amended Canon VII(C)(6) to the District Court for 
a full review. 


This decision was well grounded in law and the record. 
Accordingly, this Honorable Court should decline to revisit the 
issue at this point in the federal judicial process.

THE COURT OF APPEALS AND DISTRICT COURT 
CORRECTLY HELD THAT THE OHIO SUPREME 
COURT’S JUDICIAL CAMPAIGN SPENDING LIMITS 
LACKED THE REQUISITE COMPELLING INTEREST 
FOR AN INFRINGEMENT ON FIRST 
AMENDMENT RIGHTS. 


When determining whether an act unconstitutionally 
burdens speech, there is a single test, a test which Petitioners 
cite, but also disregard: 


When considering whether a campaign finance law 
unconstitutionally infringes freedom of speech, this 
Court’s task is to decide whether the provision in 
question actually burdens the exercise of political 
speech and, if it does, whether it is narrowly tailored 
to serve a compelling state interest. 


Austin v. Michigun Chamber of Commerce, 494 U.S. 652, 657, 
110 S. Ct. 1391, 1396, 108 L. Ed. 2d 652 (1990) (citing Buckley 
[v. Vaieo], 424 U.S. 1, 96 S. Ct. 612, 46 L. Ed. 2d 659). The 
District Court and Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals correctly 
followed this test by holding that the judicial spending limits 
burdened political speech, do not serve a compelling state 
interest, and were not narrowly tailored. 


A. The Judicial Campaign Spending Limits Do Not 
Address a Compelling State Interest. 


This Court has set forth only two interests which can justify 
a reduction in political speech: preventing corruption or the 
appearance of corruption. 


11 


In FEC v. NCPAC, 470 U.S. 480, 496-497 (1985), this 
Court ruled: 


We held in Buckley and reaffirmed in Citizens 
Against Rent Control that preventing corruption or 
the appearance of corruption are the only legitimate 
and compelling government interests thus far 
identified for restricting campaign finances. In 
Buckley we struck down the FECA’s limitation on 
individuals’ independent expenditures because we 
found no tendency in such expenditures, 
uncoordinated with the candidate or his campaign, 
to corrupt or to give the appearance of corruption. 


«x * * 


Corruption is a subversion of the political process. 
Elected officials are influenced to act contrary to 
their obligations of office by the prospect of 
financial gain to themselves or infusions of money 
into their campaigns. The hallmark of corruption is 
the financial quid pro quo: dollars for political 
favors. But here the conduct proscribed is not 
contributions To the candidate, but independent 
expenditures in support of the candidate. ... The 
Court concluded Buckley that there was a 
fundamental constitutional difference between 
money spent to advertise one’s views independently 
of the candidate ’s campaign and money contributed 
to the candidate to be spent on his campaign. 


(Emphasis added.) This Court has yet to identify any other 
compelling state interests permitting core political speech to 
be burdened. Petitioners do not cite any Supreme Court or Sixth 
Circuit cases which expand the list of compelling state interests 
that jusitfy the regulation of political speech. 


12 


None of the state interests indentified in Petitioners’ Writ 
are associated with addressing corruption or the appearance of 
corruption. 


First, Petitioners assert that campaign speech by judicial 
candidates is at odds with preserving judicial independence and 
ensuring public trust and confidence in the courts. This has 
nothing to do with preventing corruption or the appearance of 
corruption. Petitioners do not assert that there is a correlation 
between public confidence in the judicial system and allowing 
a judicial candidate the opportunity simply to express his or 
her qualifications, background and expertise for the bench. 
Indeed, it should have just the opposite effect as citizens elect 
to the bench people they know are qualified. 


This Court can, should, and does encourage respect for the 
judiciary and judicial system, but it may not limit speech to 
achieve such goals. It is not a proper governmental function to 
instill confidence in public institutions through the regulation 
of speech. This Court’s flag burning cases make this precise 
point. In Texas v. Johnson, 491 U.S. 397, 409 (1989), this Court 
protected Johnson’s flag burning as an “expression of 
dissatisfaction with the policies of the Federal Government.” 
In Eichman v. United States, 496 U.S. 310, 318 (1990), this 
Court further pointed out that government may, by persuasion 
and example, encourage respect for national symbols and unity, 
but it may not proscribe speech to achieve those goals. 


Second, Petitioners assert a purported “compelling” interest 
in restricting judicial primary campaign spending because it 
undermines the public perception about fairness and impartiality 
of judges. Petitioners fail to explain how a judge who spends 
one dollar on his campaign is more fair or impartial than one 
who spends ten thousand dollars. Following through with this 
illogical argument, lower court judges, saddled with lower 


13 


spending limits than their appellate brethren, must be viewed 
by the public as much more fair to a litigant than an Ohio 
Supreme Court Justice. 


Finally, the Petitioners contend that a “compelling” interest 
in need of protection is that the judiciary appear to be “neutral 
and not beholden to contributors”. Petitioners cite a popular 
opinion poll which states that a majority of Ohioans believe 
campaign contributions influence judicial decisions and that 
spending limits would solve the problem. This argument is 
contrary to Ohio history and the Ohio Constitution. Through 
their Constitution, the citizens of Ohio have decided that they 
want politics in the selection of their judiciary. This is why 
judges in Ohio, at all state court levels, are elected. This is 
why judges in Ohio are elected in party primaries. Politics 
entails campaigning and money toward that effort. To suggest 
that the wisdom of the people of the State of Ohio, speaking 
through their Constitution, should be thrown out the window 
based upon a voodoo-like popular opinion poll is downright 
scary. It is even more disturbing that such an opinion is being 
advocated by these Petitioners. 


It cannot seriously be suggested that the State has shown a 
compelling interest for judicial campaign spending limits. 
Indeed, spending limits are directly contrary to a compelling 
state interest in fostering our “propound national commitment 
to the principle that debate on public issues should be 
uninhibited, robust, and wide open.” New York Times Company 
v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254, 270 (1964). One of those issues is 
electing qualified persons to the bench. An informed and 
knowledgeable electorate is a prerequisite to such elections. 


14 


B. Judicial Campaign Spending Limits Burden Political 
Speech and Do Not Advance Impartiality and/or the 
Appearance of Impartiality. 


The District Court held that judges advocating their eiection 
to the bench are engaging in political, not judicial, speech. 
Petitioners would have this Court believe that speech advocating 
the election of judges is not “political speech” and therefore is 
not entitled to the same First Amendment protections as speech 
advocating election of candidates to the legislature. According 
to Petitioners, “political speech” is “viewpoint related speech 
necessary for a voter to make informed decisions for election 
to political office.” 


Petitioners narrow definition of political speech is 
unsupported, in fact or by law. Political speech “relates to the 
advocacy of the election or defeat of candidates,” Buckley, 424 
U.S. at 47. In accord, Geary v. Renne, 911 F.2d 280 (9 Cir. 
1990) (Rhinehart, J. concurring), ACLU v. Florida Bar, 744 F. 
Supp. 1094, 1097 (N.D. Fla. 1990) (“Indeed, when a state 
decides that its trial judges are to be popularly elected, as Florida 
has done, it must recognize the candidate’s right to make 
campaign speeches and the concomitant right of the public to 
be informed about the judicial candidates.”) 


Buckley's holding is clear, concise, and firm: campaign 
spending limits are an unconstitutional restriction on the First 
Amendment protected right to free speech. This Court went on 
in Buckley to explain the effects of campaign spending limits 
on the First Amendment’s guarantee of freedom of speech: 


A restriction on the amount of money a person or 
group can spend on political communication during 
a campaign necessarily reduces the quantity of 
expression by restricting the number of issues 


15 


discussed, the depth of their exploration, and the 
size of the audience reached. This is because 
virtually every means of communicating ideas in
