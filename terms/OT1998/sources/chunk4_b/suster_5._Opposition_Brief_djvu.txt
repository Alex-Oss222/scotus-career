STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


On September 10, 1996, the trial court issued an order 
enjoining the Petitioners (“the State”) from enforcing 
Canon VII(C)(6) of the Ohio Code of Judicial Conduct. 
(A-85). Canon VII(C)(6) imposes limits on campaign 
expenditures for candidates for the bench in Ohio judicial 
elections. At issue in this case is Canon VII(C)(6)(d), 
which in 1996 limited candidates seeking seats on Ohio’s 
courts of common pleas to spending $75,000 to advance 
their own elections. The trial court’s original order pre- 
vented the State from enforcing the canon against any 
judicial candidate in Ohio. At the State’s urging, however, 
the court issued an Amended Order on September 25, 
1996, limiting its order to the parties directly before the 
court. (A-55). 


The Honorable Gail Rose Kane, the Honorable Chris- 
topher A. Boyko, and the Hororable Stuart Saferin (“the 
Cleveland Intervenors”) were all sitting judges on the 
Court of Common Pleas for Cuyahoga County when they 
joined this action. (A-4 — A-5). The Cleveland Intervenors 
also were candidates in the November 5, 1996 general 
election and subject to the Ohio Code of Judicial Conduct, 
including the spending limits imposed by Canon 
VII(C)(6)(d). (Id.) Each of the Cleveland Intervenors had 
the wherewithal to spend, and intended to spend, sums 
in excess of the campaign spending cap contained in 


Canon VII(C)(6)(d) in order to communicate with the 
voters of Cuyahoga County regarding his or her individ- 
ual candidacy. However, if the Cleveland Intervenors 
exceeded the spending cap, they faced investigations 
under Rule Il, § 5 of the Supreme Court Rules for the 
Government of the Judiciary of Ohio and possible disci- 
plinary sanctions, including probation suspension or dis- 
barment from the practice of law, fines, cease and desist 
orders, and an assessment of costs. (Id.); Ohio Gov. Jud. R. 
Il, § 5(E)(1); Ohio Gov. Bar R. V, § 6 (A) — (B). 


Consequently, on October 15, 1996, the Cleveland 
Intervenors filed a Verified Complaint in Intervention, a 
Motion to Intervene, and a Motion for Temporary 
Restraining Order and Preliminary Injunction. The Court 
granted the Cleveland Intervenors’ Motion to Intervene 
and Motion for Preliminary Injunction. (A-30). In grant- 
ing the Motion for Preliminary Injunction, the Court spec- 
ifically found that any harm which the injunction might 
cause to the opponents of the Cleveland Intervenors 
would be overwhelmingly outweighed by the other three 
factors required for granting injunctive relief: a “ ‘very 
high likelihood of success on the merits,’ the clear pres- 
ence of irreparable harm, and the public’s core interest in 
hearing plaintiffs’ speech.” (A-33). 


The State filed a timely appeal to the United States 
Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit. In accordance with 
the Revised Briefing Schedule for the case, the Cleveland 
Intervenors were required to file their proof brief with 
the Court on April 28, 1997. The Cleveland Intervenors 
filed their brief in a timely manner. 


However, after the appeal was perfected and the 
proof briefs were filed, the Ohio Supreme Court adopted 
amendments to the Code of Judicial Conduct effective 
June 1, 1997. These amendments involved changes to 
Canon VII(C)(6) to include population-based campaign 
expenditure limits for candidates for the office of judge 
for common pleas courts in Ohio (hereinafter the 
“Amended Canon”). The Cleveland Intervenors object to 
any references to Amended Canon in these proceedings. 
None of the evidence in the record for this case involves 
the Amended Canon. The record before this Court con- 
tains testimony and documentary evidence gathered at a 
hearing before the district court for preliminary injunc- 
tive relief from the Canon as it existed when the Respon- 
dents sought election to the bench in 1996. The Amended 
Canon did not become effective until almost two months 
after the Cleveland Intervenors filed their proof brief 
with the court of appeals, effectively foreclosing any 
meaningful discussion of the changes in the Canon in the 
briefs. Final briefs were filed on July 30, 1997. On Febru- 
ary 17, 1998, almost seven months after the filing of the 
final briefs with the court of appeals, the State filed a 
motion requesting that the Sixth Circuit review the 
Amended Canon. The Cleveland Intervenors opposed the 
motion. Following oral arguments on the merits, the Sixth 
Circuit remanded the case to the district court to consider 
the Amended Canon. (A-8). 


In attempting to turn this appeal into an evidentiary 
hearing, the State refers to documents outside the record. 
The Cleveland Intervenors object to any references to any 
evidence not considered by the trial court, including the 
American Bar Association’s Report and Recommendation 


of its Task Force on Lawyer’s Political Contributions, and 
Pennsylvania’s Report of the Special Commission tc Limit 
Campaign Expenditures. Neither report is included in the 
record in this case and cannot be considered. Adickes v. 
Kress & Co., 398 U.S. 144, 157 n. 16 (1970). 


The Cleveland Intervenors also take exception to the 
State’s claim that the Sixth Circuit erroneously considered 
Canon VII(C)(6) as it existed when the Cleveland Inter- 
venors ran for office. The specific issue before the Sixth 
Circuit was whether the district court abused its discre- 
tion in enjoining the enforcement of Canon VII(C)(6) 
against the Respondents. In making its ruling, the district 
court had to weigh four factors: (1) the likelihood of the 
plaintiffs’ success on the merits; (2) irreparable harm in 
the absence of the injunction; (3) whether the injunction 
will harm others; and (4) whether the public interest will 
be served by an injunction. International Longshoreman’s 
Ass‘n Local 1937 v. Norfoik Southern Corp., 927 F.2d 900, 903 
(6th Cir.), cert. denied, 502 U.S. 813 (1991), In determining 
whether the district court had abused its discretion in 
granting the injunction, the Sixth Circuit of necessity had 
to examine whether the record supported the district 
court’s findings. Having determined that the record 
indeed did support the granting of injunctive relief, the 
Sixth Circuit has rightly remanded the case to the district 
court for determination of the constitutionality of the 
Amended Canon. 


REASONS FOR DENYING THE PETITION 


The Cleveland Intervenors respectfully request that 
this Court deny the Petition for a Writ of Certiorari 
seeking review of the decision of the United States Court 
of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit in this case. Contrary to 
the State’s argument, the court of appeals’ decision does 
not conflict with the decision of any other court of 
appeals. Instead, the Sixth Circuit has taken the prudent 
course of remanding the case to allow the district court to 
examine the Amended Canon in the first instance. 


I. The Sixth Circuit did not abuse its discretion in 
remanding the cas<« io the district court. 


The Supreme Court of Ohio altered the judicial canon 
that the Respondents sought to enjoin after the district 
court made its ruling granting the preliminary injunction. 
The State did not ask the court of appeals to consider the 
Amended Canon until almost seven months after the 
final briefs were filed. Consequently, the court of appeals 
was asked to rule on the constitutionality of a provision 
that the district court had not reviewed and that the 
parties had not briefed. Rather than rule on the constitu- 
tionality of the Amended Canon, the court of appeals 
remanded to the district court so that it could consider 
the amendments. 


Far from being an abuse of discretion, remanding the 
case to the district court is the preferred procedure when 
a change in law does not extinguish a controversy. See, 
Concerned Citizens of Vicksburg v. Sills, 567 F.2d 646, 650 
(Sth Cir. 1978). Such a course of action comports with the 
Sixth Circuit’s general rule that issues raised for the first 


10 


time on appeal are not properly before the court. The 
court of appeals has “deviated from the general rule in 
‘exceptional cases or particular circumstances’ or when 
the rule would produce ‘a plain miscarriage of justice.’ ” 
Foster v. Barilow, 6 F.3d 405, 407 (6th Cir. 1993) (citations 
omitted). See, also, Holly Farms Corp. v. NLRB, 517 U.S. 
392, 400 n. 7 (1996) (United States Supreme Court gener- 
ally does not “address arguments that were not the basis 
of the decision below”). Factors the Sixth Circuit must 
consider in determining whether to exercise its discretion 
to deviate from this traditional rule include: 


1) whether the issue newly raised on appeal is a 
question of law, or whether it requires or neces- 
sitates a determination of facts; 2) whether the 
proper resolution of the new issue is clear and 
beyond doubt; 3) whether failure to take up the 
issue for the first time on appeal will result in a 
miscarriage of justice or a denial of substantial 
justice; and 4) the parties’ right under our judi- 
cial system to have the issues in their suit con- 
sidered by both a district judge and an appellate 
Court. 


Friendly Farms v. Reliance Ins. Co., 79 F.3d 541, 545 (6th Cir. 
1996). 


The court of appeals’ refusal to address the Amended 
Canon was not an abuse of discretion. Trial on the merits 
has not occurred in this case. By remanding the Amended 
Canon to the district court, the court of appeals is allow- 
ing for the full development of the factual record and the 


11 


legal arguments of the parties.! In our system of justice, 
such development is crucial to the judiciary’s ability to 
reach meaningful, well reasoned, and correct conclusions. 
On remand, the State will have ample opportunity to 
build a record regarding the Amended Canon, and the 
parties will be given the opportunity to have the issue 
decided by both the district judge and the appellate 
court. Justice is served for all parties if the normal course 
of proceedings is followed. Because no exceptional cir- 
cumstances dictated otherwise, the court of appeals deci- 
sion to remand was not an abuse of discretion.

Court of Appeal’s decision below. However, Rosenstiel is 
clearly distinguishable because in that case, “[b]oth sides 
strenuously contend[ed] that remand is unnecessary.” Id. 
at 1548. In the case below, the Cleveland Intervenors 
opposed the Sixth Circuit’s consideration of the Amended 
Canon for the first time on appeal. The Respondents face 
possible disciplinary sanctions under the Canon as it 
stood in 1996, when they sought election to the common 
pleas bench in Cuyahoga County. Ohio Gov. Jud. R. H,

The State alleges that the court of appeals erred in

should have issued, the court of appeals considered four

constitutionality of the former Canon VII(C)(6). The same 
argument can be made every time a court of appeals

the reviewing court must examine the likelihood of suc- 
cess of the litigants, and the trial court will be bound by 
the decision of the court of appeals. Even if the court of 
appeals somehow could have avoided any discussion of 
the merits of the former Canon, the district court would 
not be writing its decision on a blank tablet. The court of 
appeals did not announce any new constitutional doc-

Ill. The court of appeals correctly found that Buckley 
v. Valeo and its progeny prohibit campaign spend- 
ing caps. 


As this Court has consistently held, expenditure 
limits 


operate in an area of the most fundamental First 
Amendment activities. Discussion of public 
issues and debate on the qualifications of candi- 
dates are integral to the operation of the system 
of government established by our Constitution. 
The First Amendment affords the broadest pro- 
tection to such political expression in order “to 
assure [the] unfettered interchange of ideas for 
the bringing about of political and social 
changes desired by the people.” . . . This no 
more than reflects our “profound national com- 
mitment to the principle that debate on public 
issues should be uninhibited, robust, wide- 
open.” 


Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 14 (1976) (citations omitted). 


This Court went on in Buckley to explain the effects of 
campaign spending limits on the First Amendment’s 
guarantee of freedom of speech: 


A restriction on the amount of money a person 
or group can spend on political communication 
during a campaign necessarily reduces the 
quantity of expression by restricting the number 
of issues discussed, the depth of their explora- 
tion, and the size of the audience reached. This 
is because virtually every means of communi- 
cating ideas in today’s mass society requires the 
expenditure of money. The distribution of the 
humblest handbill or leaflet entails printing, 
paper, and circulation costs. Speeches and rallies 


16 


generally necessitate hiring a hall and publiciz- 
ing the event. The electorate’s increasing depen- 
dence on television, radio, and other mass 
media for news and information has made these 
expensive modes of communication indispens- 
able instruments of effective political speech. 


Id. at 19. 


The Canon at issue here, which restricts expenditures 
of Ohio judicial candidates, cuts at the core of political 
expression as described in Buckley. The First Amendment 
right of free speech is a fundamental right, and an 
infringement of that right, though justified, must be 
accomplished by the least restrictive means available. See, 
e.g., Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780, 806 (1983). Politi- 
cal speech, such as that implicated here, is subject to even 
stricter scrutiny. “When a law burdens core political 
speech, we apply ‘exacting scrutiny,’ and we uphold the 
restriction only if it is narrowly tailored to serve an 
overriding state interest.” McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Com- 
mission, 514 U.S. 334, 337 (1995); Shrink Missouri Govern- 
ment PAC v. Maupin, 892 F. Supp. 1246, 1249 (E.D. Mo. 
1995). 


This Court does not need to address the State’s argu- 
ment that the Sixth Circuit erred in “rejecting” its pur- 
ported compelling interest, because the State failed to 
show that its Canon is the least restrictive means of

appearance of the impartiality of the judiciary. (A-20). In

Despite the argument of the State to the contrary, the 
Sixth Circuit did not hold that the State lacks a compel-

held that preventing corruption or the appearance of 
corruption are the only legitimate and compelling inter-

identified to date. (A-19); Federal Election Comm'n v.

unconstitutional those provisions that limit: 1) a candi-

2) a candidate’s right to spend his or her own money, Id.
