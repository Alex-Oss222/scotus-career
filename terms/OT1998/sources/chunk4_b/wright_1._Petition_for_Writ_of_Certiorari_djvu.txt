STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This action (the "Action") was brought as a class ac- 
tion to remedy alleged violations by defendant Ernst & 
Young LLP ("E&Y") of Section 10(b) of the Securities Ex- 
change Act of 1934 (the "Exchange Act"), on behalf of pur- 
chasers of BT Office Products International Inc. ("BT" or the 
“Company") common stock between January 30, 1996 (the 
date BT published its 1995 full year and fourth quarter finan- 
cial results based on defendant E&Y’s representations to BT’s 
management as to their accuracy), through March 28, 1996 
(when BT revealed that the previously announced 1995, as 
well as 1994, financial results were materially misstated), in- 
clusive (the "Class Period"), who were damaged thereby (the 
"Class"). 


Plaintiff claims that defendant E&Y, the Company’s 
outside auditor, violated the Exchange Act by affirmatively 
telling the Company that its financial results for its fiscal year 
ended December 31, 1995, were sufficiently accurate for dis- 
semination to the investing public. Plaintiff further alleges 
that E&Y assured BT that it could publicly disseminate the 
subject financial results in a press release despite E&Y’s 
knowledge of myriad and substantial violations of Generally 
Accepted Auditing Standards ("GAAS") and despi > learning 
in January 1996 that the Company had an unexplained 
$3,900,000 under-accrual in accounts payable at 1995 
year-end. 


The parties all agree that BT’s 1995 financial results 
published on January 30, 1996, as well as the Company’s 
1994 financial statements, were false. At issue in this case 
was whether defendant E&Y, which was directly or indirectly 


Wo Sh te pann ions esata 


aS 


responsible tor the publication of those false financial results, 
is liable under Section 10(b). 


On September 10, 1997, without entertaining oral ar- 
gument, United States District Court Judge for the Southern 
District of New York Shira A. Scheindlin issued her opinion 
and order dismissing the case, holding that defendant E&Y’s 
oral assurances to BT that the 1995 figures were accurate 
and that its just-completed field work complied with GAAS 
did not constitute an actionable misstatement. 


The United States Court of Appeals for the Second 
Circuit affirmed the District Court on August 6, 1998. The 
Second Circuit held that E&Y could not be liable for mis- 
statements contained in the press release published by BT be- 
cause EGY was not identified in that press release as having 
made any statement and, therefore, the market did not rely 
on the conduct of E&Y. 


The bases for federal jurisdiction in the District Court 


for the Southern District of New York, the tederal court of 
first instance, were 15 U.S.C. § 78aa and 28 U.S.C. § 1331. 


ARGUMENT IN SUPPORT OF PETITION 


Since this Court’s decision in Central Bank of Denver 
v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, 511 U.S. 164 (1994), hold- 
ing that no claim existed! for aiding and abetting under Sec- 
tion }O(b) of the Exchange Act, federal district and circuit 
courts nave struggled to determine the boundaries of primary 
liability under the Exchange Act tor parties other than the 


-4- 


actual speaker. Central Bank simply states that the Exchange 
Act "prohibits only the making of a material misstatement (or 
omission) or the commission of a manipulative act." Central 
Bank, 511 U.S. at 177. Seizing on this language, some courts 
have read the Central Bank decision to mean that there can 
be no liability if a defendant does not actually make the pub- 
lic statement. Other courts have interpreted the decision 
less restrictively to mean that a defendant must, in some way, 
be responsible for a misstatement. 


Further complicating matters, at least two Cir- 
cuit Courts of Appeal have offered differing views about what 
it means to "make" a public statement. In Anixter v. Home- 
Stake Prod. Co., 77 F.3d 1215 (10th Cir. 1996), the Tenth 
Circuit held that a statement uttered indirectly to the public 
may give rise to liability under Section 10(b), but the Second 
Circuit in this Action has now held to the contrary -- that a 
misstatement could only serve as the basis for liability if pub- 
licly attributed to the alleged wrongdoer, thereby seeming to 
contradict the holding of Anixter. The Second Circuit held 
that: "a secondary actor cannot incur primary liability under 
the Act for a statement not attributed to that actor at the 
time of its dissemination." Wright at Appendix, p. 12a. The 
Second Circuit explained that to hold otherwise would effec- 
tively eviscerate the requirement of reliance as an element of 
a claim under Section 10(b) of the Exchange Act. 

In fact, plaintitf here alleged implicit reliance on E&Y because 
the market knew that a public company such as BT would not publish its 
year-end results until the accountants had completed their field work and 
authorized the publication of these resu!ts. The market further knew that 
BT's accountants were E&Y because they were the auditors on BT’s ini- 
tial public offering in July 1995 and there had been no announcement of 
any change in auditors. Thus, E&Y, although not specifically identified 


as such here, was clearly the known indirect speaker. 


-5. 


However, as discussed in detail below, the Third Cir- 
cuit, in a remarkably similar case involving attorneys’ liability 
under the Exchange Act, recently held that direct attribution 
is unnecessary to plead reliance in a 10b-5 case, thus support- 
ing the Anixter formulation. See Klein v. Boyd [Current 
Transter Binder] Fed. Sec. L. Rep. (CCH) 4 90, 136 (3d Cir 
Feb. 12, 1998). That decision has been vacated by the full 
panel and is currently under en banc review with rehearing 
scheduled for November 23, 1998. Klein v. Boyd, [Current 
Transfer Binder] Fed. Sec. L. Rep. (CCH) % 90, 165 (3d Cir. 
Mar. 9, 1998). 


The Third Circuit explained that its holding was in 
no way inconsistent with Central Bank or Anixter because it 
agreed that a defendant must make a statement for Section 
1O0(b) liability to attach. Klein, (CCH) % 90,136 at 90,325. 
Simply, the Third Circuit found, contrary to the Second Cir- 
cuit’s ruling in Wright, that a secondary actor’s involvement 
in creating a false statement may be "so significant that the 
secondary actor can fairly be said to have made the state- 
ment,” albeit indirectly. Id. at 90,326 n.7. 


Thus, review should be granted here to resolve the 
confusion and inconsistent rulings amongst the Circuits in- 
terpreting this Court’s decision in Central B. nk as to the ex- 
tent a secondary actor may be liable for making or 
participating in the making of talse statements when the sec- 
ondary actor is not specifically identified in the misstatement. 


Can a secondary actor not identified in a public statement 
incur primary liability under Section 10(b) of the Exchange Act 
where he communicates false information to an issuer knowing 
that the issuer will publish that information?

In Central Bank, this Court enunciated the narrow 
rule "that a private plaintiff may not maintain an aiding and 
abetting suit under § 10(b).". 511 U.S. at 191. The Court 
expressly noted, however, that its ruling did not insulate ac- 
countants, such as defendant EGY here, from liability under 
Section 10(b), stating: 


The absence of § 10(b) aiding and abetting li- 
ability does not mean that secondary actors 
in the securities markets are always free from 
liability under the securities Acts. Any person 
or entity, including a lawyer, accountant, or 
bank, who employs a manipulative device or 
makes a material misstatement (or omission) 
on which a purchaser or seller of securities re- 
lies may be liable as a primary violator under 
1Ob-5, assuming all of the requirements for 
primary liability under Rule 10b-5 are met. 


Id. (first emphasis added). Determining what actions support 
primary liability for secondary actors has thus been left to 
Central Bank’s progeny. District courts interpreting Central 
Bank have generally followed one of two approaches in ana- 
tyzing potential claims. Several courts have adopted a bright 
line test that a person must actually make the alle edly false 
or misleaaing statement for primary liability to attach under 
Section 10(b). See, e.g., In re JWP Inc. Securities Litigation, 
928 F. Supp. 1239, 1256 (S.D.N.Y. 1996); In re Kendail 
Square Research Corp. Securities Litigation, 868 F. Supp. 26, 28 
(D. Mass. 1994); O’Neil v. Appel, 897 F. Supp. 995, 999 (W. 
D. Mich. 1995); Vosgerichian v. Commodore International, 862 
F. Supp. 1371, 1378 (E.D. Pa. 1994).

Other courts have’ adopted the view that a person 
who lends substantial assistance to the preparation of a false 
statement of another may also be held primarily liable. See. 
e.g., Phillips v. Kidder, Peabody & Co., 933 F. Supp. 303, 316 
(S.D.N.Y. 1996); Cashman v. Coopers & Lybrand, 877 F. 
Supp. 425, 432-34 (N.D. Ill. 1995); Adam v. Silicon Valley 
Bancshares, 884 F. Supp. 1398, 1401 (N.D. Cal. 1995): Whirl- 
pool Financial Corp. v. GN Holdings, Inc., 873 F. Supp. 111, 
119 (N.D. Ill. 1995); In re ZZZZ Best Securities Litigation, 864 
F. Supp. 960, 972 (C.D. Cal. 1994). 


More recently, the Second, Ninth, and Tenth Circuit 
Courts of Appeal have provided their interpretations of Cen- 
tral Bank resulting in three different standards. In Anixter, 
the Tenth Circuit held that "[t}here is no requirement that 
the alleged violator directly communicate misrepresentations 
to plaintiffs for primary liability to attach." 77 F.3d at 1226 
(citing SEC v. Holschuh, 694 F.2d 130, 142 (7th Cir. 1982) 
("[A]ctual or first-hand contact with offerees or buvers lis 
not] a condition precedent to primary liability for antifraud 
violations.")). The Anixter court explained that an account- 
ant may be liable for misrepresentations not made directly to 
the public so long as there is a "showing that lit} knew or 
should have known that [its] representation would be com- 
municated to investors ...." 77 F.3d at 1226. 


This interpretation appears to be supported by Central 
Bank itself which foreclosed Section 10(b) liability from ex- 
tending to aiding and abetting, because "aiding and abetting 
liability extends beyond persons who engage, even indirectly, 
in a proscribed activity; aiding and abetting liability reaches 


persons who do not engage in the proscribed activities at all. 


-8- 


but who give a degree of aid to those who do." 511 USS. at 
176 (emphasis added). 


The Ninth Circuit, in In re Software Toolworks, Inc.. 
90 F.3d 615, 628 n.3 (9th Cir. 1994), endorsed an even more 
liberal test, holding that a secondary actor, such as an ac- 
countant, may be primerily liable under Section 10(b) where 
it played a "significant role" in the dissemination of mislead- 
ing statements. This "substantial participation" test, as it has 
come to be known, has been rejected by the Second Circuit. 
See, e.g., Shapiro v. Cantor, 123 F.3d 717, 721-22 (2d Cir. 
1997). Nevertheless, different panels of the Second Circuit 
have rendered three decisions, in addition to Wright, which 
are inconsistent in their interpretation of Central Bank, indi- 
cating that the time is ripe for this Court to clarify its deci- 
sion in Central Bank. 


In SEC v. First Jersey Securities Inc., 101 F.3d 1450, 
[471 (2d Cir. 1996), the Second Circuit explained that 
"[p]rimary liability may be imposed 'not only on persons who 
made fraudulent misrepresentations but also on those who 
had knowledge of the fraud and assisted in its perpetration.”” 
(citing Azrielli v. Cohen Law Offices, 21 F.3d 512, 517 (2d 
Cir. 1994)). However, in Shapiro, the Second Circuit ap- 
peared to foreclose the notion that liability could attach to 
anyone but the speaker when it cited with approval the lan- 
guage trom In Re MTC Electronic Technologies Shareholders 
Litigation, 898 F. Supp. 974, 987 (E.D.N.Y. 1995). stating 
that if Central Bank: 


[I]s to have any real meaning, a defen- 
dant must actually make a false or misleading 
statement in order to be held liable under

matter how substantial that aid may be, it is

To turther confuse the landscape, more recently, the 
Second Circuit, in SEC v. U.S. Environmental, Inc., 1998 U.S. 
App. LEXIS 21416, at *13-14 (2d Cir. Aug. 25, 1998), ap- 
pears to have retreated from this hard line, acknowledging 
that one who commits a manipulative and deceptive act may 
still be primarily liable under Section 10(b) even if he does 
not make an affirmative misstatement. 


Against this backdrop we turn to the Wright case 
where the Second Circuit apparently rejected Anixter’s indi- 
rect misstatement theory by holding that reliance is a deter- 
minative element in examining whether a third party is liable 
for making a false statement. 


Plaintiff alleges that E&Y, as the outside auditor re- 
sponsible for approving the accuracy of BT’s year-end finan- 
cial statements, made an_ actionale affirmative 
misrepresentation when it orally told BT’s Chief Financial 
Ofticer that the Company’s year-end financial statements 
were sufficiently accurate for dissemination to the public. As 
plaintiff's Complaint alleges, E&Y and the market knew that 
the Company’s public release of the year-end figures was con- 
tingent on receiving approval from E&Y. In affirming the 
District Court’s dismissal, the Second Circuit, citing Anixter 
77 F.3d at 1226-27, noted that a misstatement need not be 


-10- 


communicated directly to the public for Section 10(b) liabil- 
ity to attach. Nevertheless, it disregarded that standard by 
holding that the offending misrepresentation must attribute 
some misstatement to a particular party for that party to be 
liable. "[T]he misrepresentation must be attributed to that 
specific actor at the time of public dissemination, it simply re- 
quires that the statement be identified as to time and place, 
that is, in advance of the investment decision.” Wright at 
Appendix, p. 12a. According to the Court, the reasoning of 
this holding is rooted in the concept of reliance. However, 
the Complaint does allege that the market knew by implica- 
tion that E&Y had approved the publication of this false 
statement. 


Plaintiff respectfully submits that the Second Circuit 
confused the requirement that a plaintiff (or the market) rely 
on a misstatement with the notion that there must be direct 
reliance on the misconduct of that particular party. By 
reaching this holding, the Second Circuit effectively elimi- 
nated liability for any indirect misstatements by a party no 
matter the degree of culpability of that party in perpetrating a 
fraud on the public. In In re ZZZZ Best Securities Litigation, 
864 F. Supp. 960 (C.D. Cal. 1994), the Central District of 
California addressed this very issue and explained why the 
market need not be cognizant of who was respon ible, even 
indirectly, for the misleading information that was relied 
upon:

The reliance that a plaintiff must es-

The question we must answer is whether, under the Act,

DISCUSSION 


On appeal, Wright argues that the district court (1) erred in 
holding chat the amended complaint failed to allege that 
Ernst & Young made an actionable misstatement within the 
meaning of the Act; (2) erred in crediting the "unaudited" 
disclaimer in BT’s press release and thus erred in rejecting 
her allegation that the market understood the press release as 
an implied statement by Ernst & Young that the financial in- 
formation contained therein was accurate; and (3) erred in 
dismissing the amended complaint because, at the very least, 
it stated a cause of action for failure to correct misstatements 
in the 1995 initial public offering prospectus. 


We review de novo the district court’s dismissal of a com- 
plaint under Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b) (6), taking all w ell-pleaded 
factual averments in the complaint as true and drawing all 
reasonable inferences in the plaintiff's favor. See Leeds v. 
Meltz, 85 F.3d 51, 53 (2d Cir. 1996). "The issue is not 
whether a plaintiff will ultimately prevail but whether the 
claimant is entitled to offer evidence to support the claims." 
Scheuer v. Rhodes, (1%4). 


1. Actionable Statements and § 10(b) 


Wright first argues that because a defendant can incur pri- 
mary liability under the Act for false statements that are not 
directly communicated to the public, there is no requirement 
that the false statement be attributed to the defendant at the 
time of its dissemination. Thus, Wright maintains that 


-§a- 


although BT’s press release attributes no statement to Ernst 
& Young, the amended complaint nevertheless alleges a talse 
statement within the meaning of § 10(b) because it alleges 
that Ernst & Young "assured" BT of the accuracy of its 1995 
financial results, knowing that BT would, in turn, promptly 
disseminate those results to investors in the press release. 


Further, Wright argues that even if the amended complaint 
does not allege conduct amounting to a false statement, it 
does state a § 10(b) cause of action under post-Central Bank 
authority because Ernst & Young is alleged to have "substan- 
tially participated" in the fraud. See SEC v. First Jersey Secu- 
rities, 101 F.3d 1450, 1471 (2d Cir. 1996) ("Primary liability 
may be imposed * not only on persons who made fraudulent 
misrepresentations but also on those who had knowledge ot 
the fraud and assisted in its perpetration.” (citation omit- 
ted)), cert. denied, 118 S.Ct. 57 (1997); see also In re Soft- 
ware Toolworks Inc. Sec. Litigation, 50 F.3d 615, 628 n.3 
(9th Cir. 1994), cert. denied, (1995); In re ZZZZ Best Sec. 
Litigation, 864 F.Supp. 960, 970 n.12 (C.D. Cal. 1994). We 


disagree on both counts.

123 F.3d 717 (2d Cir. 1997). Section 10(b) of the Act pro-

those who aid and abet such a violation. See Central Bank,

In Central Bank, the Supreme Court addressed the legiti- 
macy of secondary liability claims in private actions brought 
pursuant to § 10(b). The Court concluded from the text of 
the Act that Congress never intended to impose secondary li- 
ability under § 10(b) and thus the Act "does not itself reach 
those who aid and abet . . . [but] prohibits only the making of 
a material misstatement (or omission) or the commission of a 
manipulative act." Central Bank, . The Court further ob- 
served that authorizing a § 10(b) cause of action based on 
aiding and abetting would circumvent the "reliance" require- 
ment of Rule 10b-5 by allowing a plaintiff to prevail "without 
any showing that the plaintiff relied upon the aider and abet- 
tor’s statements or actions.” Id. at 180; see also Basic v. Lev- 
inson, (1988) (rejecting liability under Rule 10b-5 without 
reliance). However, the Court did not hold that secondary 
actors are always free from liability under the Act. Rather, 
secondary actors like accountants may be held liable as pri- 
mary violators if all the requirements for primary liability are 
met, including "a material misstatement (or omission) on 
which a purchaser or seller of securities relies." Central Bank,

2. The "Unaudited" Disclaimer 


Wright next argues that the district court erred in giving ef- 
fect to BT’s disclaimer in the press release which stated that 


-15a- 


the financial results were "unaudited." Wright prays that by 
rendering that disclaimer ineffective, we might breathe new 
life into her averment that the market understood BT’s press 
release as an implied assertion by Ernst & Young that BT’s fi- 
nancial statements were accurate. Specifically, Wright argues 
that the district court (a) erred in relying on the "bespeaks 
caution doctrine" in crediting that disclaimer; (b) erred in re- 
solving disputed facts regarding what the market would im- 
plicitly understand by a company’s release of financial results 
and an accountant’s role in that release; (c) erred in giving 
effect to the word "unaudited" when such a disclaimer cannot 
negate Ernst & Young’s own pivotal conduct in causing the 
dissemination of the false financial results; and (d) erred by 
allowing accountants to hide behind the word "unaudited," in 
contravention to the broad remedial purposes of the Act. 


Wright cannot prevail regardless of how we treat the dis- 
claimer. As noted, Ernst & Young’s assurances were never 
communicated to the public either directly or indirectly. BT 
issued the press release without a whisper of Ernst & Young’s 
involvement. Thus, in order to resurrect Wright’s claim that 
Ernst & Young made an actionable statement within the 
meaning of § 10(b), we would have to do more than just re- 
fuse to give effect to the "unaudited" disclaimer. We would 
also have to ignore the absence of any mention of Ernst & 
Young in BT’s press releaS¢’and focus instead on what the 
market might have implicitly "understood" about Ernst & 
Young’s involvement in that press release. In other words, we 
would have to grant Wright an exception to the rule estab- 
lished by the Supreme Court that secondary actors such as 
accountants may not be held primarily liable unless they 
themselves have made "a material misstatement (or omission) 
on which a purchaser or seller of securities relies." Central 
Bank, . We find no justification for granting such an excep- 
tion. See, e.g., In re Fidelity/Micron Sec. Litigation, 964 


-|6a- 


F.Supp. 539, 543-44 (D. Mass. 1997) (holding that a mutual 
tund, Magellan, cannot be held primarily liable on a theory of 
respondeat superior for the alleged misstatements of its fund 
manager, notwithstanding plaintif!’s contention "* that the 
market understood that [the fund manager] spoke for Magel- 
lan” (emphasis added)). We also perceive no affront to the 
remedial purposes of the Act. While the Act does carry broad 
remedial purposes, the Act may not be "amend[ed] . . . to 
create liability for acts that are not themselves manipulative 
or deceptive within the meaning of the statute." Central 
Bank, -78 (emphasis added). Aiding and abetting liability is 
simply not covered by the Act. Accordingly, we conclude 
that the district court did not err in so holding. 


3. The Amended Complaint and Other Actionable 


Statements 


In addition to its allegations regarding the press release, the 
amended complaint alleged that Ernst & Young had made a 
false statement in connection with BT’s 1995 initial public 
offering prospectus. Specifically, in the amended complaint, 
Wright alleged that in a Report of Independent Auditors in- 
cluded in BT’s prospectus, Ernst & Young certified the accu- 
racy of BI’s 1994 financial statements and BT’s earnings for 
the first quarter of 1995, but "did not disclose that it only had 
performed a limited review of BT Summit." This report, the 
amended complaint alleged, was false and misleading. How- 
ever, the district court concluded that the amended com- 
plaint failed to allege fully a § 10(b) cause of action on that 
ground because it failed to allege all the requirements for a 
Rule 10b-5 violation. Accordingly, the court sua sponte 
granted Wright leave to cure the defect. Wright, however, 
elected to file this appeal rather than replead. 


-17a- 


On appeal, Wright argues that the amended complaint ade- 
quately states a cause of action for failure to correct the false 
financial information contained in that prospectus because by 
January 1996, Ernst & Young discovered facts tending to un- 
dermine the accuracy of those financial statements. Specifi- 
cally, Wright argues in her brief that: 


Plaintiff alleged a misstatement -- the Report of Independent 
Auditors, signed by [Ernst & Young]; that the misstatement was 
material as evidenced by the fact that [BT’s] financial results for 
1994 eventually were restated; that the market relied on this 
misinformation as reflected in [BT’s]} stock price; and that defen- 
dant [Ernst & Young] was reckless, at least by January 1996, in 
not correcting this misstatement. 


We disagree. In order to survive a motion to dismiss, a plain- 
tiff bringing an action pursuant to § 10(b) and Rule 10b-5 
must "plead that the defendant made a false statement or 
omitted a material fact, with scienter, and that plaintiffs reli- 
ance on defendant’s action caused plaintiff injury." San Lean- 
dro Emergency Medical Group Profit Sharing Plan v. Philip 
Morris Cos., 75 F.3d 801, 808 (2d Cir. 1996). Accounting 
firms "do have a duty to take reasonable steps to correct mis- 
statements they have discovered in previous financial state- 
ments on which they know the public is relying." Cornfeld, 
619 F.2d at 927; see also SEC v. Manor Nursing Centers, 458 
F.2d 1082, 1095 (2d Cir. 1972) ("Post-effecti: 2 developments 
which materially alter the picture presented in the registra- 
tion statement must be brought to the attention of public in- 
vestors." (footnote omitted)); Lanza v. Drexel & Co., 479 
F.2d 1277, 1304-05 (2d Cir. 1973) (scienter requires that a 
defendant act deliberately or recklessly). Silence where there 
is a duty to disclose can constitute a false or misleading state- 
ment within the meaning of § 10(b) and Rule 10b-5. See Ba- 
sic, n.17. 


-18a- 


In this case, however, although the amended complaint al- 
leges that the prospectus contained false statements (i.e., the 
inaccurate 1994 results) in 1995, it does not allege the mis- 
representation claim that Wright presses on appeal -- namely, 
that Ernst & Young made a false statement (by omission) in 
1996 as a result of its duty and subsequent failure to correct 
those statements. In fact, an alleged duty to correct does not 
appear anywhere in the amended complaint and did not en- 
ter the case until Wright mentioned it for the first time in 
her opposition memoranda to the motion to dismiss. See 
Cornfeld, 619 F.2d at 914 & n.6 (a party is not entitled to 
amend its complaint through statements made in motion pa- 
pers); see also Jacobson v. Peat, Marwick, Mitchell & Co., 
445 F.Supp. 518, 526 (S.D.N.Y. 1977) (party may not amend 
pleading through statements in briefs). Furthermore, the 
amended complaint fails to allege that Ernst & Young knew 
or was reckless in not knowing in 1995 that the Report of In- 
dependent Auditors contained false financial information, 
that the market or those who purchased BT’s stock during 
the 1996 class period relied on the 1995 report, or that the 
1995 report caused damages. Accordingly, we find no basis 
for Wright’s contention that the amended complaint states a 
§ 10(b) cause of action. 


Because Wright elected to stand on the amended cemplaint 
rather than replead by September 29, 1997, Ernst & Young 
argues that Wright has waived her opportunity to file a sec- 
ond amended complaint. We conclude that because the dis- 
trict court entered judgment in this action on September 30, 
1997, Wright may not file a second amended complaint with- 
out first obtaining vacatur of that final judgment, which she 
has not done on this appeal. 


CONCLUSION 


-19a- 


Central Bank of Denver v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, 
(1994), and Shapiro v. Cantor, 123 F.3d 717 (2d Cir. 1997), 
foreclose primary liability under the Act for false or mislead- 
ing statements made by Ernst & Young because no false or 
misleading statement was attributed to Ernst & Young at the 
time of public dissemination. Accordingly, the district court 
did not err in dismissing the amended complaint on that 
ground. Further, we also agree with the district court that the 
amended complaint fails to state a cause of action under § 
10(b) and Rule 10b-5 for failure to correct misstatements 
made in connection with the sale or purchase of securities. 
Accordingly, the judgment of the district court is affirmed.

I. Legal Standard for Motion to Dismiss 


In deciding a 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss, the court must ac- 
cept as true material facts alleged in the complaint and draw 
all reasonable inferences in the nonmovant’s favor. See . 
Such a motion cannot be granted simply because recovery 
appears remote or unlikely on the face of a complaint, be- 
cause "[t]he issue is not whether a plaintiff will ultimately 
prevail but whether the claimant is entitled to offer evidence 
to support the claims." (quoting ). Rather, dismissal can only 
be granted if "it appears beyond doubt that the plaintiff can 
prove no set of facts in support of his claim which would enti- 
tle him to relief." (quoting ). 


In ruling on a motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim, 
the Court may take into consideration documents which are 
integral to the coniplaint and upon which it relies, such as 
the press release at issue in the instant case, without convert- 
ing the motion into one for summary judgment. See ; .

Ill. Discussion 
A. 10b-5 Claim 


To recover under Rule 10b-5, a plaintiff must prove that the 
detendant, acting with scienter, made a false material repre- 
sentation or omitted to disclose material information and 
that plaintift relied, to her detriment, on this representation 
or omission. See . A threshold question here is whether the 
plaintift has satistied the first part of this test; namely, 
whether E & Y’s alleged actions rise to the level of an action- 
able representation or omission. Plaintiff advances two theo- 
ries on this point. 


First, plaintiff argues that E & Y can be held responsible for 
BT’s January 30, 1996 press release because E & Y "signed 
off” on the accounting figures that formed the basis of the re- 
lease. Because BT would not have released its financial re- 
sults without E & Y’s approval, according to plaintiff, the 
market understood the réitvase as an implied assertfon by E & 
Y that the statements were accurate. See Cmplt. # 11. 


The problem with this theory is that the press release explic- 
itly stated that the financial statements were unaudited. It 

may be possible that some investors would infer an auditor’s 
approval from a company’s release of financial data. Such an 
inference, however, was simply not warranted in this case, as 


BT’s data was accompanied by a clear and express warning 
that no audit had yet been completed: E & Y’s only 


-24a- 
“representation” as to the accuracy of those figures was that it 
made no representation at all. Under plaintiff's reasoning, E 
& Y's disclaimer would be transformed into a guarantee of 
the statements’ accuracy. Such a result would not only be il- 
logical, but would seriously deter disclosure of unaudited fi- 
nancial information as well. 


*3 Plaintiff's second theory is that BT’s financial statements 
were express (though indirect) representations by E & Y that 
BT had earned a profit of $1.5 million in 1995 and had oper- 
ating income of $23.7 million. By "authorizing" BT to release 
these figures, plaintiff argues, E & Y effectively adopted them 
as its own. See Plaintiff's Memorandum of Law in Opposition 
to Defendant’s Motion to Dismiss at 8. While BT’s state- 
ments may have contained actionable misrepresentations, 
they were not made by, and cannot be attributed to, E & Y. 


In , the Supreme Court held that aiding and abetting securi- 
ties fraud was not prohibited by Rule 10b-5. Since that deci- 
sion, there has been considerable disagreement among the 
lower courts as to when primary 10b-5 liability can be im- 
posed upon defendants who have some connection with, but 
do not actually make, actionable misrepresentations. Com- 
pare (an accounting firm can be primarily liable under 10b-5 
when it plays a "significant role" in drafting a statement con- 
taining misrepresentations released by its client) with (10b-5 
liability can only attach when the defendant itself makes a 
misrepresentation). 


Virtually all cases to have considered the issue, however, 
have required a greater degree of participation in the fraudu- 
lent conduct than the mere review and approval of unaudited 
figures. See, e.g., (accounting firm’s review and approval of fi- 
nancial statements released by client cannot create 10b-5 li- 
ability); (allegations that accounting firm consulted, advised,

~~ 


and guided client in making misrepresentations insufficient to 
state a 10b-5 claim); (allegations that underwriter partici- 
pated in drafting and circulating a client’s misleading pro-

Rule 10b-5 in spite of its clearly tangential role in the alleged 
traud would effectively revive aiding and abetting liability un- 
der a different name, and would therefore run afoul of the 
Supreme Court’s holding in Central Bank.
