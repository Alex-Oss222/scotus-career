INTERESTS OF THE AMICI 


The State of lowa and twenty-one other states! join 
this brief in suppert of the petition for certiorari filed by 
John W. Marshall, et al., of the Ohio Supreme Court. At 
issue is whether Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), bars all 
judicial campaign spending limitations without regard to 
the unique interests of states in preserving the indepen- 
dence and integrity of the judiciary and protecting the 
due process rights of litigants. If Buckley does mandate 
rejection of judicial spending limits, Buckley should be 
reconsidered and overruled in light of new facts and 
circumstances and new compelling governmental inter- 
ests justifying such limits. 


This is a case of great significance. All states, how- 
ever they select judges, have a compelling interest in 
preventing partiality in adjudication, assuring judicial 
integrity, and assuring public belief in the judicial system. 
Thirty-eight states use some type of elective system to 
select or retain judges for all or some of their state courts. 
Of those states, thirteen utilize nonpartisan elections to 
select all judges: Georgia, Idaho, Kentucky, Michigan, 
Minnesota, Mississippi, Montana, Nevada, North Dakota, 
Ohio, Oregon, Washington, and Wisconsin. Eight states 
hold partisan elections to select their judiciary: Alabama, 
Arkansas, Illinois, Louisiana, North Carolina, Pennsylva- 
nia, Texas and West Virginia. An additional seventeen 
states elect a portion of their judges or require judges to 
stand for retention elections: Alaska, Arizona, California, 


' The State of New Hampshire, which has no judicial 
elections, joins in parts II and III. 


Colorado, Florida, Indiana, lowa, Kansas, Maryland, Mis- 
souri, Nebraska, New Mexico, Oklahoma, South Dakota, 
Tennessee, Utah, and Wyoming. American Judicature 
Society, Judicial Selection in States (March 1998). 


The amici states have a compelling interest in pre- 
venting corruption and the appearance of corruption in 
all state political elections. The amici states also have 
compelling interests in maintaining the integrity, inde- 
pendence and impartiality of judges and limiting the 
negative impacts of fund-raising on the performance of 
judges. Public distrust of government because of cam- 
paign finance issues adversely affects all government offi- 
cials. In addition, the excessive costs of judicial 
campaigns substantially affect all states where elections 
play a role in selection of judges, harming not only public 
trust in the judiciary, but also the due process rights of 
litigants appearing before elected judges. Each state join- 
ing this brief has a vital interest in (a) preserving the free 
speech and due process rights of our citizens; (b) preserv- 
ing the integrity of the judiciary and avoiding the appear- 
ance of partiality in judicial offices; (c) reducing 
corruption and the appearance of corruption in govern- 
ment generally; and (d) assuring public faith and partici- 
pation in the democratic process. The State of Ohio has 
established a compelling interest in shielding its judiciary 
from the appearance of partiality and potential for 
improper influence which flow from excessive campaign 
expenditures. The expenditure limitations at issue in this 
case are narrowly tailored to serve this interest and 
should have been upheld. 


The Court's application of Buckley to strike the judi- 
cial campaign spending limitations enacted by the Ohio 


Supreme Court effectively bars adoption of judicial cam- 
paign spending limits regardless of the offered justifica- 
tion. This Court should grant certiorari in light of the 
enormous consequences to our system of government 
stemming from this misapplication of Buckley. 


The Amici submit that the events of the last twenty 
years reveal that reasonable ceilings on campaign spend- 
ing are needed to ensure the robust and “unfettered 
interchange of ideas” that is at the core of the First 
Amendment. Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. at 14, quoting Roth 
v. United States, 354 U.S. 476, 484 (1957). In the last twenty 
years the amici states have seen marked increases in the 
amount of campaign expenditures. The amici states are 
concerned that limits on individual campaign contribu- 
tions alone do not prevent corruption or the appearance 
of corruption. 


REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


The Court below refused to consider the unique 
nature of judicial elections, refused to give credence to 
the compelling interests offered by the State of Ohio, and 
refused to even consider the possibility that an interest 
other than preventing corruption or the appearance of 
corruption could justify judicial campaign expenditure 
limitations. The Court essentially held that Buckley v. 
Valeo forever barred the recognition of compelling gov- 
ernmental interests sufficient to justify judicial campaign 
spending limits, no matter how narrowly tailored those 
limitations might be. This cannot be a proper application 
of the law. Especially in an issue of such importance, 


which is dependent upon assessments of facts that may 
change over time, constitutional rules cannot be frozen. 
Only this Court can correct this misapplication of Buckley. 


I. STATES HAVE A COMPELLING INTEREST IN 
MAINTAINING AN UNBIASED AND INDEPEN- 
DENT JUDICIARY. 


The people in each state “have a legitimate, indeed 
compelling, interest in maintaining a judiciary fully capa- 
ble of performing the demanding tasks that judges must 
perform.” Gregory v. Ashcroft, 501 U.S. 452, 464 (1991) 
(mandatory retirement age for appointed state judges 
exempt from Federal Age Discrimination in Employment 
Act). Restrictions on judicial election spending implicate 
not only the First Amendment interests of candidates but 
also the interest in an impartial judiciary which underlie 
the Due Process Clause. 


The First Amendment analysis by its nature 
tends to highlight the electoral interests of the 
judge as a candidate and the citizens as voters. 
It often fails to recognize that these men and 
women are candidates to a single end, that of 
becoming judges, and once judges, the interests 
of the litigants they serve and the judicial sys- 
tem as a whole should be paramount. Once this 
is appreciated, the restraints on judicial elec- 
tioneering can be seen for what they are, due 
process protections for litigants arguing before 
the elected judges. 


Randall Shepard, Campaign Speech: Restraint and Liberty in 
Judicial Ethics, 9 Geo. J. Legal Ethics, 1059, 1060 (1996).2 
Even a judge’s indirect financial interest in a pending 
case can violate the Due Process Clause. This Court has 
found undue risk of bias, for example, where a police 
court judge received a small amount of each fine, Tumey v. 
Ohio, 273 U.S. 510, 515 (1927), or where the town treasury 
received traffic fines imposed by a mayor acting as traffic 
judge, Ward v. Village of Monroeville, Ohio, 409 U.S. 57, 58 
(1972). The mere appearance of bias can in some cases 
create sufficient risk to the right to fair trial to violate due 
process. Taylor v. Hayes, 418 U S. 488, 501 (1974) (requiring 
trial judge’s recusal in attorney contempt case). See Scott 
D. Weiner, Popular Justice: State Judicial Elections ana Pro- 
cedural Due Process, 31 Harv. C.R.-C.L. L. Rev. 187, 190-191 
(1996). 


The need for an impartial judiciary is a sufficiently 
compelling state interest to permit reasonable regulation 
of judicial campaign speech necessary to achieve that 
purpose. Thus, judicial candidates can be restricted from 
commenting on issues likely to come before them as 
judges. Stretton v. Disciplinary Board of Supreme Court of 
Pennsylvania, 944 F.2d 137, 142 (3rd Cir. 1991); Ackerson v. 
Kentucky Judicial Retirement & Removal Commission, 776 
F.Supp. 309, 315 (W.D. Ky. 1991); Berger v. Supreme Court 
of Ohio, 598 F. Supp. 69, 75 (S.D. Ohio 1984), aff’d, 861 F.2d 
719 (6th Cir. 1988).° “The state’s interest in ensuring that

Procedural Due Process, 31 Harv. C.R.-C.L. L. Rev. at 194. 
Lawyers and litigants are also the persons most likely

cases. A 1998 study of Texas judicial campaign contribu-

Judicial elections are becoming much more expensive 
nationwide. ABA Taskforce Report, pp. 13-17. This is true 
of non-partisan and retention-only elections, as well as 
partisan elections. Id., p. 16. The most expensive judicial 
campaign ever was a retention-or’y election involving 
three California Supreme Court Justices in which spend- 
ing exceeded ten million dollars. Id. Increased spending 
occurs at all levels and throughout the country. The 
median spending in non-partisan elections for the Los 
Angeles Superior Court jumped from $3,000 in 1976 to 
$70,000 in 1994. Id. Spending for a non-partisan election 
for one seat on the Kentucky Supreme Court in 1978 was 
$52,000, but rose to an average of $412,000 for two seats 
in 1996. Id. at 17. 


The high cost of judicial elections is itself a critical 
problem. As the American Bar Association Task Force on 
Lawyers’ Political Contributions concluded, “Of course 
the greater the sums raised, the greater are all the prob- 
lems: the pressure on lawyers to contribute, the judges’ 
time and attention that will likely be given to fund- 
raising, the judges’ dependence - or appearance of 
dependence — on the fund-raisers and the contributors, 
and the erosion of public confidence.” ABA Task Force 
Report, p. 16. The ABA Task Force did not address the 
merits of campaign spending limitations because of this 
pending case. Id. at p. 23, n.37. The Task Force did, 
however, conclude that ” . . . the need for reform could 
not be clearer nor more urgent, given the value we place

dence and integrity, and on making the bench attractive 
to the kinds of people we need as judges.” ABA Taskforce 
Report, p. 17. 


Caps on individual campaign contributions do not 
address the problems created by competition for contri- 
butions from two or more special interest groups or seg- 
ments of the bar. This and the increased use of expensive 
advertising media are cited as the two most significant 
factors causing a dramatic increase in the dependency on, 
and influence of, campaign contributions in judicial elec- 
tions in the last twenty years. Weiner, Popular Justice: State 
Judicial Elections and Procedural Due Process, 31 Harv. C.R.- 
C.L. L. Rev. at 193. The Mobile Register in an editorial 
dated December 29, 1994, claimed that the “public’s esti- 
mate of judicial elections was pushed to its lowest point” 
by a recent state supreme court election. The reason, 
asserted the paper, was that “Plaintiffs’ lawyers on the 
one side and business interests on the other pour millions 
of dollars into judicial campaigns to get their respective 
allies elected to the bench.” A Judicature article asserted 
that the Texas Supreme Court, and some lower court, 
races have seen “campaign finance wars between plaintiff 
and defense lawyers.” Champagne, Judicial Reform in 
Texas, 72 Judicature at 149.

citizens the protection of a functioning and impartial 
judiciary. These interests are different from the interests 
considered in Buckley and are not precluded by that deci- 
sion. 


II. BUCKLEY V. VALEO, 424 U.S. 1 (1976) DOES NOT 
PRECLUDE JUDICIAL RECOGNITION OF COM- 
PELLING GOVERNMENTAL INTERESTS SUFFI- 
CIENT TO JUSTIFY SPENDING LIMITATIONS. 


Perhaps because this Court in Buckley considered 
only an abstract challenge to campaign spending limita- 
tions and the United States did not defend those, the 
Court found that “no governmental interest that has been 
suggested is sufficient to justify the restriction on the 
quantity of political expression imposed by 608(c)’s cam- 
paign expenditure limitations.” 424 U.S. at 55. As shown 
above, there are additional compelling and unique state 
interests protected by judicial campaign spending limits. 


The Court’s factual assumptions in Buckley cannot 
permanently preclude courts from acknowledging that 
those assumptions were erroneous or from hearing new 
factual matters to support campaign spending limita- 
tions. “[A] decision of the Court, if, in essence, merely the 


13 


determination of a fact, is not entitled in later controver- 
sies between other parties, to that sanction which, under 
the policy of stare decisis, is accorded to the decision of a 
proposition purely of law. For not only may the decision 
of the fact have been rendered upon an inadequate pre- 
sentation of then existing conditions, but the conditions 
may have changed meanwhile.” Burnet v. Coronado Oil & 
Gas Co., 285 U.S. 393, 412 (1931) (Brandeis, J. dissenting). 


Where less restrictive alternatives fail to accomplish 
the compelling state interest, stare decisis does not pre- 
clude a state from enacting more burdensome laws neces- 
sary to meet the need. Federal Communications Commission 
v. League of Women Voters of California, 468 U.S. 364, 401, 
n.27 (1984) (“over a century of governmental experience 
with less restrictive alternatives that proved to be inade- 
quate to maintain the effective operation of government” 
supported constitutionality of Hatch Act) describing CSC 
v. Letter Carriers, 413 U.S. 548, 557-563 (1973). 


Changed factual circumstances provide an appropri- 
ate basis for distinguishing a decision of the Supreme 
Court. See Patterson v. McLean Credit Union, 491 U.S. 164, 
174 (1989) (“a precedent becomes more vulnerable as it 
becomes outdated and after being ‘tested by experience, 
has been found to be inconsistent with the sense of justice 
or with the social welfare’ ”), quoting Runyon v. McCrary, 
427 U.S. 160, 191 (1976) (Stevens, concurring). Overruling 
a prior case is also appropriate where “facts have so 
changed or come to be seen so differently, as to have 
robbed the old rule of significant application or justifica- 
tion. ...” Planned Parenthood v. Casey, 505 U.S. 833, 854 
(1992).

STATE RULE, RATHER THAN THE CURRENT 
RULE, IMPROPERLY DISCOURAGES STATES 
FROM CORRECTING RULES WHICH BURDEN 
THE EXERCISE OF FEDERAL CONSTITU-

Canon VII(C)(6)(d), as enacted in 1995, established a 
$75,000 expenditure limitation for all judicial candidates 
for a court of common pleas and a $15,000 limitation for 
campaign expenditure for municipal or county courts. 
The District Court enjoined enforcement of these limuia- 
tions, concluding that the challengers would likely suc- 
ceed in establishing that the canon unconstitutionally 
infringed on judicial candidate’s First Amendment rights. 
Petition Appendix, A-40. Appeal was taken to the Sixth 
Circuit Court of Appeals. Prior to the completion of brief- 
ing on the appeal, the Ohio Supreme Court amended 
Canon VII(C)(6)(d), replacing the across-the-board expen- 
diture limitations with a graduated scale of population- 
based expenditure limitations, thus providing more nar- 
rowly tailored spending limitations. 


Under these circumstances, where a challenged law 
is amended after entry of judgment, but prior to resolu- 
tion of a pending appeal, the appellate court is bound to 
review the judgment of the district court in light of the 
amended provision, rather than the law as it stood before 
the lower court. Diffenderfer v. Central Baptist Church, 404 
U.S. 412, 414 (1972). The Court of Appeals acknowledged 
this precedent, indicating that it “could no longer con- 
sider the former version of Canon VII(C)(6)” as it [had] 
recently been revised. App. A-7. The Court then ignored 
pronouncements of this Court regarding appropriate

Court’s review of the amended canon was directly con-
