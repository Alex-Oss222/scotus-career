STATUTES AT ISSUE 


This case addresses principally the interpretation of 
section 2(b) of the McCarran-Ferguson Act, 15 U.S.C. 
§ 1012(b), which provides, in pertinent part: 


"No Act of Congress shall be construed to 
invalidate, impair or supersede any law enacted by 
any State for the pui,ose of regulating the business 
of insurance . . . unless such Act specifically relates 
to the business of insurance... .” 


The civil remedy provisions of RICO, 18 U.S.C. §§ 1961- 
1968 are also implicated by this case. The full text of the 
McCarran-Ferguson Act is set forth in the Appendix hereto. 
The pertinent provisions of RICO are set forth in Appendix 
C to the Petition for Writ of Certiorari. 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. Summary of Material Facts and Allegations. 

This action arises out of disputes respecting the 
administration and interpretation of employee group health 
insurance policies sold by Humana Insurance in the mid- 
1980’s in the State of Nevada. These policies contained a 
“co-payment” provision pursuant to which patients covered 
by the policies were obligated to pay all charges up to a 
specified deductible and to pay 20% of charges above the 
deductible (up to a specified annual maximum). Humana 


Insurance was responsible for the remaining 80%. Joint 
App. at JA-117. 


Among other coverages, the policies covered treatment 
at certain Nevada hospitals, including a hospital owned by 
Humana, Humana Hospital-Sunrise ("Sunrise"). Appendix A 
to Petition for Writ of Certiorari at 2a. (114 F.3d at 1472.) 
Humana Insurance negotiated discounts with Sunrise for 
hospital services provided to its policy beneficiaries, 
substantially reducing the costs on many procedures. 
Record, CR 804, Vol. 8, Nos. 8-10. In administering the 
co-payment feature of the policies, Humana Insurance 
credited those discounts entirely to its 80% obligation. 


Policy beneficiaries continued to be billed for their 20% of 
the undiscounted hospital charge. Record, CR 804, Vol. 9, 
No. 28. 


In amendments to its insurance law effective in 1988, 
Nevada prohibited this practice and required that the benefits 
of such discounts be applied, pro rata, to an insured’s co- 
payment obligation. See Nev. Rev. Stat. § 689B.061 
(1997). Humana Insurance complied with that change in law 
and has calculated co-payment obligations accordingly since 
its enactment. Record, CR 804, Vol. 3, No. 13. 


Respondents, on their own behalf and on behalf of a 
class of policy beneficiaries who made co-payments,’ have 
alleged that Humana Insurance misrepresented to its insureds 
the pertinent facts regarding its method of accounting for 
discounts during the period when those discounts were 
credited solely to its 80% obligation. Respondents have 
further alleged that these misrepresentations were made in 
the various mailings sent by Humana Insurance to its 
insureds regarding their co-payment obligations, or in 
telephone conversations with insureds, and that these 
communications constitute multiple predicate acts of mail or

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


As noted in the Petition for Writ of Certiorari, the 
decision below is one of numerous conflicting decisions of 
the several circuits respecting the interpretation of the 
McCarran-Ferguson Act generally, and of its applicability to 
RICO specifically. That split among the circuits is reflective 
of a basic difference in jurisprudential approaches to the 
issue at hand. The Ninth Circuit, like other circuits rejecting 
the proposition that the McCarran-Ferguson Act precludes 
the application of RICO to the business of insurance, uses an 
“inverse preemption” analysis that applies to the McCarran- 
Ferguson analysis the strict “direct conflict” rules that are 
frequently applied in cases involving implied federal 
preemption of state law.° Merchants Home Delivery Serv., 


* This test for McCarran-Ferguson Act preclusion was 
first espoused by the Seventh Circuit in NAACP v. 
American Family Mutual Insurance Co., 978 F.2d 287, 
296-97 (7th Cir. 1992), a case that did not involve RICO. 
Other decisions embracing it include Sabo v. Metropolitan 
Life Insurance Co., 137 F.3d 185, 194-95 (3d Cir. 1998); 
Villafane-Neriz v. FDIC, 75 F.3d 727, 735-36 (ist Cir. 
1996); and Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Cisneros, 
52 F.3d 1351, 1360-63 (6th Cir. 1995), cert. denied, 516 
U.S. 1140 (1996). 


Inc. v. Frank B. Hall & Co., supra, 50 F.3d at 1492. The 
circuits finding McCarran-Ferguson preclusion have not 
constrained their analysis by the special restrictive rules 
applicable to implied federal preemption. Instead, these 
courts have examined the question of whether the McCarran- 
Ferguson Act applies to RICO using generally applicable 
principles of statutory interpretation. ° 


The “inverse” preemption analysis embraced by the 
Ninth Circuit below is logically flawed. The restrictive 
implied federal preemption rules of construction are 
predicated upon principles of federalism and reflect the 
strong presumption that the laws of the states are to be 
preserved, despite overlapping federal legislation. See, e.g., 
Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc., 505 U.S. 504, 516 (1992). 
The McCarran-Ferguson Act does not involve federal 
interference in state regulation, but rather Congress’ 
voluntary withdrawal from a field seen best left to the states. 


* Doe v. Norwest Bank Minnesota, N.A., 107 F.3d 1297,

Adoption of an “inverse preemption” analysis has the 
perverse result of applying a rule of construction created out 
of a concern for states’ rights to circumscribe a statute that 


is intended to foster those same rights. 


The ordinary rules of statutory interpretation should be 
applied to the McCarran-Ferguson Act. Under those rules, 
the McCarran-Ferguson Act must be interpreted as 
precluding the application of RICO to the business of 
insurance. The language and history of the statute bespeak 
a broad preclusionary intent, not limited to “direct conflicts,” 
but intended to withdraw Congress from the field absent an 
express congressional statement to the contrary. A federal 
statute that imposes materially different remedies or 
procedures than does an otherwise parallel state law 
effectively renders superfluous the state legislative decisions 
respecting those remedies or procedures. Accordingly, the 
application of such a statute to the business of insurance 
would “invalidate, impair or supersede” state law in 
derogation of the McCarran-Ferguson Act even if the federal 
law and the state law were parallel with respect to their 
substantive prohibitions.

ARGUMENT 


The McCarran-Ferguson Act was the congressional 
response to this Court’s decision in United States v. South- 
Eastern Underwriters Ass'n, 322 U.S. 533 (1944), which 
Overturned a long-standing presumption that the sale of 
insurance was not commerce and, hence, was not reached by 
federal law regulating interstate commerce. See, ¢.g., 
Paul v. Virginia, 75 U.S. (8 Wall.) 168, 183 (1869). Asa 
consequence of South-Eastern, state laws on insurance, 
which had been thought to be the exclusive source of 
regulation, were threatened with eclipse by federal law 
which, if applicable at all, becomes ascendant under the 
supremacy clause. The McCarran-Ferguson Act was 
specifically intended to prevent that result. Department of 
Treasury v. Fabe, 508 U.S. 491, 500 (1993). 


The federal legislation at issue in South-Eastern was the 
Sherman Act, 15 U.S.C. §§ 1-7. Not surprisingly, 
therefore, most of the congressional debate on the Act 
centered on its effect on the antitrust laws, which proved to 
be the most contentious aspect of the legislation.’ The

Circuits apply a broad rule of McCarran-Ferguson preclusion 
derived from the breadth of the language of the statute. On 
their reading of the Act, those circuits have concluded that 
material differences between state and federal law on 
remedies alone may result in preclusion of the federal law. 
See, e.g., Doe v. Norwest Bank Minnesota, N.A., supra, 107 
F.3d at 1307-08. In contrast, the Ninth Circuit, and the 
circuits that share its view, apply a narrow rule derived from 
an "inverse preemption" analysis that reasons that McCarran- 
Ferguson preclusion of federal law in deference to state law 
is analogous to implied federal preemption of state law and 
should be governed by the same rules. On this reasoning, 
these circuits require a “direct conflict" between state and 
federal law, a conflict that they do not find in differences in 
the procedures or remedies provided by state and federal 
law. See, e.g., Merchants Home Delivery Serv., Inc. v. 
Frank B. Hall & Co., supra, 50 F.3d at 1492. 


The conclusion reached by the Ninth Circuit and the others 
adopting this analysis is not free from doubt even if the 
“inverse preemption” analysis used were otherwise 
appropriate. In concluding that even material differences in 
procedures or remedies would not be a “direct conflict” 
sufficient for implied federal preemption, the Ninth Circuit 
relies on Silkwood v. Kerr-McGee Corp., 464 U.S. 238 
(1984). In Silkwood, however, this Court made note of the 
fact that the legislative history of the federal statute in 
question showed a congressional intent to preserve the state 
tort remedies at issue. 464 U.S. at 251-56. This Court has 


14 


elsewhere found a difference in remedies to be sufficient 
state interference with federal law to require preemption. 
E.g., Garner v. Teamsters, 346 U.S. 485, 498-99 (1953); 
San Diego Bldg. Trades Council v. Garmon, 359 U.S. 236, 
247 (1959). Moreover, if a federal preemption analysis 
were to be adopted for application of the McCarran- 
Ferguson Act, the “field” preemption of Rice v. Santa Fe 
Elevator Corp. , 331 U.S. 218 (1947) would appear to be the 
better analogy given the statutory structure and legislative 
history that demonstrates the congressional intent to leave the 
entire field of insurance regulation to the states.* 


The principal flaw in the Ninth Circuit’s reasoning 
below is not, however, in the manner in which the court 
applied the law of implied federal preemption. Rather, it is 
in the determination that those rules properly apply to the 
McCarran-Ferguson Act at all. As will be discussed in the 
balance of this brief, they do not. The ordinary rules of 
statutory interpretation apply and they compel the opposite 
of the determination reached by the Ninth Circuit below.

- The "Inverse Preemption" Analysis Underpinning _ 
the Decision Below Is Inappropriate for Application 
of the McCarran-Ferguson Act. 


One can hardly quarrel with the observation that 
McCarran-Ferguson preclusion of federal law has an effect 
that is analogous to federal preemption of state law. It does 
not follow, however, that the two must, or should, be 
governed by the same rules. Given that the “direct conflict” 
test has no basis in the language of the statute, whether 
federal preemption rules should apply to a McCarran- 
Ferguson analysis depends upon whether application of those 
rules would foster the goals of the Act. Examination of that 
question -- which is not addressed in the decisions of the 
Ninth Circuit or of the other circuits sharing its view -- 
quickly leads to the conclusion that the application of rules 
governing issues of federal preemption fosters not merely 
different goals than the McCarran-Ferguson Act, but totally 
contrary goals. 


The restrictive rules governing federal preemption are 


predicated upon principles of federalism. |Community 
Communications Co. v. Boulder, 455 U.S. 40, 61 (1982) 
(Rehnquist, J., dissenting). They reflect the structure of our 
Constitution, in which the powers of the federal government 
“are few and defined [while those] which are to remain in 
the State governments are numerous and indefinite.” 
Gregory v. Ashcroft, 501 U.S. 452, 458 (1991) (quoting The

state laws are to be preserved if possible, despite overlapping 
federal legislation. E.g., Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc., 
supra, 505 U.S. at 516. 


Simply put, the goal served by the rules of implied 
federal preemption is deference to the sovereignty of the 
states. When these rules are applied in the “inverse” case of 
McCarran-Ferguson preclusion, as the Ninth Circuit does, 
their effect is itself inverted, with the rules now fostering the 
goal of deference to federal sovereignty. 

No federalist principle requires such a rule since 
McCarran-Ferguson preclusion is a voluntary relinquishment 
of power by Congress and not a result forced upon the 
federal government by a clash of sovereignties. Under the 
supremacy clause, federal law prevails in such a clash in any 
event, a circumstance that further illustrates the 
inappropriateness of using a principle of interpretation 
intended to protect the subservient sovereign. While the 
states can do nothing about federal laws that preempt their 
own, Congress need only state that a federal statute applies 
to insurance to avoid the preclusive effect of the McCarran- 
Ferguson Act. 


More to the point, limiting McCarran-Ferguson 
preclusion by applying a rule of strict deference to federal 
sovereignty would run directly contrary to the goals of the 
Act, which this Court itself has characterized as restoring 
"the supremacy of the States in the realm of insurance 
regulation." Department of Treasury v. Fabe, supra, 508

B. RICO’s Materially Different Remedies "Impair or 
Supersede" Nevada’s Otherwise Parallel Insurance 
Law. 


1. The Language of the McCarran-Ferguson Act 
Compels the Conclusion That It Bars RICO’S 
Application To the Business of Insurance. 


The critical interpretive question presented is the 
meaning of “invalidate, impair or supersede” as used in the 
Act. Interpretation must begin with the words themselves, 
as this Court has often cautioned. All of the words must be 
considered, Garcia v. United States, supra, 469 U.S. at 73, 
and the words must be given their ordinary English meaning. 
Barnett Bank, N.A. v. Nelson, supra, 517 U.S. at 38-39. 

As already noted, “invalidate” could be equated with 
“preempt” and limited to the narrow circumstance of a 
conflict between state end federal law so severe as to qualify 
for federal preemption but for the Act. ‘“Impair" and 
“supersede” cannot be so constrained, and Congress could 
not have intended so restrictive a meaning when the Act was 
passed. As reflected in the leading dictionaries of the time, 
“impair” is to “make worse, less valuable, or weaker;” or 
"to diminish in quantity, value, excellence, or strength." 
OXFORD ENGLISH DICTIONARY 72 (1933); WEBSTER’S NEW 
INTERNATIONAL DICTIONARY 1246 (2d Ed. 1934). 
"Supersede” is "to make unnecessary or superfluous,” or “to 
force out of use as_ inferior.” WEBSTER’S NEW 


21 


INTERNATIONAL DICTIONARY 2533 (2d Ed. 1934). Any 
federal statute that lessens the force or significance of a state 
Statute, or renders that statute superfluous, “impairs or 
supersedes” that state statute. Such a result can follow as 
readily from a disparity in state and federal remedies as from 
a disparity in substantive prohibitions. 

A regulatory scheme necessarily has three components: 
(i) substantive prohibitions or mandates; (ii) remedies for 
non-compliance; and (iii) procedural means for determining 
compliance and imposing the remedies. Each of these three 
components is found in the law of Nevada regulating 
insurance and in that of all the other states as well. Each of 
these components was the subject of legislative consideration 
by Nevada and the other states, and nothing in the 
McCarran-Ferguson Act limits the preclusionary effect of 
that Act to only the first of these three components. By its 
terms, the Act also precludes the application of any federal 
Statute (other than those specifically related to insurance) that 
“impairs” or “supersedes” state law governing the remedies 
or enforcement procedures included in state insurance 
regulation. 

It is not a sufficient response to this point to suggest, as 
have some courts, that a disparity in remedies may be 
supportive of state law if the more potent federal remedies 
offer a greater chance “that a state norm will be 
vindicated ...." NAACP v. American Family Mut. Ins. 
Co., supra, 978 F.2d at 295. The McCarran-Ferguson Act 
does not contain any provision for balancing a federal

statute’s impairment of state law on remedies against that 
federal statute’s supposed enhancement of the state law’s 
substantive prohibitions or mandates. 

Nor is there any basis for assuming, as this argument 
implicitly does, that state legislative decisions regarding 
remedies and procedures are less reflective of policy choices 
than are the decisions on substantive prohibitions.”

2. The History and Purpose of the McCarran- 
Ferguson Act Further Support the Conclusion That 
it Bars RICO’s Application to the Business of 
Insurance. 


The interpretation that disparity in remedies and 
procedures “impairs or supersedes” state law also finds 
support in the purpose and history of the McCarran-Ferguson 
Act. As already noted, the Act was a response to South- 
Eastern and an attempt, with limits, to turn the clock back 
to the time when the states were the sole regulators of the 


26 


business of insurance. What conduct was allowed or 
prohibited was exclusively the province of the states, and so 
too were the remedies afforded for violation of the norms set 
by the states. As both the Senate and House reports on the 
bill that was to become the Act noted: “From its beginning 
the business of insurance has been regarded as a local 
matter, to be subject to and regulated by the laws of the 
several States.” S. Rep. No. 79-20, at 1 (1945); H.R. Rep. 
No. 79-68, at 1 (1945). The purpose of the Act was 
restoration of that state control, and there is nothing in the 
Act or its history that suggests that Congress did not intend 
that control to include control over remedies. 


The debates on the floor of Congress are consistent with 
this interpretation. Various remarks of Senators Ferguson 
and Pepper and of Representative Gwynne have already been 
noted above. There are many others to the same effect.’
