          COUNTER-STATEMENT OF THE CASE

     Petitioner bases its request for certiorari on legalisms
divorced from the case itself. It “States the Case” without stating
what it is about. The result is it seeks review to obtain an advisory
opinion on the Warsaw Convention unrelated to the facts proved
at trial. The petition’s Statement of the Case describes them in a
few threadbare words under the Statement’s subheading, “Nature
of the Case”:

            Plaintiff was escorted into a private EL AL
            security room where her baggage and person
            were searched. EL AL was searching for
            “[e)xplosives in any size, detonators ...
            from small, like the cap of the pen, and...
            up from that.” Defendant did not, at any time,
            use force to question or search plaintiff and
            plaintiff did not, at any time, object to the
            search.

      If that was all there was to it, there would have been no
case to start with. Wholly omitted by Petitioner is its willful
humiliating violation of Respondent’s body and liberty,
amounting to assault and battery and false imprisonment in New
York State where the cause arose, all proved and deemed true
when considering the dismissal which the Second Circuit
reversed and petitioner now asks the Court to review. Simply
seeking to visit Israel on a vacation, she was handled like an
imprisoned criminal without rights before being permitted to
board her flight.

      At bottom, petitioner seeks review of its claim that the
Warsaw Convention be construed to immunize airlines from
liability for injuries due to their willful misconduct while
allowing passenger suits against them if injury is caused by
accident.

RULING OF THE SECOND CIRCUIT IS THAT IT
REFUSED TO GIVE AN ABSURD AND IRRATIONAL
CONSTRUCTION TO THE WARSAW CONVENTION.

when one bows to a display of authority. People v. Cameron, 48
A.D. 2d 783, 369 N.Y.S. 2d 418 (1975). This is no consent at
all. But horrifying as the experience was to plaintiff, its conduct
was not “accidental” but standard for El Al (A113, 118).

     Under New York and generally accepted common law
principles, Petitioner’s conduct amounted to intentional assault

and battery and false imprisonment.’ Petitioner asks the
Convention that allows airline exposure to liability for accidental

It is at odds with the teaching of this Court and others to avoid

     THE SUPPOSED NEED FOR “UNIFORMITY” IN
CONSTRUING THE CONVENTION FOR CHOICE OF
LAW PURPOSES HAS BEEN REJECTED BY THIS
COURT.

     The judgment of the House of Lords in Abnett v. British
Airways PLC, | All. E.R. 193 (H.L. 12 December, 1996) lends
no support to El Al’s contention that the Warsaw Convention
renders it immune to common law claims for willful wrongdoing.
Intentional tort was not involved in Abnett. Negligence and
breach of implied contract of reasonable care were. The opinion
by Lord Hope of Craighead related to common law passenger
claims for damages due to a flight delay in Kuwait allowing the
passenger to be taken prisoner by the invading Iraqis. Article 17
did not apply. In context of the non-willful wrongdoing case
before the Lords, Lord Hope ruled that Article 17 nonetheless
was the exclusive remedy for passengers. He rested on a felt
need for uniformity in construing the Convention.

     While avowing a pursuit of uniformity, Lord Hope omitted
to note this Court’s landmark Zicherman v. Korean Air Lines
Co., Lid., U.S. __ , 116 S. Ct. 629, 636 (16 January, 1996),
which came down almost a year earlier and flatly rejected
uniformity as a guiding principle in Convention construction.

            The most natural reading of this Article is

that, in an action brought under Article 17,
the law of the Convention does not affect the
substantive questions of who may bring suit
and what they may be compensated for.

Congress may choose to enact special
provisions applicable to Warsaw- Convention
cases, as some countries have done. See
supra, at 635. Absent such legislation,
however, Articles 17 and 24(2) provide
nothing more than a pass-through,
authorizing us to apply the law that would

govern in the absence of the Warsaw
Convention. There is little doubt what that
law is in this case.

The Convention neither adopted any uniform
rule of its own nor authorized national courts
to pursue uniformity in derogation of
otherwise applicable law. Petitioners argue,
in effect, that the Convention contains an
implicit authorization for national courts to

over-sea accidents governed by their

respective domestic laws, even though it
 leaves the vast discrepancies among the
 various domestic laws untouched. That is

 most unlikely.

     PETITIONER SEEKS TO READ INTO THE WARSAW
CONVENTION CONTRARY TO THE GOVERNING LAW
AN UNEXPRESSED PREEMPTION OF THE FOREIGN
SOVEREIGN IMMUNITY ACT WHICH MANDATES THE
APPLICABLE CHOICE OF LAW RULE IN THIS CASE
TO BE THAT OF NEW YORK.

     The events in Zicherman occurred over the high seas.
Domestic choice of laws rules made Death on the High Seas
Act (DOHSA) applicable. In the present case involving an “over-
land” occurrence in New York, under Zicherman principles the
                                      10

domestic choice-of-law rules under the Foreign Sovereign
Immunities Act, specifically 28 U.S.C. § 1606, make New York
tort law applicable on the nature of the harm which is legally
cognizable. As provided by Section 1606, under circumstances
in which it is not entitled to immunity (as here), a

            foreign state shall be liable in the same
            manner and to the same extent as a private
            individual under like circumstances.

     Petitioner as a foreign state within the FSIA (28 U.S.C.
§ 1602, et seg.), which availed itself of FSIA in removing the
case from state to federal court, is subject to the provisions of
§ 1606 which make New York law applicable to this New York

tort.’

       As was noted in Zicherman, (116 S. Ct., 636)

            Congress may choose to enact special
            provisions applicable to Warsaw-Convention
            cases, as some countries have done.

But Congress has not chosen. Neither in FSIA or elsewhere did
it choose to enact a special provision excluding airlines owned
by foreign states in “international transportation” from state law
liability that they would otherwise incur under 28 U.S.C. § 1606
as private individuals “under like circumstances.”

personal injury law governing passenger suits against airlines.

     CONTRARY TO THE GOVERNING LAW
PETITIONER WOULD READ INTO IN THE
CONVENTION A BAN ON RECOURSE TO STATE LAW
FOR NON-ACCIDENTAL WILLFULLY INFLICTED

Review”:

does not state that it exclusively governs and precludes non-
accidentally caused (or willful tort caused) injury recoveries,
and so it does not under Zicherman, Amerada Hess, Banco Para
El Comercio and the FSIA. Petitioner offers no compelling
reason why the case at bar should present the occasion to re-
review this settled law.

in Article 24(2). This provides that in

v. Kerr-McGee Corp. 464 U.S. 238, 251 (1984):

            It is difficult to believe that Congress would,

     PREEMPTION OF THE STATES’ COMMON LAW OF
PERSONAL INJURY TORTS IS DISFAVORED AND
THERE IS PRESUMPTION AGAINST IT.

     Were there no FSIA or Section 1606, there remains a strong
principled presumption against pre-emption of state common
law in the area of personal injury torts. Though there are none
here, even legislatively expressed pre-emptive clauses are
presumed inapplicable.

     Medtronic, Inc. v. Lohr, 116S. Ct. 2240, dealt with an argued
preemption of state common law personal injury claims by a
federal statute. Unlike the Convention here, that statute did
contain specific preemptive language. The Court noted:
