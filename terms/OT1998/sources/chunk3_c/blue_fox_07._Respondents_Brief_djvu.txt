QUESTION PRESENTED 


Is a subcontractor’s suit to enforce an equitable lien against 
the United States a suit for “money damages” within the 
meaning of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5. U.S.C. § 
702, and this Court’s decision in Bowen v. Massachusetts, 
487 U.S. 879 (1988).

STATEMENT 


In September, 1993, pursuant to Section 8(a) of the 
Small Business Act, 15 U.S.C. § 637(a), the United States 
Department of the Army awarded a contract to the United 
States Small Business Administration. Pet. App. 2a. The 
contract involved the installation and testing of a telephone 
switching system and the construction of a facility to house 
the switching system at the Umatilla Army Depot, Oregon. 
Id. SBA agreed to furnish the supplies and services requested 
by the Army by subcontracting with Verdan Technology, Inc. 
Id. 


Verdan subcontracted the construction portion of its 
contract to Respondent Blue Fox. Pet. App. 4a. The total 
cost of the work to be performed by Blue Fox was 
$186,347.80. Jd. Blue Fox did not know until it had 
completed its performance under the contract that Verdan had 
not furnished a payment bond. /d. Verdan failed to pay Blue 
Fox its full contract price -- the sum of $46,586.14 remained 
due. /d. Blue Fox informed the Army and SBA in writing 
on May 26, 1994, and again on June 15, 1994, that it had not 
been fully paid. /d. In its June 15, 1994 letter to the Army, 
Respondent requested “suspension or reduction of payment be 
administered by your agency.” J.A. 33.' Nevertheless, the 
Army subsequently disbursed a total of $86,132.33 to Verdan 
between July and October, 1994. /d. 


‘The Progress Payments clause of the Verdan contract permitted the 
Contracting Officer to “reduce or suspend progress payments” aller 
finding on substantial evidence that any of several performance 
deficiencies had occurred, including “delinquen{cy] in payment of the 
costs of performing this contract in the ordinary course of business.” 
Clause 1.74 52.232-0016 I, page I-16, Exhibits to Plaintiff's Motion for 
Summary Judgment, Tab A, p. 96.

On January 3, 1995, the Army terminated Verdan’s 
contract for default. Pet. App. Sa. In its notice of intention 
to terminate the contract, the Army indicated that one of its 
“most severe items of concern” was Verdan's failure to pay 
Blue Fox. /d. After termination of the Verdan contract, the 
Army chose Dynamic Concepts, Inc. to finish the 
uncompleted portion of the work, under an existing contract 
between the Army and Dynamic. The modification contract 
with Dynamic was for $126,772.78 and was partially funded 
with the undisbursed balance on the Verdan contract, 
$84,910.52. Jd. The Army did not authorize payment to 
Dynamic for its work in completing the proyect until July 27, 
1995, two months after Blue Fox filed suit in district court, 
asking inter alia, that the court enjoin the Army and SBA 
from paying any further monies or making any payments from 
funds available or appropriated for a follow-on contract for 
completion of the Umatilla Army Depot Electronic Switching 
System project, until Blue Fox’s unpaid bill is satisfied. /d. 


The original solicitation issued by the Army required 
the contractor to furnish payment and performance bonds if 
the contract exceeded $25,000. Pet. App. 3a, Choate Aff. 
par. 6 (J.A. 59); Sawyer Aff. par. 6 (J.A. 63). On July 22, 
1993, the Army's Contracting Specialist, Elizabeth Faler, 
spoke with the Army Judge Advocate General's Office, in 
regard to the bond requirement on the project. According to 
Ms. Faler’s Memorandum of the conversation, she was told 
that “only VERDAN could get the bonds and that we did not 
have a contract with VERDAN’s subs. She was very adamant 
about the subject." The following day, Ms. Faler spoke with 
SBA’s Ms. Witty. Ms. Faler wrote that “I related what the 
Department of the Army Judge Advocate’s Office had said. 
She [Ms. Witty] completely disagreed with their 
recommendations.” Among other reasons given to Ms. Faler, 
Ms. Witty felt that Verdan would not qualify for a bond 
“because of its lack of experience in construction.” J.A. 45

Under 13 CFR § 124.305 (1990), the SBA was 
empowered to exempt an 8(a) participant from Miller Act 
bonding requirements where SBA determines that the 8(a) 
concern is unable to obtain the requisite bonds. To protect 
subcontractors, these regulations required, inter alia, that 
disbursement of contract proceeds be made through a special 
bank account, so that payment can be made directly to 
subcontractors, 13 CFR § 124.305(d)(2), and that the 8(a) 
concern provide written notification to subcontractors 
informing them that the primary contractor is not furnishing 
a Miller Act bond. 13 CFR § 124.305(d)(4). Nothing in the 
record evidences compliance with these regulations in

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


The equitable rights of unpaid subcontractors to 
construction balances are long-recognized in this Court's 
decisions. Ninety years ago, in Henningsen v. United States 
Fidelity & Guar. Co., 208 U.S. 404, 410 (1908), this Court 
stated that the government had “equitable obligations to see 
that the laborers and supplymen were paid." This Court 
reaffirmed that recognition in Pearlman v. Reliance Insurance 
Co., 371 U.S. 132, 141 (1962), stating that “laborers and 
materialmen had a right to be paid out of the fund...” 


In this suit, Respondent Blue Fox, Inc., an unpaid 
federal subcontractor, seeks to declare and enforce such 
equitable rights. For years, the federal courts have permitted 
sureties On government contracts to enforce related rights. 
Thus, in Pearlman, this Court upheld a surety's right to 
reimbursement from remaining contract monies, reasoning that 
the surety, having paid the laborers and materialmen, was 
entitled to the benefit of the rights of the persons it paid. /d. 
at 141. If the surety's right to reimbursement rests on the 
rights of the subcontractors it paid, it stands to reason that the 
subcontractors themselves should be able to assert those rights 
where there is no surety. 


Nevertheless, unpaid subcontractors traditionally have 
been unable to sue the government directly. Although 
sympathetic to their plight, courts were helpless to act, 
because of the bar of sovereign immunity. Absent sovereign 
immunity, however, the obstacle to enforcement no longer 
exists. Thus, when sovereign immunity is waived, unpaid 
subcontractors can directly enforce equitable rights to 
remaining contract monies. Ap, lying this rationale, at least 
three circuit courts of appeal have permitted unpaid 
subcontractors to bring equitable lien actions against an 
agency of the government whose immunity Congress waived. 


6 


See Active Fire Sprinkler Corp. v. United Siates Postal Serv., 
811 F.2d 747, 756 (2d Cir. 1987); Kennedy Electric Co., Inc. 
v. United States Postal Serv., 508 F.2d 954 (10th Cir. 1974), 
W right v. United States Postal Serv., 29 F 3d 1426 (9th Cir. 
1994). 


The Administrative Procedure Act waives sovereign 
immunity over Respondent's claim here. In 1976, Congress 
amended the APA to add what this Court has described as “an 
important part of a major piece of legislation..." Bowen v. 
Massachusetts, 487 U.S. 879, 896 (1988). In that amendment, 
Congress “eliminate[d] the sovereign immunity defense in all 
equitable actions for specific relief against a Federal agency. 
.. ." Bowen, 487 U.S. at 898-900. Congress did so by 
stating that "[a]n action in a court of the United States 
seeking relief other than money damages * * * shall not be 
dismissed nor relief therein be denied on the ground that it is 
against the United States ..." 5 U.S.C. 702 


The issue in this case is whether direct subcontractor 
enforcement of Peariman rights seeks relref “other than money 
damages.” For several reasons, this Court should conclude 
that enforcement of an equitable lien does moi present a 
money damages claim. First, an equitable lien is historically 
distinct from damages. It is a charge, or security interest, on 
specific property. As its name implies, it is an equitable 
remedy, and thus stands in contradistinction to damages, a 
traditional legal remedy. Second, as specific relief, the 
equitable lien fits squarely within this Court's definition in 
Bowen of relief “other than money damages." 5 U.S.C. 702. 
The equitable lien does not substitute for a loss, instead it 
gives the claimant the “very thing" to which it is entitled. 
Bowen, 487 U.S. at 896. This is especially apparent as 
regards equitable liens on construction balances; the lien is 
limited to retained funds, if any, held by the owner 
Damages, by contrast, would provide a judgment executable 


> 


against the owner's general assets. Third, the equitable lien 
is limited. Respondent is not asking for the government to 
pick up the tab whenever the prime contractor was supposed 
to pay, but did not. That would be damages. Rather, 
Respondent seeks to be paid from remaining funds, if any. If 
no funds remain, then there is nothing to lien. In this regard, 
Respondent seeks nothing more than the ability--which 
sureties have had for decades--to enforce payment from 
remaining contract monies. The plain language of Section 
702 gives Respondent that right. 


Try as it might, Petitioner is unable to demonstrate 
that an equitable lien constitutes damages. Applying Buwen's 
test, Petitioner argues that giving Blue Fox money would 
substitute for its loss. But Petitioner's substitutionary 
arguments are directed toward theories Respondent is noi 
advancing. Blue Fox does not seek to impose liability for 
deleting the Miller Act bond requirement, however lawless 
that may have been. Nor does Respondent seek money as a 
substitute for what Verdan failed to pay; under that theory, 
Blue Fox should be made whole, irrespective of the content 
of the fund. Finally, Petitioner argues that the liened fund 
"no longer exists,” and an award of money from elsewhere 
must be damages. But that overlooks settled law that the 
government has the obligation as stakeholder to pay the 
proper party; it runs the risk of paying twice when 
disregarding valid claims. Moreover, the government cannot 
pay out money after a party has filed suit to enjoin transfer. 
Pending lawsuits cannot be so easily disposed of 


Perhaps recognizing that an equitable lien differs from 
damages, Petitioner directs attention away from the 
unambiguous language of the statute, and toward an 
Appropriations Clause argument. But payment of an 
equitable lien does not raise an Appropriations Clause issue. 
First, an equitable lien is payable from the contract balance, 


thus a new appropriation is not called for. Second, 
Petitioner's reliance on this Court's decision in OPM v. 
Richmond, 496 U.S. 414 (1990) is misplaced. In Richmond, 
a statute forbade payments to persons in Mr. Richmond's 
income level. Invoking the Appropriations Clause, this Court 
refused to force the government to pay a claimant otherwise 
statutorily ineligible. By contrast, no statute prohibits the 
government from paying Respondent here. On the contrary, 
Congress (through its accounting arm the General Accounting 
Office) has encouraged such payments. So has the Court of 
Claims. Third, since a lienable fund has been identified, this 
case does not present the broader appropriations issues 
Petitioner suggests. This Court need not decide here all the 
types of equitable claims section 702 allows. 


Petitioner also argues that the APA was never intended 
to permit this type of suit. The government complains that 
neither the Ninth Circuit nor Respondent ever identified the 
precise “agency action” being challenged, However, until its 
brief on the merits to this Court, the Army never asked for an 
explanation. Since this Court ordinarily does not consider 
issues not raised or resolved below, Petitioner's “agency 
action” argument should not be considered. In any event, the 
Army's failure to honor Blue Fox's request for “suspension or 
reduction” in payments to Verdan, falls squarely within the 
definition of “agency action” as contained in 5 USC. 
551(13). Even if it did not, APA review would be proper. 
Congress intended Section 702 to waive immunity for 
challenges to al/ illegal governmental conduct, not just that 
falling within the technical definition of “agency action” in 
the APA. 


Petitioner also argues that, in any event, the Tucker 
Act impliedly forbids the Section 702 waiver. But the plain 
language of section 702 states that “Nothing herein * * * 
confers authority to grant relief if any other statute shat grants 


9

2. An Equitable Lien Falls Outside Bowen's 
Definition Of Damages 


In Bowen, the state of Massachusetts sought to compel 
the Secretary of Health and Human Services to make 
reimbursement for certain Medicaid-related expenditures. 
This Court determined the relief to be “other than money 
damages,” and upheld Section 702 jurisdiction. The Court 
drew a distinction between damages, which are given to 
substitute for a suffered loss, and specific remedies, which 
“are not substitute remedies at all, but an attempt to give the 
plaintiff the very thing to which he was entitled.” Bowen, 
487 US. at 895. In so holding, the Court stated that "(t]he 
fact that a judicial remedy may require one party to pay 
money to another is not a sufficient reason to characterize the 
relief as ‘money damages.” /d., at 893. 


Applying Bowen's test for damages, the equitable lien 
does not “substitute” for a loss. /d., at 895. Rather, as "a 
charge which will be enforced in equity against specific 
property,” McClintock, supra, § 118 at 319, the claimant is 
given “the very thing to which it ts entitled." Bowen, 487 
U.S. at 895. See also Armstrong v. United States, 364 US. 
40, 44 (1960) (recognizing that subcontractors holding liens 
"were entitled to resort to the specific property for the 
satisfaction of their claims."). Granted, the equitable lien 
results in the payment of money. See Resiatemeni (Third) 
of Suretyship and Guaranty, p. ||, cmt. b ("Where the 


17 


equitable lien is on a fund, for example a bank deposit, it is 
enforced by a direction to pay the claimant out of the fund."). 
However, "(t]he fact that a judicial remedy may require one 
party to pay money to another is not a sufficient reason to 
characterize the relief as 'money damages. Bowen, 487 US. 
at 893. See also 3 D. Dobbs, supra, at 9 ("[i]f the theory for 
the claims is ‘restitution’ based upon the defendant's unjust 
enrichment, then the recovery is not ‘damages’ even if, in 
dollar amount, it is equivalent to a compensatory damages 
recovery ."). 


Moreover, in the case of equitable liens on 
construction balances, the lien is limited to retained funds, if 
any, held by the owner, see 3 D. Dobbs 471, supra, and 
cannot exceed the amount of funds held by the landowner. 
Id. Thus, if insufficient funds remain, the subcontractor is 
out of luck; he is not made “whole,” since the lien “is not a 
claim against the landowner's general assets." /d. In such 
situations, the unpaid subcontractor is left uncompensated. 
This stands in distinction to “damages,” where the claimant 
receives a compensatory award enforceable by execution or 
garnishment. D. Dobbs, Handbook on the Law of Remedies, 
Sections 1.3, 10-11; 3.1, 135 (1973) [hereinafter D. Dobbs). 
This limitation also underscores that the claimant is awarded 
specific relief, in a defined fund.
