REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 


The Solicitor General asks this Ceurt to review a decision of 
the court of appeals that resolves an issue of first impression in the 
federal courts. He cites no other case — not one — that even 
discusses the question whether the United States is immune from a 
subcontractor’s suit to enforce an equitable lien under the 
Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 702, and this Court’s 
decision in Bowen v. Massachusetts, 487 U.S. 879 (1988). Since 
there are no other cases that decide (much less address) this issue, 
there necessarily is no conflict in the lower courts that might justify 
this Court’s intervention. The best that the government can do is 
to point to two decisions of other circuits that, the government 
says, have given somewhat different interpretations to Bowen in 
the ten years since Bowen was decided. But this Court ordinarily 
does not grant review to resolve abstract differences in how the 
courts of appeal have interpreted one of this Court’s cases, in the 
absence of a true conflict. Here, that hesitation makes especially 
good sense — the alleged conflict in approach is illusory; it has 
arisen only rarely in the past decade; and the government has 
waived, in the courts below, the alternative interpretations of Bowen 
it now advances to this Court. Under these circumstances, the 
question whether the court of appeals properly applied Bowen to 
this novel situation plainly does not merit this Court’s attention. 
See S. Ct. Rule 10 (“A petition for certiorari is rarely granted when 
the asserted error consists of . . . the misapplication of a properly 
stated rule of law.’’) 


Perhaps recognizing this problem, the government labors (as 
Cid the dissent in the court of appeals) to portray the consequences 


4 


of the court of appeals’ holding as dire and far-reaching. That too 
is mistaken. As the government acknowledges (Pet. App. 4a-5Sa), 
Congress passed the Miller Act, 40 U.S.C. §§ 270a-270d, in order 
to give subcontractors such as respondent Blue Fox the right to 
proceed in the government’s shoes in a suit on the bond against a 
defaulting primary contractor. Had the government complied with 
the requirements of the Miller Act, this lawsuit would never have 
been brought (and the immunity issue would never have been 
raised). Thus, it is fair to say that the novel issue presented in this 
case arises only when the United States fails to comply with federal 
law. For that reason, the government has the power — merely by 
complying with federal law — to make this issue go away and to 
reduce to zero or almost zero the fiscal impact of the Ninth Circuit’s 
decision. There is simply no good reason for this Court to expend 
its limited resources on resolving an issue that the Executive Branch 
can moot merely by complying the its legal obligations under the 
Miller Act.

A. The Decision Below Is Further Limited To The Rare 
Instance Where the Miller Act Bonding Scheme Has Failed. 


The purpose of the Miller Act “is to protect those who would 
have materialmen’s and workmen’s liens under state law if they 
were not working on a structure exempt as a federal public work 
or building.” Pet. App. 3a, quoting United Bonding Ins. Co. v. 
Catalytic Constr. Co., 533 F.2d 469, 473 (9th Cir. 1976). 


The government has conceded that a Miller Act bond should 
have been furnished, but was not in this case. Pet. App. 3a. Had a 
bond been furnished, Blue Fox would have been paid by the Miller 
Act surety, and this case would never have arisen. Indeed, it was 
only through misfeasance by government officials that this case 
ever arose. See Statement, pp. 2-3, supra. A case which, by 
definition, arises only when federal officials fail their duty under 
law, cannot be said to present a situation likely to recur. This Court 
should not expend its limited resources to resolve a question that 
arises only when the federal government fails to comply with federal 
law. 


B. Review May Have the Inadvertent Effect of Disturbing 
Established Commercial Practices Based Upon This Court’s 
Decision in Pearlman. 


If this Court grants certiorari, an issue will be whether an 
equitable lien on these facts constitutes “money damages” or 
“specific relief.” To answer that question, the parties and the Court
