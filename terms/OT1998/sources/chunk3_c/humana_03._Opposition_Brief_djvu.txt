OPINION BELOW 


The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals’ decision in 
this case is reported as Forsyth v. Humana, Inc., 114 
F.3d 1467 (9th Cir. 1997) (withdrawing and 
superseding its own decision in Forsyth v. Humana, 
Inc. , 99 F.3d 1504 (9th Cir. 1996)). The District 
Court’s opinion, also entitled Forsyth v. Humana, Inc., 
is found at 827 F. Supp. 1498 (D.Nev. 1993). The 
decisions of both courts are included in the petition 
for certiorari of the Petitioners. 


JURISDICTION 


review was entered by a panel of the Ninth Circuit 
Court of Appeals on May 23, 1997. Humana, Inc., and 
Humana Health Insurance of Nevada, Inc., 
(hereinafter, "Petitioners") submitted a Petition for 
Writ of Certiorari to this Court on August 18, 1997. 
This Court has jurisdiction to review that decision as 
stated therein by Petitioners. Respondents bring this 
Opposition to Humana’s Petition for Writ of 
Certiorari pursuant to Rule 15 of the Rules of the 
Supreme Court of the United States. 


The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals exercised 
jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. The district court 
had jurisdiction over this Class Action pursuant to 28 
U.S.C. § 1331.

concede is beneficially interested. 


Humana Insurance sold employee group health 
care insurance policies to the class. The typical policy 
was based on an 80%/20% co-pay system by which

The Respondents consist of two classes of 
insureds: those who made only health insurance 
premium payments (Premium Payors), and those who 
also made co-payments for medical treatment (Co-

ERISA, RICO, and Section 2 of the Sherman Act. 
Although the district court granted summary judgment 
on several aspects of these claims, the pre-emption of 
RICO by the McCarran-Ferguson Act is the only 
subject of Humana’s Petition and this Opposition. 


The Humana Defendants moved the district 
court for summary judgment on the ground that 
application of RICO to the fraudulent billing practices 
alleged here was pre-empted by Section 2 of the 
McCarran-Ferguson Act, arguing that the plaintiff 
class was limited to state remedies and that no state 
remedy existed for their damages. The district court 
embraced the defendants’ argument, agreed that all 
four prongs of the McCarran-Ferguson test were 
satisfied, and granted the defendants’ motion. 


The Ninth Circuit reversed based upon the 
disharmony of the district court’s decision with well- 
reasoned precedent of this Court and other courts of

violation of the McCarran-Ferguson Act because each 
Statutory scheme permitted a plaintiff to recover for 
his or her injuries.

circuit court decisions reached conclusions contrary to 
that of the Ninth Circuit panel in the instant case and 
this asserted conflict requires this Court to resolve the 
issue. In reality, there is no conflict or minimal 
conflict. However, in the event that this Court rejects 
Respondents’ arguments and elects to hear this case, 
Respondents have filed, concurrently herewith, in the 
alternative and under separate cover, a Conditional 
Cross-Petition for Writ of Certiorari pursuant to Rule

REASONS FOR DENYING 
HUMANA’S PETITION 


Humana represents that an inter-circuit split in 
the treatment of the pre-emption of RICO claims by 
the McCarran-Ferguson Act so cleaves the law in this 
regard that reconciliation by this Court is necessary. 
This is simply not true. 


The inter-circuit split identified by Humana 
springs solely from a recent misinterpretation of 
precedent by the Sixth and Eighth Circuits. Only two

Court’s pre-emption decisions. Hence, this purported

Petitioner first cites Loe v. Norwest Bank Minn., 
107 F.3d 1297, 1306-08 (8th Cir. 1997). The Doe court 
mistakenly adopted a broad reading of the fourth 
prong of the McCarran-Ferguson Act (MFA) and 
found an impairment because “the extraordinary 
remedies of RICO would frustrate, even supplant, [the 
state’s] carefully developed scheme of [insurance] 
regulation," by permitting the recovery of treble 
damages and attorneys fees in a private action where 
state law only provided for administrative relief. This 
decision ignores clearly contrary precedent of this 
Court as well as the legislative intent and express 
language of the RICO statutes. 


Since the inception of the RICO statutes, the 
Congress, this Court and the Courts of Appeal have 
consistently required that RICO, a remedial statute, be 
broadly interpreted. Sedima S.P.R.L. v. Imrex Co., 473 
U.S. 479, 491 n.16, 497-98 (1985); United States v. 


Ginsburg, 773 F.2d 798, 802 (7th Cir. 1985) (quoting 
Pub. L. No. 91-452, § 904(a), 84 Stat. 947, and stating

the mature, final word of the circuit.

(1986). In contrast, Congress intended -- and the 
courts repeatedly reinforce -- that the MFA must be 
narrowly construed.* Doe’s broad construction of the 
MFA and narrow application .of RICO is therefore 
contrary to established precedent. 


Additiona'®, the Doe holding flies in the face 
of this Court’s general pre-emption jurisprudence by 
ignoring the parallels between federal pre-emption of 
state laws and state pre-emption of federal laws. “The 
MFA establishes a form of inverse pre-emption. 
Accordingly, basic principles governing federal pre- 
emption of state law are germane to the analysis of 
MFA pre-emption. See Merchants Home Delivery 
Serv., Inc. v. Frank B. Hall & Co., Inc., 50 F.3d 1486, 
1492 (9th Cir. 1995), cert. denied sub nom Brometheus 
Funding Corp. v. MHDS, Inc., 116 S. Ct. 418 (1995); 
American Family, 978 F.2d at 296. This Court’s 
decisions unequivocally reflect the clear teaching that

Additional and different yet non-conflicting remedies 


are repeatedly held to be not pre-empted. 


Silkwood v. Kerr-McGee, 464 U.S. 238, 104 S. 
Ct. 615, 78 L. Ed. 2d 443 (1984) is the quintessential 
case in this regard. In Silkwood, this Court observed 
that the proper approach to pre-emption was to 
consider “whether there is an irreconcilable conflict 
between the federal and state standards or whether 
the imposition of a state standard in a damages action 
would frustrate the objectives of the federal law." /d. 
at 256. That approach in hand, this Court squarely 
upheld the concurrent imposition of federal fines and 
state punitive damages for the same tort, rejecting the 
related argument that the award of punitive damages 
for radiation hazards was barred because it conflicted 
with the federal remedial scheme, and finding no 
conflict between the federal and state laws.° 


Subsequent opinions of this Court consistently 
follow the course set by Silkwood, and have found pre- 
emption inapplicable to the concurrent assessment of 
federal fines and state fraud damage awards for 
cigarette advertising disclosure violations, Cipollone v.

damages), and medical safety breaches. Hillsborough 
County v. Automated Medical Laboratories, Inc. , 471 
U.S. 707, 105 S. Ct. 2371, 85 L. Ed. 2d 714 (1985). 
Thus, this Court has established beyond question that 
where “state and federal rules [ ] are substantially 
identical but differ in penalty, [they] do not conflict 
with or displace each other." American Family, 978 
F.2d at 297. Accordingly, the only appropriate holding 
on the effect of the MFA on RICO’s application to 
health care fraud is that offered by the Ninth Circuit 
in Merchants and the instant case. 


The Doe decision is also at odds with the 
legislative purpose reflected in the text of the RICO 
statutes. Doe holds that RICO cannot be applied 
consistently with state insurance laws under the MFA. 

"Congress[, however,] enacted RICO in order to 
supplement, not supplant the available remedies since 
it thought those remedies offered too little protection 
for the victim.” Haroco, Inc. v. American Nat’l Bank & 
Trust Co. of Chicago, 747 F.2d 384, 392 (7th Cir. 1984), 
aff'd, 473 U.S. 606 (1985). RICO’s sanctions and 
those incorporated by other laws federal and state are, 
according to the RICO text, to exist side by side. Pub. 
L. No. 91-452, § 904(a), 84 Stat. 947 (1970); United 
States v. Turkette, 452 U.S. 576, 586 n.9, 101 S. Ct. 
2524, 69 L. Ed. 2d 246 (1981) (stating that states are 
free to exercise power "to the fullest ’extent’"). The 
existence of such cumulative remedies furthers their 
remedial purposes. Herman & McLean v. Huddleston, 


10 


459 U.S. 375, 386, 103 S}. Ct. 683, 74 L. Ed. 2d 548 
(1983). Any holding thaat RICO’s civil sanctions impair 
state law is, therefore, scquarely inconsistent with 
RICO’s express text. 


The second case cited by Petitioner is Kenty v. 
Bank One, Columbus, N/.A., 92 F.3d 384 (6th Cir. 
1996). Kenty is wrongly and inadequately reasoned 
and it also ignores the reelevant case law in the area. 
The Kenty opinion is dewoid of any analysis of 
Merchants or American lFamily, the two most highly 
cited circuit cases on MIFA pre-emption. Kenty’s only 
reference to Merchants notes only its 
holding regarding the third prong of the MFA, 
irrelevant to the resolutiion of the issue at hand. 


Although the Kernty court notes its own decision 
in Nationwide Mut. Ins. ‘Co. v. Cisneros, 52 F.3d 1351 
(6th Cir. 1995), cert. demied, 116 S. Ct. 973 (1996), it 
mischaracterizes the holiding of the case as protective 
of state laws, when the ‘Cisneros court actually 
concluded, in accordancee with Merchants and the 
’ Ninth Circuit panel in thhe instant case, “that the 
presence of additional reemedies" in a federal law does 
not impair the state insuurance law at issue. Cisneros, 
52 F.3d at 1363. 


Moreover, Kenty may not be sufficiently 
factually analogous to thhe instant case to qualify as 
conflicting. The Kenty ccourt based its conclusion 
partly on its observation, that "Ohio law may employ a 
different standard than ‘the one under RICO to 
determine when" the staatute is violated. Kenty, 92 F.3d 
at 392. If this is the caSse, the decision is based not on 
RICO’s upset of the baldance of the state law and its

intended purpose, but on a direct conflict between the 
state and federal statutes, and it therefore does not 
decide the issue raised by this case. See Dornberger v. 
Metropolitan Life Ins. Co., 961 F. Supp. 506, 518 n.6 
(S.D.N.Y. 1997) (rejecting the Eighth Circuit “upset 


and permitting RICO and state insurance fraud claims 
to co-exist despite their different penalties.)’ 
Accordingly, as no real inter-circuit conflict 


exists that warrants this court’s resolution, Humana’s 
Petition for Writ of Certiorari must be denied. 


’ The district court was simply wrong when it held, without
