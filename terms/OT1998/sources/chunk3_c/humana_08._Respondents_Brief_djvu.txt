STATUTES AT ISSUE 


This case concerns the Racketeer Influenced and 
Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO) 18 U.S.C. § 1951 et 
seq., and Section 2(b) of the McCarran-Ferguson Act 
(MFA), 15 U.S.C. § 1012(b). Relevant portions of RICO 
and the MFA are included within Appendix C of Humana's 
Petition for Writ of Certiorari.

B. Procedural History 


Respondents accept the procedural history as 
presented by Petitioners. 


'? The excessive hospital co-payment reduced hospital 
usage in two fundamental ways. First, by over-charging 
Co-Payors, Humana made the co-payment charges for 
specific services proportionately greater for each 
prospective co-payment. For example, if a hysterectomy 
costs $2,500, Humana's co-payment charge was 20% of 
$2,500 or $500. If the 89% discount is applied to the co- 
pay and retro-actively refunded, a hysterectomy would only 
have cost $275 with a $55 co-pay. By demanding $500 
instead of $55 as the co-payment charge, Co-Payors were 
discouraged from using medical benefits. Second, the co- 
pay over-billing took away monies from the Co-Payors that 
were otherwise available for additional hospital treatment. 
In the hysterectomy example, if the $55 co-payment based 
on the discount had been charged instead of $250, the Co- 
Payor would have almost $200 left available for further 
medical treatment. Even moderate over-charges to Co- 
payors greatly reduced benefit usage. Lacey v. Cohen, 
596 F. Supp. 1010, 1018 (E.D. Penn. 1984) (concluding 
that “imposition of recipient copayment decreases the 
overall utilization of medicaid services. ... . requiring 
individuals to meet even a nominal assessment for 
medically necessary goods and services forces those 
individuals to economize, and sometimes to forego 
treatment”), aff'd, 774 F.2d 1152 (3 Cir. 1985) (emphasis 
added).

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The McCarran-Ferguson Act (“MFA”) preempts the 
application of federal laws of general applicability to the 
business of insurance only when such federal laws 
“invalidate, impair, or supersede” state insurance law." 
The interpretation and application of this phrase has created 
a rift between the circuits — one faction finding MFA 
preemption triggered only by a “direct conflict” between 
federal and state law, and the other deeming federal law 
preempted merely by potential upset to the balance of a 
state enforcement scheme (i.e. “inverse field preemption” 
given state law is preempting federal law). 


As this Court recently summarized in English v. 
General Electric Company, 496 U.S. 72, 78-79 (1990), 
preemption may take one of three (3) forms: field 
preemption, express preemption, or direct conflict 
preemption. Respondents contend that the simple legal 
issue before the Court is whether direct conflict preemption 
or field preemption applies. Humana seeks to avoid proper 
preemption analysis by arguing that the MFA is not 


° Courts generally apply a four part test to determine 
whether the MFA operates to preclude the application of a 
federal law not expressly addressed by the MFA. If 1) the 
federal statute does not “specifically relate” to the business 
of insurance; 2) the challenged acts constitute the business 
of insurance; 3) the state has enacted laws to address the 
challenged acts; and 4) the application of the federal law 
would “invalidate, impair, or supersede” the state law, then 
the federal law may not be employed. Department of the 
Treasury v. Fabe, 508 U.S. 491, 515 (Kennedy, J., 
dissenting). Only the fourth prong is at issue in this case.

B. Federal Rico Does Not Conflict with Nevada’s 
Insurance Or Racketeering Laws 


A direct conflict exists between state and federal 
law where “it is impossible for a private party to comply 
with both state and federal requirements,” see English, 496 
U.S. at 79 (citing Florida Lime & Avocado Growers, Inc., 
373 U.S. at 142-43), or where one law “stands as an 
obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full 
purposes and objectives of” the legislative body adopting 
the other. /d., (quoting Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 
67 (1941)). RICO does not conflict with Nevada’s 
insurance regulatory system because an insurer can -- and 
should -- simultaneously comply with federal RICO and 
with the Nevada insurance regulations, as well as all other 
aspects of Nevada law, including Nevada’s racketeering 
statutes. The application of federal RICO’s civil remedial 
provisions erects no barrier to the full enforcement of 
Nevada's regulatory remedies by the State Insurance 
Commissioner, or by Nevada’s Attorney General. This lack 
of direct conflict is evident under the precedent of this 
Court because of the harmonious policies of the state and 
federal system, the peaceful coexistence of their cumulative 
remedies, and the presence of multiple state remedies 
applicable and actually applied to the exact same fraudulent 
conduct.
