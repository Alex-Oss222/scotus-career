                  STATEMENT OF THE CASE

   Respondent Tsui Yuan Tseng sued EL AL in the Supreme
Court of the State of New York, New York County. She
alleged assault, false imprisonment and property damage in
connection with a security screening of plaintiff conducted by
EL AL prior to her travelling on an EL AL flight to Israel. EL
AL removed the case to federal court under 28 U.S.C. §§ 1331
and 1441 because EL AL is a “foreign state” within the mean-
ing of 28 U.S.C. § 1603. Following a non-jury trial, the dis-
trict court dismissed plaintiff's personal injury claims and
awarded damages with respect to her baggage claim. The
court of appeals reversed the dismissal of the personal injury
claims, affirmed the property damage award without comment
and remanded the case to the district court to address the
issue of recovery for plaintiff's personal injury claims under
state law.

A. Nature of the Case

   On May 22, 1993, plaintiff arrived at the EL AL terminal at
John F. Kennedy International Airport in New York to travel
on EL AL flight 008 to Israel. Upon presenting her ticket and
passport to a security guard, plaintiff was admitted into the
EL AL terminal. Plaintiff then approached the security area
and, in accordance with standard EL AL pre-boarding pro-
cedures, was questioned about her destination and travel
plans. Based on plaintiff's responses to these questions, she
was considered a “high risk” passenger with an “illogical
story.” Plaintiff was escorted into a private EL AL security
room where her baggage ard person were searched. EL AL
was searching for “[e]xplosives in any size, detonators, . . .
from small, like the cap of the pen, and. . . up from that.”
Defendant did not, at any time, use force to question or search
plaintiff and plaintiff did not, at any time, object to the
search.
   The parties do not dispute that plaintiff was in the course of
embarkation of her “international transportation” by EL AL
when she allegedly sustained her emotional injuries.

   Plaintiff testified that she discovered items were missing
from her carry-on baggage during the flight and from her
checked baggage when she arrived in Tel Aviv, but does not
dispute that it was forty (40) days after her flight when she
first notified EL AL in writing the’ various items in her bag-
gage were missing or had been damaged, and when she first
ever claimed that she had been “badly treated” during the
security screening procedure. Plaintiff testified that she had
made a telephone call to EL AL in Israel to make a complaint
about her baggage, but that she was told to make her com-
plaint in the United States.

B. Proceedings Below

   The Complaint seeks damages arising out of the alleged
assault and false imprisonment of plaintiff and for lost and/or
damaged property. Although the Complaint alleges that the
pre-boarding search was performed in connection with plain-
tiff’s flight to Israel, it does not make reference to the Warsaw
Convention or assert any rights thereunder.

   EL AL timely removed the action to the district court on
the basis that EL AL is a foreign state within the meaning of
28 U.S.C. § 1603.

   A non-jury trial was held March 7, 8 and 11, 1996 before

the Honorable Louis L. Stanton of the United States District
Court for the Southern District of New York.

   At the conclusion of the trial, Judge Stanton ruled, in accor-
dance with his decision in Shen v. Japan Airlines, 918

F. Supp. 636 (S.D.N.Y.), aff'd mem, 43 F.3d 1459 (2d Cir.

1994), that: (1) EL AL’s allegedly offensive security search
of plaintiff Tseng constituted an “accident” under Article 17;
(2) although EL AL’s security suspicions may have been
                                       6

mistaken, there was no evidence of wilful misconduct; and (3)
plaintiff is not entitled to any recovery for her emotional dis-
tress injuries because she sustained no bodily injury. App.
27a-28a.

   The district court also ostensibly applied the Warsaw Con-
vention to plaintiff’s property damage claim. Although the
district court noted that plaintiff failed to timely submit writ-
ten notice of her claim to EL AL, as required by Article 26 of
the Convention, the court concluded that EL AL’s alleged
instruction to plaintiff that she should make a claim in the
United States (presumably rather than advising plaintiff to put
her claim in writing) constituted fraud, negating the Article
26 written notice requirement. App. 30a-31a.

   Plaintiff appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for
the Second Circuit pursuant to 28 U.S.C. §§ 2106 and 2107,
seeking to reverse the dismissal of plaintiff’s emotional dis-
tress claims. EL AL cross-appealed pursuant to 28 U.S.C.
§ 1291, seeking to vacate the property damage award on the
basis that the award improperly failed to uphold the fraud
standard expressly prescribed by Article 26(4) of the Con-
vention.

   On appeal, the Second Circuit held that: (1) plaintiff’s per-
sonal injuries were not the result of an “accident” within the

meaning of Article 17 of the Convention; (2) the Warsaw

Convention does not exclusively govern ail of plaintiff’s
claims, i.e., the Convention arplied to plaintiff’s property
damage but not her personal injury claims; and (3) plaintiff is
entitled to see’ recourse under state law for her personal
injury claims. [he decision, entered June 13, 1997, reversed
and remanded the District Court judgment with respect to
plaintiff’s personal injury claims and affirmed without com-
ment the property damage award. A motion to recall and stay
the mandate (issued July 8, 1997) was granted September 8,
1997.
       REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION

   Certiorari should be granted to resolve a direct conflict
among the Second, Third and Fifth Circuit Courts of Appeals
as to whether the Warsaw Convention, a treaty of the United
States, exclusively governs and precludes state law causes
of action for a passenger’s injuries sustained in the course
of “international transportation” if the injuries were not
caused by an “accident” within the meaning of Article 17 of
the Convention.

   At issue is the exclusive applicability of the Warsaw Con-
vention to personal injury claims sustained in the course of
“international transportation” by air which are not caused by
an “accident” under Article 17. In this case, the court below
concluded that, because the security search of passenger
Tseng did not constitute an “accident” (App. 12a), the injuries
arose “outside” the Convention even though plaintiff indis-
putably was in the course of embarkation. App. 23a. The
court below thus was able to conclude that plaintiff’s state
law claims are not precluded by the Warsaw Convention. /d.

   The basic error of the court below was its determination
that the Convention does not apply if a passenger’s alleged
injuries are not caused by an Article 17 “accident.” The deci-
sion begins with the faulty premise that _Article 17 must
“apply” for the Convention to govern a passenger’s claims,
fails to read the express language of the Warsaw Convention
as a whole and rests on a mistaken understanding of the his-
torical basis for the Convention.

   It is important to resolve the issues raised in this petition
because of the clear and present conflict which exists among

the Circuits. See Tseng v. EL AL Israel Airlines, Ltd. (Second

Circuit); Potter v. Delta Air Lines,’ (Fifth Circuit); Abramson

v. Japan Airlines Co. Ltd.,* (Third Circuit); see also Krys v.

CERTIORARI SHOULD BE GRANTED TO RESOLVE
A DIRECT CONFLICT AMONG DECISIONS OF THE
CIRCUIT COURTS OF APPEALS AND TO ENFORCE

     THE EXPRESS LANGUAGE OF A TREATY TO
        WHICH THE UNITED STATES IS A PARTY

   The Court has granted certiorari in the past to ensure the
correct interpretation and application of the Warsaw Con-
vention. Chan v. Korean Air Lines, 490 U.S. 122 (1989).’ The
Court should grant certiorari in this case to provide a clear
pronouncement as to the scope of the Convention. Just as an
“adequate notice” requirement could not properly be read into
the Convention based on the premise that courts have gener-
ally read such a requirement into the Convention (Chan, 490
U.S. at 134 n. 5), a right of recourse under state law should
not be read into Article 17 based on the premise that some
courts have read such a right into this treaty.

   The Court, in United States v. Belmont, 301 U.S. 324
(1937), recognizing that the provisions of a treaty are exclu-
sive of all local laws on the subject matter dealt with by the
treaty, held:

       In respect of all international negotiations and compacts,
       an® in respect of our foreign relations generally, state
      lines disappear. As to such purposes the State of New
       York does not exist. Within the field of its powers, what-
      ever the United States rightfully undertakes, it neces-
       sarily has warrant to consummate. And when judicial
       authority is invoked in aid of such consummation, state
       constitutions, state laws, and state policies are irrelevant
       to the inquiry and decision. It is inconceivable that any

             The Court's decision in Chan resolved the conflict between the

correct interpretation of Article 3 of the Convention.

       of them can be interposed as an obstacle to the effective
       operation of a federal constitutional power.

301 U.S. at 331-32.

   As Justice Scalia confirr< in Chan, “we must be governed
by the text.” 490 U.S. at | 3... The Court should resolve the
present conflict among the circuits because denial of certio-
rari would permit further judicial intrusion into the subject
matter of this treaty and perpetuate the conflict.

   In 1984, the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit ruled in
Abramson that the district court erred in failing to address
state law causes of action after concluding that the passen-
ger’s injuries “[did] not constitute an ‘accident’ ” under Arti-
cle 17. 739 F.2d at 133. The Court denied Japan Airlines’
certiorari petition.

   Twelve years later, the Court of Appeals for the Fifth
Circuit squarely held in Potter that Article 17 creates the
exclusive cause of action and the exclusive remedy for all
claims arising out of “international transportation” by air.
The Fifth Circuit’s decision in Potter resulted in a direct con-
flict between the Third and Fifth Circuits with respect to the
issue of exclusivity, but the plaintiff in Potter did not seek
certiorari.

   The Fifth Circuit in Potter, unlike the court below (App.
15a), upheld the preemptive scope of Article 24(2). The Fifth
Circuit stated:

       Mrs. Potter thus reads the preemption language in article
       24(2) to prohibit state law causes of action only where a
       claim has been adjudicated an “accident” and deemed
       compensable under the Warsaw Convention. We dis-
       agree.

98 F.3d at 884 (footnotes omitted).

   The decision below reaffirmed the live and direct conflict
between the decisions of the Third and Fifth Circuits as to the

proper interpretation of the Warsaw Convention and affords
the Court a ripe opportunity for review of this important
issue.

   The court below noted that the Supreme Court has not
answered the question whether a plaintiff denied a remedy
under Article 17 of the Convention may pursue recovery for
those injuries under state law. App. 2a. In the absence of such
guidance, the court below elected to apply dictum from its
decision in Jn re Air Disaster at Lockerbie, Scotland on Dec.
21, 1988, 928 F.2d 1267, 1271 (2d. Cir.), cert. denied, 502
U.S. 920 (1991), to conclude that a plaintiff may seek recov-
ery under state law when recovery is not permitted under the
Warsaw Convention. App. 15a. The court below, accordingly,
held that, because plaintiff’s injuries were not the result of an
Article 17 “accident,” she was entitled to seek recovery under
state law. App. 23a.

   The decision below starts with the faulty premise that “the
terms of the Convention do not apply” unless there is an Arti-
cle 17 “accident.” App. 7a; see also App. 8a (“Article 17

D. The Court Should Apply the Rule of Strict Treaty
       Construction to the Written Notice Provisions
       Prescribed By Article 26 of the Convention

   The proper application of the express written notice of
claim provisions set forth in Article 26 also is a significant
issue of treaty interpretation. Despite Article 26(4), which
expressly provides that “no action shall lie against the carrier,
save in the case of fraud on his part,” the court below implic-
itly rewrote the terms of this treaty when it failed to overturn
plaintiff's baggage damage award. The court below should
have addressed the district court's failure to uphold the
express fraud standard necessary to overcome the written
notice of claim provisions prescribed by Article 26.

   It is undisputed that failure to provide a carrier with timely
written notice bars a plaintiff’s claim for damages with
respect to baggage or cargo absent a showing of fraud. Plain-
tiff does not dispute that she did not provide written notice of
her claim to EL AL (alleging that various items in her bag-
gage were missing or had been damaged) until 40 days after
her flight. The court below should have enforced Article
26(4) because the evidence did not rise to the requisite level
of fraud so as to circumvent the Article 26 express written
notice provisions.

    In Chan, Justice Scalia clearly stated:

        We must thus be governed by the text—solemnly
        adopted by the governments of many separate nations—
        whatever conclusions might be drawn from the intricate
        drafting history that petitioners and the United States
        have brought to our attention. The latter may of course

        where the text is clear, as it is here, we have no power to
        insert an amendment. . .

 490 U.S. at 134 (citation omitted).

     In this case, Articles 26(2), (3) and (4) are unambiguous.
 Because plaintiff failed to provide timely written notice of her
 baggage claim. Article 26 permits no recovery from EL AL
 unless plaintiff proves the requisite elements of fraud. Plain-
 tiff Tseng’s trial testimony failed to meet the fraud standard

required by Article 26(4). The Court should apply the rule of

 strict treaty construction to Article 26, as it did in Chan, to
 ensure that the courts of the United States do not engage in
 further judicial treaty making on this issue. Courts should not
 be free to reduce the fraud standard set forth in Article 26(4)
 to avoid its terms. Contrary to the express terms of the War-
 saw Convention, the decision of the court below upholds the
 “right” of every shipper or consignee making a cargo claim
 and every passenger making a baggage claim to disregard a
 clear and unambiguous prescription set forth in this treaty.

     In summary, the court below has incorrectly decided recur-
 ring, important questions of treaty law on which the courts of
 the United States are in disarray. The proper interpretation of
 this treaty, which is a matter of importance to the courts, to
 EL AL and all other international air carriers and to the trav-
 elling public, should be reviewed and settled by the Court in
 order to ensure the uniform and proper application of this
 treaty and to end the conflict among the Second, Third and
 Fifth Circuit Courts of Appeals.
