                   STATEMENT OF THE CASE

      Petitioner advances a single assertion — that the sub-
ject matter claimed in United States Patent No. 5,193,056

(the “ ‘056 Patent”) is the equivalent of abstract mathema-

tics and, therefore, not directed to patentable subject mat-
ter under 35 U.S.C. § 101. The Federal Circuit, however,
properly recognized that the claims of the ‘056 Patent,
when construed in accordance with 35 U.S.C. § 112, { 6,
are directed to one of the explicitly enumerated catego-
ries of statutory subject matter contained in § 101 -
namely, a new and useful machine. State Street Bank and
Trust Company v. Signature Financial Group, Inc., 149 F.3d
1368, 1372, 47 USPQ 2d 1596, 1599-1600 (Fed. Cir. 1998).!
The Federal Circuit also properly rejected the argument
that a new and useful machine is abstract mathematics.
149 F.3d at 1373-74, App. A to Petition at A-5 to A-7. In
reaching its conclusion, the Federal Circuit applied the
plain language of § 101 and the teachings of this Court to
conclude that a claim directed to a new machine that
produces a useful, concrete and tangible result is statu-
tory subject matter.

     The patented invention relates generally to a new
form of investment structure developed by Signature
Financial Group, Inc. (“Signature”). Importantly, State
Street Bank & Trust Company (“State Street”) concedes
that the claimed invention is not the financial concept per
se, but rather a data processing system capable of admin-
istering the structure. See Petition at p. 4. In short, this

       Once the claims were properly construed as a

 machine, the Federal Circuit correctly concluded that the

 clairns were directed to statutory subject matter. First,
Judge Rich,4 writing for a unanimous panel, noted that a
 “machine” is proper statutory subject under the plain
 terms of § 101. 149 F.3d at 1372, App. A to Petition at A-7.
Next, he examined whether the claimed subject matter
fell into any of the narrow, judicially-created exceptions.
In holding that it did not, the Federal Circuit applied the
fundamental principles announced by this Court’s hold-
ings on § 101 together with its own substantial body of
precedent.

         REASONS FOR DENYING THE PETITION

         The Question Presented by State Street Is
     Hypothetical and Was Not Determinative of the

                     Federal Circuit’s Decision
      State Street’s entire argument for certiorari is based
upon a flawed reading of the Federal Circuit’s decision.
State Street’s arguments all .ncorrectly presume that the
Federal Circuit held that the computer application of a

     * It should be noted that Judge Rich was the co-author of
the Patent Act of 1952.
mathematical algorithm to produce     a useful result, with-
out more, satisfies the requirements  of § 101. Because this
fundamental premise is inaccurate,    the petition for writ
of certiorari should be denied.

     The Federal Circuit’s holding itself is crystal clear.
First, the Federal Circuit held that when the claims of the
‘056 Patent are properly construed, they are directed to a
new “machine” and not a “process.” State Street, 149 F. 3d
at 1371-72, App. A to Petition at A-5 to A-7. Next, the
Federal Circuit explained why this particular new
machine is patentable subject matter under § 101.

Today, we hold that the transformation of data,
representing discrete dollar amounts by a
machine through a series of mathematical calcu-
lations_into a final share price, constitutes a
practical application of a mathematical algo-
rithm, formula, or calculation, because it pro-
duces “a usefui, concrete and tangible result” —
a final share price momentarily fixed for record-

ing and reporting purposes and even accepted

and relied upon by regulatory authorities and in
subsequent trades.

149 F.3d at 1373, App. A to Petition at A-10 (emphasis

added).

     The Federal Circuit’s holding is specific and remarka-
bly non-controversial: the application of an algorithm -
the executing software interacting with a general purpose
computer - as part of a new and useful machine - the
data processing system - capable of administrating a
particular financial services configuration, is patentable
subject matter. Rather than challenge the specific holding,
State Street distorts selected dicta to create an issue that
was not before the Federal Circuit. Consequently, the
question posed by State Street is solely an academic ques-
tion and granting the writ would bring before this Court
a hypothetical issue that the Federal Circuit did not
decide.

    The Federal Circuit’s Decision Properly Applied
            the Statute and This Court’s Holdings

     The Federal Circuit properly concluded that a new
and useful machine that solves a practical problem by
producing a concrete and tangible result is patentable
subject matter under § 101. The Federal Circuit correctly
distinguished abstract scientific principles that are not
patentable —- a mathematical formula standing alone -
from claims that apply a scientific principle or algorithm
to otherwise statutory subject matter to produce a new
and useful result - a new machine capable of administer-
ing a financial services configuration. The Federal Cir-
cuit’s decision was instructed by the language of the
statute and this Court’s decisions.

     As this Court has explained, an analysis of patentable
subject matter begins wiih the statute. Diamond v. Diehr,
450 U.S. 175, 182 (1981). Section 101 reads:

      Whoever invents or discovers any new and use-
      ful process, machine, manufacture or composi-
      tion of matter, or any new and useful
      improvement thereof, may obtain a patent there-
      for, subject to the conditions and requirements
      of this title.

35 U.S.C. § 101. This Court noted that the statute con-
tained a simple, concise legislative mandate that a patent
           may issue for “any new and useful process, machine,
           manufacture or composition of matter.” Diehr, 450 U.S. at
           182.

                 Noting the broad nature of the statute, this Court
           observed that Congress intended § 101 to include “any-
           thing under the sun that is made by man.” Diamond v.

            Chakrabarty, 447 U.S. 303, 309 (1980) (quoting S. Rep. No.

           1979, 82d Cong., 2d Sess.5 (1952); H.R. Rep. No. 1923,
           82d Cong., 2d Sess., 6 (1952)). This Court further recog-
           nized that, by casting statutory subject matter in broad
           terms, Congress was fulfilling its duty under the United

             States Constitution. Chakrabarty, 447 U.S. at 315 (“The

           subject-matter provisions of the patent law have been cast
           in broad terms to fulfill the constitutional and statutory
           goal of promoting ‘the Progress of Science and the useful Arts’
           with all that means for the social and economic benefits
           envisioned by Jefferson.”) (quoting U.S. Const. Art. L, § 8,
           cl. 8.) (emphasis added). In recognition of these princi-
           ples, the Federal Circuit explicitly reiterated this Court’s
           warning in construing § 101: “Thus, it is improper to read
           limitations into § 101 on the subject matter that may be
           patented where the legislative history indicates that Con-
           gress clearly did not intend such limitations.” State Street,

             149 F.3d at 1373, App. A to Petition at A-8 to A-9; see also

           Diehr, 450 U.S. at 182 (“[I]n dealing with the patent laws,
           we have more than once cautioned that ‘courts should
           not read into the patent laws limitations and conditions
           which the legislature has not expressed.’ ”).

                 State Street’s entire argument violates these funda-
           mental tenets. State Street does not challenge the Federal
           Circuit’s finding that the claims of the ‘056 Patent are
           directed to a new and useful machine that produces a

rere me
concrete and tangible result. Rather, State Street contends
that this particular new and useful machine is not patent-
able subject matter. In short, if this Court adopts State
Street’s argument, then the language in the statute - “any
new and useful . .. machine” - would not be applied to
any new and useful machine. Rather, this Court would be
creating a brand new statutory exception not justified by
the plain terms of the statute or legislative history.

      The Federal Circuit recognized that this Court has
identified only three categories “of subject matter that are
unpatentable, namely ‘laws of nature, natural phenom-
ena, and abstract ideas.’ “5 State Street, 149 F.3d at 1373,
App. A to Petition at A-9 (quoting Diehr, 450 U.S. at 185).
This Court explained the rationale behind this narrow
limitation:

        “TA] new mineral discovered in the earth or a

       new plant found in the wild is not patentable
       subject matter. Likewise, Einstein cculd not pat-
       ent his celebrated law that E=mc?, nor could
       Newton have patented the law of gravity. Such
       discoveries are manifestations of . . . nature, free
       to all men and reserved exclusively to none.”

 Diehr, 450 U.S. at 185 (quoting Chakrabarty, 447 U.S. at
 309.)

     In applying that rationale to mathematical algo-
rithms, this Court recognized that a mathematical for-
mula may describe a law of nature, a scientific truth or an
abstract idea. Importantly, this Court also recognized that
mathematics may be used to describe steps of a statutory
method or elements of a statutory machine. Id. at 188; see
also Arrhythmia Research Technology, Inc. v. Corazonix Corp.,
958 F.2d 1053, 1056, 22 USPQ 2d 1033, 1035 (Fed. Cir.
1993). The crucial distinction, properly recognized and
applied by Judge Rich below, is whether each claim,
when viewed as a whole, recites a mathematical formula
either in the abstract or as part of an invention, such as a
machine, within § 101. Id. at 192; see also In re Alappat, 33
F.3d at 1545, 31 USPQ 2d at 1558 (“a computer .. . is
apparatus, not mathematics.”).

     Measuring the claims of the ‘056 Patent against the
plain text of § 101 and the fundamental principles in
Diehr and Chakrabarty, the Federal Circuit concluded that
the claims are directed to patentable subject matter. In
reaching its conclusion, the Federal Circuit recognized
the crucial distinction between abstract science that is not
patentable subject matter — pure mathematics — and
applied science to achieve a particular, useful result —
programming a computer to create a new machine to
solve a particular problem. State Street, 149 F.3d at
1373-74, App. A to Petition at A-9 to A-10.

     State Street attempts to avoid this distinction by
improperly focusing solely on the part of the claim that
can be expressed as an algorithm while ignoring other
parts of the claim — such as the “computer processor
means” and the “storage means” —- that clearly define
apparatus to implement a specific functionality. State
                                          11

Street’s approach was rejected by this Court in Diehr: “It
is inappropriate to dissect the claims into old and new
elements and then to ignore the presence of the old
elements in the [§ 101] analysis.” 450 U.S. at 188. As the
Federal Circuit correctly observed, the focus in any statu-
tory subject matter analysis must be on the “claim as a

whole.” Diehr, 450 U.S. at 192; Alappat, 33 F.3d at 1543, 31
USPQ 2d at 1557. When the claims of the ‘056 Patent are

viewed as a whole, they are clearly directed to a new and
useful machine capable of solving a practical problem by
producing a tangible and concrete result. According to
the plain terms of the statute and this Court’s teachings,
such claims are statutory.

      The Federal Circuit also examined its own substantial
body of established precedent to reach the result below.
Specifically, the Federal Circuit examined its precedent
since Diehr in 1981 and applied the holdings and logic in
those cases to the technology and claims involved in the
‘056 Patent.® State Street, 149 F.3d at 1373-75, App. A to
Petition at A-10 to A-13. That precedent clearly confirmed
that claims directed to an admittedly new and useful
machine capable of producing a concrete and tangible
result to solve a practical problem meet the requirements

of § 101.
