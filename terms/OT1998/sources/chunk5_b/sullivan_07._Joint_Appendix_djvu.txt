I. PRELIMINARY STATEMENT 

1. This is a civil rights action brought by named and 
class plaintiffs who had been receiving medical benefits pursuant 
to the Pennsylvania Worker's Compensation Act, 77 P.S. § 1, 
et _seg., and who have been deprived of those benefits. without 
prior notice and opportunity to be heard pursuant to the 
utilization review procedures contained in Sections 306(f.1)(5) 
and (6) of the Pennsylvania Workers’ Compensation Act, as 
amended, 77 P.S. § 531(5) and (6) (Supp. 1994). Plaintiffs 
contend that Section 306(f.1)(5) and (6), which were passed by 
the Pennsylvania General Assembly in July, 1993 as part of a 
larger cost containment measure known as “Act 44", violate the 
Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment and 42 
U.S.C. § 1983. Plaintiffs seek to have Section 306(f.1)(5) and 
(6) declared unconstitutional and further, to enjoin the named 
and class defendants from enforcing, acting or relying upon 
these provisions to withhold workers compensation medical 
benefits from the named and class plaintiffs. Plaintiffs 
additionally seek restoration of all benefits which have been 
withheld as a result of the unconstitutional utilizatior review 


procedures, as well as compensatory and punitive damages. 
Il. UR SUIT LION 


2. This is an action arising under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 
seeking to redress the deprivation under color of state law of 
rights secured to the named and class plaintiffs by the United 
States Constitution. Jurisdiction is therefore predicated upon 28 
U.S.C. § 1343(3) and (4). Jurisdiction is also conferred upon 
this Court by 28 U.S.C. §1331, since this case presents a federal 
question. 

3. Declaratory and injunctive relief is sought pursuant to 
28 U.S.C. §§ 2201-2202. 


4. The named and class plaintiffs have no adequate 
remedy at law and have suffered, and will suffer, immediate and 
irreparable harm from the state action complained of herein, 
unless equitable relief is granted to them.

Vv. STATEMENT OF FACTS 


39. The Pennsylvania Workers’ Compensation Act of June 
2, 1915, P.L. 726, as amended, 77 P.S. § 1 et seg. (Supp. 
1993) (hereinafter referred to as “the Act”), is a statute of 


statewide applicability. Its purpose is to substitute a state- 
regulated insurance system which provides a reasonable 
compensation for injuries to employees arising in the course of 
their employment for the common law rights and liabilities of 
employees and employers. 


40. The Act requires employers to obtain insurance or to 
seek permission from the state to self-insure, so as to guarantee 
the availability of sufficient funds to pay eligible workers’ 
benefits pursuant to a statutory formula. Employers who do not 
self-insure may insure the payment of workers’ compensation 
benefits with the Commonwealth-administered SWIF, or with 
any other insurance company or mutual association authorized 
to insure such liability in the Commonwealth, 77 P.S. § 501. 

41. SWIF is required to insure all risks including those 
rejected or cancelled by private carriers. 

42. The employer and/or insurer are required to promptly 
investigate each industrial injury reported or known to the 
employer, and within 2! days from the date reported, either: 

a) enter into an agreement which requires the 
payment of compensation; 

b) commence the payment of compensation pursuant 
to a Notice of Compensation Payable; or 

c) notify the employee or his dependent on a 
prescribed form that it is the employer’s/insurer’s opinion that 
compensation is not payable. 

43. The Department of Labor and Industry, upon being 
notified by the employer of the employer’s intention to 
controvert the payment of compensatio:, +s required to notify the 
injured employee of his right to file a claim petition with the 
Department of Labor and Industry. Subsequently, the 
Department assigns each claim petition to a state workers’ 
compensation judge. 

44. All agreements and awards may be modified, 
suspended, reinstated, or terminated at any time by an agreement 


19 


or supplemental agreement, as the case may be, if the incapacity 
of the injured employee has increased, decreased, reoccurred, 
terminated, or changed in any other way. 


45. All such agreements and/or supplemental agreements 
must be sent to the Pennsylvania Department of Labor and 
Industry which, through its Bureau of Workers’ Compensation 
(hereafter “the Bureau”), examines such documents to determine 
whether each agreement or supplemental agreement, as the case 
may be, conforms with the provisions of the Act and the rules 
and regulations promulgated thereunder. 


46. In the absence of an agreement or supplemental 
agreement, the employer/insurer must petition the Department 
of Labor and Industry in order to suspend, terminate, reduce or 
otherwise modify any agreement or award of compensation. 
This applies both to wage loss benefits and to medical benefits 
payable under the Act. 


47. As a result of a decision by a three-judge court in 
Baksalary_v. Smith, 579 F. Supp. 218 (E.D. Pa. 1984), 
employers or insurance companies seeking to cease workers’ 
compensation benefits pending litigation over the right to 
terminate, suspend, or reduce benefits, is required to seek a 
discretionary supersedeas from a workers’ compensation judge 
which can be granted or denied after a hearing at which 
recipients have an opportunity to be present and give evidence. 


48. Prior to Baksalary, employers and insurers were 
entitled to automatically cease wage loss and medical benefits 
upon the filing of a petition with the Bureau accompanied by a 
physician’s affidavit of recovery pursuant to Section 413(a) of 


' the Act. 


49. Baksalary declared the automatic supersedeas 
procedure contained in Section 413(a) to be unconstitutional and 
enjoined its further use. 


50. On July 2, 1993 Act 44 was passed by the 
Pennsylvania Legislature, containing several amendments to the 
Pennsylvania Worker’s Compensation Act intended to contain 


20 


workers’ compensation costs. It became effective on August 31, 
1993. 


51. Act 44 contained provisions, now known as Sections 
306(f.1)(S) and (6), for utilization review of the reasonableness 
and necessity of medical treatment, which like the invalidated 
Section 413(a), operates as an automatic supersedeas of workers’ 
compensation medical benefits upon the filing of a petition with 
the Bureau by the insurer or employer. 


52. Like the invalidated Section 413(a), no notice to the 
claimant or his/her attorney is required to be provided to the 
recipient prior to the cessation of benefits. 


53. Like the invalidated Section 413(a), the utilization 
review procedure contained in Section 306(f.1)(5) provides no 
opportunity for the claimant or his/her counsel to be heard prior 
to the cessation of benefits. 


54. Pursuant to Section 306(f.1)(5), an insurer or self- 
insured employer may seek utilization review of the 
reasonableness and necessity of current, prospective, or 
retrospective medical treatment. 


55. There is no penalty for an insurer or self-insured 
employer who invokes the utilization review process 
unreasonably. 


56. The Bureau administers all requests for utilization 
review, acting as a “traffic cop” for the entire process. 


57. Each request for utilization review is made by filing 
a petition with the Bureau on a form designed and approved by 
it. 


58. The Bureau screens all petitions for utilization review 
for conformity with Section 306(f.1)(5) and (6) and assigns them 
to a utilization review organization certified by the Department 
of Labor and Industry. Any petition found not to be in 
conformity is returned to the petitioner with specific instructions. 


21 


59. -The period between the filing for utilization review 
and assignment by the Bureau to a URO may encompass several 
weeks or even months. 


60. At the time of the assignment to the URO, the Bureau 
forwards a Notice of Assignment to the recipient, the employer, 
and the medical provider under review. 


61. Neither the Request for Utilization Review nor the 
Notice of Assignment notify the claimant that pursuant to the 
request or its assignment, payment for his or her medical 
benefits will be stopped pending the review. 


62. The provider under review is given an opportunity to 
discuss the challenged treatment with the URO. 


63. There is no opportunity for the recipient or his counsel 
to be heard by the URO or to submit evidence supporting the 
recipient’s claim for benefits. 


64. The Bureau provides no substantive guidance to the 
URO to define the terms “reasonable and necessary”, thus 
leaving the construction of the standard to be applied to the 
discretion of the individual URO’s. 


65. The URO is responsible for rendering a determination 
within thirty days of receipt of the providers medical records, or 
sixty days of the assignment, whichever occurs first. 


66. This time frame is often not adhered to, causing many 
claimants to wait more than thirty days for a decision during 
which most are without access to medical care due to the 
employer/insurer’s right to supersede payment. 


67. The URO must file its report withthe Bureau, along 
with a Bureau-designed face sheet, and mail copies to all 
involved parties. 

68. A party dissatisfied with the outcome of a utilization 


review may file for reconsideration with the Bureau on a form 
prepared by the Bureau within thirty days of the initial decision. 


22 


69. The Bureau will review the form for compliance with 
the statute, and assign the case for disposition by a URO 
certified by the Department of Labor and Industry. 


70. The period between the filing for reconsideration and 
assignment by the Bureau to a URO may encompass several 
weeks, or even months. 


71. Even if the claimant receives a favorable decision from 
the initial URO, the supersedeas of his/her medical benefits 
remains in effect if the employer requests reconsideration. 


72. Although the claimant receives a copy of the 
employer/insurer’s request for reconsideration, and the Notice 
of Assignment issued by the Bureau, neither document notifies 
the claimant that his/her benefits will be suspended throughout 
the pendency of the request for reconsideration. 


73. During reconsideration, the provider of medical 
services is extended an opportunity to discuss the treatment 
under review, but no opportunity to be heard or to submit 
evidence is afforded the recipient or his counsel. 


74. A claimant who seeks reconsideration unsuccessfully 
is liable for payment of the URO’s expenses. 

75. Likewise, if reconsideration of an initial utilization 
review decision which was favorable to the claimant is sought by 
the insurance company or employer, and the decision is reversed 
on reconsideration, the claimant is liable for payment of the 
URO’s expenses. 


76. Neither the Request for Reconsideration nor the Notice 
of Assignment notify the claimant that he will be liable for the 
URO’s expenses if he does not prevail on reconsideration. 


77. The cost of utilization review at the reconsideration 
level is not fixed by the Bureau, but is left to the discretion of 
the individual URO. The typical charge involves several 
hundred dollars. 


23 


78. There is no in forma pauperis procedure to excuse 
‘indigent claimants from the assessment of the reconsideration 
fee. 


79. The Bureau takes collection action against a claimant 
for the reconsideration fee notwithstanding the claimant’s ability 
or inability to pay. 


80. The fee is assessed against the claimant as the “losing 
party” without any opportunity for the claimant to be heard as 
to the reasonableness and necessity of the medical treatment 
under review. 


81. In the event that a recipient is dissatisfied with the 
URO decision on reconsideration, he/she may file a Petition for 
Review with the Bureau within thirty days on a form prepared 
by the Bureau. 


82. During the pendency of the Petition for Review filed 
by a claimant, the supersedeas of the claimant’s medical benefits 
remains in effect. 


83. If the claimant prevails on reconsideration, the 
supersedeas is lifted, even if the employer files a Petition for 
Review. 


84. The Bureau reviews all Petitions for Review for 
compliance with the statute, and assigns the petition to a 
workers’ compensation judge for a de novo hearing. 

85. A claimant is not eligible for a hearing before a 
workers’ compensation judge unless he/she first seeks 
reconsideration and risks assessment of the utilization review 
fee. 


86. Proceedings before the workers’ compensation judge 
may extend for several months and claimants may await a 
decision for several months or even years. 


87. The Bureau has promulgated no regulations setting 


forth how workers’ compensation judges are to handle petitions 
for review from utilization review reconsideration decisions. 


24 


88. Accordingly, procedures for the determination of 
petitions for review vary widely according to the individual 
discretion of the assigned judge, as to who has the burden of 
proof, what evidence will be allowed, the type and extent of 
hearing provided, whether the utilization review decisions will 
be permitted as evidence or whether the utilization reviewer will 
be permitted to testify, whether the claimant will be required to 
undergo a medical examination, whether depositions will be 
required, and a variety of other procedural matters. 


89. The withholding of medical benefits during the 
utilization review process can be very prolonged, extending 
months or years during completion of the utilization review 
process. 

90. For many workers’ compensation recipients whose 
medical benefits are unilaterally terminated pursuant to Section 
306(f.1)(5) and (6), pain, prolonged recuperation, and/or other 

91. Throughout the utilization process, however long it 
takes, the claimant is not permitted to pay for medical treatment 
him or herself either directly or through medical insurance from 
another source, and is thus precluded from seeking or securing 
medical treatment altogether.
