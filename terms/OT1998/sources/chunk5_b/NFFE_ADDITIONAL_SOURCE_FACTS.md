# NFFE / FLRA v. Interior — additional source facts from the three principal merits briefs

This is an attributed source supplement for independent validation. It does not select the questions to decide, determine preservation, construe current law, approve a neutral packet, or make Justice commitments. Every legal proposition below describes an argument in a party's brief unless expressly identified as the party's report of a procedural event. Authorities invoked by the parties have not been independently verified here or admitted as current law.

Source abbreviations refer to the saved papers in this directory: **FLRA** = `nffe_05._Petitioners_Brief_djvu.txt` (July 1998); **NFFE** = `nffe_06._Petitioners_Brief_djvu.txt` (July 1998); **Interior** = `nffe_07._Respondents_Brief_djvu.txt` (September 1998). All page references are printed pages. All three texts, including footnotes, were read in full. Original papers are preserved in the [Archive NFFE records item](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0155).

## Proposal, procedure and requested relief

1. **Actual proposal.** FLRA pp. 5–6 identifies it as Article 7, section 2.b, Union-initiated Bargaining. Interior p. 6 recites the same operative language. The following text is copied mechanically from Interior's saved text; the line-break hyphen is an OCR-layout feature:

~~~text
The Union may request and the Employer will be 
obliged to negotiate on any negotiable matters not cov- 
ered by the provisions of this agreement.
~~~

   The proposal addresses negotiable matters not covered by the agreement. FLRA's general statutory rule additionally contains a waiver qualification; that waiver language is not written into this particular quoted clause. FLRA pp. 7–8, 34–36; Interior pp. 6, 40. This distinction is a source comparison, not a conclusion that the proposal legally excludes otherwise applicable waiver rules.

2. **Reported administrative basis and posture.** FLRA pp. 5–6, 11–12 reports an unfair-labor-practice charge following the Survey's refusal to negotiate; its order found the proposal substantially identical to a previously negotiable proposal in *Merit Systems Protection Board*, 30 FLRA 852 (1988), and relied on *Department of Energy*, 51 FLRA 124 (1995). It reports two grounds: the clause restates an existing statutory duty, and, independently, a contractual duty can be negotiated even absent a general statutory duty. NFFE pp. 4–7 and Interior pp. 6–7 give materially matching accounts of those grounds. Interior p. 6 identifies sections 7116(a)(1) and (5). Interior p. 7 describes the ordered action as bargaining over the proposal, not adoption of it or an actual Impasses Panel award in this dispute.

3. **Preservation account.** FLRA pp. 17–18 n.5 expressly says that, because the Fourth Circuit's *SSA* decision had rejected a statutory duty, the Authority did not argue in the present Fourth Circuit litigation or *Energy* that the clause was negotiable because it restated such a duty. It nevertheless presses that ground here because it remained a basis of the administrative decision and because the circuit's decisions rested on *SSA*. It invokes predicate-question and prior-presentation principles, and the importance and recurrence of the question. This is the Authority's preservation argument; this supplement does not resolve it. NFFE's questions presented separately ask about the statutory duty and, in any event, the negotiability of a contractual clause (front matter p. i).

4. **Renewed negotiations.** Interior p. 8 n.2 reports that the parties had recently renegotiated their collective bargaining agreement, and had again disagreed about the negotiability of the same midterm bargaining provision. The footnote gives no renegotiation date, replacement contract text, or independent mootness analysis. It does not report abandonment or settlement of the dispute.

5. **Requested relief.** FLRA p. 47 asks reversal and remand to the court of appeals with directions to enforce the Authority's order. NFFE p. 32 asks reversal. Interior p. 47 asks affirmance. None of these conclusions asks this Court itself to impose the proposed clause as a contract term.

## Competing statutory presentations

6. **General duty: petitioners' text argument.** FLRA pp. 18–23 and NFFE pp. 13–17 rely on sections 7114(a)(4), 7103(a)(8) and (12), and related section 7114 provisions as creating a broad, continuing duty without an express temporal exception. NFFE pp. 14–16 specifically treats the statutory definition of collective bargaining agreement as including a midterm agreement, relies on section 7114(b)(3)'s requirement to meet as often as necessary, and cites the plural in section 7114(a)(1). FLRA pp. 21–22 disputes inference of a single comprehensive agreement from the article “a.” Both distinguish limits on negotiable subjects from limits on the circumstances or timing of negotiation.

7. **General duty: Interior's text argument.** Interior pp. 11–19 emphasizes section 7114(a)(4)'s purpose of arriving at a collective bargaining agreement, rather than the broader ongoing bargaining formulation of NLRA sections 8(a)(5) and 8(d). It treats a collective bargaining agreement as a comprehensive term contract, with subsequent arrangements supplementing it rather than constituting independent agreements. Interior pp. 16–17 invokes sections 7118(a)(7)(B) (renegotiation and amendment) and 7111(f)(3) (agreement as a recognition bar), including FLRA's substantial-terms requirement for the latter. Interior p. 14 n.3 treats section 7114(b)(3) as regulating how bargaining occurs once another provision requires it, and the plural in section 7114(a)(1) as successive agreements. These arguments are not limited to the singular article.

8. **Management-change bargaining dispute.** FLRA pp. 22–23 argues that section 7106 reserves management rights, and its subsections (b)(2) and (3) qualify those reservations rather than independently establish the sole circumstances for a midterm duty. NFFE p. 17 n.5 adds that management must bargain over changes outside the enumerated rights, citing parking-fee/procedure litigation. Interior pp. 19–21 treats subsections (b)(2) and (3) as the specific source of the required bargaining about procedures and appropriate arrangements when management changes employment conditions, contrasted with the agency-election language of (b)(1). Interior p. 21 n.9 alternatively discusses a prohibition on unilateral material changes under section 7116(a)(5), arguing that this still would not establish a general union initiative unconnected to a workplace change. Interior pp. 31–33 disputes petitioners' asserted symmetry, saying the change-triggered duty rests on the agency.

9. **NLRA comparison and covered matters.** FLRA pp. 28–34 and NFFE pp. 21–23 invoke the private-sector midterm rule associated with *Jacobs Manufacturing*, but recognize that the private and public schemes are not identical. They treat NLRA section 8(d)'s exception for contractual matters as a limit on a previously broader obligation; NFFE calls the covered-matter principle sufficiently established to explain its absence from the federal text. Interior pp. 12–13, 18–19 draws the opposite inference: the absence of an explicit section 8(d)-type limitation fits the absence of a general midterm duty. **Interior p. 19 n.7 expressly agrees that, if Congress had created an unrestricted midterm right, FLRA would have and would need to exercise authority to carve out a covered-matter exception.** It says the disputed point is Congress's creation of the right, not that contingent administrative authority.

10. **Scope of the Authority's asserted statutory duty.** FLRA pp. 8, 34–36 limits it to negotiable matters not contained in or covered by the term agreement, absent waiver. Its p. 35 n.15 identifies two covered-matter branches: expressly contained matters, and matters inseparably bound up with and plainly an aspect of an expressly covered subject. FLRA p. 35 also treats matters fully explored and discussed in term negotiations as removed from available midterm bargaining, and pp. 35–36 describes agency ability to propose subjects during term negotiations. These are the Authority's descriptions of its case law; they do not establish that any particular unpresented proposal falls within a branch.

11. **Waiver and zipper clauses remain qualified.** FLRA p. 36 describes negotiated zipper clauses that waive midterm bargaining, but n.16 admits the Authority had not decided whether such clauses themselves are mandatory subjects. It relies on two D.C. Circuit judges' concurrence for that further position. NFFE p. 24 n.11 likewise describes agreed waivers. Interior pp. 27–30 stresses the unresolved power to compel acceptance at impasse, challenges the practical likelihood of such waivers, and argues that covered-matter limits might encourage withholding subjects. Thus agreed waiver, mandatory negotiation of a waiver, and compelled adoption of a waiver are separate propositions in the briefing.

## Historical construction and deference arguments

12. **Administrative change and its asserted basis.** FLRA pp. 7–9 reports that *IRS I*, 17 FLRA 731 (1985), rejected the general duty; the D.C. Circuit's *NTEU* decision set it aside; and *IRS II*, 29 FLRA 162 (1987), adopted the court's reasoning and announced the qualified midterm duty. FLRA pp. 19–21 and p. 20 n.9 seeks deference to a permissible construction despite that earlier view. Interior pp. 37–39 separately challenges deference because (a) it considers congressional intent clear, (b) it treats section 7101(b)'s efficiency instruction as an express limiting rule, and (c) it says the change reflected compelled acquiescence in a court ruling rather than an independent agency policy choice. It cites later FLRA decisions that adhered to *IRS II* without reexamining its merits. These are competing accounts of the significance of the same administrative sequence, not an independently established agency finding that the statute compelled only one interpretation.

13. **Executive-order record dispute.** FLRA pp. 26–28 says the Federal Labor Relations Council never decided whether union-initiated midterm bargaining was required under Executive Order 11,491; it characterizes the contrary material as a single unreviewed Assistant Secretary decision in *Army and Air Force Exchange Service*, Case 22-6657(CA), and argues that section 7135(b) allowed later statutory decisions to supersede predecessor rules. FLRA p. 28 n.12 describes *IRS II* as a total repudiation of *IRS I*, including inconsistent prior decisions. NFFE pp. 20–21 n.7 separately cites *Army and Air Force Exchange Service*, 5 FLRC 167 (1977), as clarifying that the disposition did not turn on the agency's no-midterm-duty claim, and cites a treatise's contrary understanding of predecessor practice. Interior pp. 34–36 relies on *IRS I*'s historical findings, the Council's 1975 report concerning agency changes, and the Assistant Secretary ruling; it says appellate counsel's current position does not undo the agency's earlier historical findings. Interior p. 35 n.14 also contests amici's reliance on an FAA parking-award case as not concerning midterm bargaining. This reader did not retrieve those originals or adjudicate the disagreement.

14. **Legislative-history dispute.** FLRA pp. 23–26 and NFFE pp. 18–20 contest reliance on the Senate report because the House compromise became law, expanded the bargaining scheme, and omitted the Executive Order's general efficiency management reservation. They also argue that the cited report does not expressly exclude other circumstances of bargaining. Interior pp. 36–37 quotes a fuller report passage encompassing basic-agreement negotiations and management changes; it argues that the House/Senate differences concerned subjects rather than nature or timing and that the passage reflects the same two circumstances found in the final text. These descriptions are conflicting litigant arguments; the Senate report is not a source of an admitted historical finding here.

## Contractual duty and qualifications requiring retention

15. **Petitioners' independent negotiability argument.** FLRA pp. 37–47 and NFFE pp. 30–32 say lack of a statutory entitlement does not itself prohibit bargaining to obtain a contractual entitlement. They rely on section 7117(a), the statutory exceptions and the travel/per-diem example associated with *BATF*, and distinguish a proposal's desirability from its legal negotiability. FLRA pp. 45–46 argues that an agency's lawful discretion concerning employment conditions ordinarily must be bargained over. Its p. 46 n.20 expressly qualifies that principle where another statute reserves sole and exclusive discretion, or section 7106(a) removes a reserved management right; it says no such express reservation was asserted here. These are reasons for bargaining over the clause, not a claim that bargaining itself obliges the agency to accept it.

16. **Interior's two distinct statutory objections to the clause.** Interior pp. 40–43 argues first that section 7103(a)(14)(C) excludes matters specifically provided for by federal statute: in its view the statute already defines and rejects this general bargaining obligation. Separately, it argues under section 7117(a) that the proposal conflicts with Congress's substantive choice against that regime. It treats that choice as nonwaivable for an agency because it concerns public fiscal and institutional interests. Interior p. 41 n.17 only assumes for argument that section 7117(a) itself affirmatively creates the broad duty petitioners describe; n.18 only assumes for argument that the structure of bargaining meets the threshold of a matter affecting working conditions. Neither assumption is an unconditional concession on the point assumed.

17. **As-drafted limit; narrower clauses reserved.** Interior p. 43 n.19 argues that negotiability must be judged as drafted and is not established by the possibility of later narrowing through bargaining. It then expressly says this case does not present whether a substantially narrower midterm clause, which would not commit the employer to the challenged general obligation, would be negotiable. It attacks the actual clause's general advance obligation, not every possible midterm contractual provision. NFFE pp. 31–32 illustrates experimental work schedules and a proposal at a mutually defined time as reasons a contractual option might aid flexibility; these are examples, not the text of a substituted proposal in this record.

18. **Voluntary bargaining concession and its limit.** Interior pp. 44–45 expressly permits voluntary midterm bargaining on particular issues even absent a management change when both parties agree at that time. It says either may walk away, and neither may compel bargaining to impasse or impose the impasse process. It denies that this freedom entails discretion to bind the agency in advance over the general universe of uncovered matters. FLRA pp. 45–47 disputes that distinction and argues that permissible discretion may be exercised prospectively by contract. Interior p. 46 adds an alternative institutional argument: even if an individual agency had discretion, FLRA could not use impasse procedures systematically to impose the assertedly rejected regime. That alternative is distinct from Interior's primary no-agency-discretion position.

19. **Impasse mechanism: disputed predictions and contingent consequences.** FLRA p. 4 states bargaining does not require agreement or concession, but p. 47 n.21 expressly recognizes that the Panel can impose a term over objection. FLRA pp. 43–44 cites one refusal to impose a midterm clause, and says that if the statutory right were rejected while the clause remained negotiable, the Panel would assess each impasse on its facts rather than treat the term as required by statute. Interior p. 40 n.16 answers that the cited Panel refusal rested on its view that the clause merely duplicated existing rights; it disputes that the example disproves an effective compelled regime. **Interior there also characterizes FLRA p. 44 n.19 as conceding an anomaly, but FLRA's footnote locates its concern in finding the proposal negotiable while leaving the general statutory-duty question unresolved.** The source does not express that concern in the same terms as a decision resolving no statutory duty but negotiability. These are attributed content differences, not an adopted prediction about any future Panel proceeding.

20. **Competing policy evidence is advocacy.** NFFE pp. 26–28 says its search found rare Panel arbitrations and no evidence of the predicted abuse, and gives hypothetical health-hazard and changing-directive examples. Interior pp. 22–31 relies on different bargaining structures, official time, taxpayer costs and an NTEU negotiator's 1989 article describing strategic uses of midterm rights. Interior p. 31 n.13 attacks NFFE's empirical inference: reports may not identify midterm posture, the threat of arbitration can matter without an award, and the circuit conflict may suppress use. Interior pp. 32–33 also argues that existing safety obligations and management-change bargaining answer the hazard examples. None is a finding that this union withheld a proposal, abused bargaining, faced a health emergency, or would inevitably receive a Panel award. The article and treatise references are party-supplied context, not legal authority.

## Exact limits of this supplement

The actual proposed clause and the relief sought are recovered. The briefs supply competing accounts on preservation, administrative construction, predecessor practice, statutory scope and impasse consequences. This supplement does not decide those disputes. It adds no assumed contractual term, future proposal, actual impasse award, waiver, settlement, or procedural disposition. The separately designated Joint Appendix remains outside this reader's recovered/read set; nothing here treats that research limit as a litigant omission. Original statutes, agency decisions, Council materials, amici and any argument transcript must be separately consulted if an eventual decision depends on a proposition not established by the attributed recitations above.
