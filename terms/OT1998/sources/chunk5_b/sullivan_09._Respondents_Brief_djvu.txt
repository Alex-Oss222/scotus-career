STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


A. Pennsylvania’s Compelling Need For Workers’ Compensation 
Reform 


Prior to the enactment of cost containment initiatives amending 
Pennsylvania’s Workers’ Compensation Act, Act of June 2, 1915, P.L. 
736, as amended, 77 Pa. Stat. Ann. § 1 et seq. (West Supp. 1998) 
(hereinafter “Act”), the School District of Philadelphia (hereinafter 
“School District”), among other Pennsylvania employers, confronted 
soaring costs of mandatory workers’ compensation.' The high cost of 
workers’ compensation insurance was crippling, prohibiting industries 
from competing successfully in Pennsylvania. Pa. Legis. J. - Senate 472 
(April 19, 1993). It is indisputable that jobs were lost every day due to 
workers’ compensation costs. Pa. Legis. J. — Senate 1046 (June 16, 
1993). 

In 1993, prior to the amendments to the Act, the costs to insurers of 
workers’ compensation was approximately $23 million. Pa. Legis. J. — 
House 18 (March 30, 1993). There were concerns regarding whether 
the State Workers’ Insurance Fund (hereinafter “SWIF’”) was actuarially 
sound. Pa. Legis. J. — House 2015 (December 11, 1991). Discussion 
indicated that SWIF, which insured approximately 450,000 employees 
working for approximately 35,000 Pennsylvania employers, was on the 
verge of bankruptcy and operating at a loss. Pa. Legis. J. — Senate 
1044-1045 (June 16, 1993); Pa. Legis. J. — Senate 473 (April 19, 1993). 

The Commonwealth’s General Assembly witnessed the exodus of 
business. Pennsylvania Bureau of Workers’ Compensation, Annual 
Report, 1996-1997 (hereinafter “Annual Report”) at ii; News Brief from 
the Office of Governor Tom Ridge, Vol. 1, Issue 8 (May 31, 1996); 
<http.//www.state.pa.us/pa_exec/governor/news_briefs/ Vol liss8.htmI>. 
News Brief from the Office of Governor Tom Ridge, Vol 1, Issue 7 (May 
24, 1996). <http.//www.state.pa.us/pa_exec/governor/news — briefs/

funding efforts to identify Pennsylvania businesses that were disenchanted 
with the business climate of Pennsylvania and attract these businesses to 


their respective states. Pa. Legis. J. — House 2019 (December 11, 
1991).

B. Statutory Background 


The Act establishes a required insurance system to provide 
compensation to employees who sustain injuries in the course of and 
related to their employment. It provides the exclusive means by which 
an employee may recover against employers for work-related injuries. 
77 Pa. Stat. Ann. § 481; Winterberger vs. CNA Ins. Co., 72 F.3d 318 (3d 
Cir. 1995). Compensation under the Act is payable without regard to 
negligence. 77 Pa. Stat. Ann.§ 431.* 


3. Particularly emphasized were the concerns that businesses were not 
expanding because of high workers’ compensation rates; businesses were closing 
because they could not absorb the costs of workers’ compensation, businesses 
were fleeing the state to surrounding states with more equitable compensation 
due to the costs. /d. at 544. 

4. Both wage replacement payments, which are contingent on a continuing 
wage loss duc to a work injury, and payment for reasonable medical services are 
benefits payable under the Act. 77 P.S. §§ 511-516, 531. 


5 


The Act requires employers to either obtain insurance or self-insure 
liability under the Act. 77 Pa. Stat. Ann. § 501. Employers who do not 
self-insure may obtain insurance from any insurance carrier authorized 
to write workers’ compensation policies within Pennsylvania, including 
the State Workman’s Insurance Fund (“SWIF”). SWIF was created by 
the Pennsylvania General Assembly to provide coverage to any employers 
who request it. 77 Pa. Stat. Ann. § 221,501. When insured, the insurer 
merely “assume[s] the employer’s liability [t}hereunder and shall be 
entitled to all of the employer’s immunities and protection. . . .” 77 Pa. 
Stat. Ann. § 501. 

Pennsylvania's compensation system is administered by the 
Department of Labor and Industry, through its Bureau of Workers’ 
Compensation (the “Bureau”). 77 Pa. Stat. Ann. §§ 27, 711. The 
compensation scheme provides three methods by which an employer 
pays compensation. The employer may file a Notice of Compensation 
Payable with the Department of Labor and Industry; enter into an 
agreement with the injured claimant to pay compensation; or pay pursuant 
to an order of a Workers’ Compensation Judge where an employee’s 
claim petition has been granted. 77 Pa. Stat. Ann. § 731. 

Prior to the enactment of the medical cost containment provisions 
of Act 44 in 1993, and the legislative refinement of the process in 1996 
under Act 57, Pennsylvania employers and insurers were required to pay 
all medical bills that a physician or other licensed practitioner of the 
healing arts submitted with a report until such time as a workers’ 
compensation referee (later defined “workers’ compensation judge”) ruled 
upon any challenge to that treatment. 77 Pa. Stat. Ann. § 531(2)(ii). 
Employers were required to have a reasonable basis to contest the bills 
(i.e., a medical opinion that the bills were not reasonable or necessary) 
before filing petitions to challenge injured employees’ entitlement to 
receipt of the benefits, 77 Pa. Stat. Ann. § 996;° and the Act expressly 
prohibited the workers’ compensation referees from granting a 
supersedeas or stay of the payment of medical bills while a challenge to 
treatment was pending review. 77 Pa. Stat. Ann. § 531(2)(ii).

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


Pennsylvania's utilization review process accords sufficient due 
process to Pennsylvania claimants. The cost containment reform 
measures enacted by the Pennsylvania legislature do not operate to 
unconstitutionally deprive claimants of compensation benefits. Rather, 
the reform measures only extend the mandatory time period for payment 
of medical bills while those bills are reviewed for determination of 
whether the treatment provided was reasonable and necessary (i.e., 
compensable under the Act). 

Claimants are not unconstitutionally deprived of their benefits during 
the utilization review process, as the Third Circuit held. Only specifically 
challenged treatment, as opposed to all medical benefits, is subject to 
utilization review. Moreover, the process sufficiently balances the 
competing private interests of claimants and employers in Pennsylvania 
consistent with the test this Court set forth in Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 
U.S. 319. The process protects any private interests claimants may have 
in the uninterrupted receipt of medical benefits because resolution of the 
compensability of the challenged treatment turns on a medical analysis 
and determination. During utilization review, all pertinent medical 
information is provided to the reviewer by the provider. The provider 


16 


may also furnish factual information as part of his/her medical records 
and has an opportunity for an oral consultation with the reviewer. The 
reviewer is required to follow well-defined procedures in conducting the 
review to determine only the reasonableness or necessity of the treatment, 
and the reviewer must be of the same specialty as the provider. Thus, 
any risk of an erroneous deprivation of medical benefits as a result of the 
institution of utilization review is slight. 

The claimants’ interest is further protected because tolling the period 
in which a bill must be paid in order to submit the bill for review affects 
neither the claimants’ means of living nor their entitlement to other 
medical treatment from the health care provider subject to review or 
another health care provider of choice. The statute prohibits the medical 
provider from collecting any unpaid bills or balance from the Claimant 
and, the bill(s) must be paid immediately upon circulation of a reviewer's 
opinion that treatment is reasonable and necessary irrespective of any 
intentions by the employer or insurer to appeal the reviewer's 
determination. Full remedies exist which lessen the potential harm to 
claimants, such as the award of benefits, plus interest, where the claimant 
prevails on an appeal to a Workers’ Compensation Judge. 

The significant interests of Pennsylvania employers are protected 
because the tolling period pending review by the utilization review 
organization relieves the mandate of payment of unreasonable and 
unnecessary medical bills. The process, prior to the Third Circuit’s 
Sullivan decision in March 1988, proved to be successful. As of February 
1, 1997, the Bureau reported a twenty-five percent overall reduction in 
workers’ compensation loss costs. Annual Report at ii. It was reported 
that the reform which sought to re-establish reasonable costs in a system 
that was out of control, while still serving the needs of legitimately injured 
claimants, was producing significant savings for the business community 
as a whole. Individual self-insured employers saved approximately 
$48,903,715 in medical compensation when comparing 1993 and 1996 
statewide figures. /d. at 31. 

The total medical compensation payment of insured and self-insured 
employers decreased 25% or $288 million, between 1993 and 1994 alone. 
Id. In short, the process was working to protect the legitimate 
governmental interests in containing Pennsylvania workers’ compensation 
costs, preserving Pennsylvania jobs, and ensuring that medical benefits 
are paid to truly injured employees of the Commonwealth. 


17 


With respect to the sufficiency of the notice furnished to the claimants 
of the review, the process provides claimants with sufficient notice that 
their treatment is being challenged. Claimants are also provided with 
notice and an explanation of the appeal process when a determination is 
made as to the reasonableness and necessity of treatment. The notice 
provided is sufficient because due process, under the circumstances 
presented, merely requires that Claimants be notified that their treatment 
is under review and be provided a meaningful opportunity for hearing 
on the matter. Cafeteria Workers v. McElroy, 307 U.S. 886, 895 (1961) 
(due process is not a fixed concept with respect to time, place or 
circumstance). 

Because no question is before the reviewer regarding the entitlement 
of a claimant to medical benefits in general and since a Claimant's 
entitlement to medical benefits will continue, there is no need for the 
employer, insurer, or Commonwealth to notify a claimant that medical 
benefits will be suspended as the Third Circuit held. When the 
determination is made that the specifically challenged treatment is not 
appropriate, claimants are notified at tha‘ time of the determination and 
of the proper channels to follow in oider to contest the determination. 
Contrary to the Third Circuit's decision, notice in this case is sufficient, 
because the claimants are apprised of the review of treatment, the 
reviewer's determination and the method to secure a meaningful hearing. 

The utilization review procedures further provide sufficient 
opportunity for claimants to participate in a meaningful hearing. 
Claimants are not deprived of due process because of the lack of an 
opportunity for either a hearing or an opportunity to submit a statement 
regarding the treatment prior to the reviewer's determination. Any such 
statement is neither valuable nor essential to the utilization review process. 
The doctor is the party to the process whose actions are being reviewed 
and the doctor, not the claimant, is in a much better position to discuss 
and persuade the reviewer to find that specific treatment is medically 
reasonable and necessary. Furthermore, the employer or insurer is 
precluded from submitting independent medical reports or other 
documentation to the reviewer. One would surely question the 
constitutionality of a system which would permit only the claimant and 
the claimant's doctor to participate, while the employer was precluded 
from submitting any documentation or provided any opportunity to 
participation in the utilization review process as it may now exist. 
Pennsylvania's utilization review system, as currently re-written by the 


Third Circuit, works this very injustice. The Third Circuit's “revision” 
of the Act had a chilling effect on Pennsylvania cost containment 
initiatives. If this Court does not reverse the Third Circuit's decision, in 
toto, Pennsylvania will be strapped with a compensation system that is 
simply not workable and in fact, costs Pennsylvania more than the pre- 
reformed measures. The Act was amended in 1993 to replace the old 
review system with the utilization review process. Therefore, employers 
may no longer even petition a Judge directly for review of medical 
treatment. See 77 Pa. Stat. Ann. § 531(6) “[e)xcept in those cases in 
which a workers’ compensation judge asks for an review under Section 
420, disputes as to reasonableness and necessity of treatment . . . shall 
be resolved” by utilization review). [emphasis added, citation omitted). 

Therefore, Pennsylvania employers and insurers must pay bills 
without remedy to recoup the funds when those bills are found to be 
unreasonable or unnecessary. Without a review petition, there is no 
supersedeas fund reimbursement and recouping the money from doctors 
or claimants is statutorily prohibited. If this Court rules as to the state 
actor issue alone, the effect of the Third Circuit’s decision is that private 
employees insured through SWIF, “state actor” employees (including 
the Commonwealth and other public employers that may ultimately be 
classified as “state actors”), and private insurers would still be forced to 
pay the high cost of pre-reform medical expenses without remedy. State 
actor employers would be precluded from delaying the payment of bills 
during review and, therefore, lose any chance of avoiding payment for 
unreasonable and unnecessary treatment. Private employers insured 
through SWIF will realize those costs directly in their premiums, and 
private insurers who fund SWIF indirectly will also realize those costs. 
The providers will no longer have accountability under the Act and the 
costly abuse that existed prior to Act 44 will return in Pennsylvania. 

Finally, as the District Court found, the assessment of a fee upon 
reconsideration review against a losing claimant does not violate due 
process. Plaintiffs’ interest in uninterrupted workers’ compensation 
benefits does not rise to the level of a “fundamental” right and employees 
have an alternative to judicial remedies which are not conditioned on the 
payment of fees. Claimants were simply not denied access to 
reconsideration, a Workers’ Compensation Judge, or the appellate courts 
as a result of the subject reconsideration fee. See C.A. App. A156; 34 
Pa. Code § 127.514(a). 


19 


Accordingly, the claims that the utilization review process does not 
comply with due process were properly dismissed by the District Court 
and the Third Circuit's decision should be reversed. The decisions of 
the District Court should be reinstated.

I. SELF-INSURED EMPLOYERS, LIKE PRIVATE 
INSURANCE COMPANIES, DO NOT BECOME STATE ACTORS 
WHEN THEY ELECT TO USE STATE AUTHORIZED 
UTILIZATION REVIEW PROCEDURES OR WITHHOLD

Self-insured employers, like private insurance companies, do not

reasonableness and necessity of the treatment. Blum v. Yaretsky, 557 
U.S. 991, 1007-1110 (1982) (state regulation of nursing homes does not 
convert actions of the homes into state action for purposes of due process);

C. Utilization Review Procedures Satisfy Due Process 


It is axiomatic that the concept of due process is flexible, focused 
on ensuring “fundamental fairness” in decision making. Walters v. 
National Ass'n of Radiation Survivors, 473 U.S. 305, 320 (1985). Not 
being a technical concept, due process is not fixed with respect to time, 
place or circumstance. Cafeteria Workers v. McElroy, 367 U.S. 886, 89 
(1961). : 
Procedural due process imposes constraints on 
governmental decisions which deprive individuals of “liberty” 
or “property” interests within the meaning of the Due Process 
Clause of the Fifth or Fourteenth Amendment. 

Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319, 331 (1976). 

The process required varies depending on the nature of the interests 
at issue and the circumstances under which deprivation may occur. 
Walters at 320; Morrisey v. Brewer, 408 U.S. 471, 481 (1972). 
Nevertheless, “legislatures are to be allowed considerable leeway to 


27 


formulate such processes without being forced tc conform to a rigid 
constitutional code of procedural necessities.” Walters, at 326. 

Whether specific procedures satisfy due process is a legal question 
for the Court to resolve. Midnight Sessions, Ltd. v. City of Philadelphia, 
945 F.2d 667, 684 (3d Cir. 1991) cert. denied, 503 U.S. 54 (1992). In 
conducting a due process inquiry, Courts must balance three factors: (1) 
the private interests that will be affected by the official action; (2) the 
risk that the procedures will result in an erroneous deprivation of the 
interest and the probable value of additional procedures; and, (3) the 
governmental interest, including the function involved and the fiscal and 
administrative burdens that the additional or substitute procedural 
requirement would entail. Mathews, 424 U.S. at 334-335. Applying 
these factors herein, it is evident that the Pennsylvania legislature enacted 
an administrative scheme which provides sufficient due process to 
workers’ compensation claimants.
