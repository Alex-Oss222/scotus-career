## Requested action and scope arguments

1. **Relief sought (p. 50).** Respondents request affirmance of the Third Circuit. Their questions presented describe joint use of statutory termination power and prior notice/opportunity before indefinite suspension; those are counsel's formulations, distinct from the petitioners' actual questions already copied in EXACT_PASSAGES.

2. **Continuing controversy and revised procedures (pp. 3-4 and n.7; p. 40 n.33).** Respondents argue that revised Bureau procedures after the Third Circuit decision make the prospective due-process question unnecessary or advisory. They identify the notice at JA 50 permitting a personal written statement and warning that an adverse review may cause loss of payment. They contend that, because petitioners did not challenge the lower ruling's notice/opportunity requirements, petitioners seek a hypothetical ruling about restoration of a prior withholding practice. They separately distinguish liability for earlier allegedly unconstitutional deprivation from any prospective decision about revised procedures. This is an attributed jurisdiction/scope argument, not a finding that all relief is moot, that the entire case ended, that any state amendment repealed the statutory withholding provision, or that another party's litigation status was conclusively resolved.

3. **Public participants and equal protection (p. 2 n.4).** Respondents say SWIF provides coverage to public and private employers and invoke unequal process for workers insured by public versus private entities if private actor status is rejected. The Third Circuit expressly did not decide that equal-protection issue. It is an asserted alternative consequence, not an adjudicated entitlement or an independent question automatically within this review.

## Additional record descriptions, maintained as attributed accounts

4. **Eligibility basis (p. 8 n.10).** Respondents say the named workers and proposed class members became eligible for compensation through voluntary agreement, unilateral employer acknowledgment of a compensable injury, or a compensation-judge decision after hearing. They argue these are existing recipients rather than applicants. They do not in that footnote supply a finding that every treatment later reviewed was independently adjudicated reasonable and necessary. General work-related eligibility, particular treatment entitlement and the consequence of earlier adjudication therefore remain separate source propositions.

5. **Employee-funded care (pp. 6-7; p. 24 n.25).** Respondents cite section 531(3)(i) and argue that workers cannot obtain the challenged care by paying their own funds. Footnote 25 expressly addresses petitioners' example of a named worker paying out of pocket: respondents say a provider violated the law by demanding or accepting that payment, and cite deposition testimony of David Weyl, Chief of the Bureau's Cost Containment Division, Third Circuit Appendix B-155. This is a reported official testimony and party construction, not a newly verified complete prohibition governing every voluntary patient-provider arrangement. It differs from petitioners' reliance on section 531(7) and section 127.211 for the balance-billing protection and cannot be merged with those provisions. If a ruling requires the precise statutory reach, the specific period text and full context should control.

6. **Actual treatment loss versus payment delay (p. 13 n.16; pp. 23-24).** Respondents say some utilization reviews concern completed discrete services, but the named plaintiffs also allege stoppage of ongoing treatment when insurers stopped paying. They argue payment is the delivery mechanism for medical care and later money cannot replace missed needed treatment. These are complaint allegations and their practical implications, not a finding that every reviewed bill caused a treatment interruption. Petitioners' examples of continued treatment do not logically establish that no plaintiff lost treatment; respondents' general claims do not establish the reverse for every plaintiff.

7. **Bureau screening (pp. 11-12 and n.14).** Respondents describe review-request rejection for unaccepted work-related liability; lack of service certification, signature, treatment identification or necessary processing information; duplicate/pending peer-review issues; treatment predating the statutory effective date; a request addressing causal relatedness rather than reasonableness/necessity; missing predecessor review where applicable; and inconsistency of provider, treatment or dates. They characterize the Bureau as a required gatekeeper, say an unapproved request cannot maintain withholding, and cite Third Circuit Appendix C-50 for a twenty-percent rejection rate. Petitioners describe materially similar screening as ministerial; the lower opinion expressly says it does not evaluate substantive legitimacy. The percentage is party-reported evidence, not a judicially found probability or an estimate independently verified here.

8. **Enforcement descriptions (pp. 7-8, 12).** Respondents identify Bureau compliance enforcement and statutory penalties for withholding without a proper request, and provider fee-review proceedings capable of producing a payment and interest order. They characterize these acts as participation in termination. The underlying institutional acts and their constitutional significance are separate; the brief does not itself establish that officials substantively approved the reasonableness of any insurer's withholding decision.

9. **Time limits and actual delays (pp. 12-13 n.15; pp. 21-24).** Respondents describe a sixty-day review period after assignment and no prescribed time for the Bureau's assignment. They cite particular pleaded assignment delays of thirty-two and forty-two days in appendix material. The lower opinion quotes the statute's thirty-day report language, describes regulatory periods separately, and notes that compensation-judge proceedings have no prescribed completion period. These descriptions must not be collapsed into a new statutory sixty-day command. The original versus post-Act-57 review structures also remain distinct. No named plaintiff's actual elapsed review time is newly found here.

10. **Earlier judicial refusal of suspension (pp. 38-39 n.32).** Respondents report that four named plaintiffs had treatment payments stopped through utilization review after a workers' compensation judge had denied a requested suspension and ordered continuation of benefits. They cite JA paragraphs 96-99, 111-113, 147-150 and 249-252. This is a potentially material allegation about existing orders, not a verified finding that the same precise treatment and issue had already been conclusively adjudicated. The cited pleading/order material would be needed for a proposition turning on that identity or the scope of an earlier order.

11. **Reconsideration-fee claim (pp. 14-15 n.18).** Respondents report that the former reconsideration-fee claim was a systemic claim against Commonwealth defendants and was settled at an August 19, 1998 meeting of all counsel. No settlement instrument, terms, filed stipulation, dismissal order or treatment of individual damages is supplied in this brief. The lower opinion had remanded that claim because it could not determine whether any plaintiff actually paid fees. The new reported settlement is a party act distinct from any Court action; it does not by itself establish a complete authorized in-world terminal disposition or the terms to record.

12. **Procedural chronology differences (pp. 14-15).** The brief states an amended-complaint filing of May 29, 1996 and public-defendant disposition dates November 8 and December 16, 1996. The primary Third Circuit opinion states May 21, November 7 and December 13. This source discrepancy should be retained if exact dates matter; the supplemental reader has not silently replaced the primary opinion's dates. Both sources agree that private carriers were dismissed on January 24, 1996, that the public matters were later dismissed, and that no plaintiff class had been certified.

13. **Pleading posture (p. 50 n.42).** Respondents object that petitioners rely on facts/statistics outside the district-court record and emphasize the Rule 12(b)(6) carrier dismissal. They contend the plaintiffs' pleaded facts govern at that stage. This is the actual party objection, not acceptance of every legal characterization in the pleading or a new merits factual finding. The separate motion/prior partial-summary-judgment and public-defendant proceedings retain their actual postures.

## Principal arguments actually made

14. **Direct facial challenge theory (pp. 16-19, 24-31).** Respondents first argue that direct challenge to a state enactment requires attention to the statute and its enforcement and avoids the separate private-actor tests petitioners propose. They invoke Tulsa, Reitman, Nixon, Smith v. Allwright, New York Times and related cases, and argue the State enlisted insurers to carry out an impermissible withdrawal without process. This is their legal theory. It does not establish a generally applicable current rule that facial pleading alone supplies private-party attribution.

15. **Alternative contextual attribution (pp. 31-40).** Respondents invoke Edmonson's governmental-assistance, governmental-function and authority-aggravated-injury considerations as a totality approach. They emphasize compulsory and exclusive coverage; an inability to choose the insurer; the Bureau's processing, enforcement and administrative hearings; the alleged public character of benefits; and the alleged harm from bypassing an earlier official assurance of benefits. They expressly do not claim every insurer act under the statute is state action (p. 36 n.29). Their discussion of prisoners and West is an analogy concerning state-created dependence, not a finding that injured workers are prisoners or that the State has the identical preexisting constitutional medical-care duty.

16. **Independent traditional attribution routes (pp. 40-50).** Respondents separately argue joint participation/symbiosis through Bureau involvement and shared cost containment; public function through the power to stop benefits previously exercised by judges; and significant encouragement from the statute's targeted permission and public cost policy. They acknowledge that extensive regulation alone is insufficient (p. 47). They contest a requirement that a delegated function have always been exclusively governmental; alternatively, they argue the post-1984 Pennsylvania allocation satisfies it. They distinguish Blum, Jackson, Rendell-Baker and Flagg Brothers by alleged direct procedural involvement and state policy, rather than deny those cases exist.

17. **No-process and Mathews grounds (pp. 19-24).** Respondents defend notice and a meaningful opportunity before the deprivation, distinguish temporary from final deprivation only to argue both receive protection, and invoke both general pre-deprivation doctrine and Mathews. They say the lower court considered the insurers' pecuniary interest as well as workers' health interests, risk of error and government burden. They seek an opportunity for a personal written statement, not necessarily a full evidentiary hearing before every review. Their view that initial requests need no supporting medical justification is an argument drawn from the described system; it is distinct from the later medical standard, reviewer qualifications and review report.

18. **Cost and incentive disputes (pp. 42-43, 48-50).** Respondents challenge the sufficiency of interest and reimbursement because a person who lost actual treatment may generate no bill to repay. They assert a statutory purpose to reduce costs and attract business and argue insurers gain from review even if later unsuccessful. Those are competing policy and incentive arguments, not a finding of bad faith, inevitable savings, actual loss in every case, or the absence of an initial-review cost. Their reported request-volume statistic is likewise not a newly authenticated adjudicated fact.

## Lower-opinion source comparison

The complete lower opinion confirms the material system and remedy facts in the existing preflight: independent insurer invocation; Bureau completeness review rather than substantive merits approval; permission rather than obligation to withhold; medical reviewers in the same profession and same/similar specialty; reasonableness/necessity review assuming work-related injury and not adjudicating continued disability, maximum improvement or fee reasonableness; the Act 57 removal of reconsideration; lifting suspension after a worker-favorable initial decision under the revised regime; an unbound compensation-judge hearing on the review report; separate rejection of inadequate-review-standards claims; separate unresolved fee and capacity/immunity questions; and the absence of class certification.

The lower court severed the disputed-treatment exception from the thirty-day payment provision and also required specified notice and personal-statement protections. Those are separate features of its actual judgment. Its property-interest statement and footnote 24 report no dispute below; the petitioners' distinct no-entitlement-to-each-disputed-treatment argument and express scope limitation appear in their source supplement. No new conclusion about the legal reach of these facts is supplied here.

The revised JA 50 notice's actual language is already mechanically copied in EXACT_PASSAGES: an adverse determination may result in treatment nonpayment; a personal written statement may be sent within twenty days; appeal instructions accompany the determination. The later notices do not prove that every earlier harm is moot or that every old claim is still live. The record and requested relief must be examined by the fresh context.
