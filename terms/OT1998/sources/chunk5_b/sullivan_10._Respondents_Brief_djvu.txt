COUNTERSTATEMENT 


This case presents a direct constitutional challenge to the 
utilization review procedures enacted in 1993 by the Pennsylvania 
legislature to amend its Workers’ Compensation Act.' Those pro- 
cedures permitted insurers or self-insured employers (hereafter 
“Petitioners” or “employers”) to stop or supersede the workers’ 
compensation medical benefits of thousands of injured workers, in 
which the workers have an undisputed property interest, without 
notice and with no pre-deprivation opportunity to be heard. These 
amendments were part of a legislative package directed at cost 
containment known commonly as “Act 44”. 

The Respondents in this case, the Plaintiffs below (hereafter 
“Plaintiffs” or “injured workers”), contended that the state, which 
administers the benefit program and has exclusive control over the 
resolution of disputes among the parties, violated their right to due 
process of law under the Fourteenth Amendment and 42 U.S.C. 
§ 1983 when it established the procedures contained in § 306 
(f.1)(5) and (6) of the Act, which delegated to the employers the 
power and authority to supersede an injured workers’ medical 
benefits without any pre-deprivation notice or opportunity to be 
heard. Zinermon v. Burch, 494 U.S. 113 (1990); Logan v. Zimmer- 
man Brush Co., 455 U.S. 422 (1982); Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 
U.S. 319 (1976).? Plaintiffs also sued ten named insurance carriers, 
contending that they were state actors in the context of the statu- 
tory scheme and therefore liable to plaintiffs for the violation of 
their due process rights. 

The Third Circuit Court of Appeals found the Commonwealth 
defendants below constitutionally liable to Plaintiffs for their role 
in enacting and administering the challenged provisions. It further 
held that the employers and insurers who use the utilization review

History and Scope of the Workers’ Compensation Act 


The Pennsylvania's Workers’ Compensation Act (hereaf- 
ter “the Act”), was passed in 1915 to compensate workers for 
disabilities or death suffered as a result of work-related inju- 
ries or occupational diseases. Prior to the enactment of the 
legislation, injured workers and their survivors could litigate 
claims against their employers for damages, including lost 
wages, medical costs, pain and suffering and other traditional 
tort remedies. However, because this was an action in tort, 
workers who failed to prove employer negligence were left 
with no remedy at all. See Krawchuk v. Philadelphia Electric 
Co., 497 Pa. 115 (1981).

The Operation of The Workers’ Compensation System 


The Bureau works hand in hand with employers to pro- 
vide benefits to injured workers. Whether benefits are paid by 
the employer directly, through SWIF, or by a commercial 
insurer, the Bureau is an integral participant in the operation 
of the statute. It actively enforces the Act's mandates and is 
authorized to ensure compliance by insurers through the 
assessment of monetary penalties for violation of its provi- 
sions. 77 P.S. § 701. Pursuant to its statutory authority, the 
Bureau has adopted regulations implementing the Act and has 
imposed numerous reporting requirements upon employers 
and insurers involved in the system. 34 Pa. Code § 121.1 er 
seq. For uniformity and ease of administration, the Bureau 
has developed numerous forms for each of these reporting 
requirements. Use of these forms is mandatory. 77 PS. 
§§ 717.1, 711, 731. 


The Bureau is more tuan a passive repository for com- 
pleted forms, however. The mandated documents reflect the 
legal status of every injured employee. The Bureau not only 
collects these documents, but reviews them for compliance 
with the Act. 77 P.S §§ 715, 731. In the event of a dispute 
between the parties, these documents will provide the basis on 
which the state ultimately determines the injured worker's 
entitlement. 77 P.S. § 731. They also provide a foundation for 
enforcement actions against employers or insurance carriers 


which the Bureau determines are in violation of the provi- 
sions of the Act. 77 P.S. § 991.

I. INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

THE UTILIZATION REVIEW PROVISIONS VIO- 
LATED DUE PROCESS 


While it is the Plaintiffs’ contention that the due process 
issue is not appropriately before this Court, the Third Cir- 
cuit’s due process decision was based on this Court's well 
established precedent and is virtually unassailable. The court 
held that the utilization review provisions violated due pro- 
cess because the statute provided no notice to Plaintiffs prior 
to the supersedeas of their medical benefits, and offered the 
injured workers no opportunity to challenge the suspension of 
their medical benefits until many months (at least) after the 
deprivation of those benefits had been effected. The court 
found the lack of pre-deprivation notice to the injured worker 


20 


to be constitutionally fatal.22 Moreover, the court observed, 
the notice arrived after the benefits had already been stopped. 
The court noted the Commonwealth's concession that no 
notice is afforded to the injured worker under the statute, and 
concluded that inadequate notice effectively strips an injured 
worker of his ability or opportunity to protest or minimize 
unjustified deprivations. The court held that due process 
requires some sort of opportunity to be heard regarding the 
basis of the proposed termination before it takes place. 


The Third Circuit's holding is a mainstream decision 
consistent with a well-settled and unbroken line of precedent 
from this Court, which sets forth two basic tenets of due 
process. First, “notice reasonably calculated under all the 
circumstances to apprise interested parties of the pendency of 
the action” is constitutionally required prior to the depriva- 
tion of a protected property interest. Memphis Light, Gas & 
Water Div. v. Craft, 436 U.S. 1, 13 (1978); Second, due 
process ordinarily requires an opportunity for “some kind of 
hearing” prior to the deprivation of such an interest. Memphis 
Light, Gas & Water, supra, 436 U.S at 19, citing, Boddie v. 
Connecticut, 401 U.S. 371, 379 (1971). See also Logan vy. 
Zimmerman Brush Co., 455 U.S. 422, 437 (1982). In a case 
such as this, in which an established state procedure is being 
challenged, this Court has always required notice and an 
opportunity to be heard before the deprivation, unless exigent 
circumstances exist or pre-deprivation process would be 
demonstrably impracticable. Zinermon v. Burch, 494 U.S. 113 
(1990); Parratt v. Taylor, 451 U.S. 527 (1981). This is true 
even if extensive post-deprivation remedies are afforded. 
Connecticut v. Doehr, 501 U.S. 1 (1991).

A. A TOTALITY OF THE CIRCUMSTANCES 
APPROACH SHOULD BE USED TO FIND 
STATE ACTION WHERE THERE IS NO 
FACIAL CONSTITUTIONAL CHALLENGE 


1. Edmonson’s Totality Test 


Edmonson was this Court’s last major foray into the 
realm of state action in a case that did not involve a facial 
challenge to the constitutionality of a state law. The Court's 
analysis in that case is contrary to the Petitioners’ contention 
that state action can only be established through the rigid 
application of formulaic rules. Rather, the Court embarked 
upon a unified state action analysis which took into account 
1) the extent to which the actor relies on governmental assis- 
tance and benefits; 2) whether the actor is performing a 
traditional governmental function; and 3) whether the injury 
caused is aggravated in a unique way by the incidents of 
governmental authority. Edmonson, 500 U.S. at 621-22. 


32 


Instead of stating a series of discrete tests and applying them 
separately to determine whether each by itself was satisfied, 
the Court considered the facts relevant to these principles and 
weighed them to determine whether, all things considered, the 
otherwise private actor fairly could be deemed a state actor. 
Essentially, the Court adopted a “totality of the circum- 
stances” test. 


The approach used by the Court in Edmonson is partic- 
ularly appropriate to discern state action, which in any given 
circumstance is necessarily a “fact-bound inquiry.” For, as 
this Court has indicated on numerous occasions, “only by 
sifting facts and weighing circumstances can the non-obvious 
involvement of the State in private conduct be attributed its 
true significance”. Lugar v. Edmondson Oil Co, 457 U.S. 922, 
939 (1982). The totality approach relied upon by the Court in 
Edmonson was not a unique or radical departure from the 
analysis applied in previous state action cases, and was con- 
sistent with a number of other decisions in which the Court 
utilized no specific litmus test, but rather, considered all the 
facts and circumstances to determine whether the challenged 
action could be fairly attributed to the state. See, e.g., West, 
487 U.S. at 55-56; Tulsa, 485 U.S. at 479; see also Burton, 
365 U.S. at 725-26. 


The approach used by the Court in Edmonson, West, and 
Tulsa, its most recent state action cases, is undoubtedly the 
most reliable method of analysis for discerning state action. 
The Court has consistently recognized the complexity of the 
state action inquiry and the factual subtleties which neces- 
sarily bear upon the analysis. There is no discrete test which 
can neatly categorize purportedly private decisions into state 
action or non-state action pigeonholes. The Petitioners’ plea 
for the adoption of such a test to resolve the “most trouble- 
some issue in constitutional law”?’ is overly simplistic and 
inconsistent with this Court’s most recent pronouncements. It 
should be rejected. In determining when private conduct can

be deemed fairly attributable to the state, each situation must 
be factually analyzed on its own terms. 


2. Application of the Totality Test To The 
Employer's Role in Utilization Review 


Application of the Edmonson totality approach to the 
utilization review process at issue here demonstrates that the 
Third Circuit correctly deemed the employers to be state 
actors. As this Court has instructed, those procedures and the 
employers’ role in that process must first be viewed in the 
context of the workers’ compensation system as a whole, with 
its dense web of interconnections between the state agency 
and the employers who fulfill the state's obligation to Penn-

the relationship among the State, the physician, and the pris-

State action is clearly warranted. 


a. Governmental Assistance and Benefits 


The extent to which the employers rely upon “govern- 
mental assistance and benefits” weighs heavily in this case in 
favor of a finding of state action. The utilization review 
process is a discrete aspect of the workers’ compensation 
program, one which is established by state law and adminis- 
tered directly by the state for the purpose of delivering public 
benefits to injured workers without regard to fault. The state 
acts as a giant funnel through which almost every transaction 
in the system must pass. The state chooses who the system's 
participants will be and defines the roles that each will play 
within the system. The state controls all entrances into and 
exits from the system and supplies all the rules, regulations, 
forms, physical offices, hearing rooms, and decision makers. 
It resolves all disputes as to injured worker eligibility and 
employer obligations. It is a “system which the government 
alone administers.” Edmonson, 500 U.S. at 622.

sedeas, one which is not premised upon a properly submitted

b. Traditional Government Function 


The second factor identified in Edmonson is whether the 
private actor is engaged in a traditional government function. 
In the context of the Pennsylvania workers’ compensation 
system, the provision of medical treatment to injured workers, 
the power to determine whether medical treatment is reason- 
able and necessary, and the power to stop or supersede those 
benefits after a determination that the treatment is not reason- 
able and necessary, have been exclusively within the province 
of the state. Injured workers must seek medical care for work 
related injuries through the state, and only through the state. 
The system is mandatory. Workers cannot opt-out of the 
system, pay for their own treatment, sue their employers for 
the cost of their treatment, nor sue the insurers for their 
failure to provide treatment. Injured workers have no choice 
but to rely on the state to fulfill its statutory obligation to 
them. 


The constitutional import of this mandatory and exclu- 
sive state structure is set forth in West. There, the state was 
under both a constitutional and statutory obligation to provide 
medical care to inmates housed in state prisons. To fulfill this 
obligation, the state contracted with a private doctor to pro- 
vide for medical care. The Court found the doctor to be a state 
actor because “in the prison context, medical care is the 


36 


‘exclusive prerogative of the State’ ”. Thus, the doctor was a 
“willful participant in joint activity with the State.” /d., 487 
U.S. at 47. 


While Plaintiffs are not prisoners in the sense of West, 
they are nonetheless captives of the state system. Employers 
and insurers, like the doctor in West, are participants in a 
wholly state-created and administered public benefit system 
which has a legal obligation to provide medical care to 
beneficiaries who have no alternative source of such care. 
Employers and insurers, by virtue of their contract with 
employers, stand in the same relation to the state and the 
recipient of medical care as did the doctor in West. Here the 
state guarantees the delivery of medical care to the injured 
worker by imposing upon the employer the obligation to pay 
for it, either directly or through a contract of insurance.?* The 
provision of workers’ compensation benefits in Pennsylvania 
is, therefore, an exclusive state function.?%

2. The Public Function Test 


The facts set forth in the Edmonson public function anal- 
ysis, see infra, at II1(A)(2)(b), are sufficient in and of them- 
selves to establish the employers’ liability as state actors under 
the public function test espoused by Petitioners. The only 
question remaining is whether the power to stop an injured 
worker’s benefits pursuant to utilization review is a function 
which has heretofore been an exclusive state function, or, more 
to the point, whether exclusivity ought to be relevant to the 
analysis at all. 


Prior to Edmonson, the public function test as articulated 
by the Court had required that the function delegated to the 
private party be one traditionally and exclusively performed by 
the state. See Flagg Brothers, 436 U.S. at 157-160. After 
Edmonson much debate ensued as to whether or not the public 
function test still required exclusivity. Edmonson did not use 
the word “exclusive”, a point which was hotly attacked in the 
dissent. 500 U.S. at 640. Some courts and commentators since 
Edmonson have opined that the exclusivity requirement was 
eliminated from the public function test, e.g., Mark v. Borough 
of Hatboro, 51 F.3d 1137 (3rd Cir.1995) (Greenberg, concur- 
ring); McKeesport Hospital v. Accreditation Council, 24 F.3d 
519 (3rd Cir. 1994) (Becker, concurring), while others have 
treated the absence of the exclusivity language in Edmonson as 
a mere oversight on the part of the Court. E.g., United Auto 
Workers v. Gaston Festivals, Inc., 43 F.3d 902, 906 (4th Cir. 


45 


1995); Gallagher v. Neil Young Freedom Concert, 49 F.3d 1442 
(10th Cir. 1995). 


As stated previously, this debate fails to perceive that in 
Edmonson, public function was identified as one of several 
factors or guiding principles to be utilized in a balancing 
analysis, not as a “test” which by itself would identify the 
presence or absence of state action. See infra, III(A)(1). 
Accordingly, it was not necessary to determine whether, if a 
public function test standing alone exists after Edmonson, 
exclusivity would still be required. 


Here, the state has had exclusive control over the stoppage 
of an injured worker’s medical benefits since 1984. Without 
the consent of an injured worker or an Order of a workers’ 
compensation judge, those benefits were required to continue. 
Even if exclusivity is required, therefore, Plaintiffs satisfy the 
test. ‘3

an element which properly belongs in the public function 
analysis.
