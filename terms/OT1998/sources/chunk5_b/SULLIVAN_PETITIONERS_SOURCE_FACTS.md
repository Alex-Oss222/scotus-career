# Sullivan — additional attributed source facts from petitioners' principal merits brief

**Source-only handoff for fresh neutral validation.** This file reports the contents of the petitioners' November 1998 brief in No. 97-2000, saved as `sullivan_08._Petitioners_Brief_djvu.txt`, from the [Archive records item](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0186). The complete saved text was read in consecutive segments, including footnotes and front matter. It makes no ruling on preservation, actor status, entitlement, process, scope, remedy or any Justice's position. Arguments and factual characterizations remain attributed to petitioners, not adopted findings. Present-matter Supreme Court procedural recitations are excluded. Earlier post-divergence Supreme Court descriptions in the source are not admitted as governing law; actual entering law must come from the authorized public-law copies.

Printed page references below refer to the brief. The saved OCR contains incomplete lines and corruption in several places. This supplement relies on legible passages, quotes no reconstructed text, and draws no inference that an item absent from the OCR was absent from the filed paper. Existing lower-opinion and appendix extracts provide independent support for the core procedure and revised notices.

## Identity, record descriptions and requested relief

1. **Identity and parties (front matter ii; pp. 6–7).** Petitioners are eight private carriers. The brief identifies American Manufacturers, Commercial Union, Continental Casualty, Donegal Mutual, Hartford Fire, Insurance Company of North America, United States Fidelity and Guaranty, and Zurich. It reports caption corrections and a stipulation substituting Insurance Company of North America for CIGNA. The School District and public administering officials are defendants below and respondents here. This description supplies party roles, not any unquoted disposition of another petition.

2. **Claim and relief below (pp. 6–7).** The brief reports requests for declaratory and injunctive relief, restoration of benefits, compensatory damages and punitive damages. It identifies the requested defendant class of insurers and self-insurers, in addition to the requested plaintiff class. These are requested classes and remedies, not certification or awards.

3. **The two presented issues and the express scope limitation (pp. 29–30).** Petitioners present private-insurer state action and withholding pending utilization review. They expressly say they have not sought review of the other features of the Third Circuit's process ruling. The exact scope passage is already mechanically copied in `EXACT_PASSAGES.md` under “Sullivan petitioners scope p30.” This is a source statement of the appeal they seek, not a decision that the Court must reach or may not reach a particular argument.

4. **Argument for reaching due process after a private-actor ruling (p. 30 n.15).** Petitioners urge consideration of due process even if they prevail on state action. They report that SWIF conceded state-actor status below and the School District's status was unresolved. They argue that different withholding rules for public and private participants would disrupt the system and that private carriers could bear part of public participants' excess-payment reimbursement costs through the assessments financing the special fund under §999. These are the claimed reasons for broader reach, not a finding of jurisdiction, standing to obtain another party's relief, unavoidable cost or a preserved individual challenge by every defendant.

5. **Requested disposition (p. 50).** Petitioners seek reversal. They ask for reversal of the Third Circuit's severance/invalidity judgment in its entirety if they prevail on both state action and due process, or on due process alone. If they prevail only on private state action and the Court does not reach due process, they ask for reversal as applied to private insurers and employers, leaving those parties free to withhold as the statute permits. Their conclusion does not request an award of money, factual findings about individual treatments or automatic resolution of the distinct reconsideration-fee claim.

## System facts as described by petitioners

6. **Private participation (pp. 2–3).** The brief says employers must secure coverage through a private insurer, the state fund or self-insurance; private insurers choose whether to sell this coverage and which employers to underwrite. It characterizes nondictated policy features as contractual and the system as financed without public tax dollars. These statements are part of its state-action argument. They do not independently decide the constitutional character of a specific public defendant.

7. **Initiation and Bureau processing (pp. 4–5; 22–23 n.11).** Petitioners describe an independently initiated request, the thirty-day payment/request period, facial screening and random assignment of a URO. The footnote details checks for names and addresses, injury date, provider specialty, treatment and dates under review, explanation of the request, statutory effective-date coverage, duplicate requests, correspondence between treatment and provider, a facial reasonableness/necessity issue and acceptance of a work-related injury. They characterize these as ministerial and deny substantive assessment of the request's merits. That characterization must remain separate from the described screening acts.

8. **Different uses of “supersedeas” (pp. 17–18 n.8).** Petitioners object to the lower court's term, saying other Pennsylvania provisions use it for a compensation judge's order suspending an award, while the challenged §531(5) operates without such an order by tolling payment when review is requested. This is a specific legal-characterization dispute, not evidence that utilization review is absent or that payment withholding is mandatory.

9. **Payment incentives and recovery (pp. 3–4, 21, 39–43).** Petitioners say insurers cannot recoup erroneous payments from providers, but may obtain reimbursement from a special fund financed by assessments on insurers and self-insurers. They cite the cost of initial review borne by the insurer regardless of outcome, retroactive payment and ten-percent annual interest when the treatment is upheld, and the insurer's proof burden under 34 Pa. Code §127.471(b). The independent statutory/regulatory text or lower record should be used if a final rule turns on the exact conditions of any one protection. The brief's inference that these incentives adequately discourage unfounded requests is advocacy, not a fact finding.

10. **Eligibility and scope (pp. 30, 35–37).** Petitioners separate general eligibility for compensation from entitlement to reimbursement for a particular reasonable-and-necessary treatment. They say invocation of utilization review concedes work-related eligibility and that the URO does not decide whether disability continues. They emphasize provider choice after the first ninety days. Page 37 n.19 acknowledges that the statute authorizes prospective and concurrent review as well as retrospective review; it explains that the insurer may not know the treatment in advance unless preapproval was requested. Thus their retrospective-bill argument is not a factual assertion that every authorized review concerns a completed past service.

11. **Continuing treatment (pp. 41–42).** The brief acknowledges that payment uncertainty can affect future treatment, while disputing equivalence between delayed payment and terminated care. It reports that at least five named plaintiffs continued disputed treatment months or years after a review request, citing JA 29, 30, 34, 36 and 38, and that one paid out of pocket, citing JA 42. It argues that other-provider access and retroactive payment protect workers. Those are identified examples and inferences, not proof that no named plaintiff lost needed care or that a replacement provider was actually accessible to each one.

12. **Balance-billing description (p. 42 n.25).** Petitioners cite §531(7) and 34 Pa. Code §127.211 in saying that providers may not collect the unreimbursed difference from an employee for treatment determined unreasonable or unnecessary. This reported protection is distinct from the brief's example of a patient choosing to purchase treatment out of pocket. The brief alone is not used here to resolve any tension or to define every lawful patient-provider arrangement.

## Distinct arguments actually pressed

13. **Actor attribution (pp. 11–29).** Petitioners argue that statutory authorization, extensive regulation and replacement of tort remedies do not themselves make private withholding state action. They invoke the distinction between state creation of a rule and attribution of a private act, deny an exclusively governmental payment function, deny compulsion or significant encouragement, and characterize Bureau assistance as ministerial. They distinguish *West v. Atkins* on the ground that the State there delegated its own constitutional medical-care duty; they do not claim Pennsylvania has such a duty to fund every worker's treatment. *Lugar*, *Jackson*, *Blum*, *Rendell-Baker*, *Flagg Brothers*, *Tulsa* and *West* are their principal identified authorities. The supplement does not endorse their account of any holding.

14. **No deprivation: two distinct grounds (pp. 32–38).** First, petitioners invoke *O'Bannon* to argue that the workers cannot demand procedures for the payment rights of third-party providers merely because delay indirectly affects their treatment. Second, they argue that reasonable-and-necessary treatment is a condition of entitlement, so utilization review determines initial eligibility for payment of that particular service rather than revoking a previously established legal right. They analogize to an initial benefits application while acknowledging in p. 37 n.20 that the Supreme Court had left the general applicants-for-benefits entitlement question unresolved. They do not simply deny every state-created interest in workers' compensation.

15. **Alternative procedural sufficiency (pp. 38–47).** Petitioners separately assume a deprivation and defend the process under *Mathews*, *Doehr* and *Brock*, asking that the insurer's private interest be considered along with workers' interests, error risk, additional safeguards and public interests. They describe review as a comparatively short delay, emphasize recovery and interest if treatment is approved, and argue that advance payment undermines cost containment because wrongful payments cannot be recovered from the provider. This is an alternative argument; it does not erase their threshold no-deprivation argument or their express exclusion of other process features from the requested review.

16. **Empirical and policy submissions are advocacy (pp. 39–45).** The brief cites studies and Pennsylvania insurance-industry/state communications concerning cost savings, the proportion of reviewed treatment found wholly or partly unreasonable or unnecessary, and reduced review requests after the lower judgment. It uses those claims to argue that advance payment would weaken useful review. These are party-supported empirical assertions and predictions, not independent adjudicated findings about the named workers, the reliability of each URO or the precise cost attributable to the challenged withholding rule. No conclusion that all questionable claims are fraudulent or that all continued treatment is unnecessary follows.

17. **Historical-practice argument (pp. 47–50).** Petitioners additionally rely on other jurisdictions' practices and the federal Longshore system to argue that withholding disputed payments pending determination is customary. They discuss particular exceptions in their cited survey rather than asserting that every State uses an identical scheme. This supplement adopts neither the survey's completeness nor the claimed constitutional consequence; a decision depending on an exact comparative statutory proposition requires its specific support.
