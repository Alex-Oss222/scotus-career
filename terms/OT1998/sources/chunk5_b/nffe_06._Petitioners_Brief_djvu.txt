STATEMENT 


A. The Federal Service Labor-Management Relations 
Statute 


1. Title VII of the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978, 
commonly known as the Federal Service Labor-Manage- 
ment Relations Statute (FSLMRS or Statute), regulates 
labor relations in the federal government. The FSLMRS 
is the first statutory codification of labor relations in the 
federal service. See Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Fire- 
arms v. FLRA, 464 U.S. 89, 91 (1983) (BATF).' Con- 
gress explicitly found that “the right of employees to

Order program was revised and continued by Exec. Order No. 
11,491, 3 C.F.R. 861 (1966-1970 comp.), as amended by Exec. Orders 
Nos. 11,616, 11,636 and 11,861, 3 C.F.R. 605, 634, 957 (1971-1975 
comp.). The various Executive Orders are reprinted in the Legisla-

Statute, Title VII of the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978, at 1211,

Hist.).

organize, bargain collectively, and participate through 
labor organizations of their own choosing in decisions 
which affect them . . . safeguards the public interest, . . . 
contributes to the effective conduct of public business, 
and . . . facilitates and encourages the amicable settle- 
ments of disputes between employees and their employers 
involving conditions of employment... .” 5 U.S.C. 
7101(a). Congress also stated that the Statute’s provi- 
sions “should be interpreted in a manner consistent with 
the requirement of an effective and efficient Government.” 
Id. at 7101(b). 


2. Under the Statute, agency-employers are required 
to bargain in good faith with the elected exclusive repre- 
sentative of employees concerning conditions of employ- 
ment. 5 U.S.C. 7116(a)(5). The term “conditions of 
employment” is defined broadly, and includes “personnel 
policies, practices, and matters, whether established by 
rule, regulation, or otherwise, affecting working condi- 
tions,” but does not extend to policies, practices, and 
matters relating to political activities or job classification, 
or to matters “specifically provided for by Federal stat- 
ute.” 5 U.S.C. 7103(a)(14). The FSLMRS further pre- 
scribes that labor and management have a “mutual obliga- 
tion” to “meet at reasonable times” for the purpose of 
arriving at “collective bargaining agreements” concerning 
conditions of employment. 5 U.S.C. 7103(a)(12), 
7114(a)(1). 


The Statute’s broad duty to bargain is subject to two 
expressly stated exceptions. First, the duty does not ex- 
tend to proposals that are “inconsistent with any Federal 
law or any Government-wide rule or regulation” or with 
agency rules or regulations for which there is a “compel- 
ling need.” 5 U.S.C. 7117(a)(1), (2). Second, the 
Statute excepts certain management rights from the obli- 
gation to bargain. 5 U.S.C. 7106(a). The enumerated 
management rights are “subject to subsection (b)” of 
Section 7106, which provides that an agency-employer 
must negotiate about “procedures” agency managers will 


4 


observe in exercising their authority and “appropriate 
arrangements” for employees who are adversely affected by 
management's exercise of its rights. 5 U.S.C. 7106 
(b) (2), (3) 


3. The Federal Labor Relations Authority (FLRA or 
the Authority) is an independent, three-member body 
created by the Statute to perform a role in federal govern- 
ment labor relations similar to that performed by the 
National Labor Relations Board in the private sector. 
BATF, 464 US. at 97. The Statute specifically assigns the 
FLRA the responsibility to determine whether particular 
bargaining proposals fall within the statutory duty to 
bargain. 5 U.S.C. 7105(a)(2)(B). 


If the FLRA finds a proposal negotiable, the parties 
are required to bargain in good faith over its inclusion in 
a collective bargaining agreement. If negotiations reach 
impasse, “either party may request the Federal Service 
Impasses Panel to consider the matter” (5 U.S.C. 7119 
(b)(1)) and “take whatever action is necessary and not 
inconsistent with [the Statute] to resolve the impasse” (id. 
at 7119(c)(5)(B) (iii) ), including, where appropriate, re- 
quiring the parties to include a proposal in the contract. 
See Veterans Admin. Med. Ctr., Tampa, Fla. v. FLRA, 
675 F.2d 260, 265 n.9 (11th Cir. 1982). When issuing 
such an order, the Federal Service Impasses Panel (Panel 
or FSIP) considers the reasonableness of any proposal at 
issue in determining what contract language will be im- 
posed. See id. 


B. The Proceedings in This Case and Relevant Prior Pro- 
ceedings 


1. Petitioner National Federation of Federal Em- 
ployees, Local 1309 (NFFE or the Union), is the exclu- 
Sive bargaining representative of certain employees of the 
United States Geological Survey of the Department of the 
Interior (the Survey). During negotiation of a collective 
bargaining agreement, the Union proposed that the Survey 


5 


agree that, during the term of the agreement, it would be 
obligated “to negotiate on negotiable matters not covered 
by the provisions of this agreement.” NFFE Pet. App. 3a. 


The Survey contended that the proposal was not within 
its duty to bargain. Pet. App. 3a-4a. In so contending, it 
relied upon the Fourth Circuit’s rejection of a holding of 
the FLRA that the FSLMRS requires an agency to bar- 
gain over union-initiated midterm proposals. Social Sec. 
Admin. v. FLRA, 956 F.2d 1280 (4th Cir. 1992) (SSA). 


The Union then commenced unfair labor practice pro- 
ceedings before the FLRA. The Authority, relying on its 
previous decisions and the decision of the D.C. Circuit in 
National Treasury Employees Union v. FLRA, 810 F.2d 
295 (D.C. Cir. 1987) (NTEU), held that the Survey had 
violated the Statute in refusing to negotiate. Pet. App. 
lla-23a. The FLRA noted (id. at 17a) that it had pre- 
viously rejected the Fourth Circuit’s SSA decision. In 
addition, it distinguished SSA from this case because SSA 
had only held that there was no statutory obligation to 
bargain midterm; the court had not reached the issue of 
whether a union could negotiate for that right. /d. at 
17a-18a. 


2. The Survey petitioned for review in the Fourth 
Circuit, the Authority filed a cross-application for enforce- 
ment, and the Union intervened in support of the FLRA. 
The court granted the Survey’s petition and denied the 
Authority’s cross-application, finding the case controlled 
by its SSA ruling and its decision in United States Depart- 
ment of Energy v. FLRA, 106 F.3d 1158 (4th Cir. 
1997) (DOE). Pet. App. 8a-10a. 


a. In SSA, the Fourth Circuit held, directly in conflict 
with the D.C. Circuit’s decision in NTEU, that the Statute 
imposes no obligation upon agencies to bargain midterm 
over union-initiated proposals. In reaching its decision 
that agencies may be required to bargain during the term 
of a collective bargaining :greement regarding subjects 


6 


not covered by the parties’ agreement, the N7EU court 
had relied on the FSLMRS’s text—including its “broad 
and unqualified” (810 F.2d at 299) definition of the term 
“collective bargaining agreement”—as well as the Statute’s 
design and history and “basic principles of labor law” (id. 
at 301). 


The Fourth Circuit’s contrary SSA ruling stated that a 
“textual analysis” of the Statute did not yield a “definitive” 
answer to an agency's obligation to engage in midterm 
negotiations. 956 F.2d at 1284. But the court nonethe- 
less determined that the FSLMRS “seemed” to “strongly 
suggest[]” that Congress contemplated that the duty to 
bargain in good faith would extend to “only one, basic 
agreement.” Jd. In reaching this conclusion, the court 
relied upon a passage in a Senate report that had accom- 
panied a version of the Statute that did not become law. 
See id. at 1284-85. 


Moreover, the SSA court took issue with the D.C. Cir- 
cuit’s reliance on well-settled private sector precedent recog- 
nizing a union’s right to initiate midterm bargaining. See 
id. at 1286-88. Finally, the court’s opinion contained an 
extended policy analysis of union-initiated midterm bar- 
gaining, and concluded that to permit such negotiations 
would contravene Congress’ goal of achieving an effective 
and efficient government. 


b. In DOE, the Fourth Circuit extended the rationale 
of SSA, determining that it actually precluded a federal 
agency even from negotiating over a contract provision 
that would permit union-initiated midterm bargaining. 
The FLRA had found the proposal negotiable, reasoning 
that SSA addressed only whether the Statute imposed on 
agencies an obligation to engage in union-initiated mid- 
term bargaining, but left open whether a union could 
obtain such a right through bargaining. United States 
Dep't of Energy, Washington, D.C. and AFGE Local 
1995, 51 F.L.R.A. 124, 127-28 (1995), rev’d, 106 F.3d 
1158 (4th Cir. 1997). 


7 


The court of appeals found the FLRA’s distinction 
unpersuasive on the grounds that midterm bargaining is 
at odds with the policies underlying the FSLMRS and is 
wholly contrary to congressional intent. See DOE, 106 
F.3d at 1163. In addition, the court asserted that the 
FLRA’s position would “vitiate SSA.” Id. The court rea- 
soned that because the FSIP followed the FLRA’s prece- 
dent regarding midterm bargaining, the Panel would in- 
variably impose midterm bargaining clauses on federal 
agencies and thus permit unions to “circumvent SSA.” 
Id. at 1164. 


c. In the present case, the court of appeals rejected the 
efforts of the FLRA and the intervenor Union to distin- 
guish SSA and DOE, the rationales of which it found 
controlling. Pet. App. 8a-10a. The court thus found the 
Union’s proposal nonnegotiable. /d. 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


As the D.C. Circuit pointed out in NTEU, Congress 
determined, in crafting the FSLMRS, that “collective bar- 
gaining contributes to stability in federal labor-manage- 
ment relations, and to effective and efficient government.” 
810 F.2d at 300. Indeed, Congress could not have been 
more explicit when it found that the right of employees 
to “bargain collectively” “safeguards the public interest” 
and “contributes to the effective conduct of public busi- 
ness.” 5 U.S.C. 7101 (a). 


In the face of these congressional findings, the court 
below held that federal employers have no obligation to 
negotiate over a proposal that would have permitted 
union-initiated midterm bargaining. In so ruling, the 
Fourth Circuit pointed to its earlier decision in SSA, 
which concluded that federal employers have no obliga- 
tion, under the FSLMRS, to negotiate over proposals ini- 
tiated by a labor organization during the term of a collec- 
tive bargaining agreement. The court also cited its DOE 
holding which extended the SSA rationale and determined 


that a federal agency is actually precluded even from ne- 
gotiating over a contract provision that would permit 
union-initiated midterm bargaining. The premise under- 
lying the Fourth Circuit’s trilogy of midterm bargaining 
decisions—that federal employers have no obligation, 
under any circumstances, to negotiate over union-initiated 
midterm proposals—cannot be sustained. 


1. The union proposal at issue in this case is nego- 
tiable because, as the Statute’s text and structure make 
clear, that proposal merely reflects the agency’s statutory 
obligation to engage in midterm bargaining. 


a. As this Court has recognized with regard to the 
Statute’s scheme, an important goal of the FSLMRS was 
to create greater equality between labor and management. 
See BATF, 464 U.S. at 92. To that end, Congress cre- 
ated a statutory structure that establishes a broadly de- 
fined duty to bargain over working conditions—a duty 
that is subject only to express and narrow statutory excep- 
tions. Thus, the Statute extends the duty to bargain to 
all “conditions of employment,” a term expansively de- 
fined. 5 U.S.C. 7103(a) (14). 


Examination of the pertinent statutory text provides 
abundant evidence that an agency is required under the 
Statute to bargain over union-initiated midterm proposals. 
The FSLMRS requires that labor and management bar- 
gain over conditions of employment for the purpose of 
achieving “collective bargaining agreements.” 5 U.S.C. 
7114(a)(1): see also id. at 7114(a)(4), (b)(1). It is 
the Statute’s definition of “collective bargaining agree- 
ment” that answers the critical issue presented by this 
case. 


The key term “collective bargaining agreement” is very 
broadly defined as any negotiated “agreement entered into 
as a result of collective bargaining.” 5 U.S.C. 7103(a)(8). 
The definition contains no exception for midterm agree- 
ments and provides no statutory basis whatsoever for con- 
cluding that an agency’s bargaining obligation is in any 


9 


way limited to negotiating only one “basic” agreement. 
The Statute, therefore, establishes that a midterm agree- 
ment is, by definition, a “collective bargaining agreement” 
and thus fully subject to the duty to negotiate. See Penn- 
sylvania Dep't of Corrections v. Yeskey, 118 S. Ct. 1952, 
1954 (1998) (Yeskey) (statutory coverage definition 
governs in absence of an explicit exception). 


b. The Fourth Circuit’s midterm bargaining analysis 
inappropriately relied on a fragment of legislative history 
from a Senate report that accompanied a bill that did not 
become law. The report language cited never addressed 
the obligation of an agency to bargain over union-initiated 
midterm proposals. In addition, as this Court has clearly 
instructed, no weight may be given to legislative history— 
particularly a “snippet of legislative history’——“that is in 
no way anchored in” or explained by “the text of the 
statute.” Shannon v. United States, 512 U.S. 573, 583 
(1994). Hence, it was entirely improper for the court 
of appeals to use a rejected Senate report to engraft onto 
the Statute an implied exception for bargaining over union- 
initiated midterm proposals. 


c. The Fourth Circuit’s position regarding union- 
initiated midterm bargaining not only fails to hew to the 
relevant text of the Statute, it also radically deviates— 
unjustifiably—from bedrock principles of applicable pri- 
vate sector labor law. From its inception, language in the 
Nations! Labor Relations Act (NLRA) that is highly 
analogous to that in the FSLMRS has been construed as 
requiring an employer to bargain with a labor organiza- 
tion during the term of a collective agreement. In NLRB 
v. Jacobs Manufacturing Co.. 196 F.2d 680 (2d Cir. 
1952) (Jacobs), the court of appeals held unequivocally 
that such a duty exists under the NLRA with regard to 
matters not waived by the union or covered by th» par- 
ties’ collective agreement. That determination—adopted 
by every court of appeals that has subsequently addressed 
the issue—is now “fully integrated into the fabric of labor 


10 


law.” NTEU, 810 F.2d at 300. The Fourth Circuit's 
reasons for repudiating the Jacobs principle were entirely 
unpersuasive and largely based on the court’s own policy 
views that union-initiated midterm bargaining is undesir- 
able. 


d. The Fourth Circuit further erroneously determined 
that union-initiated midterm bargaining would be at odds 
with the Statute’s statement that the FSLMRS must be 
read in a way consistent with an “effective and efficient 
government.” 5 U.S.C. 7101(b). The court of appeals’ 
assumption that such bargaining inherently contributes to 
inefficiency flies in the face of Congress’ explicit contrary 
determination that collective bargaining “contributes to 
the effective conduct of public business... .” Id. at 
7101(a)(1). In making its decision about whether union- 
initiated midterm proposals are subject to the duty to bar-

This Court has instructed that “{c]ollective bargaining 
is a continuing process,” involving, among other things, 
the resolution of “new problems not covered by existing 
agreements .. . .” Conley v. Gibson, 355 U.S. 41, 46 
(1957). Consistent with this view, Congress, in fashion- 
ing the Federal Service Labor-Management Relations 
Statute, created a flexible collective bargaining scheme and 
expressly recognized that collective bargaining is integral 
to the effective administration of the federal civil service. 
Congress explicitly made the key policy determinations 
that “statutory protection of the right of employees to 
organize, bargain collectively, and participate through or- 
ganizations of their own choosing in decisions which affect 
them . . . safeguards the public interest,” “contributes to 
the effective conduct of public business,” and, moreover, 
“facilitates and encourages the amicable settlement of dis-

ditions of employment.” 5 U.S.C. 7101(a)(1). 


The midterm bargaining analysis of the court below ig- 
nores the dynamic nature of collective bargaining and, 
more important, seriously conflicts with the fundamental 
policy choices Congress made in the FSLMRS. With rea-

with the court’s policy views about collective bargaining, 
the Fourth Circuit has declared that any union-initiated 
midterm bargaining is unlawful. In the court's view, only 
management may initiate changes during the term of an 
agreement. Thus, if an unanticipated problem arises in 
the workplace—discovery of radon in a government build- 
ing, for example-—-a federal sector union is powerless to 
respond by initiating a bargaining proposal on behalf of 
the employees whom the union is statutorily charged with 
representing. Indeed, the court of appeals has gone so 
far as to bar an agency employer even from negotiating 


about a contract provision authorizing union-initiated mid-

The court of appeals’ decision is deeply flawed in two 
respects. First, contrary to the holding of the Fourth 
Circuit, examination of the Statute, its scheme and the 
relevant case law establishes that federal employers are 
obligated to bargain over union-initiated midterm pro-

that statutory obligation and thus is fully negotiable pur- 
suant to the Statute. In any event, even if the FSLMRS 
does not confer a right on unions to initiate midterm bar- 
gaining, the union’s proposal is at least negotiable: it is 
not inconsistent with the Statute or any other law or ap-

ception to the FSLMRS’s broad duty to bargain.

A. The Text and Structure of the FSLMRS Establish 
That an Agency-Employer Bears an Obligation To 
Bargain over Union-Initiated Midterm Proposals 


In determining that federal employer agencies have no 
obligation to negotiate over union-initiated midterm bar- 
gaining proposals, the Fourth Circuit in SSA reasoned 
that the Statute “seems to contemplate” that an employer's 
bargaining obligation extends to “only one, basic, agree- 
ment.” 956 F.2d at 1284. That conclusion, however, 
cannot be squared with the FSLMRS’s text and structure. 


1. This Court has observed that in designing the Stat- 
ute, “Congress unquestionably intended to strengthen the 
positions of federal unions... .” BATF, 464 U.S. at 107. 
Congress’ firm commitment to promoting collective bar- 
gaining in the federal sector is made clear by the Statute’s 
explicit finding that bargaining “safeguards the public in- 
terest” and “contributes to the effective conduct of public 
business.” 5 U.S.C. 7101(a)(1)(A), (B). To effectuate 
these policy choices, Congress created a statutory frame- 
work that contains a broadly defined duty to bargain over 
conditions of employment—-a duty that Congress made 
subject only to express and narrow statutory exceptions.”

midterm bargaining is seriously defective because it refuses 
to accept that union-initiated midterm bargaining can 
clearly enhance the public interest in an effective and effi-

Midterm negotiations pcrmit labor and management to 
address unanticipated matters, such as the discovery of 
life-threatening health hazards in the workplace in the form 
of radon, asbestos, or outbreaks of Legionnaire’s disease. 
Midterm negotiations also permit the parties to respond

at furthering the public’s interest in good government. 
To illustrate, in April 1998, the President issued a direc-

interest in carrying out that worthy goal if a labor organ-

Even assuming that the FSLMRS does not confer a right 
on labor organizations to initiate midterm bargaining, a 
union proposal—such as the one at issue in this case—that 
would authorize such negotiations is within the federal 
employer’s obligation to bargain. In other words, the Stat- 
ute does not, contrary to the holding of the court below, 
bar unions from attempting to negotiate for the right to 
engage in union-initiated midterm bargaining. 


This conclusion is firmly supported by the text and 
structure of the Statute. As explained supra, under the 
FSLMRS, the obligation to bargain extends to all pro- 
posals concerning conditions of employment unless they 
either contravene a management right or are inconsistent 
with law, government-wide regulations, or agency regula- 
tions for which a compelling need exists. See 5 U.S.C. 
7106, 7117(a). Simply stated, a contractual provision 
obligating an agency to negotiate over union-initiated pro- 
posals does not fall within any of these exceptions and, 
therefore, is a mandatory subject of bargaining. 


Our position that a union can bargain for a right that 
is not conferred by the Statute is buttressed by this Court’s 
decision in BATF. In that case, the Court instructed that, 
although union negotiators were not entitled to receive 
travel and per diem payments under the FSLMRS, “unions 
may presumably negotiate for such payments through 
collective bargaining.” Jd. at 107 n.17. The D.C. Circuit 
subsequently followed that instruction and agreed with the 
FLRA and NTEU that a contract proposal for travel and 
per diem payments fell within the duty to bargain because 
it did not conflict with applicable law or regulation.

In its DOE decision, the Fourth Circuit determined 
that a contract clause providing for union-initiated mid- 
term bargaining was not negotiable because such midterm 
bargaining was “inconsistent” with the Statute. 106 F.3d 
at 1164. That ruling was plainly erroneous. First, the 
court of appeals never identified any law or regulation 
that would preclude negotiating over a proposal seeking 
the right to midterm bargaining. Instead, the court pointed 
to 5 U.S.C. 7101(b) and grounded its decision on the 
court’s view of what constitutes an “effective and efficient 
government.” As discussed above, the Statute’s reference 
to an “effective and efficient government” is not a catch-all 
standard for adjudging whether a matter falls within the 
duty to bargain. Hence, the Fourth Circuit’s objections to 
the adoption of a proposal like that advanced by the Union 
in this case go to the merits of the proposal, not whether 
the proposal is negotiable as a matter of law. 


Moreover, as also shown above, the court of appeals’ 
“effective and efficient” government analysis does not with- 
stand examination. The extreme stance of the court below 
would bar an agency from negotiating over—much less 
agreeing to—a contract provision authorizing union- 
initiated midterm bargaining even when the agency 
wanted to do so. There are, as indicated, frequent situa- 
tions where a contract proposal sanctioning union-initiated 
midterm bargaining would foster management's interest 
in flexibility. During term negotiations management may 
wish to change conditions of employment; it may, for in- 
stance, be interested in an experimental trial period of 
modified starting and quitting times. The union might 
well be amenable to considering the change if it believed 
that it would have a chance to make a midterm proposal 
—perhaps at a defined and mutually agreed upon time— 
modifying the arrangement. Without that option, however, 


32 


the employees’ representative may be unabie to convince 
its constituency to agree to the change. In short, manage- 
ment’s interest in experimenting as to conditions of em- 
ployment would be thwarted by the Fourth Circuit’s rigid 
view of midterm bargaining—a result that would hardly 
serve an “effective and efficient” government. 


To summarize, even if there is no statutory duty to 
negotiate over a union’s midterm proposals, a contract 
provision authorizing such bargaining is within the Stat- 
ute’s obligation to bargain because it is not inconsistent 
with any applicable law. Endorsement of the skewed col- 
lective bargaining regime prescribed by the Fourth Cir- 
cuit would hobble the role of employees’ collective bar- 
gaining representatives in a way that cannot be squared 
with the FSLMRS’s fundamental goal of creating greater 
equality between labor and management. See BATF, 464 
U.S. at 92.
