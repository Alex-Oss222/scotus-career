INTEREST OF THE AMICUS CURIAE’ 


The Anti-Defamation League (ADL) was organized in 
1913 to advance good will and mutual understanding 
among Americans of all creeds and races, and to combat 
racial and religious prejudices in the United States. The 
ADL has always adhered to the principle that these 
goals, and indeed the general stability of our democ- 
racy, are best served through the separation of Church 
and State and the preservation of the fundamental 
right to free exercise of religion. Over the last several 
decades, in an effort to further this value, the ADL has 
participated in almost every Establishment Clause case 
to reach this Court. 


ARGUMENT 


I. THE WISCONSIN SUPREME COURT'S DECISION 
IN JACKSON v. BENSON MANIFESTLY VIOLATES 
THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE 

The principles embodied in the Establishment Clause 
are far too important to be subjected to a referendum 
on the quality of our inner city public schools. These 
principles cannot and should not be compromised, not- 
withstanding the difficult problems state and local gov- 
ernments admittedly must confront regarding the state 
of public education in our urban communities. The

Given the uncanny similarities between this case and 
this Court’s decision in Nyquist, amicus curiae finds the 
Wisconsin Supreme Court’s decision wholly indefensi- 
ble. Nyquist involved a tuition-reimbursement plan for 
low income parents whose children attended private 
secular and sectarian schools. Nyquist, 413 U.S. at 780. 
This Court declared the New York statute at issue 
in Nyquist unconstitutional precisely because the plan 


4 


provided no guarantee that public funds would be used 
exclusively for “secular” and “nonideological” purposes. 
Id. The amended Milwaukee school voucher program 
similarly provides no guarantee that public funds will 
not be used for pervasively sectarian purposes. The 
ability of parents to use vouchers at non-sectarian 
schools simply does not relieve the state of respon- 
sibility for constitutional encroachment when state 
funds are used by, and at the discretion of, the religious 
institutions which comprise the overwhelming majority 
of participating schools. Nyquist also rejected the notion 
that transferring public funds to pervasively sectarian 
institutions via the parents of participating children 
rendered the otherwise unconstitutional plans constitu- 
tional. 


In addition, since the Wisconsin Supreme Court in 
Jackson misapplied the three-prong Establishment 
Clause compliance test first articulated in Lemon v. 
Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602 (1971), it is necessary to 
remedy that critical error. The court failed to present 
any meaningful analysis of statutory purpose, beyond 
the summary statement that a secular purpose is 
presumed under Mueller v. Allen, 463 U.S. 388, 394 
(1983). Amicus curiae submits that any suggestion that 
the designation “educational choice” magically trans- 
forms the amended Milwaukee school voucher program 
into an educationally neutral program is a breach of 
constitutional integrity. Axiomatically, it follows then, 
teachers can lead sectarian prayers in the classroom as 
long as they designate the exercise as a “classroom 
choice.” The Jackson court also failed to consider the 
purpose of the amended Milwaukee school voucher pro- 
gram independent of the purpose of the original Mil-

waukee voucher program, which reveals statutory modi- 
fications made for the sole purpose of advancing reli- 
gion and strengthening religious institutions. 


The Wisconsin Supreme Court, in its analysis of the 
effects of the amended Milwaukee school voucher 
program under Lemon’s second prong, focused exclu- 
sively on the “neutrality” of the statute and failed to 
consider the coercion test and endorsement test this 
Court has previously employed to assess the effects of 
a statute or activity. See Lee v. Weisman, 505 U.S. 577 
(1992); County of Allegheny v. ACLU, 492 U.S. 573 
(1989). While neutrality is unquestionably a facet of 
Establishment Clause analysis, it has never been held 
out as the sole controlling test for determining whether 
a statute has the effect of advancing religion under 
Lemon. Over the past decade, this Court has introduced 
additional tests which should have been applied to the 
amended Milwaukee school voucher program. The Wis- 
consin Supreme Court in Jackson, therefore, erred in 
relying solely on the neutrality test. Had the Wisconsin 
Supreme Court applied systematically the full range of 
tests furnished by this Court to ensure compliance with 
the effects prong of Lemon, it would have concluded 
that the amended Milwaukee school voucher program 
has the effect of advancing religion. 


Clearly, there is dissension and a lack of clarity 
among the federal circuits and state courts regarding 
current Establishment Clause jurisprudence. The Fifth 
Circuit, in Jones v. Clear Creek Indep. Sch. Dist., 977 
F.2d 963 (5th Cir. 1992), applied the three prongs of 
Lemon, and also applied the coercion and endorsement 
tests independent of its Lemon analysis. More recently, 


6 


however, the Ninth Circuit, in Doe v. Madison Sch. 
Dist. No. 321, 147 F.3d 832 (9th Cir. 1998), failed to 
consider either the neutrality or endorsement tests, but 
did apply the coercion test independent of the Lemon 
test. In the aggregate, these decisions significantly con- 
flict with the analysis used by the Wisconsin Supreme 
Court, which strategically incorporated the neutrality 
test into its application of Lemon’s effects. prong, but 
disregarded both the coercion and endorsement tests 
announced by this Court. For the sake of Establishment 
Clause jurisprudential consistency, courts cannot be 
allowed to freely pick and choose which precedents and 
doctrines they wish to apply in a given case. 


In addition, the “entitlements” furnished by the 
amended Milwaukee school voucher program provide a 
financial incentive for existing sectarian schools to 
expand, and for new schools to open, in reliance upon 
the public for funding. This Court specifically discussed 
this potential for expansion in Nyquist: 


... [Aid programs of any kind tend to become 
entrenched, to escalate in cost, and to generate 
their own aggressive constituencies. . . . In this 
situation, where the underlying issue is the 
deeply emotional one of Church-State relation- 
ships, the potential for seriously divisive politi- 
cal consequences needs no elaboration. 
Nyquist, 413 U.S. at 797-98. If this Court does not act 
in this case, the inevitable entrenchment and escalation 
of the amended school voucher program in Milwaukee, 
and other programs nationwide, threatens to entangle 
the government with religious institutions well beyond 
constitutionally permissible bounds. To the extent that 
such entanglement is permitted, it will, as a practical 


7 


matter, be virtually impossible to modify or restrict the 
funding of pervasively sectarian schools without making 
budgetary decisions on a sectarian basis. 


The Establishment Clause confusion, further exacer- 
bated by the Wisconsin Supreme Court in Jackson and 
evidenced by disparate goal-oriented analyses in lower 
court decisions, is in immediate need of redress. In the 
absence of this Court’s immediate action, the erosion of 
the Establishment Clause will likely be irreparable. 


Il. PUBLIC SCHOOLS ARE THE CORNERSTONE OF 
A DEMOCRACY 


Permitting the amended Milwaukee school voucher 
experiment to continue not only abridges the Establish- 
ment Clause, but also undermines the deep-rooted 
societal value of public education and permits state and 
local governments to delegate away their responsibility 
to educate the youth of this country and to inculcate 
them with community values. As the Court eloquently 
acknowledged over forty years ago: 


Today, education is perhaps the most important 
function of state and local governments. Com- 
pulsory school attendance laws and the great 
expenditures for education both demonstrate 
our recognition of the importance of education to 
our democratic society. It is required in the 
performance of our most basic public responsi- 
bilities, even service in the armed forces. It is 
the very foundation of good citizenship. Today it 
is a principal instrument in awakening the child 
to cultural values, in preparing him for later 
professional training, and in helping him to 
adjust normally to his environment... . 


8 


Brown v. Board of Educ., 347 U.S. 483, 493 (1954). The 
proponents of the amended Milwaukee voucher experi- 
ment ignore not only the special status public schools 
have as shapers of values, ideas and knowledge which 
are central to the notion of community and the ability 
to exercise fundamental constitutional rights, but also 
disregard almost half a century of jurisprudence recog- 
nizing this critical link between public education and 
the common good of our democracy.” 


Public education is fundamental to a democratic soci- 
ety, for it has responsibility for transmitting the very 
values and skills which sustain it. Commenting on pub- 
lic education in general, this Court has noted that “[t]he 
classroom is peculiarly the ‘marketplace of ideas.’” 
Keyishian v. Board of Regents, 385 U.S. 589, 603 
(1967). Certain lessons cannot be taught in an environ- 
ment filtered to exclude the vast majority of the popu- 
lace. Wygant v. Jackson Bd. of Educ., 476 U.S. 267, 315 
(1986) (Stevens, J., dissenting) (“[O)ne of the most im- 
portant lessons that the American public schools teach 
is that the diverse ethnic, cultural, and national back- 
grounds that have been brought together in our famous

Giving up on public schools by diverting public funds 
to private schools is not a community solution to a 
community problem. Government, acting in the interest 
of the public good, must act in a supportive way toward 
an institution as important as public schools rather 
than abandon the citizens and the public infrastructure 
of our urban communities. Whatever the solutions are 
to the problems of our public schools, they are to be 
found not in so-called: private school “choice,” but in 
unwavering support for both the public schools in our 
troubled urban communities and the parents and chil- 
dren who look to those schools to prepare them to 
realize some measure of the American dream. 


Providing public schools ranks at the very apex of the 
functions of a State. Yoder, 406 U.S. at 213. For this 
reason, virtually every state provides a mandate for 
public education for its children in its constitution. San 
Antonio Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 1, 111- 
112 (1973) (Marshall, J., dissenting) (“No other state 
function is so uniformly recognized as an essential ele- 
ment of our society’s well-being.”). Removing children 
from the public school mix and diverting them to 
private—let alone religious—institutions compromises 
this objective. The plan articulated in Milwaukee’s 
original voucher experiment reflects a shift of the 


10 


state’s responsibility for providing a public education to 
the private arena. The amended school voucher pro- 
gram would allow Wisconsin to abdicate further its 
fundamental role of providing its children a public 
education. Quite simply, paying for private education— 
which allows governments to escape all but their fi- 
nancial responsibility—does not achieve the same public 
good as providing a public education. Respondents fail 
to acknowledge the central responsibility public schools 
have in a democratic society. If public schools are 
troubled, the way to improve them, while promoting the 
public good, is to fix them, not to abandon them. 


Moreover, diverting public funds to private schools 
will not improve the overall education of ov children. 
It is true that the public schools in Milwaukee, as well 
as those in many of our other major cities, do not 
always achieve perfection in their goal of teaching our 
children. In contrast, the state-mandated data collected 
on the amended Milwaukee school voucher experiment 
shows mixed results at best. The Wisconsin Department 
of Public Instruction commissioned Professor John F. 
Witte of the University of Wisconsin-Madison Depart- 
ment of Political Science to evaluate the voucher ex- 
periment. 


In his reports, Witte and his researchers conclude 
that students participating in the original Milwaukee 
school voucher program did not perform better than 
their counterparts attending public schools. Witte 
reported that “[o]utcomes after five years of the Choice 
Program remain mixed.” John F. Witte, et al., Fifth- 
Year Report Milwaukee Parental Choice Program, Ex- 
ecutive Summary, Dept. Political Science and The 


11 


Robert M. La Follette Institute of Public Affairs at the 
Univ. of Wis.-Madison (Dec. 1995). Specifically, “read- 
ing scores increased in the first year, fell substantially 
in the second year, and have remained approximately 
the same in the next three years. . . . In math, choice 
students were essentially the same in the first two 
years, recorded a significant increase in the third year, 
and then significantly declined this last year.” Id. See 
also John F. Witte & Christopher A. Thorn, Fourth- 
Year Report Milwaukee Parental Choice Program, Ex- 
ecutive Summary, Dept. of Political Science and The 
Robert M. La Follette Institute of Public Affairs at the 
Univ. of Wis.-Madison (Dec. 1994). In contrast, the 
success of other states’ public education reforms shows 
that commitment to improving educational achieve- 
ment can be attained without sacrificing public school 
systems. As United States Secretary of Education, 
Richard W. Riley, has stated, “Vouchers are a pessi- 
mist’s response to the problems facing some of our 
public schvols. The way to improve schools is not to 
give up on them, but to raise academic standards, 
restore discipline, promote more parental involvement 
and get computers into the classroom.” Richard 
W. Riley, Address at Families First Forum (July 24, 
1996) (transcript available in U.S. Senate Web Page 
(http://www.senate.gov/~dpc/families_first/riley.htm])). 


At a time when parents and educators are demanding 
increased achievement and accountability in public 
education, it is no surprise that some state and local 
governments uncomfortable with tackling these chal- 
lenges are starting to turn to privatization as a pana- 
cea. The amended Milwaukee school voucher program 
provides the Wisconsin government a means to escape 


12 


a difficult responsibility by paying for public school 
students to attend private schools. The Wisconsin Leg- 
islature’s 1995 amendments to the voucher experiment 
cleverly eliminate the evidence of non-achievement and 
escape accountability by ending all funding for data 
collection and evaluations, effectively eradicating criti- 
cal study of the voucher experiment. See 1995 Wis. 
Legis. Serv. 27, §§ 4007(m), 4008(m). 


The amended Milwaukee school voucher program 
presents questions no less important than under what 
conditions a democracy can or cannot function. In the 
final analysis, the issue is how we perceive ou: col- 
lective responsibilities to each other. As has been sug- 
gested, 


Education is more important than ever as our 
nation adapts to tremendous changes in the 
economy, technology, and even the structure of 
our society. If providing the best possible educa- 
tion to all children remains something Ameri- 
cans value, then we owe it to ourselves to make 
sure our public school system is as strong as we 
can make it. In the end, public schools reflect 
the needs, desires, and concerns of the public 
itself—all of us. Our schools will only be as 
strong, as fair, as equitable, and as well funded 
as we demand that they be. 


School Funding: How much do we value good public 
education?, Our Children Mag., Nov./Dec. 1996. Unam- 
biguous support for an all-inclusive public school 
system is an absolute necessity for a free and demo- 
cratic society.

Hl. THIS COURT SHOULD SETTLE THE QUESTION 
NOW 

Because this case not only implicates fundamental 
constitutional principles, but also affects the settled 
expectations of millions of children, parents, educators 
and legislators, the Cour’ should consider the issues 
presented by the Wisconsin Supreme Court’s holding in 
Jackson now instead of deferring review to a later date. 
The constitutionality of voucher programs which remove 
children from public schools and place them in private 
institutions, including religious schools, is ripe for re- 
view. The possibility that the education of our nation’s 
‘children may be compromised warrants immediate 
action in and of itself. Additionally, deferment will be 
construed as tacit approval of Milwaukee’s amended 
school voucher program, and numerous other state and 
local governments will surely follow suit. In short, there 
will be no more opportune moment to provide an 
answer to the Establishment Clause questions on which 
this case hinges. 


Amicus curiae recognizes that the public education 
system in this country often struggles to achieve its 
educational goals and that many urban systems are 
undergoing restructuring. Government attempts to 
ameliorate the existing problems have sparked the 
current wave of experimentation with alternatives to 
public schools.* The amended Milwaukee school vouch-
