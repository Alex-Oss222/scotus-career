SUPPLEMENTAL BRIEF IN SUPPORT OF 
PETITION FOR A WRIT OF CERTIORARI 


Petitioners Warner Jackson ef al. submit this Supple- 
mental Brief to call to the Court’s attention an Order of 
the Wisconsin Supreme Court issued in this matter on the 


2 


day after the Petition for Certiorari was filed, to explain 
why they believe this Court’s jurisdiction to grant the 
August 31 Petition for Certiorari to be unaffected by that 
Order, and to ask this Court, should it deem such action 
appropriate in order to avoid a potential jurisdictional 
issue, to treat the Petition for Certiorari as if filed on the 
date of entry of the Wisconsin court’s Order, rather than 
the day before. 


BACKGROUND 


The Petition for Certiorari asks this Court to review 
the judgment of the Supreme Court of Wisconsin, entered 
on June 10, 1998, which upheld the constitutionality of 
the Amended Milwaukee Parental Choice Program 
(“Amended MPCP”). 


Sixteen days after judgment was entered, on June 26, 
one of the prevailing parties, intervening defendants Par- 
ents for School Choice et al. (“PSC”), filed a document 
styled “Motion for Clarification” (attached hereto as Ap- 
pendix A), in which PSC asked the court to clarify that 
its June 10 judgment allowed certain schoolchildren to 
participate in the Amended MPCP. PSC stated its belief 
that the eligibility of the children in question had already 
been established by the June 10 judgment, but requested 
the “clarification” because the state Department of Public 
Instruction had taken a different position. The issue on 
which clarification was sought is a purely state-law matter, 
related to a question briefly discussed by the Wisconsin 
court at the end of its opinion, see Appendix to Petition 
for Certiorari 56a-57a. It has no bearing on the federal 
constitutional question that is presented in this Court. 


Concerned that an amendment by the Wisconsin court 
of its June 10 judgment in response to the Motion for 
Clarification might conceivably create a question about 
the finality of the June 10 judgment, petitioners delayed 
filing their Petition for Certiorari in the hope that the 
Wisconsin Supreme Court would dispose of the motion 


3 


before the time to petition for certiorari expired on Sep- 
tember 8, thus permitting the filing of a Petition unclouded 
by any even arguable jurisdictional question. When by 
late August the Wisconsin court still had not acted, peti- 
tioners prepared to file an application for extension of 
time for their Petition for Certiorari, based on the pen- 
uency before the Wisconsin court of the Motion for 
Clarification. 


The filing of such an application was not necessary, 
however. The beginning of the new school year in Mil- 
waukee rendered the issue raised by the Motion for Clari- 
fication largely moot. Accordingly, on August 27 PSC 
withdrew its motion by filing a document styled “With- 
drawal of Motion for Clarification,” which is attached 
hereto as Appendix B. 


On the same day, counsel for petitioners verified with 
the office of the Clerk of the Wisconsin Supreme Court 
that the “Withdrawal of Motion for Clarification” had in- 
deed been filed. Counsel was further assured by the 
deputy clerk that the court would not issue any order 
pertaining to the withdrawal of the motion, but rather 
would simply return the record to the trial court. See 
Affidavit of Jeffrey J. Kassel (attached as Appendix EB), 
4. Petitioners therefore filed their Petition for Certio- 
rari on Monday, August 31. The Petition was docketed 
the same day. 


The next day, September 1, the Wisconsin Supreme 
Court issued an Order, attached hereto as Appendix C, in 
which it “granted” what it characterized as a “Motion to 
Withdraw the Motion for Clarification.” More important 
for present purposes, in the course of its Order the court 
stated that it had “construed” that motion “as a Motion 
for Reconsideration to Clarify the Opinion.” App. SA-11. 


Motions for reconsideration under Wisconsin Supreme 
Court practice are governed by Wis. Stat. § 809.64, which 
provides that “[a] party may seek reconsideration of the 
judgment or opinion of the supreme court by filing a 


4 


motion under § 809.14 for reconsideration within 20 days 
of the filing of the decision of the supreme court.” While 
PSC’s Motion for Clarification had been filed within the 
20-iay window for motions for reconsideration, it was not 
styled as a Motion for Reconsideration, and it neither 
relied upon nor made any reference to § 809.64." 


On September 3, the Wisconsin Supreme Court issued 
its Remittitur (attached hereto as Appendix D), in which 
it returned the record to the trial court. The Remittitur 
cited only the court’s “opinion filed on June 10, 1998,” 
and made no reference to any subsequent proceedings. 


ARGUMENT 


While petitioners believe it is their obligation to bring 
these developments to the attention of this Court, they 
submit that this Court’s jurisdiction to review the judg- 
ment of June 10 pursuant to the August 31 Petition for 
Certiorari is unaffected by the Wisconsin Supreme Court’s 
Order of September 1, notwithstanding its characterization 
of the Motion for Clarification as a “Motion for Recon- 
sideration.” 


1. The problem, to the extent there is one, arises from 
this Court’s longstanding practice of treating “{a] timely 
petition for rehearing . . . [as] operat[ing] to suspend the 
finality of the . . . [lower] court’s judgment, pending the 
court's further determination whether the judgment should 
be modified so as to alter its adjudication of the rights 
of the parties.” Missouri v. Jenkins, 495 U.S. 33, 46 
(1990) (quoting Department of Banking v. Pink, 317 
U.S. 264, 266 (1942)). Thus, “while the petition for 
rehearing is pending, there is no ‘judgment’ to be re- 
viewed.” Id. Under the most extreme view of things, 
PSC’s Motion for Clarification—which the Wisconsin Su- 
preme Court on September 1 announced it had “con- 


1PSC’s Motion for Clarification cited only Wis. Stat. § 809.14, 
which is the generic rule governing motions practice in the Wis- 
consin appellate courts. 


5 


strued” as a Motion for Reconsideration (the Wisconsin 
equivalent of a motion for rehearing under federal prac- 
tice)—-was still “pending” (notwithstanding its “with- 
drawal” by its proponent on August 27) until the Wis- 
consin court on September 1 “granted” what it described 
as a motion to withdraw it, and thus there was “no ‘judg- 
ment’ to be reviewed” when the Petition for Certiorari 
was filed on August 31. That view of the impact of the 
September 1 Order on this Court’s jurisdiction cannot be 
correct. 


It is, of course, understandable that this Court will 
decline to entertain a petition for certiorari during the 
pendency of a motion for rehearing, see, e.g., Craig v. 
United States, 298 U:S. 637 (1936) (mem.) (dismissing 
petition as “premature,” without prejudice to its renewal 
upon disposition of the motion for rehearing), and it is 
equally apparent why, as this Court’s Rule 13(3) pro- 
vides, the time to petition for certiorari is treated as run- 
ning from the disposition of a timely motion for rehearing. 
But it does not follow that where, as here, this Court has 
before it a petition to review a judgment that is unques- 
tionably final—and which, indeed, appeared to be final 
on the basis of everything in the record at the time the 
Petition for Certiorari was filed—its jurisdiction to review 
that judgment is negated by an order of the lower court 
issued after the filing of the Petition for Certiorari, which 
in fact confirmed the finality of the judgment sought to be 
reviewed. 


In the first place, this Court’s cases discussing the sus- 
pensive effect of a rehearing petition on the finality of the 
lower court judgment uniformly address the question of 
whether the time period for the filing of a petition for cer- 
tiorari should be extended. Those cases need not be read 
as holding that the filing of a petition for rehearing neces- 
sarily and invariably deprives this Court of jurisdiction 
to entertain a petition for certiorari that was filed “pre- 
maturely” during the pendency of a rehearing petition. 
That is a question these cases do not address, and at least 


6 


under the circumstances presented here there is no reason 
this Court cannot review the June 10 final judgment of 
the Wisconsin Supreme Court, as the Petition for Cer- 
tiorari asks it to do. 

Here, in the first place—unlike the situation this Court 
faced in Craig, supra—there is no petition for rehearing 
pending at the time the petition for certiorari comes be- 
fore this Court for decision. Even accepting as deter- 
minative the Wisconsin court’s characterization of PSC’s 
Motion for Clarification as a “Motion for Reconsidera- 
tion,” that motion has been disposed of, without change 
in the original judgment, since at least September 1, the 
day after the Petition for Certiorari was filed. The June 
10 final judgment the Petition for Certiorari asks this 
Court to review is still, without any question, the final 
judgment of the Wisconsin Supreme Court. 


Equally important, the Petition for Certiorari clearly 
was timely when it was filed on August 31—at least as 
far as could have been ascertained from the record as it 
existed on that date. It was only the next day that the 
Wisconsin Supreme Court announced that it had “con- 
strued” PSC’s Motion for Clarification as a Motion for 
Reconsideration. 


The pendency of the Motion for Clarification—even 
assuming it was indeed still pending on August 31, not- 
withstanding its withdrawal four days previously—in no 
way called into question the finality of the June 10 judg- 
ment. While a timely petition for rehearing suspends the 
finality of the lower court’s judgment during the time it 
is pending, regardless of its ultimate disposition, the same 
is not true where a motion merely seeks “clarification” of 
the judgment without asking the court “to ‘reconsider [a] 
question decided in the case’ in order to effect an ‘alter-

7 


ation of the rights adjudicated.’” United States v. Dieter, 
429 U.S. 6, 9 (1976) (quoting Pink, 317 U.S. at 266). 


Here, PSC merely asked the Wisconsin Supreme Court 
to clarify what it believed the court’s June 10 opinion 
already had held. See App. SA-5. Under such circum- 
stances, the finality of the original judgment, for purposes 
of the timing of a petition for certiorari, will be affected 
only if the lower court makes substantive changes in that 
judgment. Compare Robert L. Stern, Eugene Gressman, 
et al., Supreme Court Practice § 6.3 (7th ed. 1993) 
(“Time Calculation When Rehearing Sought Below”), 
with id., § 6.4 (“Time Calculation When Judgment Be- 
low Is Changed”) (citing cases).3 


What could not have been anticipated when petitioners 
filed their Petition for Certiorari on August 31 was that 
the Wisconsin court not only would issue an order in 
response to PSC’s withdrawal of its Motion for Clarifica- 
tion, but that in so doing the court would announce that 
it had construed that Motion as a motion for reconsider- 
ation. In sum, the Petition for Certiorari was indisputably 
timely when it was filed. To the extent that it now could 
be said to be premature, it was rendered so only by action 
of the court below subsequent to its filing.*

the fact that the import of that order, as well as that of

The June 10 judgment clearly is the Wisconsin Supreme

September 1 was well within the period for filing a peti-

Supreme Court. That 90-day period did not expire until 
September 8, and it is pure happenstance that the Peti- 
tion actually was filed late on the afternoon of August 
31 rather than early the next morning. 


This is an important case which fully merits this 
Court’s review. The Court should not allow its jurisdic - 
tion to be defeated under the circumstances presented.® 


CONCLUSION 


For the reasons stated above and in the Petition for 
Certiorari, the Petition should be granted.

In its June 10 decision, this Court made clear that it 
was aware both that thousands of children who were 
eligible for full tuition under the program had already 
enrolled in private schools when it was enjoined and that 
if they remained in their private schools they would now 
be ineligible if the injunction is lifted because they are 
already attending private schools. Stating that “[t]heir 


SA-4 


ineligibility is no fault of their own, but instead is solely 
a consequence of this litigation. Those children certainly 
are among the intended beneficiaries of this program. To 


require them to return to MPS for a year to reestablish 


eligibility would be manifestly inequitable and disruptive 
to the public schools, to the private schools, and most 
importantly, to the children themselves,” this Court dis- 
solved the injunction and held harmless the intended ben- 
eficiary school-children. Specifically, this Court said, “[i]n 
dissolving the injunction, we therefore remove the dis- 
ability the injunction placed on schoolchildren, so that 
with respect to educational status, eligibility under the 
amended MPCP is determined on the date the injunction 
was issued.” (4 113). 


On June 25, 1998, the Wisconsin Department of Pub- 
lic Instruction issued a document entitled “Milwaukee 
Parental School Choice Program Student Application 
Form 1998-1999 School Year,” a copy of which is at- 
tached to the affidavit of Daniel M. McKinley that is ap- 
pended to this motion. This document excludes from 
Program eligibility certain children who otherwise became 
eligible for the Program after the August 25, 1995 entry 
of the injunction halting portions of the Program While
