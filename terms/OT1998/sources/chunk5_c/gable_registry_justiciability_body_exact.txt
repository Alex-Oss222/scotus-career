Seen in this light, Gable’s fanciful Hobson’s choice is 
in reality little more than a choice between two perfectly 
constitutional options: less spending and public funds, or 
more spending by participating and nonparticipating 
slates alike. Gable is perfectly free to walk away from the

He did so in the 1995 campaign. 


2. Because Gable Is Not A Declared Candi- 
date In The Current Gubernatorial Cam- 
paign, He Lacks Standing. 


In his petition, Gable suggests that he “has expressed 
his interest” in running for governor of Kentucky in the 
1999 gubernatorial race. (Petition 5). In fact, Gable has not 


22 


filed papers with the Kentucky Board of Elections declar- 
ing his candidacy in the Republican Party primary for the 
1999 gubernatorial election. Under Kentucky law, he is no 
longer eligible to do so, since the filing deadline for the 
May 25, 1999 primary election was January 26, 1999. KRS 
§ 118.165. 


Consequently, Gable’s asserted interest in running 
for governor at this point is entirely academic, and he 
lacks standing. The standing question involves an anal- 
ysis concerning “whether the litigant is entitled to have 
the court decide the merits of the dispute or of particular 
issues.” Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490 (1975). The standing 
inquiry has two parts: 

First, whether the plaintiff-respondents allege 

“injury in fact” that is, a sufficiently concrete 

interest in the outcome of their suit to make it a 

case or controversy subject to a federal court’s 

Art. Ill jurisdiction, and, second, whether as a 

prudential matter, the plaintiff-respondents are 

proper proponents of the particular legal rights 

on which they base their suit. 

Singleton v. Wulff, 428 U.S. 106, 112 (1976). See also Valley 
Forge Christian College v. Americans United For Separation of 
Church and State, Inc., 454 U.S. 464, 471 (1982). 


Without question, Gable at this point lacks the suffi- 
ciently concrete interest in the outcome of the suit he 
originally filed to make it a case or controversy for pur- 
poses of Article III jurisdiction. His interest is no more 
concrete than that of more than 3.7 million other Ken- 
tuckians who will not run for the nomination of their 
party for the office of governor in the upcoming election. 
As such, he plainly lacks a sufficiently concrete interest in 
the matter to confer standing. Singleton, 428 U.S. at 112. 
See also, Spector Motor Service, Inc. v. McLaughlin, 323 U.S. 


23 


101, 105 (1944) (the Court will “not to pass on questions 
of constitutionality . . . unless such adjudication is 
unavoidable.”). Gable’s failure to file papers declaring 
himself a candidate in the 1999 gubernatorial election in 
Kentucky removes any vestige of standing he may once 
have had to assert this facial challenge to the constitu- 
tionality of Kentucky’s campaign finance laws, and ren- 
ders his challenge moot. 


Even if Gable could meet the technical requirement 
for injury in fact, he plainly is not a proper proponent of 
the particular rights in question. Because he only raised 
-and spent $95,835, Gable never approached the threshold 
for triggering the release to his Republican primary oppo- 
nent of the additional public funds. Absent a clearer 
indication of serious intent in the current campaign, 
Gable’s somewhat half-hearted 1995 campaign ought not 
to serve as the basis for the frontal assault on Kentucky’s 
unique comprehensive approach to campaign finance 
reform and the elimination of corruption that he has 
mounted. 


Moreover, Gable may not assert standing - as he 
attempts to do — because of any concern for the rights or 
interests of other candidates generally, whether in future 
Kentucky elections or in elections to be held in other 
states. (Petition 7). Thus, Gable’s general references to 
campaign financing laws in other states, as well as his 
refer nce to the need for this Court to “clarify the appli- 
cation of Buckley to such measures,” amounts to little 
more than an attempt to bootstrap his own abstract inter- 
est into a sufficiently concrete interest. Equally unhelpful 
to the standing analysis is Gable’s effort to invoke the 
rights of hypothetical candidates in elections yet to be 
called, whose claim to office “might [be] . . . cast into 


24 


doubt” (Petition 7), absent this Court’s decision to con- 
sider his challenge to Kentucky’s unique system of incen- 
tives and public subsidies. 


A plaintiff generally must assert his own legal rights 
and interests, and cannot rest his claim to relief on the 
legal rights or interests of third parties. Warth, 422 U.S. at 
499. See also, Barrows v. Jackson, 346 U.S. 249, 255-56 
(1934). Especially in light of his failure to file as a candi- 
date in the 1999 Republican primary, Gable’s claim for 
relief rests almost entirely on the abstract rights or inter- 
ests of others. As such, he fails to establish either element 
of standing, and this Court ought not review such an 
intricate constitutional question under the circumstances.

Warrant Review.

campaign finance legislation in light of the First Amend- 
ment that Gable claims is in order.®
