WILLIAMS IN OPPOSITION 


The question in this case is whether the Establishment 
Clause of the First Amendment is a bar to one of the most 
important experiments in educational reform now underway in 
the Nation. Under the Milwaukee Parental Choice Program (the 
“Program” or “MPCP”), some of the poorest urban families in 
the country are offered the option of attending the accredited 
school of their choice, whether it is public or private, secular or 
religious. Their per capita share of the state education fund is 
made available to pay the cost of attendance. The Wisconsin 
Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of the Program. We 
believe that decision should not be disturbed. 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


A. Factual Background 


Respondent Annette “Polly” Williams is a State Repre- 
sentative for the 10th District of Wisconsin, representing inner- 
city Milwaukee, and was the primary legislative sponsor ofthe 
Milwaukee Parental Choice Program. Respondents Marquelle 
Miller, Cynthia Miller, Angela Gray, Zachary Gray, Shon 
Richardson, George Richardson, Latrisha Henry, Faye Henry, 
Reigne Barrett, Valerie Barrett, Candice Williams, Senton 
Williams, Clintrai Giles, and Sharon Giles (collectively, “Miller 
respondents”) are children and parents of children who reside in 
Milwaukee and wish to participate in the Program. Respondents 
were granted leave to intervene as defendants in these consoli- 
dated actions. 


Because they lack the financial wherewithal to pay for any 
alternative, the Miller respondents have no choice but to receive 
their education in the Milwaukee Public Schools (“MPS”), one 
of the most troubled school systems in the Nation. See generally 
Davis v. Grover, 480 N.W.2d 460, 469 (Wis. 1992). During 
the 1993-94 school year, for instance, dropout rates among

MPS students in grades 9-12 reached 15 percent each year— 
seven times the rate among students in Wisconsin as a whole. 
Wis. J.A. 170-174. In fact, only 48 percent of students who 
enter the Milwaukee Public Schools as freshmen ultimately 
graduate (ibid.), and of those who do finish, some 36 percent 
squeak by with a “D” average. Davis, 480 N.W.2d at 470. This 
is despite the fact that spending in the Milwaukee Public Schools 
exceeds $7600 per pupil—well above national averages. Not 
surprisingly, there is a strong correlation between dropping out 
of school and future dependence upon public assistance (or 
worse). As the Wisconsin Supreme Court recently observed, 
“{h]jigh school dropouts comprise 75 percent of the prison 
population and 80 percent of the families receiving Aid for 
Families with Dependent Children. Only 55 percent of the male 
dropouts under age thirty have jobs and only 20 percent have 
full-time jobs.” Jbid. In short, “[t]he consequences of school 
dropouts and inadequate education are shocking.” Jbid. 


These problems are particularly pronounced for members 
of racial minority groups. For example, while MPS students 
generally score below the national average on basic skills tests, 
“minority students score dramatically below the average.” 
Davis, 480 N.W.2d at 470. And while MPS students’ grade- 
point average (on a 4.0 scale) generally is 1.60, the average for 
African-American students is just 1.31. See MILWAUKEE 
PUBLIC SCHOOLS, INDICATORS OF EDUCATIONAL EFFECTIVE- 
NESS (1990). Given that some two-thirds of MPS students are 
racial minorities, Milwaukee’s high dropout rates have a 
disproportionate racial impact as well. See Wis. J.A. 170-174. 


The troubled condition of Milwaukee’s public education 
system was not lost on Wisconsin Democratic Representative 
Annette “Polly” Williams, a respondent herein whose district 
includes many of the families that are caught in the embattled 
Milwaukee Public Schools. Representative Williams came to 
understand school choice as a tool of “empowerment of low-

B. Proceedings Below 


In August 1995, shortly after the Program’s enactment, 
petitioners filed these two lawsuits in Wisconsin state court, 
alleging that the Program violates the Establishment Clause of 
the United States Constitution as well as various provisions of 
the Wisconsin Constitution. The trial court consolidated the 
actions, and two groups of private parties—including the Miller 
respondents—were granted leave to intervene on the side of the 
state defendants. 


The Wisconsin Supreme Court assumed original jurisdiction 
over ft’ - matter and stayed further proceedings in the trial court. 
After preliminarily enjoining implementation of the Program, an 
equally divided court dismissed the suit and lifted the stay on 
proceedings in the trial court. Three justices held that petition- 
ers failed to demonstrate a constitutional violation under either 
the Establishment Clause or the Wisconsin Constitution, and 
three justices voted to invalidate the Program solely on state 
constitutional grounds. See Pet. App. 190a-191a. No member 
of the court held that the Program violated the Establishment 
Clause. Ibid. 


Upon return to the trial court, the parties filed cross 
motions for summary judgment. Principally citing Article I, 
§ 18 of the Wisconsin Constitution, Wisconsin’s own religion 
clause, the court granted petitioners’ motion for summary 
judgment and invalidated the amended Program. Pet. App. 
188a. Like the three dissenting justices on the Wisconsin

Constitution, the court of appeals affirmed the trial court’s

Wisconsin Constitution, stating that it is “settled law” that

Pet. App. 75a. Citing “recent Wisconsin precedents which more

The Supreme Court of Wisconsin reversed. The court 
relied on the three-part test of Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 
602, 612-614 (1971), under which a law violates the Establish- 
ment Clause if it lacks a secular purpose, has a primary effect 
of either advancing or inhibiting religion, or leads to an 
excessive entanglement of church and state. The court noted that 
Lemon has been subject to criticism, but explained that “[uJntil 
a majority of the Supreme Court directly holds otherwise, * * * 
we continue to apply the Lemon test.” Pet. App. 14a n.5. 


The court had no difficulty concluding that “the purpose of 
the state legislation is secular in nature.” Pet. App. 14a. “The 
purpose of the program,” the court explained, “is to provide 
low-income parents with an opportunity to have their children 
educated outside of the embattled Milwaukee Public School 
system.” Jd. at 14a-15a. “The propriety of providing educa- 
tional opportunities for children of poor families in the state,” 
the court added, “goes without question.” Jd. at 15a.

Turning to Lemon’s second prong—the effects prong—-the 
court derived its analysis from “the cumulative criteria devel- 
oped over the years and appl[ied] to a wide range of educational 
assistance programs challenged as violative of the Establishment 
Clause.” Pet. App. 16a. The court carefully traversed this 
Court’s modern establishment decisions, beginning with Everson 
v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1 (1947), and culminating in 
Agostini v. Felton, 117 S. Ct. 1997 (1997), and reasoned that 
those cases 


establish{] the general principle that state educational 
assistance programs do not have the primary effect of 
advancing religion if those programs provide public 
aid to both sectarian and nonsectarian institutions 
(1) on the basis of neutral, secular criteria that neither 
favor nor disfavor religion; and (2) only as a result of 
numerous private choices of the individual parents of 
school-age children. 


Pet. App. 25a. “The amended MPCP,” the court held, “is 
precisely such a program.” /bid. 


First, “eligibility for benefits under the amended MPCP is 
determined by ‘neutral, secular criteria that neither favor nor 
disfavor religion.’” Pet. App. 25a (citing Agostini, 117 S. Ct. 
at 2014). Program beneficiaries are “selected on a random 
basis” from among children who “reside in Milwaukee, attend 
public schools (or private schools in grades K-3) and meet 
certain income requirements.” bid. Moreover, “beneficiaries 
are eligible for an equal share of per pupil public aid regardless 
of the school they choose to attend.” Jd. at 26a. Thus, the court 
below explained that “[a] student qualifies for benefits under the 
amended MPCP not because he or she is a Catholic, a Jew, a 
Moslem, or an atheist; it is because he or she is from a poor 
family and is a student in the embattled Milwaukee Public 
Schools.” Jd. at 27a. 


10 


Similarly, “[p]articipating private schools are also selected 
on a religious-neutral basis and may be sectarian or nonsectar- 
ian.” Pet. App. 25a. In short, the court held, because students 
and their families may choose from “a wide range of school 
choices—mainly public, but some private or religious” (id. at 
26a n.16), the Program “provides a neutral benefit to beneficia- 
ries selected on religious-neutral criteria” and “neither leads to 
‘religious indoctrination’ nor ‘creates [a] financial incentive for 
students to undertake sectarian education.’” Jd. at 27a (citations 
omitted). 


Second, the court explained that “[a]ny aid provided under 
the amended MPCP that ultimately flows to sectarian private 
schools * * * does so ‘only as a result of genuinely independent 
and private choices of aid recinients.’” Pet. App. 27a (quoting 
Witters v. Washington Dep’t of Servs., 474 U.S. 481, 487 
(1986)). The court rejected the argument that the Program was 
invalid merely because “the State sends the checks directly to 
the participating private school and the parents must restrictively 
endorse the checks to the private schools.” Jd. at 28a. The 
important point, the court held, is that “[a]s with the programs 
in Mueller and Witters, not one cent flows from the State to a 
sectarian private school under the amended MPCP except as a 
result of the necessary and intervening choices of individual 
parents.” Jbid. In this regard, the court observed that the 
Program “places on equal footing options of public and private 
school choice, and vests power in the hands of parents to choose 
where to direct the funds allocated for their children’s benefit.” 
Ibid. 


The court considered and rejected petitioners’ argument 
that Committee for Public Education v. Nyquist, 413 U.S. 756 
(1973), requires invalidation of the Program under Lenon’s 
second prong. Pet. App. 18a-19a & n.9. The court explained 
that there are “significant distinctions” between the amended 


11 


Program and the program invalidated in Nyquist. Id. at 19a 
n.9. Most importantly, the court stated (ibid.): 


In Nyquist, each of the facets of the challenged pro- 
gram directed aid exclusively to private schools and 
their students. The MPCP, in contrast, provides a 
neutral benefit to qualifying parents of school-age 
children in Milwaukee Public Schools. Unlike the 
program in Nyquist, the only financially-qualified 
Milwaukee students excluded from participation in the 
amended MPCP are those_in the fourth grade or 
higher who are already attending private schools. The 
amended MPCP, viewed in its surrounding context, 
merely adds religious schools to a range of pre-exist- 
ing educational choices available to MPS children. 
This seminal fact takes the amended MPCP out of the 
Nyquist construct and places it within the framework 
of neutral education assistance programs. 


Finally, the court concluded that the Program “will not 
create an excessive entanglement between the State and 
religion.” Pet. App. 29a. The court noted that the Program’s 
performance, reporting, and auditing requirements, as well as 
applicable nondiscrimination, health, and safety obligations, 
“already exist” under prior law and “do not create excessive 
entanglement merely because they are part of the amended 
MPCP’s requirements.” Jd. at 29a-30a. The court went on to 
conclude that “[t]he program does not involve the State in any 
way with the schools’ governance, curriculum, or day-to-day 
affairs.” Id. at 30a. Thus, the “‘[rjoutine regulatory interac- 
tion’” required by the Program does not entail “the authority to 
impose a ‘comprehensive, discriminating, and continuing state 
surveillance’ over the participating sectarian private schools” or 
even “approach the level of constitutionally impermissible 
involvement.” Jd. at 29a, 30a (citations omitted).

Because the court concluded that the Program did not 
violate the Establishment Clause, it was required to consider 
petitioners’ state constitutional challenges. The court rejected 
those challenges as well, with two justices dissenting and 
adopting by reference the court of appeals’ analysis as their 
own. Pet. App. 58a. No court or judge during the entire course 
of this litigation has reached the conclusion that the Program 
violates the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment.° 


REASONS FOR DENYING THE PETITION 


The petition for certiorari rests almost entirely on the 
claim that the decision below is in “direct and irreconcilable 
conflict” with Committee for Public Education v. Nyquist, 413 
U.S. 756 (1973). Pet. 10, 11, 13. But the Wisconsin Supreme 
Court carefully considered Nyquist and correctly concluded that 
it is distinguishable. Pet. App. 18a-19a. Indeed, petitioners’ 
assertion that this case “is on all fours with Nyquist” (Pet. 12), 
and accordingly that “this is an open-and-shut case” (id. at 15), 
is based on a misreading of Nyquist and a misunderstanding of 
the nature of the Milwaukee Parental Choice Program. Perhaps 
more significantly, if the court below had followed petitioners’ 
mistaken notion of Establishment Clause doctrine, it would have 
created a conflict with a long line of this Court’s cases recogniz- 
ing the legitimac’y of government programs that aid religious 
and secular institutions on a neutral and evenhanded basis 
through the independent choices of private individuals. And it 
would have set the Establishment Clause on a collision course 
with other clauses of the First Amendment, which not only do 
not command, but positively forbid, discrimination against

Petitioners’ policy arguments are equally unavailing. 
Fundamentally, those arguments are based on the assumption 
that the purpose of the state’s education fund is to benefit public 
schools, rather than to educate children. The First Amendment 
was not written to protect the exclusive emoluments of the 
public school system, which did not even exist at the time of the 
Framing. The people of Wisconsin are entitled to experiment 
with the theory that competition and choice will improve the 
previously dismal performance of schools available to the 
children of inner-city Milwaukee. If the court below had 
blocked the Parental Choice experiment in the name of a rigid 
and outmoded interpretation of the federal Constitution, this case 
might indeed be certworthy. But the sole consequence of the 
decision below is to allow one State to engage in a promising 
experiment in educational reform, for the benefit of some of the 
neediest children in America, in a manner entirely consistent 
with the values of pluralism, diversity, and choice that are 
enshrined in the First Amendment. The Program may well 
succeed in improving the educational opportunities of these 
children, in which case other States will probably follow 
Wisconsin’s lead. It may fail, in which case we will have 
learned from the experience. But there is no reason for this 
Court to stop the experiment before it has had a fair test.

mandated by the Establishment Clause.” Texas Monthly, Inc. v. 
Bullock, 489 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1989) (plurality opinion). 


Perhaps the clearest precedent in support of the decision 
below is Witters. In Witters, the Court unanimously held that 
public funds could be used to pay the tuition of a student at a 
Bible college for training for the ministry, pursuant to a 
program of vocational assistance for the blind. Justice 
Marshall’s opinion for the Court identified three aspects of the 
program as “central to our inquiry” (474 U.S. at 487), and 
these factors still stand as this Court’s most pertinent statement 
of the meaning of the Establishment Clause in this context. 


First, Justice Marshall noted that funding goes to the 
recipient institution only through the choice of the individual

ultimately flows to religious institutions does so only as a result 
of the genuinely independent and private choices of aid 
recipients.” 474 U.S. at 487. Second, he noted that 
“Washington’s program is ‘made available generally without 
regard to the sectarian-nonsectarian, or public-nonpublic nature 
of the institution benefitted,’ and is in no way skewed towards 
religion.” Jd. at 487-488 (quoting Nyquist, 413 U.S. at 782-783 
n.38). Third, he stressed that the Washington program “creates

Petitioners’ position is at odds with the fundamental logic

in the religious practice. See Sherbert v. Verner, 374 U.S. 398,

now exists.

sense to interpret cognate provisions harmoniously and 
consistently.

D. Petitioners’ Argument That Certiorari Should Be 
Granted To Advance The “National Policy Debate” 
Should Be Rejected 


Petitioners repeatedly sound the refrain that the Parental 
Choice Program will “divert millions of tax dollars from the 
Milwaukee Public Schools” to be used for private schools. Pet. 
3, 5, 20. This choice of words is highly revealing. It suggests 
that petitioners view the state education fund as serving the 
purpose of subsidizing a particular set of schools. We believe, 
and the Wisconsin legislature evidently agreed, that the purpose 
of the education fund is to provide the best possible education 
for the children of the State. If state taxpayers’ money is being 
used for that purpose, it is not being “diverted.” 


Educational choice raises legitimate issues of educational 
policy, about which reasonable people disagree. The premise of 
the Wisconsin program is that, when the educational system has 
manifestly failed the children, as it has in Milwaukee, it is time 
to explore alternatives. Supporters of educational choice believe 
that the past policy, in which the public school establishment 
was given a protected monopoly over the education fund, has 
stifled innovation and created a sclerotic, unresponsive, 
inefficient bureaucracy—with poor, minority, and inner-city 
children bearing the brunt of the system’s inadequacies. See 
generally John E. Chubb & Terry M. Moe, POLITICS, 
MARKETS, AND AMERICA’S SCHOOLS (Brooking Inst. 1990); 
James S. Coleman et al., HIGH SCHOOL ACHIEVEMENT: PUBLIC, 
CATHOLIC, AND PRIVATE SCHOOLS COMPARED (1982). 
Researchers predict that introducing choice and competition will 
enable parents to obtain a superior education for their children 


25 


and stimulate improvements for the entire system. See Chubb & 
Moe, supra, at 185-229; Coleman, supra, at 122-178; James S. 
Coleman & Thomas Hoffer, PUBLIC AND PRIVATE HIGH 
SCHOOLS: THE IMPACT OF COMMUNITIES 57-96 (1987); 
Anthony S. Bryk et al., CATHOLIC SCHOOLS AND THE COMMON 
Goop 55-78 (1993) (summarizing research data). " 


To be sure, the argument for educational choice is at 
present primarily predictive and theoretical, since there has 
never been a comprehensive program of educational choice in 
any city in America.” But as the Wisconsin legislature 
recognized, it is a promising source of hope for genuine reform 
of elementary and secondary education in Milwaukee. The great 
virtue of the decision below is that it will allow the theory to be 
tested under the real-world conditions of inner-city Milwaukee, 
where educational reform is desperately needed. Cf. Washington 
v. Glucksberg, 117 S. Ct. 2258, 2292-2293 (1997) (Souter, J., 
concurring) (explaining why courts should permit legislative 
experimentation when dealing with “emerging” issues where 
effects and consequences are “currently unknown”).
