disposal of low-income parents to choose schools for their 
children, and adds private religiously affiliated schools to 
a preexisting array of public and private nonsectarian 
educational choices, violate the Establishment Clause of 
the First Amendment?

INTRODUCTORY STATEMENT 


Respondents Parents for School Choice, Pilar 
Gonzalez, Dinah Cooley, Julie Vogel, Kate Helsper, Blong 
Yang, Gail Crockett, Yolanda Lassiter, and Jeanine Knox 
join petitioners in urging the Court to resolve the ques- 
tion presented. After years of waiting, these parents and 
thousands of others with limited economic means in Mil- 
waukee now have the opportunity to direct their chil- 
dren's state education funds to the schools of their choice. 
But so long as claims of unconstitutionality shroud paren- 
tal choice programs, those parents cannot be secure about 
their children’s future. 


The crisis in urban public education is perhaps the 
gravest challenge facing Americans today, and parental 
choice constitutes one of the most vibrant and promising 
responses. Constitutional arguments are raised against 
parental choice in both the judicial and legislative arenas. 
Attempting to apply this Court’s Establishment Clause 
jurisprudence, different courts have reached conflicting 
conclusions as to the constitutionality of parental choice. 
Given the resulting legal uncertainty, conscientious legis- 
lators need guidance from this Court. This case presents a 
well-developed factual record and a functioning parental 
choice program that is a model for education reform 
efforts around the nation, and thus provides an ideal 
opportunity to resolve the constitutional issue in the most 
broadly applicable context. The decision on review pro- 
vides a thorough and well-reasoned analysis of the con- 
Stitutional issue, and carefully and correctly applies this 
Court’s Establishment Clause jurisprudence. Yet other 
courts, interpreting the same precedents, have reached 
contradictory resuits. Respondents therefore respectfully 


urge this Court to take the case and to affirm the judgment 
of the Wisconsin Supreme Court.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Respondents add the following to petitioners’ State- 
ment of the Case. 


Respondents are defendants-intervenors consisting of 
Parents for School Choice, a community organization that 
advocated expansion of the Milwaukee Parental Choice 
Program, and eight parents acting on behalf of their chil- 
dren, who are among the intended beneficiaries of the 
expanded parental choice program. 


~ The Milwaukee Parental Choice Program arises in the 
context of the state’s largest urban public school system, a 
system mired in chaos and dismal performance. As the 
Wisconsin Supreme Court observed in Davis v. Grover, 480 
N.W.2d 460, 469 (Wis. 1992), “the Milwaukee Public School 
District has significantly greater education and poverty prob- 
lerms than any other school district in the state.” Among the 
legislature’s responses were the “Chapter 220” interdistrict 
public school transfer program, magnet schools, charter 
schools, and out-placement of “at-risk” children in private 
schools. During the 1990s, the state also significantly 
increased its financial commitment to the Milwaukee Public 
Schools, both in absolute dollars and as a relative share of 
the district's budget. See Affidavit of Milwaukee School 
Board Member John Gardner (Jt. App. 246).! 


! jt. App. refers to the Joint Appendix prepared for use 
before the Wisconsin Supreme Court. 


In 1989, the legislature enacted the Milwaukee Paren- 
tal Choice Program, Wis. Stat. § 119.23, allowing up to 
1,000 economically disadvantaged youngsters? to use 
their portion of state public school funds at participating 
nonsectarian private schools which would agree to admit 
the students on a random selection basis and to accept 
the state’s funding as full payment of tuition. The state’s 
payment is capped at the school’s tuition or actual per- 
pupil operating costs, whichever is greater. Checks are 
made payable to the parents, who sign them over to the 
schools of their choice.% 


For the first several years, data from the program 
were restricted to a single researcher chosen by the Wis- 
consin Department of Public Instruction.* After the infor- 
mation was made more widely available in 1995, a team 
of researchers led by Dr. Paul Peterson of Harvard Uni- 
versity’s John F. Kennedy School of Government found 
substantial academic gains for youngsters in their third 
and fourth years in the choice program. See Jay P. Greene, 
Paul E. Peterson, and Jiangtae Du, The Effectiveness of

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


I. THE FAMILIES IN THE MILWAUKEE PARENTAL 
CHOICE PROGRAM NEED TO HAVE THE CON- 
STITUTIONAL CLOUD REMOVED. 


The journey for several thousand economically dis- 
advantaged families now participating in the Milwaukee 
Parental Choice Program has been a tumultuous one. By 


the time of the Wisconsin Supreme Court's preliminary 
injunction in August 1995, more than 4,000 children had 
applied for the expanded program, and over 3,400 had 
been admitted to participating private schools. Pet. App. 
8a n.3. The injunction had the effect of rolling back enroll- 
ment in the program to approximately 1,600 students and 
excluding religious schools from the range of options, 
thereby throwing both families and schools into disarray. 
See, e.g., Testimony of Pilar Gonzalez (Jt. App. 396-97); 
Testimony of Valerie Johnson (Jt. App. 406-08). Following 
remand from the Wisconsin Supreme Court, the trial 
court provided partial interim relief, lifting the injunction 
with respect to the number of eligible children, but then 
reinstated the rollback following an adverse final deci- 
sion. Pet. App. 9a. 


Now that the Wisconsin Supreme Court has upheld 
the program in its entirety, approximately 15,000 children 
from low-income families are eligible for the expanded 
educational opportunities the program provides. Accord- 
ing to press accounts, approximately 6,000 children and 
more than 80 private schools are participating in the 
program in the 1998-99 school year.5 Twice so far during 
the pendency of this litigation, many of the economically 
disadvantaged families in this program have looked for- 
ward to new educational opportunities for their children 
only to have them snatched away. Those families cannot 
be secure until this Court has affirmed the constitu- 
tionality of parental choice. Moreover, the current cloud

of constitutional uncertainty limits the full potential of 
the Milwaukee program. One premise of school choice 
policies is that the availability of paying students will 
induce new private schools to form and existing private 
schools to expand. This, however, requires the investment 
of capital that is not likely to occur if there is a substantial 
threat that parental choice may be held unconstitutional 
in the near future. The likely result is to confine families 
to whatever places happen to be unfilled at existing 
schools. This is an immediate negative consequence to 
our clients caused by the legal uncertainty that only this 
Court can resolve. 


As a consequence, respondent parents take the 
unusual step of supporting the writ, for the purpose of 
securing for their children and others similarly situated - 
not only in Wisconsin but throughout America — desper- 
ately needed educational opportunities. 


Il. BECAU >i PARENTAL CHOICE PLAYS A CEN- 
TRAL ROLE IN EDUCATION REFORM, ITS CON-

A. Parental choice is a central component of 
efforts around the nation to deliver high-sual- 
ity educational opportunities to disadvantaged 
and minority schoolchildren. 


“[E]ducation is perhaps the most important function 
of state and local governments.” Brown v. Board of Educa- 
tion, 347 U.S. 483, 493 (1954). The program at issue in this 
case represents a major facet of the State of Wisconsin’s 
sweeping efforts to deliver on the promise made in 
Brown: that education, “where the state has undertaken to 


provide it, is a right which must be made available to all 
on equal terms.” Id.; see also Davis v. Grover, 480 N.W.2d 
at 547 (Ceci, J., concurring) (“The Wisconsin legis- 
lature . . . has attempted to throw a life preserver to those 
Milwaukee children caught in the cruel riptide of a school 
system floundering upon the shoals of poverty, status- 
quo thinking, and despair.”). 


It is difficult to overstate the crisis in urban educa- 
tion. In the Milwaukee Public Schools, only 48 percent of 
the students graduate, and the annual dropout rate is 
more than seven times the statewide average. See How- 
ard L. Fuller and Sammis B. White, Expanded School Choice 
in Milwaukee (1995) at 16 (Jt. App. 165). The system’s 
failure is most pronounced among minority and low- 
income children. In 11 MPS high schools that enroll more 
than three-fourths of the city’s black students, the median 
grade-point average is less than 1.5 on a four-point scale, 
and the failure rate in core academic courses ranges from 
26 to 43 percent. Id. at 4 (Jt. App. 153). 


This crisis reflects nationally in poor achievement 
among minority students, who are disproportionately 
consigned to failing large urban school districts. The 
National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) 
reported in 1995 that only 12 percent of black seniors 
(compared to 40 percent of whites) were proficient in 
reading.© The 1997 NAEP also found that while 76 per- 
cent of white fourth-graders were proficient in basic 
mathematics skills, only 41 percent of Hispanic and 32

ciency.” The typical black high school senior scores four 
academic years behind the typical white senior — a gap 
that has widened substantially over the past decade. See 
Stephan Thernstrom and Abigail Thernstrom, America in 
Black and White: One Nation, Indivisible (1997) at 355. 
Despite the massive investment of public and private 
resources, little progress has been made toward deliver- 
ing high-quality educational opportunities to poor inner- 
city children, a fact that likely is attributable in large part 
to the highly bureaucratic nature of large urban public 
school systems. See, e.g., John E. Chubb and Terry M. 
Moe, Politics, Markets & America’s Schools (1990) at 181, 
cited with approval in Davis v. Grover, 480 N.W.2d at 472. 


Against this discouraging backdrop, parental choice 
offers real promise. As a model for education reform, 
parental choice encompasses two essential elements: (1) it 
transfers control over state education funding from state 
officials to parents; and (2) it creates competition among 
schools for low-income students and the dollars they 
command. The Milwaukee Parental Choice Program 
exemplifies this reform model. The program serves an 
exclusively low-income and overwhelmingly minority 
student population. See Fuller and White at 22 (Jt. App. 
170). Though expert assessments of academic perfor- 
mance differ, in 1996 the Kennedy School team for the 
first time compared test scores of students in the Mil- 
waukee Parental Choice Program who were admitted to 
private schools through random selection with students

241).

is not before this Court. Rather, it is clear that parental

B. The Court should not wait to resolve the consti- 
tutional issue because it is frequently raised in 
legislative debates over parental choice, neces- 
sitating clear guidance from this Court. 


The debate over parental choice is unusual because 
arguments over constitutionality often are raised in the 
legislative context. Not only courts but legislators need 
guidance on the constitutional issues so that states are 
not impeded in fully exercising the educational authority 
reserved to them by the Tenth Amendment. A vibrant 
model for urgently needed education reform should not 
be held hostage to jurisprudential uncertainty. 


Despite the potential that parental choice holds for 
enhancing the quality of education received by economi- 
cally disadvantaged children across the nation, the consti- 
tutional cloud over parental choice programs serves as a 
formidable barrier to progress. As petitioners note, school 
choice legislation has been considered in at least 21 state 
legislatures and the United States Congress. Pet. 20. But 
whenever such proposals are discussed, legislators inev- 
itably debate their constitutionality. 


Thus, for example, in the Commonwealth of Pennsyl- 
vania, a constitutional point of order killed -a school 
choice plan in 1991.9 No further consideration of the bill’s

A. The Wisconsin Supreme Court's decision cor-

Petitioners urge that the present case is virtually 
identical to the programs struck down by this Court in 
Committee for Public Education v. Nyquist, 413 U.S. 756

the sectarian-nonsectarian, public-nonpublic nature of the

the students who patronize them. By contrast, as the 
Wisconsin Supreme Court observed, the Milwaukee Par- 
ental Choice Program operates within a system “in which 
public funds follow each child, [which] now encompasses

Thus, Nyquisi’s distinction between state programs 
that channel aid exclusively to private (predominantly 
religious) schools, and programs that provide aid to both 
public and private institutions, is of great significance

within the latter context. Under the state’s pupil aid 
formula, needy students in the Milwaukee district are
