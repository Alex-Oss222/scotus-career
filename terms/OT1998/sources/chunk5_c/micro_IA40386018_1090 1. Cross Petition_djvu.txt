QUESTION PRESENTED FOR REVIEW 


Cross-Respondent Robert Gable has petitioned this 
Court for a Writ of Certiorari on two questions relating to 
the constitutionality of Kentucky’s campaign finance law. 
Pet. at i. Should the Court grant the Petition, Cross- 
Petitioner Paul E. Patton, Governor of Kentucky, respect- 
fully requests this Court’s review of the following third 
question relating to the constitutionality of an essential 
component to the same legislation: 


Whether it is constitutional to induce candidates to 
participate in a voluntary system of public financing by 
prohibiting a gubernatorial candidate from waiting until 
after the final preelection reporting deadline to make 
personal contributions to her own campaign in excess of 
the voluntary spending limits.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


The court of appeals and the district court before it 
upheld the constitutionality of all but one component of 


Kentucky’s campaign financing reform system. This Con- 


ditional Cross-Petition seeks review of the constitu- 
tionality of that discreet, yet vital, provision of a law 
which works to prevent corruption and preserve public 
confidence in the election process by providing incentives 
to encourage candidates to accept voluntary spending 


limits. 


Against the backdrop of a history of political corrup- 
tion in state elections, the Kentucky General Assembly in 
1992 enacted campaign finance reforms. The reforms 
were designed to restore and protect the integrity of 
elections in this Commonwealth. Central to the legisla- 
tion was public financing for candidates, a legally estab- 
lished means for limiting a candidate’s aggregate 
contributions and expenditures. Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 
1, 57 n.65 (1976). 


Participation in Kentucky’s public financing pro- 
gram, and its attendant expenditure limits, is entirely 
voluntary. The legislation encourages participation, how- 
ever, by matching $2 in public funds to each $1 of pri- 
vately raised funds for gubernatorial slates choosing to 
accept public financing (“Participating Slates”). 
121A.060(3)(c). Participating Slates voluntarily accept a 
limit on private contributions in each of the primary and 
general elections of $600,000, making a maximum of $1.2 
million available in matching funds, for a total voluntary 
spending limit of $1.8 million for Participating Slates in 
each election. KRS 121A.080(1). 


Gubernatorial slates electing not to accept public 
financing (“Nonparticipating Slates”) are not limited by 


the $1.8 million spending limit. However, if a Nonpar- 
ticipating Slate accepts more than $1.8 million in contri- 
butions in a primary or general election campaign, it will 
“trigger” the suspension of the spending limit on an 
opposing Participating Slate (“the Trigger”). KRS 
121A.030(5). The Participating Slate may then resume 
accepting contributions in excess of the limit. To insure 
that a Participating Slate will continue to be less depen- 
dent on donors, it will continue to receive the $2 for $1 
match of public funds once the Trigger has been pulled 
and the limit has been lifted. 


For the Trigger provision to have any practical effect, 
there must be a mechanism through which to determine 
when a Nonparticipating Slate has accepted contributions 
exceeding the $1.8 million limit. This determination must 
be made sufficiently prior to the election to allow a 
Participating Slate a meaningful opportunity to resume 
its own fundraising. Otherwise, the Trigger would be 
ineffective and, consequently, candidates would be 
deterred from accepting public financing and voluntary 
spending limits. 


Accordingly, the legislation regulates the time period 
in which slates may accept contributions by prohibiting 
any gubernatorial slate (whether Participating or Nonpar- 
ticipating) from knowingly accepting any campaign con- 
tribution after the final preelection reporting deadline, 
which is 28 days before a primary or general election! 


1 The final preelection reporting period is 28 days before a 
primary or general election, or 14 days before a run-off primary. 
See KRS 121A.030(5). The 1996 General Assembly changed the 
timing window from 30 days to 28 days to have it coincide with 


(“the 28-Day Timing Window”). KRS 121A.030(5). How- 
ever, if a Nonparticipating Slate “pulls” the Trigger by 
accepting contributions in excess of the $1.8 million limit, 
all slates may resume accepting contributions during the 
28-Day Timing Window. 


Robert E. Gable, original Petitioner and Cross- 
Respondent in this action, was an unsuccessful candidate 
for the 1995 Republican nomination for Governor of Ken- 
tucky. Gable elected not to receive transfers from the 
public fund and therefore was not subject to the $1.8 
million spending limit in Kentucky’s campaign finance 
reform legislation. His slate was subject to the reporting 
requirements and timing restrictions for contributions 
during the 28-Day Timing Window. Gable filed suit in the 
United States District Court for the Eastern District of 
Kentucky challenging the constitutionality of numerous 
provisions of Kentucky’s campaign finance reform law. 
Defendants included the Kentucky Registry of Election 
Finance as the agency responsible for enforcement of the 
legislation, Governor Paul E. Patton, and other state and 
local officials. 


On March 29, 1996, the district court, pursuant to 28 
U.S.C. §§ 1331 and 1343, declared constitutional all but a 
portion of one of the law’s provisions. Pet. App. at 28a. 
While recognizing that this Court in Buckley v. Valeo never 
addressed the question of “whether a ‘time’ restriction 
limiting when a candidate may contribute to his own 
campaign is constitutionally permissible,” the district

court nonetheless relied on Buckley to enjoin enforcement 
of KRS 121A.030(5) to the extent it prohibits a candidate 
from contributing to her own campaign during the 28- 
Day Timing Window. Pet. App. at 3la-33a. Both sides 
appealed. On April 30, 1998, the United States Court of 
Appeals for the Sixth Circuit affirmed the district court 
judgment in Gable v. Patton, 142 F.3d 940 (6th Cir. 1998). 
Pet. App. at la. Both sides petitioned for rehearing and 
rehearing en banc, which the court of appeals denied on 
July 28, 1998. 


The court of appeals unanimously upheld the Trigger 
provision of the campaign finance legislation as an 
inducement which is narrowly tailored to further Ken- 
tucky’s compelling interest in eliminating actual and per- 
ceived corruption in the election process. Pet. App. at 
15a. The court also unanimously upheld the 28-Day Tim- 
ing Window insofar as it prohibits contributions by third 
parties to the candidate during the 28 days preceding the 
election. In so doing, the court explicitly recognized that 
the Window forms an important part of the public financ- 
ing scheme and merely requires candidates to “rearrange 
their fundraising by concentrating it in the period before 
the 28-Day Window begins.” Pet. App. at 19a. 


By a two to one vote, the court of appeals, while 
professing sympathy for its administrative goals, found 
that the 28-Day Timing Window was unconstitutional 
insofar as it prohibits contributions by a candidate to her 
own campaign during the 28 days preceding the election. 
Pet. App. at 24a. The majority relied entirely on Buckley v. 
Valeo in reaching its decision, though conceding “one 
possible distinction between the 28-Day Window and the 


internal expenditure provisions struck down in Buckley.” 
Pet. App. at 20a. 


Although Buckley pointed out that a candidate’s 
use of his own money does not lead to corrup- 
tion, Kentucky’s interest in preventing actual 
and apparent corruption is served by the 28-Day 
Window's application to internal expenditures, 
albeit indirectly. Extension of the 28-Day Win- 
dow to internal expenditures increases the effec- 
tiveness of the Window, which increases the 
effectiveness of the Trigger, which, in turn, 
strengthens the incentives for participating in 
Kentucky’s campaign finance scheme, a scheme 
which is clearly aimed at the prevention of 
actual and apparent corruption. 


Pet. App. at 20a. 


By separate Opinion, Circuit Judge Cudahy con- 
curred with the panel on all points except for that part of 
the majority opinion which invalidates the 28-Day Timing 
Window as it applies to internal contributions by candi- 
dates. His dissent took issue with the majority’s reliance 
on Buckley's prohibition against direct quantity restric- 
tions on political communication, stating that the Ken- 
tucky provision affects only when contributions are made, 
not how much. 

Nor does the 28-Day Window involve the evil at 

which Buckley was aimed. Buckley was con- 

cerned that a candidate not be prohibited from 
contributing to, and spending money on, her 
own candidacy. See id. at 52-53. Certainly that 
freedom is not at stake here. Candidates are free 
to make contributions, as frequently as they 


wish and of whatever magnitude they choose, 
before the advent of the 28-Day Window. 


Pet. App. at 260. 


Judge Cudahy recognized that “[a]li the 28-Day Win- 
dow provides is xotice that the contribution has been 
made and an opportunity for the opponent to respond.” 
Pet. App. at 26a (emphasis in original). As a result, the 
Window “removes the unfairness of last-minute, unre- 
ported contributions.” Pet. App. at 26a. 


In sum, the majority is troubled that the 28-Day 
Window is a “burden” on a candidate’s freedom 
to speak with her own dollars. But the alterna- 
tive is to construct a special right to speak with 
unreported dollars at the last minute when no 
response is possible. I do not believe that the 
First Amendment requires such a perverse con- 
struction, and I therefore respectfully dissent 
with respect to this issue.

REASONS FOR GRANTING 
CONDITIONAL CROSS-PETITION 


In his opening Petition docketed with the Court on 
November 30, 1998, Petitioner/Cross-Respondent Gable 
urges the Supreme Court to re-examine the constitu- 
tionality of Kentucky’s campaign finance reform frame- 
work, specifically the law’s “Trigger” provision ar. the 
application of its “28-Day Timing Window” to outside 
contributions. Should the Court in its discretion deter- 
mine that Petitioner Gable has presented compelling rea- 
sons for review and accordingly grants the Petition, then 
the Court also should grant this Conditional Cross-Peti- 
tion. All of Petitioner Gable’s stated reasons for review 
must and do apply with equal force to this Cross-Petition. 
In short, if Kentucky’s campaign finance reform law is to 


be reevaluated at all, it should be evaluated in its entirety, 


with all its essential working components. 


The Petition states that “|t]his case raises issues of 
national importance that were not resolved by this 
Court's decision in Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1 (1976).” 
Pet. at 6. Certainly this sweeping generalization applies 
with equal force to the question at issue in this Condi- 
tional Cross-Petition — namely, whether the First Amend- 
ment precludes a law that prohibits a gubernatorial 
candidate from making contributions to her own cam- 
paign after the final preelection reporting deadline. The 
purpose and ultimate effect of the timing requirement is 
to provide publicly funded candidates adequate time to 
respond with additional fundraising when their privately 
funded opponent has accepted contributions in excess of 


the voluntary spending limits 


Every court that has ruled on Kentucky's timing lim- 
itations on candidate contributions has conceded to vary- 
ing extents that Buckley does not speak directly to the 
issue. See supra at 4-6. As the court of appeals dissent 
most cogently points out, the Buckley decision only invali- 
dated a ceiling on a candidate’s expenditure of her own 
money. It did not even address, much less invalidate, any 
other regulation of the candidate’s contributions of 


money to her campaign. 


Because Buckley held only that candidates cannot be 
limited in the amount of their own money spent in sup- 
port of their campaigns, that holding is inapplicable in 
this case. Buckley said nothing about “time, place or man- 


ner” regulations that might limit when candidates could 


make those contributions. In Buckley, the Court 


explained: 


[T]he critical difference between this case 
and ... time, place, and manner cases is that the 
present Act’s contribution and expenditure limita- 
tions impose direct quantity restrictions on political 
communication and association by persons, 
groups, candidates, and political parties in addi- 
tion to any reasonable time, place, and manner 
regulations otherwise imposed. A restriction on 
the amount of money a person or group can 
spend on political communication during a cam- 
paign necessarily reduces the quantity of 
expression by restricting the number of issues 
discussed, the depth of their exploration, and 
the size of the audience reached 


Buckley, 424 U.S. at 18-19 (emphasis added). 


The 28-Day Timing Window does not restrict the 
amount of the candidate’s own money which he may 
spend on “speech” supporting his candidacy. Nonpar- 
ticipating Slates may spend as much as they please. They 
mereiy have to inform the Registry of Election Finance 
prior to the 28-Day deadline that they intend to spend 
more than the voluntary limit, thereby pulling the Trig- 
ger. Unlike the expenditure limitations at issue in Buckley, 
the 28-Day Timing Window regulates only the time in 
which the candidate’s money must be deposited to the 
candidate’s campaign account and reported. The candi- 
date continues to be free to expend as much as he wishes 
on his campaign during the 28 days prececing the elec- 
tion. As Judge Cudahy recognized in his dissent: 


To justify its result, the majority is not, as it 
claims, merely applying Buckley v. Valeo, 424

Pet. App. at 25a. 


Based on the foregoing, and using Petitioner Gable’s 
own reasoning, it is clearly a matter of national impor- 
tance for this Court to clarify whether the 28-Day Timing 
Window is a reasonable, and hence constitutional, time, 
place, and manner regulation that furthers Kentucky’s 
interest in combating corruption and upholding the integ- 
rity of its election process. 


Petitioner Gable cites to several state initiatives relat- 
ing to campaign finance reform and suggests that the 
Court should clarify the application of Buckley to such 
reform measures to “avoid multiple, disruptive lawsuits 
over the next electoral cycle.” Pet. at 7. To the extent this 
Court’s review would be helpful in that regard, it would 
likewise be helpful in clarifying the extent to which a 
State may restrict the timing of a candidate’s contribu- 
tions to her own campaign to assure the effectiveness of 
its entire public financing framework. All of the initia- 
tives cited by Petitioner Gable include some type of dis- 
closure or notification requirement whereby a candidate 
who voluntarily participates in spending limits is

afforded sufficient notice that a nonparticipating oppo- 
nent has exceeded those limits. For instance, the Califor- 
nia statute, cited in the Petition, raises the spending limit 
for participating candidates once a nonparticipating can-

certain percentage of its ceiling. Cal. Gov. Code § 85404. 
To effectuate that measure, the statute requires “candi- 
dates and independent committees to provide sufficient 
notice to the commission and to all candidates for the 
same office that they are approaching and exceeding the 
thresholds set forth in this section.” Id. 


Kentucky’s 28-Day Timing Window is in effect noth- 
ing more than a “notice” requirement similar to one in 
the California statute.2 As Judge Cudahy explained in his 
dissent, “[aJll the 28-Day Window provides is notice that 
the contribution has been made and an opportunity for 
the opponent to respond.” Pet. App. at 26a. The timing 
restriction assures Participating Slates that they will not 
be surprised by “last-minute, unreported contributions” 
by wealthy opponents that cannot be countered by their 
own fundraising and matching funds. Truly, if a candi- 
date were allowed to spend millions of dollars after the 
final report of contributions was filed, there would be no 
way to afford a Participating Slate any meaningful oppor- 
tunity to respond. Without the reassurance of the 28-Day 
Window, few candidates would agree to accept the cam- 
paign spending limitations required by the statute and
