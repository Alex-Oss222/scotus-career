REASONS TO DENY WRIT 


Review on a writ of certiorari is a matter purely of 
judicial discretion, to be granted only for the most com- 
pelling reasons. Petitioner Gable states that the questions 
raised in his Petition are ones of serious national impor- 
tance. However characterized, the fact remains that these 
very questions were resolved fully and correctly in a 
court of appeals decision that comports entirely with 
precedents both from this Supreme Court and other cir- 


cults. 


I. THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY FOL- 
LOWED ESTABLISHED PRECEDENT IN HOLD- 
ING KENTUCKY’S TRIGGER PROVISION 
CONSTITUTIONAL. 


In Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 57 n.65 (1976), this 
Court sanctioned the use of public financing plans as a 
means for limiting a candidate’s aggregate contributions 
and expenditures, which in turn works to combat actual 
and perceived corruption in the election process. As long 
as the plan is voluntary, and the candidate remains free to 
choose private financing over limited public financing, 
there is no First Amendment infringement on the rights 


of the candidate or supporters. Id.; Republican National 
Committee v. Federal Election Commission, 487 F. Supp. 280, 
284 (S.D.N.Y.), aff'd mem., 445 U.S. 955 (1980). 


Years later, finding that a state has a compelling 
interest to stimulate participation in its public financing 
plans, federal courts agreed that states may, and often 
must, offer additional inducement to encourage candi- 
dates to accept voluntary spending limits. Rosenstiel v. 
Rodriguez, 101 F.3d 1544 (8th Cir. 1996); Vote Choice, Inc. v. 
DiStefano, 4 F.3d 26 (1st Cir. 1993). The Trigger provision 
in Kentucky’s campaign finance law effectuates just such 
an inducement. 


Clearly, the biggest obstacle to a campaign finance 
reform program and its attendant spending constraints is 
the very real fear of participant candidates that they will 
be vastly outspent by their limitless, privately financed 
opponents. The Kentucky Trigger provision operates to 
alleviate that concern for participant candidates by 
releasing them from their voluntarily accepted expendi- 
ture limit of $1.8 million if their nonparticipant oppo- 
nents’ contributions exceed that mark. As a further 
incentive, and reward for voluntarily limiting expendi- 
tures to that point, participant candidates continue to 
receive public subsidy after their opponents’ contribu- 
tions trigger the suspension of the limit. 


The court of appeals correctly held that the Kentucky 
incentives were no different in kind than clearly constitu- 
tional incentives and therefore did not impermissibly 
burden the First Amendment rights of Petitioner. Pet. 
App. at 15a. To find otherwise, the court stated, “would 
be making a distinction based on degree,” which the 
Buckley decision explicitly forbids. Id. Certainly the intent 
and effect of the Trigger is to make participation in public 
funding the preferred choice among candidates. But, as 


the court of appeals aptly recognized, “a voluntary cam- 
paign finance scheme must rely on incentives for partici- 
pation, which, by definition, means structuring the 
scheme so that participation is usually the rational 
choice.” Id. 


Gable disagrees with the court of appeals that partici- 
pation in Kentucky’s public financing program is a ratio- 
nal choice, arguing instead that participation is the only 
choice thereby leaving candidates “no choice at all.” Pet. 
at 8-9. Nothing could be further from the truth. Voluntar 
public subsidy alternatives have vastly increased the 
options for campaign financing. To the extent Gable per- 
ceives his decision whether or not to accept public financ- 
ing as a Hobson’s choice endangering his First 
Amendment rights, such is a dilemma peculiar to him. As 
the Eighth Circuit explained in upholding a similar Min- 
nesota trigger provision, 


In sum, the State has created a public financing 
scheme for certain elected offices which is avail- 
able to candidates who meet certain threshold 
qualifications. This scheme presents candidates 
with an additional, optional campaign funding 
choice, the participation in which is voluntary. 
Under this choice-increasing framework, candi- 
dates will presumably select the option they feel 
is most advantageous to their candidacy. Given 
this backdrop, it appears to us that the State’s 
scheme promotes, rather than detracts from, 
cherished First Amendment values. 


Rosenstiel v. Rodriguez, 101 F.3d 1544, 1552 (8th Cir. 1996) 
(citations omitted). See Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 92-93 
(1976) (“[Public funding provisions are] a congressional 
effort, not to abridge, restrict or censor speech, but rather 


to use public money to facilitate and enlarge public dis- 
cussion and participation in the electoral process... ”). 


Extraordinarily contrary to his “no choice” lamenta- 
tions, Gable in fact retains, at least up to the final pre- 
election reporting period, not just choices regarding the- 
Trigger but sole control over whether it is activated. In 
Rosenstiel, a Minnesota trigger provision originally 
released a participating candidate from expenditure lim- 
itations at the moment his opponent opted out of the 
public finance program. The legislature amended the pro- 
vision to, like Kentucky’s Trigger, releasing the partici- 
pant only if and when his opponent exceeds the 
voluntary expenditure cap. 101 F.3d at 1551. The Eighth 
Circuit sanctioned the change, noting the extent to which 
the amendment puts the nonparticipating candidate, such 
as Gable, squarely in the driver’s seat. 


Rather than releasing the participating candi- 
date from the expenditure limits at the outset, 
the statute as it now stands permits the nonpar- 
ticipating candidate to control whether and 
when the participating opponent will be freed 
from the limits. Thus, in a sense, the amendment 
works in favor of, rather than to the detriment 
of, the nonparticipating candidate. 


Id. (citing Wilkinson v. Jones, 876 F. Supp. 916 (W.D.Ky. 
1995)). 


It is therefore quite puzzling how Gable can complain 
that “the Kentucky trigger provision both fetters the con- 
stitutionally protected choices of candidates who forego 
public campaign subsidies and leaves such candidates 
worse off than they would have been without any public 
financing program at all.” Pet. at 11. The fact that a 
candidate is now given the additional option to accept 


public financing with its attendant trigger incentives and 
spending limitations cannot be said to “fetter” the private 
funding choice. Increased choices often require hard 
decisions. Candidates such as Gable are free to choose the 


alternative most advantageous to their particular cam- 
paign strategies. In that respect, First Amendment values 
are furthered, not smothered. Vote Choice, Inc. v. 
DiStefano, 4 F.3d 26, 29 (1st Cir. 1993). See also Weber v. 
Heaney, 793 F. Supp. 1438, 1457 (D. Minn. 1992) (“The 
First Amendment is not implicated where candidates 
remain free to choose between funding alternatives.”). 


Gable characterizes the Trigger incentive not as a 
carrot dangled by the state to lure candidates into public 
finance, admittedly constitutionally acceptable, but as a 
“substantial stick” designed to inflict on nonparticipants 
the “pain of Kentucky’s publicly-financed retaliation.” 
Pet. at 8-9. Courts have viewed such distinctions, even 
when expressed less dramatically, as more “semantic than 
substantive.” Vote Choice, 4 F.3d at 38. The Trigger provi- 
sion is, in reality, simply “a premium earned by meeting 
statutory eligibility requirements rather than a penalty 
imposed on those who either cannot or will not satisfy 
the requirements.” Id. 


Gable points to no explicit statutory restrictions on 
the amount of funds a nonparticipating candidate can 
raise. Instead, he claims to experience the penalty, the 
burden, the coercion, the “chill” on his constitutional 
rights by watching the benefits enjoyed by his publicly 
financed opponent. Courts have resoundingly rejected 
such a “benefits equal burdens” argument. The Rosenstiel 
Court explains the fallacy of Gable’s position in the con- 
text of Minnesota’s trigger provision: 


These inducements, however, do not per se ren- 
der the State’s scheme coercive because they are 
not inherently penal. Further, the inclusion of 
these additional inducements in the State’s pub- 
lic financing package does not cause the pack- 
age to become so benefit-laden as to create such 
a large disparity between benefits and restric- 
tions that candidates are coerced to publicly 
finance their campaigns. Rather, by including 
these additional inducements, the State’s 
scheme achieves a relative balance between the 
benefits provided to publicly financed candi- 
dates and the restrictions the candidates must 
accept. The expenditure limitation waiver, 
which permits a publicly financed candidate to 
exceed the expenditure limits while retaining 
the public subsidy when opposed by a nonpar- 
ticipating candidate who has spent or received 
contributions beyond the triggering amounts 
spelled out in the statute, is simply an attempt 
by the State to avert a powerful disincentive for 
participation in its public financing scheme: 
namely, a concern of being grossly outspent by a 
privately financed opponent with no expendi- 
ture limit. We believe the statute is not coercive 
because it permits the nonparticipating candi- 
date to raise a certain measure of funds before 
triggering the expenditure limitation waiver for 
his participating opponent. 


101 F.3d at 1549-1551. See also Vote Choice, Inc. v. DiStefano, 
4 F.3d 26, 38 (1st Cir. 1993); Republican National Committee 
v. Federal Election Commission, 47 F. Supp. 280, 285 
(S.D.N.Y.) (three-judge court), aff.i mem., 445 U.S. 955 
(1980); Weber v. Heaney, 793 F. Supp. 1438, 1457 (D. Minn. 
1992). 


ee 


The Trigger mechanism contained in Kentucky’s cam- 
paign finance law does not in any way restrict the speech 
of a nonparticipant candidate, as Gable claims. It does, 
however, work to un-restrict the speech of the participant 
candidate. By removing the government-imposed shackle 
on subsidized candidates, the ultimate net effect of an 
activated Trigger is to increase “dissemination of infor- 
mation from diverse and antagonistic sources” thereby 
promoting, rather than burdening, First Amendment 
values. New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254, 266 
(1964). 


Il. THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY UPHELD 
THE 28 DAY TIMING WINDOW AS A CRITICAL 
COMPONENT TO KENTUCKY’S PUBLIC 
FINANCING SYSTEM. 


The 28 Day Timing Window prohibits any slate of 
candidates for governor and lieutenant governor from 
knowingly accepting any campaign contribution after the 
final pre-election reporting deadline (twenty-eight days 
before a primary or general election, or fourteen days 
before a run-off primary), unless it or another slate has by 
that date reported contributions exceeding the $1.8 mil- 
lion voluntary expenditure limit. If the Trigger is pulled, 
both Nonparticipating and Participating Slates are per- 
mitted to accept contributions during the Window. 


Gable equates the Window’s restrictions to a ban on 
cheering in the final moments of a football game, a mea- 
sure he suggests would “miss the very point of the enter- 
prise.” Pet. at 15. The problem with Petitioner’s football 
analogy, of course, is that he wants the cheering at the 


end of the game - or the eve of the election — to be all for 
him. Without the Window’s timing restrictions, nonpar- 
ticipant candidates like Gable would be able to blitz their 
expenditure-limited opponents with last-minute spend- 
ing that could not be countered. Contrary to Gable’s 
sports metaphor, the very point of the Window enterprise 
is not to ban speech at a critical period but to assure 
even-handed cheering that is reflective of the support for 
and views of both teams and/or candidates. See Anderson 
v Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 779, 788 n.9 (1983) (“State has right 
to prevent distortion of electoral process by device of 
“party raiding,” the organized switching of blocks of 
voters from one party to another in order to manipulate 
the outcome of the other party’s primary election”). 


Truly, it is not the sheer number, but rather the diver- 
sity of views that ensures the “robust, democratic discus- 
sion” protected by the First Amendment. Gable’s First 
Amendment rights are not burdened by a mechanism that 
assures a publicly financed candidate the opportunity to 
respond to last-minute, unreported contributions and 
accordingly to maintain meaningful participation in the 
election process. Without such an assurance, few candi- 
dates would agree to participate in Kentucky’s campaign 
financing program designed to prevent corruption and 
preserve public confidence in the election process. The 
court of appeals correctly upheld the 28 Day Timing 
Window insofar as it applies to third party contributions.’
