REPLY TO BRIEFS IN OPPOSITION 


1. The Brief in Opposition of the Kentucky Registry 
Respondents asserts that Petitioner Gable lacks standing 
to challenge the constitutionality of Kentucky’s campaign 
finance regulations because he is not a declared candidate 
in this year’s gubernatorial campaign even though he ran 
for governor in 1995 and has expressed interest in running 
again. Registry Opp. Br. 21-24. This assertion is without 
merit. 


It is well established that persons who have been de- 
terred from acting by the very laws they allege to be un- 
lawful have suffered sufficiently personal and concrete 
injury to challenge those laws. See, e.g., International 
Brotherhood of Teamsters v. United States, 431 U.S. 324, 
362-67 (1977) (holding that non-applicants for a job may 
receive relief from racially discriminatory employment prac- 
tices that deterred them from applying). This is especially 
true in the election context. For example, in Anderson v. 
Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983), this Court entertained a 
challenge to a state’s early filing deadline for a place on 
the presidential ballot, even though the candidate bringing 
the challenge had missed that deadline. Similarly, in 
Clements v. Fashing, 457 U.S. 957, 962 (1982), this Court 
recognized the standing of state officeholders to challenge 
a state rule that required them to resign from their jobs if 
they ran as candidates for another office, even though none 
of the challengers had chosen to resign in order to run. 


Gable likewise is entitled to challenge a campaign fi- 
nance law he alleges violates his First Amendment rights, 
even if he declines to campaign for the governorship unless 
and until that constitutional defect is removed. Kentucky’s 
election finance laws plainly act as a deterrent to those 
challengers who, like Gable, do not think that running a 
race under such restrictions is worth the candle. See Al 
Cross, Election 1999; GOP Adds Candidate for Governor; 


2 


Peppy Martin files; Other Races Conceded, Louisville Cou- 
rier-Journal, Jan. 27, 1999, p.1b (noting that Kentucky 
Republican leaders attributed their party’s “lack of inter- 
est in the governor’s race” in large part to the fact that it 
“was unwinnable because of the law that limits spending 
by candidates who agree to take public financing”). It is 
hardly Gable’s fault that Kentucky has kept the challenged 
regulations in force while this litigation has exceeded the 
duration of an entire election cycle. 


Nor is Gable’s challenge “moot,” as the Registry Re- 
spondents allege, see Registry Opp. Br. 23. Precisely be- 
cause constitutional questions about election practices are 
difficult to resolve within a single election cycle, this Court 
has repeatedly declined to find election challenges moot 
even after the election that gave rise to the challenge is 
long since over. See, e.g., Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 724, 
737 n.8 (1974) (holding that a challenge to restrictions on 
independent candidacies could proceed even after an elec- 
tion because the issues it presented would “persist as the 
. . . Statutes are applied in future elections”); Moore uv. 
Ogilivie, 394 U.S. 814, 815-16 (1969) (likewise holding that 
a challenge to election statutes was justiciable because “ca- 
pable of repetition, yet evading review”). These precedents 
plainly foreclose any mootness holding here. 


2. Both Briefs in Opposition claim that Kentucky's 
public campaign financing law is a mere selective subsidy 
scheme that does not burden First Amendment rights be- 
cause it leaves a candidate who declines to participate no 
worse off with the program than he would be without it. 
See Registry Opp. Br. 5-21; Patton Opp. Br. 2-8. This 
claim is incorrect. Kentucky is of course free under this 
Court’s precedents to subsidize only those candidates who 
agree to participate in the state’s scheme, and such a se- 
lective subsidy is not in itself a burden on the nonpartici- 
pating candidate’s rights. But that is not what Kentucky 
has done. Instead, Kentucky has made its grant of addi- 


3 


tional benefits to a participating candidate contingent upon 
a nonparticipating candidate’s own exercise of First Amend- 
ment rights. If and only if the nonparticipating candidate 
spends above $1.8 million does the state lift spending re- 
strictions on participating candidates and match their fur- 
ther fundraising efforts 2-for-1 with public funds. Such a 
scheme crosses over the line from a simple nonsubsidy of a 
nonparticipating candidate's speech to a penalty upon that 
speech, and thus warrants this Court’s review. 


Nothing in this Court’s precedents concerning speech 
subsidies authorizes a government to make a particular 
exercise of private speech the trigger for a state-subsidized 
response. To the contrary, the Court’s only decisions re- 
viewing analogous triggers have invalidated them. See 
Miami Herald Pub. Co. v. Tornillo, 418 U.S. 241 (1974); 
Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. Public Util. Comm’n, 475 U.S. 
1 (1986). The Registry Respondents attempt to distin- 
guish those cases on the ground that they involved a tax 
upon the burdened speakers, who were required in effect 
to pay for the dissemination of their opponents’ speech. 
See Registry Opp. Br. at 13-14. But this attempt is un- 
availing. For one thing, Tornillo and PG&E rested on 
twin First Amendment concerns: the right not to speak 
was infringed if the compulsory reply was carried, but the 
right to speak was infringed if the speaker chose silence to 
ensure that it was not. As the Court stated in Tornillo, 
“Faced with the penalties that would accrue to any news- 
paper that published news or commentary arguably within 
the reach of the right-of-access statute, editors might well 
conclude that the safe course is to avoid controversy,” in 
which case “political and electoral coverage would be 
blunted or reduced.” 418 U.S. at 257. The Kentucky cam- 
paign finance statute has precisely such a deterrent effect 
on protected political expenditures, and for that reason 
alone warrants this Court’s review. In addition, the Ken- 
tucky scheme itself imposes an effective tax upon the non-

participating candidate’s speech: once he spends more than 
$1.8 million, he must raise three times as much as the 
participating candidate in order to stay even. 


The difference between a mere nonsubsidy and a sub- 
sidy penalizing the exercise of constitutional rights would 
be clear in any other context. For example, it is one thing 
for the government to subsidize childbirth but not abor- 
tion; it would be quite another for the government to pay a 
doctor, for every abortion he performed, three times his fee 
for that service earmarked for the delivery of newborns. It 
is one thing for the government to offer to pay for a court- 
appointed psychiatrist to assess a criminal defendant’s com- 
petence; it would be quite another for the government to 
provide that if he chooses to pay for his own psychiatric 
expert the government will fund three psychiatrists to en- 
sure adequate rebuttal. Similarly, the government is free 
to choose what speech to fund, but it may not deploy subsi- 
dies invidiously “in such a way as to [aim] at the suppres- 
sion of dangerous ideas.” Regan v. Taxation with 
Representation, 461 U.S. 540, 548 (1983) (citations and in- 
ternal quotes omitted). Thus, contrary to Respondents’ 
assertions, the decision below is not consistent with the 
relevant precedents of this Court. 


3. Registry Respondents suggest that Kentucky’s cam- 
paign finance scheme is too idiosyncratic to warrant this 
Court’s review. Registry Opp. Br. at 24-27. Yet they them- 
selves suggest that any rational public financing scheme 
would adopt a “trigger” mechanism like Kentucky’s. See 
Registry Opp. Br. at 7-8 (“Were it otherwise, it would likely 
be impossible for a rational candidate to agree to spending 
limits ... .”); see also Patton Opp. Br. at 9. If that is so, 
then it is all the more likely that the issues presented here 
will continue to arise in more and more states. Registry 
respondents concede thatjat least several states have al- 
ready enacted laws like Kentucky’s. Registry Opp. Br. at 
25. Accordingly, the Petition presents issues of national 


5 


importance and electoral urgency warranting this Court’s 
review. 


4. Respondents each assert that Kentucky’s total ban 
on campaign contributions during the twenty-eight days 
preceding an election is a “vital” or “critical” component of 
the state’s efforts to combat the corruption of candidates. 
See Registry Opp. Br. at 29; Patton Opp. Br. at 8. But they 
fail to offer a single explanation why that is so. While they 
colorfully chronicle “the pervasive history of corruption in 
Kentucky politics,” Registry Opp. Br. at 29, they offer not 
an iota of evidence to suggest when in an election cycle 
such corruption allegedly occurs. Nor do they explain why 
limits on the amount of each contribution are insufficient 
to prevent capture of political candidates by large donors, 
without any additional restrictions on the timing. Like a 
law that authorized the publication of newspapers or books 
on politics during only eleven months of the year, the 28- 
day ban does not merely channel but limits the quantity of 
speech at the core of the First Amendment, and thus war- 
rants this Court’s review. 


CONCLUSION

Certiorari, Petitioner respectfully requests that this Court 
grant the Petition for Certiorari.
