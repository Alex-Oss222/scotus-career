INTRODUCTORY STATEMENT 


Respondents John T. Benson, ef al. (“the State of 
Wisconsin” or “the State”) hereby oppose the Petition for Writ 
of Certiorari. The issue presented in this case is whether the 
Milwaukee Parental Choice Program—a “school choice” 
program which, in conjunction with existing programs within 
the Milwaukee Public School (“MPS”) system, affords low- 
income MPS families the opportunity to educate their children 
at public, private non-sectarian, or private sectarian 
schools—violates the Establishment Clause of the First 
Amendment (as incorporated by the Fourteenth Amendment). 
That issue is of profound legal and practical importance, both 
to Wisconsin and to the Nation. Nonetheless, two factors 
counsel in favor of awaiting a later opportunity to address the 
constitutionality of school choice programs. 


First, contrary to Petitioners’ assertion, this important 
federal question has not been decided by the Supreme Court of 
Wisconsin “‘in a way that conflicts with relevant decisions of 
this Court.’” Pet. at 9 (quoting Sup. Ct. R. 10(c)). To the 
contrary, a ruling invalidating the Milwaukee Parental Choice 
Program (“the expanded Choice Plan”) on Establishment 
Clause grounds would have been in conflict with a half- 
century’s worth of this Court’s precedents—stretching from 
Everson v. Board of Educ., 330 U.S. 1 (1947), through more 
recent decisions such as Mueller v. Allen, 463 U.S. 388 (1983), 
Witters v. Washington Dep't of Servs. for the Blind, 474 U.S. 
481 (1986), Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills Sch. Dist., 509 US. 1 
(1993), Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of Univ. of 
Virginia, 515 U.S. 819 (1995), and Agostini v. Felton, 117 
S. Ct. 1997 (1997). The Wisconsin court conducted a thorough 
review of this Court’s Establishment Clause jurisprudence, and, 
based on that review, correctly ruled that any state funding that 
flows to sectarian schools under the expanded Choice Plan 
does so only on a neutral and indirect basis, and therefore is 
constitutionally permissible under the Establishment Clause. 


Second, the State agrees with Petitioners that this Court 
would provide a valuable service by clarifying whether its 1973 


2 


decision in Committee for Public Educ. & Religious Liberty v. 
Nyquist, 413 U.S. 756, remains “good law.” This case, 
however, presents a poor opportunity for such clarification. 
The Wisconsin court did not dispute that Nyquist constitutes 
“good law”; it simply determined—and correctly so—that 
Nyquist was “not controll{ing]” because the expanded Choice 
Pian is distinguishable from the New York tuition 
reimbursement program invalidated in Nyquist. For example, 
the state court concluded that whereas the Nyquist program 
“directed aid exclusively to private schools and their students,” 
the expanded Choice P!an, “viewed in its surrounding context, 
merely adds religious schools to a range of pre-existing 
educational choices available to MPS children.” Pet. App. 19a~ 
n.9. Because Nyquist is distinguishable in this and other ways, 
this case does not present a fitting opportunity for the Court to 
declare definitively whether Nyquist remains “good law,” 
which is the sort of ruling Petitioners appear to be seeking. 
Instead, the case presents only an opportunity to determine 
once again the extent to which certain dictum in Nyquist has 
relevance in other factual settings. A better vehicle for 
accomplishing Petitioners’ goal—i.e., for clarifying, once and 
for all, whether Nyquist remains “good law”—would be a case 
in which a lower court found the challenged law 
indistinguishable from Nyquist, and expressly stated that the 
court was constrained to follow Nyquist, notwithstanding its 
apparent obsolescence. It is possible that such a ruling will 
emerge in the near future: Although there is no current lower 
court conflict regarding the constitutionality of school choice 
programs, the high courts of Arizona, Maine, Ohio and 
Vermont are presently considering similar challenges to school 


choice programs. 


For these reasons, the State respectfully suggests that 
resolution of the critically important question of the 
constitutionality of school choice programs should await a 
more suitable vehicle, which may emerge as more lower courts 
are called upon to resolve that question.

COUNTERSTATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Any consideration of the constitutionality of the expanded 
Choice Plan must begin with a frank recognition of the serious 
problems facing the MPS. It is beyond dispute that the 
students and schools in the MPS face the most profound 
educational challenges in the State of Wisconsin. Indeed, ina 
decision upholding the constitutionality of the original version 
of the Choice Plan, the Wisconsin Supreme Court recognized 
that “the Milwaukee Public School District has significantly 
greater education and poverty problems than any other school 
district in the state.” Davis v. Grover, 166 Wis.2d 501, 529, 
480 N.W.2d 460, 469 (1992). The court based that assessment 
on “[t]he statistical data,” which “clearly illustrates that the 
socioeconomic disparities and the educational problems are 
greater in the large urban area of Milwaukee than any other 
part of Wisconsin.” /d. at 529-30, 480 N.W.2d at 470; see also 
Kukor v. Grover, 148 Wis.2d 469, 482-83, 436 N.W.2d 568, 
573-74 (1989). 


By any measure of educational performance, MPS has 
performed dismally. For example, in 1989, when the original 
Choice Plan was under consideration, the dropout rate for high- 
school students reached 14.4 percent. See Davis, 166 Wis.2d 
at 529, 480 N.W.2d at 469. At the same time, schools in the 
rest of the state experienced a much lower dropout rate of 3.11 
percent. See id. In 1989, less than 45 percent of MPS students 
graduated from high school within six years—down from 57 
percent just five years earlier. See id. at 531, 480 N.W.2d at 
470. And of those who did graduate within six years in 1989, 
more than one-third did so with a “D” average. See id. 


The Wisconsin Supreme Court in Davis v. Gre ver found that 
these realities “dramatically show the need for legislative 
attention.” Jd. at 529, 480 N.W.2d at 469.

That legislative attention led to the enactment of the original 
Milwaukee Parental Choice Program in 1989 (“the original 
Choice Plan”), and continued with the expanded Choice Plan 
now under challenge. 1995 Wis. Act 27, §§ 4002-09; Wis. 
Stat. § 119.23. As described below, all of these efforts at 
educational reform by the Wisconsin legislature have been 
directed, first and foremost, at solving an educational crisis 
affecting students in the State’s largest public school 
system—not at promoting private sectarian education. 


A. The original Choice Plan. 


Over the past 25 years, a variety of programs—including 
desegregation, special school financing, and the introduction of 
specialized magnet schools—have been initiated to address 
MPS’ unique problems. None of these programs averted the 
worsening situation. As the Wisconsin Supreme Court 
acknowledged in Davis, the original Choice Plan was a direct 
response to the continuing poor performance of Milwaukee 
students despite decades of state programming targeted at 
reforming MPS: “The program [was] an attempt to identify 
factors which could improve the quality of education.” Davis, 
166 Wis. 2d at 512 n.2, 480 N.W.2d at 462 n.2. 


The Wisconsin legislature, long familiar with choice- 
inspired solutions to educational problems, responded to the 
troubles of the MPS system in 1989 by enacting the original 
Choice Plan, Wis. Stat. § 119.23 (West 1991 & Supp. 1994). 
The original Plan provided a limited number of MPS students'

schools. For example, § 4003 of the amendments expanded the 
number of Milwaukee area children eligible to participate in 
the Plan—from 1.5 percent of MPS students in the original 
Plan, to 15 percent under the expanded Plan. Other 
amendments, meanwhile, related to the opening of the Plan to

Il. Prior Proceedings and Disposition. 


Shortly after the original Choice Plan was enacted in 1989, 
opponents of school choice programs— including several of the 
Petitioners who are now before this Court—filed suit to enjoin 
the Plan on various state constitutional grounds. The Supreme 
Court of Wisconsin ultimately rejected their challenge to the 
original Plan. Davis v. Grover, supra. 


When the legislature enacted the 1995 Amendments, school 
choice opponents once again promptly sought to enjoin 
implementation of the expanded Choice Plan. The present 
litigation thus began on August 1, 1995, when two groups of 
plaintiffs—Petitioners Warner Jackson, ef al. (“ACLU”), and 
Petitioners Milwaukee Teachers Educational Association, e/ 
al.—filed complaints in the Circuit Court of Dane County, 
Wisconsin, challenging the expanded Plan on a host of 
constitutional grounds.’ To the various state-constitution- 
based, non-religion-clause claims that Petitioners had advanced 
in their attempt to defeat the original Plan, Petitioners now 
added (1) a claim under the Establishment Clause of the First 
Amendment to the federal constitution, and (2) a similar claim 
based on Art. I, § 18 of the Wisconsin Constitution,’ 
contending that the expanded Choice Plan impermissibly 
conferred a “benefit” on religious institutions.

After receiving Petitioners’ complaints, the State—acting on

action in the Supreme Court of Wisconsin, seeking a

The state supreme court accepted original jurisdiction, and on 
March 29, 1996, announced that it had deadlocked 3-3 on the 
constitutional issues (with the seventh Justice not 
participating). See Pet. App. 189a-19la. Having divided 
equally, the state supreme court dismissed the petition, 
effectively remanding the case to the Circuit Court of Dane 
County for further proceedings. /d. at 19 1a. 


In January 1997, the circuit court granted summary 
judgment for Petitioners. See Pet. App. 138a-188a. The court 
concluded, inter alia, that “by permitting religious schools to 
participate in the program,” the expanded Plan violated Art. I, 
§ 18 of the Wisconsin Constitution. /d. at 169a. Having found 
the expanded Plan invalid on state grounds, the circuit court 
did not reach the Establishment Clause issue. 


The court of appeals affirmed by a 2-1 vote. See Pet. App. 
59a-137a. Like the circuit court, the court of appeals 
“conclude[d] that the amended program violates the religious 
benefit clause of Article I, section 18 of the Wisconsin 
Constitution,” id. at 61a, and therefore found it “unnecessary 
to address” Petitioners’ Establishment Clause challenge, id. at 
87a. Although it did not formally rule on the Establishment 
Clause issue, the court of appeals “consult[ed] United States 
Supreme Court [Establishment Clause] cases applying the 
‘primary effect’ test” in conducting its Art. I. § 18 analysis. /d. 
at 80a. Relying exclusively on Nyquist, the court found that the 
“primary effect” test did “not undermine” its conclusion that 
the expanded plan was invalid under Art. I, § 18. /d.

The Supreme Court of Wisconsin granted Petitions for 
Review, and, by a 4-2 margin,’ reversed the court of appeals’ 
ruling and rejected a// of Petitioners’ various challenges to the 
expanded Plan. 


The state supreme court rejected Petitioners’ Establishment 
Clause claim following a thorough review of this Court's 
jurisprudence. See Pet. App. 1 1a-31a. The court analyzed the 
expanded Choice Plan’s constitutionality under the Lemon 
framework, explaining that “[w]hile the continued authority of 
the test established in Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602 
(1971), is uncertain, we have no choice but to apply it in this 
case.” Pet. App. 14a n.5. Disposing of the “secular purpose” 
and “excessive entanglement” inquiries in fairly short order, 
see id at 14a-15a, 29a-31a, the court devoted the bulk of its

body of law, the court “distill[ed] . . . an underlying theory

citizens in a class defined without reference to religion do not 
have the primary effect of advancing religion.” Pet. App. 16a- 
17a (footnotes omitted). With respect to neutrality, the 
Wisconsin court noted that ever since Everson v. Board of 
Educ., 330 U:S. 1 (1947), “[t}he Supreme Court has historically

Bowen v. Kendrick. 487 U.S. 589 (1988); Roemer v. Md. Bd. of 
Public Works, 426 U.S. 736 (1976); Hunt v. McNair, 413 U.S.

REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 


I. The Supreme Court of Wisconsin Correctly Ruled 
That, Under This Court’s Precedents, the Expanded 
Choice Plan is a Neutral and Indirect State Funding 
Program That Does Not Violate the Establishment 
Clause. 


Contrary to Petitioners’ claim, the court below did not
