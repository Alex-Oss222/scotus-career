STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Kentucky law prohibits all slates of candidates for 
Governor and Lieutenant Governor, including those who 
elect not to participate in the state’s public financing 
scheme, from accepting any contributions during the 
twenty-eight days preceding a primary or general election. 
Ky. Rev. Stat. § 121A.030(5). This prohibition applies to 
candidates’ expenditures of their own funds upon their own 
campaigns as well as to contributions from outside sources. 
Pet, App. 4a. The question presented for review in the 
cross-petition, Cross-Pet. at i, incorrectly suggests that this 
prohibition extends solely to a candidate’s “personal con- 
tributions to her own campaign in excess of the voluntary 
spending limits” (emphasis added). But the prohibition is 
not so limited; it bars any expenditure of the candidate’s 
own funds in the twenty-eight days before an election re- 
gardless of how much that candidate has spent before that 
period begins. 

In reviewing the constitutionality of this twenty- 
eight day “window,” both the district court and the court of 
appeals distinguished its effects on candidates’ expendi- 
ture of personal funds from its effects on contributions 
from outside sources. In a portion of the decision below 
that petitioner/cross-respondent Gable challenges in his 


> 


_ 


3 


petition for certiorari, the court of appeals held that “the 
28-day Window is constitutional with respect to external 
contributions.” Pet. App. 19a. The court reasoned that, 
while “the application of Buckley to the instant case is far 
from straightforward,” forcing candidates to “rearrange 
their fundraising by concentrating it in the period before 
the 28-Day Window begins” was “justified by Kentucky's 
interest in combating corruption.” Pet. App. 19a. 

But the court of appeals reached the opposite con- 
clusion with respect to internal expenditures, holding that 
“the 28-Day Window is unconstitutional when it prohibits 
a non-participating candidate from contributing to his own 
campaign.” Pet. App. 24a. The court accepted that “[t]he 
central holding” in Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 54 (1976), 
“is that spending money on one’s own speech must be 
permitted.” Pet. App. 20a (quoting Colorado Republican 
Fed. Campaign Comm. v. Federal Election Comm'n, 116 S. 
Ct. 2309, 2321 (1996) (Kennedy, J., concurring in the judg- 
ment and dissenting in part)). The court likewise acknowl- 
edged that restrictions upon expenditures of political funds 
impose a far greater burden on free speech than do restric- 
tions upon political contributions. Pet. App. 21a. And the 
court found Kentucky’s spending ban indistinguishable 
“from the internal expenditure provision struck down in 
Buckley.” Pet. App. 24a. 


The court of appeals rejected outright Kentucky’s 
efforts to depict its twenty-eight-day spending ban as a 
mere “financial technicality,” Pet. App. 22a, or “time, place 
and manner restriction.” Pet. App. 23a. To the contrary, 
the court held, the effects of the spending ban on a 
candidate’s campaign are “substantial, rather than just be- 
ing a matter of timing.” Pet. App. 23a. As the court 
elaborated: 


[The ban] forces a candidate to decide in ad- 
vance how much of his own money he will 
spend, before he has a chance to assess the 
public opinion polls and actions of his oppo- 
nents in the last month of the campaign. 
Moreover, it would simply not be feasible for 
a candidate to deposit virtually all of his per- 
sonal resources in the campaign account in 


4 


advance, just in case he decides, during the 

last twenty-eight days, to spend those re- 

sources on his campaign. ... [I]t is unlikely 

that all of the candidate’s personal resources 

will be liquid. . . . [And] the candidate can- 

not easily take back his money ii it is unex- 

pended at the end of a campaign [see Ky. 

Rev. Stat. § 121.180(10)}. In sum, it is disin- 

genuous for Defendants to argue that the 

28-Day Window does not burden a candidate’s 

ability to spend personal funds on his own 

campaign. 
Pet. App. 22a-23a (footnote omitted). Accordingly, the court 
of appeals held itself bound by this Court’s “clear state- 
ment that ‘the First Amendment simply cannot tolerate’ 
this type of restriction on the freedom of a candidate to 
speak on his own behalf.” Pet. App. 24a (quoting Buckley, 
424 U.S. at 54). 


REASONS FOR DENYING THE CROSS-PETITION 


The cross-petition raises no novel or pressing issue 
worthy of this Court’s review. Contrary to cross-petitioner’s 
assertions, the issue presented in the cross-petition was 
resolved by this Court’s decision in Buckley v. Valeo, 424 
U.S. 1 (1976), as the court below correctly decided. Buckley 
established unambiguously that limitations upon political 
expenditures trigger strictest scrutiny. It also made clear 
that such limitations cannot be justified by any interest in 
preventing corruption or the appearance of corruption, since 
a candidate cannot possibly corrupt himself, nor by any 
interest in equalizing the amounts of money spent by vari- 
ous candidates in a campaign, since such an interest is 
“wholly foreign to the First Amendment,” 424 U.S. at 45. 


Because the legal principle Buckley established with 
respect to political expenditures is so clear, the cross-peti- 
tion stands or falls on its attempt to distinguish Buckley 
on the facts. As cross-petitioner would have it, Kentucky 
has not limited “the amount of money” that candidates 
may spend in support of their own campaigns, Cross-Pet. 
at 8, but rather merely “the time in which the candidate's 
money must be deposited to the candidate’s campaign ac- 
count and reported,” Cross-Pet. at 9. 


5 


As the court of appeals correctly observed, however, 
this attempted distinction is “disingenuous.” Pet. App. 
23a. An absolute ban upon political expenditures during 
the last month of an election campaign limits the quantity, 
not merely the timing, of a candidate’s speech. It irrevoca- 
bly forecloses a candidate’s expenditure of funds to respond 
to late-breaking events in a campaign. It irrevocably bars 
him from using his own money to respond to an opponent’s 
unanticipated negative attacks. It irrevocably bars him 
from using his own money to state his position on unan- 
ticipated developments in the news or unanticipated policy 
initiatives announced by the incumbent government. It 
irrevocably bars him from using his own funds to announce 
new endorsements as a campaign draws to a close. In 
short, it imposes upon a candidate an irreversible penalty 
for failing to be a perfect clairvoyant one month before an 
election. 


Kentucky’s law thus imposes a “direct quantity re- 
striction on political communication” that is necessarily 
prohibited by this Court’s decision in Buckley, 424 U.S. at 
18. To call the twenty-eight-day ban on expenditures a 
“time” regulation is wordplay. Unlike classic time regula- 
tions such as bans on soundtrucks after midnight, which 
aim at ends such as residential tranquility that are unre- 
lated to the suppression of free expression, Kentucky’s law 
aims quite intentionally at the restriction of speech. As 
cross-petitioner Patton frankly admits, Kentucky’s goal is 
to protect candidates who participate in its public financ- 
ing scheme from “be[ing] surprised by” expenditures on 
speech “by wealthy opponents” in the late stages of a cam- 
paign. Cross-Pet. at 11. Such a goal is obviously related 
to the suppression of free expression. In short, the month- 
long Kentucky ban is no more a mere timing restriction on 
political speech than a law that authorizes the publication 
of newspapers or books on politics during only eleven 
months of the year. 


Even if the Kentucky law were characterized as a 
mere time restriction on electoral speech, this Court’s deci- 
sions make clea= that restrictions inhibiting political speech 
during a time period close to an election are highly sus- 
pect. See, e.g., Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983) 
(invalidating early filing deadline for independent candi- 


6 


dates); Dunn v. Blumstein, 405 U.S. 330 (1972) (invalidat- 
ing durational residency requirement for voting); Mills v. 
Alabama, 384 U.S. 214 (1966) (invalidating twenty-four- 
hour blackout of political editorials on election eve). See 
also Harman v. Forssenius, 380 U.S. 528, 539 (1965) (not- 
ing that poll taxes that had to be paid “long before the 
election” penalized those “voters who did not plan so far 
ahead”). For this reason, even independent of Buckley, the 
result reached by the court of appeals with respect to in- 
ternal expenditures was correct. 


Finally, resolution of the question presented in the 
cross-petition is not a pressing matter of national impor- 
tance. Kentucky is unable to name a single other state 
that has imposed such a substantial restriction on per- 
sonal expenditures by a candidate in the weeks preceding 
an election as the one struck down below. The California 
law that it cites, for example, merely requires nonpartici- 
pating candidates to provide “sufficient notice” that they 
are approaching the spending ceilings imposed upon par- 
ticipating candidates, Cal. Gov. Code § 85404 (cited in 
Cross-Pet. at 11), and cross-petitioner neglects to mention 
that the only court yet to review California’s campaign 
finance scheme has enjoined its key provisions as presump- 
tively unconstitutional. California Prolife Council Politi- 
cal Action Committee v. Scully, 989 F. Supp. 1282 (E.D. 
Cal. 1998); affd, 1999 U.S. Lexis 16 (9th Cir., Jan. 5, 1999). 
Accordingly, there is no danger of electoral disruption or 
confusion if the decision of the court of appeals with re- 
spect to internal expenditures is left standing. 


7 


CONCLUSION 


In contrast to the issues presented in the petition 
for certiorari in Gable v. Patton, No. 98-897, the issue 
presented in the cross-petition is resolved by Buckley and 
does not warrant this Court’s review. Thus, if this Court 
grants the petition, it should nonetheless deny the cross- 
petition.
