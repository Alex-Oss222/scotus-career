QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


The State of Wisconsin has enacted a private school 
voucher program for the City of Milwaukee. Under this 
program, the State will expend millions of tax dollars 
each school year to pay for thousands of elementary and 
secondary school students to attend private schools, the 
overwhelming majority of which are pervasively sectarian, 
and the private schools that receive these tax dollars will 
be free to use them for any secular or sectarian purposes 
they deem appropriate. The Supreme Court of Wisconsin 
held that the Milwaukee voucher program does not vio- 
late the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment 
to the United States Constitution. The following questions 
are presented for review: 


1. Does the Milwaukee voucher program violate the 
Establishment Clause? 


2. Is the decision of the Supreme Court of Wisconsin 
in conflict with this Court’s decision in Committee for 
Public Education v. Nyquist, 413 U.S. 756 (1973), and, 
if so, does Nyquist continue to state the law?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. Statement of Facts 


This case presents an Establishment Clause challenge 
to the 1995 amendments to the Milwaukee Parental 
Choice Program (“Amended MPCP”), Wis. Stat. 


3 


§ 119.23. Under the Amended MPCP, the State of Wis- 
consin will divert millions of tax dollars each school year 
from state aid that otherwise would be provided to the 
Milwaukee Public Schools (as much as $70,000,000 
during the 1998-99 school year alone), and use that 
money to pay for up to 15,000 students to attend private 
schools, the overwhelming majority of which are per- 
vasively sectarian. 


The Amended MPCP had its genesis in a much smaller, 
exclusively nonsectarian, program enacted in 1989 (“Orig- 
inal MPCP”). The Original MPCP, as amended in 
1993, permitted Milwaukee students from low-income 
families—up to a maximum of 1.5% of the approximately 
100,000 total student population of the Milwaukee Public 
Schools—to attend at state expense private nonsectarian 
schools in Milwaukee that chose to participate in the Pro- 
gram. For each student attending a private school pur- 
suant to the Program, the State paid directly to the par- 
ticipating private school an amount equal to the state aid 
that otherwise would have been paid to the Milwaukee 
Public Schools. The private school was not permitted 
to charge any additional tuition. 


The 1995 amendments opened the Program to all sec- 
tarian private schools in Milwaukee and increased the 
cap on the number of participating students from 1.5% 
of the Milwaukee Public Schools student population (ap- 
proximately 1,500) to 15% (approximattely 15,000). 
The stipulated record in this case establishes that. as of 
the 1995-96 school year, 122 private schools in Milwaukee 
were eligible to participate in the Amended MPCP. Of 
these 122 schools, 33 were nonsectarian, and 17 of those 
had elected to participate in the Original MPCP. The 
remaining 89 private schools eligible to participate in the 
Program—comprising 73 percent of the total number of 
Milwaukee private schools—were sectarian. During the 
1994-95 school year, these sectarian schools enrolled ap- 


4 


proximately 84 percent of all Milwaukee private-school 
students. 


The stipulated record includes as exhibits handbooks, 
mission statements, and other written materials issued by 
many of the sectarian private schools that had notified 
the Wisconsin Department of Public Instruction of their 
intent to participate in the Amended MPCP during the 
1995-96 school year. The parties have agreed that these 
materials accurately describe the purpose, structure, and 
operation of those schools. As the trial court found, these 
materials make it clear that the “mission” of those schools 
is “religious,” App. 147a, that “religious doctrine will be 
instilled in their students,” id., and that “one of the pri- 
mary means by which these schools accomplish their 
religious missions is by integrating the religious and sec- 
ular aspects of the schools’ educational programs.” App. 
149a.' In addition, as the court of appeals found, these 
materials “indicate that many [of these schools] charge 
tuition that is hundreds of dollars less than what the 
schools deem to be their actual cost of educating each stu- 
dent,” and are able to do so by virtue of subsidies they 
receive from the churches or other religious organizations 
that operate them. App. 67a. 


Under the Amended MPCP, for each student enrolled 
under the Program the State pays “an amount equal to 
the private school’s operating and debt service cost pet

Amended MPCP and less than a month before it was

to advancing several claims under the Wisconsin Consti- 
tution, each of the three complaints sought to have the 
Amended MPCP struck down as violative of the Estab- 
lishment Clause of the First Amendment to the United 
States Constitution.”

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


In this case, the Wisconsin Supreme Court “has de- 
cided an important federal question in a way that con- 
flicts with relevant decisions of this Court.” Sup. Ct. R. 
10(c). That question is central both to the development 
of this Court’s Establishment Clause jurisprudence and 
to the formulation of this nation’s education policy, and it 
therefore warrants the attention of this Court. 


10 


1. In Nyquist, supra, this Court struck down a private- 
school voucher program that had been enacted by the 
State of New York, holding that the use of public money 
to “subsidize and advance the religious mission of sec- 
tarian schools,” 413 U.S. at 779-80, crossed the line be- 
tween church and state drawn by the Establishment 
Clause. Although the Wisconsin Supreme Court acknowl- 
edged that the Amended MPCP “closely parallels” the 
New York program, it concluded that the two programs 
were distinguishable. Its reasoning in this regard cannot 
bear scrutiny, and, in point of fact, the highest court of 
the State of Wisconsin has rendered a constitutional deci- 
sion that is in direct and irreconcilable conflict with a 
decision of this Court. 


Nyquist is this Court’s last word on the constitution- 
ality of private-school voucher programs such as the 
Amended MPCP. But the jurisprudential situation is com- 
plicated by the fact that, in several post-Nyquist cases, 
this Court has sustained the constitutionality of programs 
that, while factually removed from those at issue here and 
in Nyquist, provided various types of aid to students who 
attended sectarian private schools. These intervening Es- 
tablishment Clause cases have led to the suggestion that 
Nyquist no longer is good law. Whether that is so only 
this Court can decide, and it should grant certiorari in 
order to do so. 


2. Resolution of the questions presented for review is 
important for practical as well as doctrinal reasons. Under 
the Amended MPCP, the State of Wisconsin will, during

reaffirmed the value of individual differences.”* In de-

plight of children attending “the inner city poverty area 
schools” which are “undersized and overcrowded.” 7 These 
are essentially the same justifications that have been ad-
