BRIEF AMICUS CURIAE OF THE NATIONAL 
COMMITTEE FOR PUBLIC EDUCATION AND 
RELIGIOUS LIBERTY IN SUPPORT OF 
THE PETITION FOR A WRIT OF CERTIORARI 


The National Committee for Public Education and 
Religious Liberty (“National PEARL”) submits this brief 
amicus curiae in support of the petition for a writ of certiorari 
filed on behalf of the petitioners, who were plaintiffs in the 
trial court and respondents in the Wisconsin Supreme Court in 
three consolidated cases challenging the constitutionality of a 
Wisconsin school voucher program. This brief is filed with 
the written consent of all parties, and their letters of consent 
have been filed with the Court.” 


INTEREST OF THE AMICUS CURIAE 


National PEARL is a diverse coalition of grassroots and 
national civic, educational, religious, labor and civil rights 
organizations and individual members that seeks to preserve 
religious freedom and maintain the separation of church and 
state in pubiic education. National PEARL believes that 
public funds should be spent in public schools and that public 
schools should remain safe havens for all children, regardless 
of their creed. 


The Wisconsin Supreme Court in Jackson v. Benson, 578 
N.W.2d 602 (Wis. 1998) (Pet. App. at la), misapplied this 
Court’s Establishment Clause jurisprudence, and as a result, 
upheld a statute providing for direct state funding of religious 
education. The Wisconsin court’s decision is flatly at odds

2 


with the basic constitutional principles that National PEARL 
and its members have fought to uphold. National PEARL 
urges review and reversal of that decision, to reaffirm the 
bedrock Establishment Clause prohibition against direct 
government sponsorship of religious activities. 


SUMMARY OF AMICUS’S POSITION 


The Milwaukee Parental Choice Program as initially 
established allowed a limited number of Milwaukee Public 
School students to attend “at no charge, any nonsectarian 
private school located” in the City of Milwaukee. In 1995, 
the state legislature removed the requirement that participating 
private schools be “nonsectarian.” Now, under the amended 
Milwaukee Parental Choice Program (“amended MPCP”) at 
issue in this case, approximately 15,000 Milwaukee students 
are allowed to attend sectarian private schools at state 
expense. The state pays these schools the lesser of the per- 
pupil state aid provided to Milwaukee Public Schools or the 
schools’ “operating and debt service cost per pupil that is 
related to education programming,” Wis. Stat. § 119.23(4), 
even though tuition at most participating sectarian schools is 
less than either figure. Once the state funds reach the schools’ 
coffers, they can be used for any purpose at all, including 
paying their clergy, purchasing religious literature or 
supporting missionary work. 


Twenty-five years ago, this Court rejected a private- 
school voucher program indistinguishable from the amended 
MPCP in Committee for Public Education v. Nyquist, 413 
U.S. 756 (1973), a case litigated by a predecessor of National 
PEARL. More recently, this Court reaffirmed that “aid to a 
religious institution unrestricted in its potential uses, if 
properly attributable to the state, is ‘clearly prohibited under 
the Establishment Clause.’” Witters v. Washington 
Department of Services for the Blind, 474 U.S. 481, 489 


3 


(1986) (citation omitted). This Court has never allowed states 
to engage in the kind of systematic funding of religious 
activity that will occur if the Wisconsin court’s decision is 
allowed to stand. 


Nonetheless, the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that the 
amended MPCP did not violate the fundamental Establishment 
Clause principle that public funds may not be used to finance 
religious indoctrination for two reasons. First, the court 
determined that the amended MPCP does not implicate the 
State in impermissible religious activity because it provides 
state funds to both sectarian and nonsectarian schools and is 
thus “neutral” toward religion. (Pet. App. at 25a-26a.) 
Second, the court held that the amended MPCP did not 
implicate the state in impermissible religious activity because 
parents, not the state, make the choice to provide state funds 
to religious schools. (Pet. App. at 27a-28a.) In so ruling, the 
Wisconsin court misinterpreted this Court’s Establishment 
Clause jurisprudence and departed dramatically from the 
precedents that govern both of the points on which its decision 
rested. 


1. In holding that the amended MPCP was “neutral” in 
the sense that program benefits flowed to both sectarian and 
nonsectarian schools, the Wisconsin Supreme Court ignored 
the body of precedent establishing that the mere fact that a 
statute benefits secular as well as sectarian schools does not 
establish that it is “neutral” toward religion. The Wisconsin 
court thus effectively substituted a simple requirement that the 
law be evenhanded for the more refined analysis this Court 
has required in order to determine whether a law has “the 
effect of advancing religion.” Agostini v. Felton, 117 S. Ct. 
1997, 2014 (1997). 


2. In holding that the amended MPCP entails an 
opportunity for parental “choice” that breaks any link between 


4 


state dollars and church coffers, the Wisconsin Supreme Court 
credited a type of sham transaction that this Court has 
repeatedly rejected, and equally important, misconstrued this 
Court’s approval of aid programs that allow for “genuinely 
independent and private choices of aid recipients.” Witters, 
474 U.S. at 487. 


National PEARL accordingly urges this Court to grant 
certiorari in order to address the Wisconsin Supreme Court’s 
departure from fundamental Establishment Clause principles, 
and to clarify these principles. The Wisconsin court candidly 
acknowledged the difficulties in “distill[ing]” an “underlying 
theory” to guide its analysis of the amended MPCP, and these 
same difficulties are likely to mislead other courts that will be 
required to judge state experiments with “school choice” 
programs. (Pet. App. at 16a.) This Court should make clear 
that its recent Establishment Clause decisions have not 
eliminated the basic constitutional principle that programs that 
involve the systematic, direct funding of religious activities, 
like the one this Court addressed in Nyquist and like the 
amended MPCP, violate the Establishment Clause. 


ARGUMENT 


I. THE DECISION OF THE COURT BELOW 
CONFLICTS WITH | WELL-ESTABLISHED 
PRECEDENT BECAUSE IT INCORRECTLY 
INTERPRETED RECENT SUPREME COURT 
DECISIONS AS ALTERING FUNDAMENTAL 
ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE PRINCIPLES 


In Committee for Public Education v. Nyquist, 413 U.S. 
756 (1973), this Court deemed unconstitutional a school 
voucher program that provided tuition reimbursements for 
children attending both sectarian and non-sectarian private 
schools. The Nyquist Court reached this result even though 


ee 


5 


the program was evenhanded in the respect that tuition for 
both religious and non-religious schools was reimbursed, and 
even though the religious schools received the state aid only 
because parents chose to send their children to those schools. 
The Court held that the program impermissibly advanced 
religion because it provided no guarantee “that the state aid 
derived from public funds [would] be used exclusively for 
secular, neutral, and nonideological purposes,” »nd because 
channeling the aid through parents was of no constitutional 
significance. 413 U.S. at 780, 783. 


In recent years, this Court’s Establishment Clause 
decisions and dicta have somewhat obscured the constitutional 
guideposts that led to the Nyquist Court’s decision. The 
Wisconsin court, lost in the underbrush that has grown out of 
a series of cases stretching from Muller v. Allen, 463 U.S. 
388 (1983), to Agostini v. Felton, 117 S. Ct. 1997 (1997), 
mistakenly believed that the Court had blazed a new, different 
trail.2 - 


The Wisconsin court thus held that the amended MPCP is 
constitutional because aid is “‘made available to both religious 
and secular beneficiaries on a non-discriminatory basis’” (Pet. 
App. at 25a, quoting Agostini, 117 S. Ct. at 2014) and 
because aid flows to sectarian private schools only “as a result 
of the necessary and intervening choices of individual parents” 
(Pet. App. at 28a), despite the fact that both characteristics 
were features of the Nyquist scheme. 


¥ The other decisions in the Muller-Agostini line of cases upon which 
the Wisconsin court relied include Witters v. Washington Department of 
Services for the Blind, 474 U.S. 481 (1986); Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills 
School District, 509 U.S. 1 (1993); and Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors 
of the University of Virginia, 515 U.S. 819 (1995). 


6 


In searching for a path to a decision, the Wisconsin court 
wrongly focused on selected attributes of other funding 
programs that the Court has analyzed over the years, and it 
mistook what were necessary conditions for constitutionality 
in those case as sufficient for upholding the very different 
program at issue here. But this Court has never renounced, 
and indeed has reaffirmed, the fundamental animating 
principle of its Nyquist decision — that the Establishment 
Clause prohibits states from providing financial support. to 
programs that advance religion. 


A. The Wisconsin Court Failed To Grasp That 
Establishment Clause Neutrality Means Not 
Merely Evenhandedness As Between Secular And 
Religious Institutions, But Neutrality With Respect 
To Advancing Religion. 


The Wisconsin Supreme Court’s decision reflects a 
fundamental misunderstanding of the difference between 
evenhandedness and neutrality in Establishment Clause 
analysis. As this Court recently explained, 
“*fe]venhandedness is a necessary but not sufficient condition 
for an aid program to survive constitutional scrutiny.’” 
Agostini, 117 S. Ct. at 2014 (quoting id. at 2025 (Souter, J., 
dissenting)). In assessing whether a statute conforms to 
Establishment Clause neutrality principles, the dispositive 
question is not whether the law treats secular and religious 
organizations evenhandedly, and is in this sense neutral, but 
rather, whether the law is neutral in the sense that it does not 
have “the effect of advancing religion.” Jd. See also Nyquist, 
413 U.S. at 788 (“‘neutrality’” means “neither ‘advancing’ 
nor ‘inhibiting’ religion”). 


The Wisconsin court clearly misunderstood this basic 
point when it found that the amended MPCP “satisfies the 
principle of neutrality” simply because program beneficiaries 


7 


and participating schools are selected without regard to 
religious orientation. (Pet. App. at 26a.) 


Evenhandedness, or “formal” neutrality as between 
secular and sectarian institutions, is a factor in Establishment 
Clause analysis, but it is not the dispositive factor. See 
Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of the University of Virginia, 
515 U.S. 819, 839-41 (1995); Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. 
Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203, 227 (1948) (opinion of 
Frankfurter, J.) (the Establishment Clause creates “a 
requirement to abstain from fusing functions of Government 
and of religious sects, not merely to treat them all equally”). 
This Court has repeatedly held that statutes that advance or 
endorse religion violate Establishment Clause neutrality 
principles even though they provide benefits to both secular 
and sectarian groups. See, e.g., Nyquist, 413 U.S. at 780 
(striking down a law providing reimbursement to students 
attending both secular and sectarian private schools); Tilton v. 
Richardson, 403 U.S. 673, 676 (1971) (striking down a law 
allowing construction grants to “all colleges and universities 
regardless of any affiliation or sponsorship by a religious 
body”); Wolman v. Walter, 433 U.S. 229, 248-51 (1977) 
(striking down a law allowing Ohio to lend instructional 
material and equipment to students attending both secular and 
sectarian private schools). 


The Wisconsin court failed to address these decisions 
because it misunderstood this Court’s Mueiler-Agostini line of 
cases as equating simple evenhandedness with Establishment 
Clause neutrality. What the Wisconsin court failed tc 
recognize is that, although evenhandedness was a necessary 
condition for upholding the programs at issue in those cases, 
see Agostini, 117 S. Ct. at 2014; Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 
839; Zobrest, 509 U.S. at 10-11; Witters, 474 U.S. at 487-88; 
Muller, 463 U.S. at 397-99, in each case this Court also 
considered whether the programs actually operated in a neutral 


8 


manner and whether any unrestricted funds that were properly 
attributable to the state flowed to religious institutions, see 
Agostini, 117 S. Ct. at 2012-14; Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 
840-43; Zobrest, 509 U.S. at 10-13; Witters, 474 U.S. at 487; 
Muller, 463 U.S. at 399-400. 


Once the Wisconsin court’s misguided reliance on 
evenhandedness is corrected, there can be no question that the 
amended MPCP fails the Establishment Clause neutrality test 
for the simple reason that it undeniably advances religion. 
Under the amended MPCP, religious schools can use state 
funds to finance the religious faccts of their students’ 
education, not merely identifiable secular or supplemental 
components of the curriculum. This feature distinguishes the 
amended MPCP from the aid programs at issue in many of the 
Muiller-Agostini cases upon which the Wisconsin court relied, 
where no unrestricted government funds ever reached the 
coffers of religious schools. See Agostini, 117 S. Ct. at 2012- 
13 (distinguishing aid that would “impermissibly finance 
religious indoctrination” because it “supplanted” instruction 
provided by sectarian schoois from the aid at issue, which 
constituted a permissible “supplement[] to the regular 
curricula”); Zobrest, 509 U.S. at 12 (aid was a “neutral 
service” and would not “relieve[] sectarian schools of costs 
they otherwise would have borne in educating their students”); 
Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at .842 (the Court was “not 
confront{ing] a case where . . . the government is making 
direct money payments to an institution or group that is 
engaged in religious activity”); Muller, 463 U.S. at 390-91 
(parents allowed to take education-related tax deductions). 
This Court has consistently recognized that substantial, 
unrestricted aid to religious schools “‘necessarily results in aid 
to the sectarian school enterprise as a whole,’ and therefore 
brings about ‘the direct and substantial advancement of 


9 


religious activity.’” Zobrest, 509 U.S. at 12 (quoting Meek v. 
Pittenger, 421 U.S. 349, 366 (1975)).2 


This Court should thus grant certiorari to reaffirm that a 
state aid program does not satisfy the Establishment Clause 
test of neutrality toward religion merely by allowing state 
funds to flow to secular as well as sectarian schools. The 
Court should make clear that programs such as the amended 
MPCP unconstitutionally advance religion by making state 
funds available to religious schools for an unrestricted range 
of sectarian activities. 


B. The Wisconsin Court Ignored The Unbroken Path 
Between State Appropriations And Church Coffers. 


The Wisconsin Court also found that the amended MPCP 
does not violate the fundamental Establishment Clause 
prohibition against state funding of religious indoctrination, 
see Agostini, 117 S. Ct. at 2012, because the state provides 
the aid “by individual checks made payable to the parent of 
each pupil attending a private school under the program” and 
therefore any aid that flows to sectarian schools does so only 
“as a result of the necessary and intervening choices of 
individual parents.” (Pet. App. at 28a.) But the Wisconsin

that either the amended MPCP’s administrative sleight-of-hand 
in making the parents payees, or the limited “choices” 
available to parents, alters the MPCP’s fundamentally 
unconstitutional character. 


This Court has consistently held that the substance of state 
aid, and not merely its form, determines whether aid to 
religious schools passes constitutional muster. The relevant 
question is not whether state aid is nominally directed to 
parents or to religious schools, but whether a “meaningful 
distinction can be made between aid to the student and aid to 
the school” or whether the purported distinction is instead “a 
transparent fiction.” Witters, 474 U.S. at 487 n.4 (internal 
quotation omitted). Under the amended MPCP, the distinction 
is in fact a transparent fiction — the checks are sent to the 
religious schools, and they “can be cashed only for the cost of 
the student’s tuition.” (Pet. App. at 27a.) These checks are 
thus very different from state paychecks that a recipient 
government employee may freely donate to a religious 
institution. Compare Witters, 474 U.S. at 486-87. 


In fact, in Nyquist, the Court found it unquestionable that 
tuition reir .Sursements could not be given directly to sectarian 
schools, a 4 considered only “whether the fact that the grants 
are delivered to parents rather than schools is of such 
significance as to compel a contrary result.” 413 U.S. at 781. 
The Court concluded resoundingly that it did not. Jd. at 781- 
86. See also Wolman, 433 U.S. at 250 (striking down a 
program of lending educational materials directly to students 
rather than religious schools because “[dJespite the technical 
change in legal bailee, the program in substance is the same 
as before”). 


Perhaps recognizing that this Court’s decisions foreclose 
an argument that using parents to launder public funds before 


11 


they are distributed to sectarian schools removes the 
unconstitutional taint, the Wisconsin court emphasized that 
money flows to religious schools only as the result of parental 
choice. (Pet. App. at 28.) The court’s “choice” argument, 
however, is also inconsistent with this Court’s precedents. 


First, as a practical matter, the amended MPCP does not 
allow for the type of “‘genuinely independent and private 
choices’” that this Court has discussed in its Establishment 
Clause cases. Zobrest, 501 U.S. at 9 (quoting Witters, 474 
U.S. at 487). Unlike aid programs this Court has approved 
in previous cases, choice is restricted because the amended 
MPCP’s benefits are not “available to all children who meet 
the [program’s] eligibility requirements, no matter what their 
religious beliefs or where they go to school.” Agostini, 117 
S. Ct. at 2014. Parents who are willing to send their children 
to religious schools possess far greater options and thus have 
a greater opportunity to enjoy the program’s benefits than 
those parents who are not.” The amended MPCP is thus 
very different from the aid programs that this Court upheld in 
the Muller-Agostini line of cases in which benefits were 
available to all students regardless of where they attended 
school, and this Court should reaffirm that the Constitution 
does not tolerate programs that “give aid recipients any

Second, the “choice” that allows public money to flow to 
religious schools is not one that “‘[could] not be attributed to 
state decisionmaking.’” Id. at 2012 (quoting Zobrest, 509 
U.S. at 10). This is not a general government benefit under 
which “aid is provided to students at whatever school they 
choose to attend.” Agostini, 117 S. Ct. at 2013. See also 
Zobrest, 509 U.S. at 19. Instead, the state has established a 
large-scale program that encourages students to attend private 
sectarian schools as an alternative to Milwaukee’s public 
schools and systematically channels public tax dollars into 
those religious schools. The Wisconsin court’s argument that 
public funds reach religious schools only as a matter of a 
“choice” that cannot be attributed to the government proves 
far too much. It would effectively eliminate any restrictions 
on government funding of religious activity as long as the 
funds were channelled through individuals. In effect, such 
logic would allow the government to create a per capita 
subsidy for various religious groups whereby the government 
could give each citizen money to donate to the religious 
organization of his or her choice. But this Court has never 
considered the sort of limited “choice” available to Milwaukee 
parents to be the cure-all that the Wisconsin court suggests it 
is. 


For example, the Nyquist Court treated as insignificant the 
fact that parents would receive tuition reimbursements whether 
they sent their children to religious or non-religious private 
schools because the program lacked “an effective means of 
guaranteeing that state aid derived from public funds will be 
used exclusively for secular, neutral, and nonideological 
purposes.” 413 U.S. at 780. Similarly, in Wolman, the 
Court held that a program under which the state loaned 
instructional material and equipment for use in both secular 


13 


and sectarian schools was not saved by making the loans to 
the students rather than directly to the schools. 433 U.S. at 
248-51. See also Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 871 (Souter, J., 
dissenting) (noting Madison’s opposition to a tax collecting 
funds to be distributed to religious institutions or “seminaries 
of learning” at the taxpayer’s choice). At heart, the argument 
that parental choice breaks the church-state link is no more 
than a reprise of the argument that “evenhandedness” does so: 
neither negates the reality that, under the amended MPCP, the 
government is advancing religion. 


Il. THIS CASE PRESENTS AN IMPORTANT 
OPPORTUNITY TO REAFFIRM FUNDAMENTAL 
ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE PRINCIPLES. 


The Court has long recognized that “[t}]he ‘establishment 
of religion’ clause of the First Amendment means at least” 
that “[nJo tax in any amount, large or small, can be levied in 
support of any religious activities or institutions.” Everson v. 
Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 15-16 (1947). The Court 
has relied upon this bedrock principle regularly to strike down 
“programs that entail an unacceptable risk that government 
funding would be used to ‘advance the religious mission’” of 
an institution receiving aid. Bowen v. Kendrick, 487 U.S. 
589, 612 (1987).* 


The Court has never renounced this fundamental 
proposition of constitutional law. See, e.g., Rosenberger, 515 
U.S. at 841 (“Our decision . . . cannot be read as addressing 
an expenditure from a general tax fund.”); id. at 852 
(O’Connor, J., concurring) (the Court is not “signal{ing] the 
demise of the funding prohibition in Establishment Clause 


¥ See, e.g., Meek v. Pittinger, 421 U.S. 349, 370 (1975); Tilton, 403 
U.S. at 683. 


14 


jurisprudence”). Unfortunately, however, this basic principle 
has been obscured by the heated battle of dicta that 
characterizes so much of Establishment Clause litigation. 


The amended MPCP undeniably conflicts with this 
elemental First Amendment principle. Under the amended 
MPCP, and other programs of its kind, regardless of the 
extent to which program benefits are distributed evenhandedly , 
and regardless of the degree to which “choice” is available to 
parents, the basic fact remains that citizens’ tax dollars are 
being spent to support religious schools. The state is 
transferring part of its revenues to religious schools, and it 
“does not prohibit the participating sectarian private schools 
from using funds for any purpose the school deems 
appropriate,” including solely religious purposes. (Pet. App. 
at 66a.) The amended MPCP presents much more than a 
mere “risk” that government funding will be used to advance 
these schools’ religious missions: it directly involves the 
impermissible “sponsorship . . . of the sovereign in religious 
activity.” Waltz v. Tax Commission, 397 U.S. 664, 668 
(1970). It is precisely this type of systematic government 
subsidization and endorsement of religion that lies at the core 
of the Establishment Clause prohibition. E.g., McCollum, 333 
U.S. at 210; Everson, 330 U.S. at 13-15. The Wisconsin 
court lost sight of this guiding principle and went astray, and 
this Court should lead it back to the correct path.
