REPLY BRIEF OF 
CONDITIONAL CROSS-PETITIONER 


The request in the Conditional Cross-Petition is 
grounded on principles of fundamental fairness and sim- 
ple judicial efficiency. If this Court is to re-evaluate the 
constitutionality of Kentucky’s campaign finance reform 
law as requested by original Petitioner Gable, such 
review, to be effective at all, must encompass all the law’s 
essential working components. Mr. Gable’s response 
urges on the Court a remarkable double standard of 
review that would carve out from this case, and hence 
leave unresolved, the critical issue concerning a state’s 
right to effectively promote its campaign finance reform. 
His flawed analysis and misstated facts warrant this 


reply. 


First, Mr. Gable insists on mischaracterizing the 
nature and effect of the 28-Day Timing Window on inter- 
nal contributions. It does not bar expenditures at all. Non- 
subsidized candidate slates can spend as much as they 
wish whenever they wish. Further, candidates can con- 
tribute as much as they wish to their own slate’s cam- 
paign. The only regulation imposed by the 28-Day Timing 
Window relates to the time in which candidates’ money 
must be deposited into the campaign account. Without 
such a notice requirement, subsidized candidates cannot 
be afforded “meaningful participation” in the election 
process. 


Respondent at no time disputes the devastating effect 
that removing the 28-Day Timing requirement on internal 
contributions would have on Kentucky’s campaign 
finance system. Judge Cudahy, dissenting from his two 


colleagues below, stated the “invalidation threatens to 
derail this reform effort.” Pet. App. at 24a. Without the 
limitation, Judge Cudahy predicted that self-financed 
candidates such as Mr. Gable would be “free to ambush 
an adversary with a torrent of new cash after the last 
reporting deadline, when a response is no longer possi- 
ble.” Pet. App. at 25a. “A provision that forbids this 
unfair practice should not require a defense.” Id. 


Second, Mr. Gable’s unequivocal contention that “the 
issue presented in the cross-petition was resolved by this 
Court’s decision in Buckley v. Valeo,” 424 U.S. 1 (1976), 
misapprehends both the issue in this case and in Buckley, 
and flatly ignores concessions to the contrary in the 
courts below. Response at 4. Buckley addressed limits on 
the amount of expenditures candidates can make in sup- 
port of their own campaigns. As the district court con- 
ceded, Buckley does not address “whether a ‘time’ 
restriction limiting when a candidate may contribute to 
his own campaign is constitutionally permissible.” Pet. 
App. at 32a. And, as the court of appeals majority 
acknowledged, the 28-Day Window is distinguishable 
from the Buckley limitation in that the Window’s applica- 
tion to candidate contributions strengthens incentives for 
participation in Kentucky’s campaign finance scheme,” 
which is clearly aimed at the prevention of actual and 
apparent corruption of the political process. Pet. App. at 
20a. See also Pet. App. at 24a-27a, where dissenting Judge 
Cudahy distinguishes Buckley on several other fronts. 
Accordingly, when Mr. Gable tells this Court in his origi- 
nal Petition that “[t]his case raises issues of serious 
national importance that were not resolved by this 
Court's decision in Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1 (1976),” one 


of those issues must be whether the 28-Day Timing Win- 
dow on internal contributions is constitutional. Pet. at 6. 


Perhaps most disturbing in the response is the prof- 
fered double standard for certiorari worthiness. In his 
original Petition, Mr. Gable tells this Court that “a grow- 
ing number of states are adopting novel schemes” relat- 
ing to campaign finance controls and that “whether such 
measures unconstitutionally penalize nonparticipating 
candidates’ rights to spend political money free of gov- 
ernment-imposed limits is an issue requiring this Court's 
urgent resolution.” Pet. at 6-7. In the response to Gover- 
nor Patton’s Cross-Petition, however, Mr. Gable states 
that a question relating to the constitutionality of an 
integral component of Kentucky’s so-called novel scheme 
is “not a pressing matter of national importance.” 
Response at 6. And, although he claims the issues he 
presents concerning the same statute may produce elec- 
toral disruption and confusion, he finds no such danger 
vis-a-vis the constitutionality of the 28-Day Timing Win- 
dow on internal contributions. The inconsistency is sim- 
ply irreconcilable. !

Patton for citing to a California statute and neglecting to 
mention an ongoing court challenge to its constitutionality. This 
rebuke is remarkable in light of the fact that it was Gable himself 
who relied on Cal. Gov. Code § 85404, without mention of the 
litigation, in his original Petition to support his premise that this 
Court’s oversight is needed to resolve constitutionality issues 
raised in the “growing number” of campaign finance laws in 
effect or under consideration in California and other cited 
states. Governor Patton’s reliance on Mr. Gable’s own cited 
authority merely points out the similarity between the notice

In sum, Mr. Gable offers no credible support for his 
request that this Court’s review of Kentucky’s campaign 
finance law, if granted, be piecemeal. To be sure, the only 
way to achieve the goals of consistency and order pro- 
moted so vigorously in the original Petition is for this 
Court, should it grant the Petition, also to grant the 
Cross-Petition and decide all issues relating to the consti- 
tutionality of the Trigger provision and the 28-Day Tim- 
ing Window. 


timetable in Kentucky’s. To say that reference to ongoing 
litigation is required of the Cross-Petitioner but not the 
Petitioner is but one example of Mr. Gable’s double standard. 
Further, contrary to Mr. Gable’s suggestion, the court of appeals 
in California Prolife Council Political Action Committee v. Scully, 
1999 WL 2549 (9th Cir., Jan. 5, 1999), made no decision with 
respect to internal expenditure provisions of § 85404. It simply 
affirmed the district court’s issuance of a preliminary injunction 
and ordered a full trial on the merits, explicitly refusing to 
review whether the result reached by the district judge was 
correct. 


CONCLUSION 


For the reasons stated above, the Court should grant 


the Conditional Cross-Petition for writ of certiorari.
