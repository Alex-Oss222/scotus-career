1. The Brief in Opposition of the Kentucky Registry 
Respondents asserts that Petitioner Gable lacks standing 
to challenge the constitutionality of Kentucky’s campaign 
finance regulations because he is not a declared candidate 
in this year’s gubernatorial campaign even though he ran 
for governor in 1995 and has expressed interest in running 
again. Registry Opp. Br. 21-24. This assertion is without 
merit. 


It is well established that persons who have been de- 
terred from acting by the very laws they allege to be un- 
lawful have suffered sufficiently personal and concrete 
injury to challenge those laws. See, e.g., International 
Brotherhood of Teamsters v. United States, 431 U.S. 324, 
362-67 (1977) (holding that non-applicants for a job may 
receive relief from racially discriminatory employment prac- 
tices that deterred them from applying). This is especially 
true in the election context. For example, in Anderson v. 
Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983), this Court entertained a 
challenge to a state’s early filing deadline for a place on 
the presidential ballot, even though the candidate bringing 
the challenge had missed that deadline. Similarly, in 
Clements v. Fashing, 457 U.S. 957, 962 (1982), this Court 
recognized the standing of state officeholders to challenge 
a state rule that required them to resign from their jobs if 
they ran as candidates for another office, even though none 
of the challengers had chosen to resign in order to run. 


Gable likewise is entitled to challenge a campaign fi- 
nance law he alleges violates his First Amendment rights, 
even if he declines to campaign for the governorship unless 
and until that constitutional defect is removed. Kentucky’s 
election finance laws plainly act as a deterrent to those 
challengers who, like Gable, do not think that running a 
race under such restrictions is worth the candle. See Al 
Cross, Election 1999; GOP Adds Candidate for Governor;

Peppy Martin files; Other Races Conceded, Louisville Cou- 
rier-Journal, Jan. 27, 1999, p.1b (noting that Kentucky

“was unwinnable because of the law that limits spending 
by candidates who agree to take public financing”). It is 
hardly Gable’s fault that Kentucky has kept the challenged 
regulations in force while this litigation has exceeded the 
duration of an entire election cycle. 


Nor is Gable’s challenge “moot,” as the Registry Re- 
spondents allege, see Registry Opp. Br. 23. Precisely be- 
cause constitutional questions about election practices are 
difficult to resolve within a single election cycle, this Court 
has repeatedly declined to find election challenges moot 
even after the election that gave rise to the challenge is 
long since over. See, e.g., Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 724, 
737 n.8 (1974) (holding that a challenge to restrictions on 
independent candidacies could proceed even after an elec- 
tion because the issues it presented would “persist as the 
. . . Statutes are applied in future elections”); Moore uv. 
Ogilivie, 394 U.S. 814, 815-16 (1969) (likewise holding that 
a challenge to election statutes was justiciable because “ca- 
pable of repetition, yet evading review”). These precedents 
plainly foreclose any mootness holding here.
