I. STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Petitioner’s Statement of the Case is accurate, so far 
as it goes. But it does not go quite far enough in explain- 
ing the background of the case or the status of the parties. 
Examined in light of Kentucky’s compelling interest in 
combating corruption in campaign fundraising, and in 
light of the particular aspects of Kentucky’s unique 
approach to public subsidies in exchange for voluntary 
limits on fundraising and spending, it is clear that the 
Court ought not grant the petition sought in this matter 
to review Kentucky’s truly unique system of public sub- 
sidies and permissible incentives for participation. 


Episodes of corruption have been all too common in 
Kentucky politics.! Just since 1979, three Democratic gov- 
ernors have been multi-millionaires who funded their 
campaigns by loaning hundreds of thousands of dollars 
to their campaign committees, only collecting contribu- 
tions to pay themselves back after they were nominated.? 
The lone Democratic candidate who was not indepen- 
dently wealthy, Martha Layne Collins, ran a campaign 
that nevertheless engaged in apparent abuse of the fund- 
raising laws. Her husband was subsequently convicted of 
conspiring to extort contributions from those who sought 
to do business with the Commonwealth of Kentucky and

spend more than $1.8 million, and continue to receive the 
$2 for $1 match.

Petitioner Robert E. Gable (“Gable”) was a declared 
candidate for the Republican Party nomination in Ken- 
tucky’s 1995 Republican gubernatorial primary. Although 
he met the filing requirements and his name appeared on 
the ballot, Gable was hardly a serious candidate. Accord- 
ing to public records available from the Registry, 
(together with Donald L. Cox, the “Registry Respon- 
dents”), Gable raised and spent only $96,835 in the 1995 
campaign. In contrast, the successful Republican candi- 
date spent $1,233,223 in the primary election. Because he 
declined to participate in the system of public funding for 
gubernatorial elections created by the Kentucky General 
Assembly and implemented to combat corruption in Ken- 
tucky politics, Gable was ineligible to receive public 
funds otherwise available pursuant to KRS § 121A.020(1). 


Shortly after the filing deadline for the 1995 Republi- 
can primary, Gable brought his challenge to the constitu- 
tionality of the entire statutory framework for public 
funding of Kentucky’s gubernatorial elections. He 
claimed principally that the laws violated the United

States Constitution, Amendment I insofar as that amend- 
ment prohibits any law abridging the freedom of speech. 
Gable acknowledges that his is a facial challenge. (Peti- 
tion 5). “A facial challenge to a legislative Act is, of 
course, the most difficult challenge to mount successfully, 
since the challenger must establish that no set of circum- 
stances exists under which the Act would be valid.” Rust 
v. Sullivan, 500 U.S. 173, 183 (1991) (quoting United States 
v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739, 745 (1987)). 

This Court has adopted a three-part inquiry for 
determining the constitutionality of a statute challenged 
on First Amendment grounds. First, the statute will be 
upheld if it does not truly burden freedom of expression. 
Second, even if it creates a burden, the statute may still be 
permissible if it furthers a compelling state interest. 
Third, if the statute furthers a compelling state interest, it 
will be upheld if it is narrowly tailored to further the 
compelling interest, instead of being overbroad and 
unnecessarily burdensome on free expression. Austin v. 
Michigan Chamber of Commerce, 494 U.S. 652, 657 (1990); 
see generally Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1 (1976). Because 
both the trigger and the 28-day window are narrowly 
tailored to serve compelling state interests in avoiding 
corruption in the election process, Kentucky’s statutory 
plan for public subsidies to candidates who agree to limit 
fundraising and spending - thereby reducing corruption 
in the electoral process — meets this test.

found that Gable’s challenge was largely without merit:

subsidies and incentives unconstitutional. As explained 
below, this Court should do likewise. 


Il. REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 


A. THE TRIGGER PROVISION OF KRS 
§ 121A.030(5)(a) IS NOT UNCONSTITU- 
TIONAL. 


1. The Trigger Is Consistent With Established 
Principles And Is Not An Unconstitutional 
Burden On The Exercise Of First Amend- 
ment Rights. 


Gable’s challenge to the Kentucky statute requires 
first and foremost that he demonstrate that the 2-for-1 
matching aspect of the trigger provision, coming into 
play after a Nonparticipating Slate of candidates exceeds 
the $1.8 million spending threshold, constitutes an uncon- 
stitutional burden on his First Amendment rights. But the 
stubborn reality of Kentucky’s unique statutory scheme 
simply makes that impossible. Gable has a right to spend 
as much as he likes on his own campaign, he asserts, and 
this Court agreed in Buckley. And the simple truth is that 
Kentucky’s plan of public subsidies in exchange for vol- 
untary limits leaves him completely unfettered in his 
right to raise and spend money. Faced with this truth, 
Gable is forced to argue that public subsidies that match 
his own expenditures beyond $1.8 million at the rate of 
2-for-1 constitute a de facto limitation on his ability to 


6 


raise and spend money. Thus, Gable suggests that his 
“fund-raising success is threatened or hammered [sic] 
with a state-financed counterattack funded out of all 
proportion to the actual popularity of the opponent's 
ideas.” (Petition 11). Therefore, Gable argues, public sub- 
sidies that match private contributions 2-to-1 above a 
designated threshold are an unconstitutional burden on 
his right to spend. 


In fact, Gable’s effort to distinguish Kentucky’s 
approach to public subsidies for campaigns from the 
essential elements of the statutes considered in Buckley is 
disingenuous. Gable asserts without basis that “a candi- 
date who seeks to walk away from the public campaign 
finance program in order to preserve his right to make 
unlimited campaign expenditures simply cannot do so.” 
(Petition 8). Regrettably, there is no basis in fact for that 
statement, for Gable is free to do exactly that. For that 
matter, Gable did exactly that in his 1995 campaign. Dis- 
regarding the plain language of the Kentucky statute, 
Gable observes that the provisions in Buckley “placed no 
limits on the amounts that nonparticipating candidates 
might raise or spend .. . ” (Petition 8), as if to suggest 
that Kentucky’s provisions do. But the reality is that 
neither Gable nor any other nonparticipating candidate in 
Kentucky is restricted in the least in the amount that he 
might raise or spend — just as in Buckley. 


The Kentucky public financing plan follows the con- 
stitutional mandates identified in Buckley. A Participating 
Slate of candidates may choose to adopt voluntary limits 
on the total amount of contributions it may receive or 
expenditures it may make, in exchange for public financ- 
ing. However, there are no limits at all on the total 


7 


amount of contributions Nonparticipating Slates of candi- 
dates who choose to reject public funding may collect. 
Consistent with Buckley, Nonparticipating Slates may also 
contribute unlimited amounts of their own money to 
their campaigns. And there is no limit at all on the 
amount a Nonparticipating Slate may spend. To the 
extent that spending is speech as Buckley teaches, the 
Kentucky law simply does not limit in any respect the 
“speech” of Gable or of any Nonparticipating Slate of 
candidates. On the contrary, if spending is speech, the 
provision for 2-for-1 matching subsidies above the trigger 
almost guarantees that there will be more speech, not 
less. 


In this regard, Gable is wrong when he suggests that 
under Kentucky’s approach, a nonparticipating candidate 
may not “raise and spend as many legal private contribu- 
tions as he or she wishes ... .” (Petition 11). Kentucky’s 
legislation leaves Gable free to do exactly that. What it 
also does is insure that the campaigns of candidates who 
agree to voluntary limits will not be “overwhelmed by a 
counterattack funded out of all proportion to the actual 
popularity” of the nonparticipating opponent’s ideas — to 
use Gable’s words. Instead, the Kentucky approach gives 
Participating Slates the opportunity to respond to their 
opponents by removing the spending cap otherwise 
applicable to Participating Slates of candidates, and pro- 
vides additional matching funds pegged not to the fund- 
raising of the Nonparticipating Slates, but to their own 
fundraising. Were it otherwise, it would likely be impos- 
sible for a rational candidate to agree to spending limits, 
simply because the opponent would be permitted to 
spend a personal fortune, mount a carpet-bombing media 


8 


campaign, and obliterate the participating candidate's 
message. 

The trigger and the 28-day window provide the 
incentive necessary to make public financing and the 
attendant spending limits pragmatically feasible for 
gubernatorial candidates. Without the assurance pro- 
vided by the trigger, few candidates could be expected to 
accept the voluntary spending limits which accompany 
public financing, and thereby risk being dramatically out- 
spent by nonparticipating opponents. Without the 28-day 
window, the Registry would be unable to pull the trigger 
far enough in advance of the election to permit Participat- 
ing Slates a meaningful opportunity to respond to their 
nonparticipating oppenents’ spending above the $1.8 mil- 
lion cap. Accordingly, the trigger and the window pro- 
vide inducements to accept public financing which are 
fully consistent with Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), 
and its progeny. 

Gable is wrong as well in his characterization of the 
statutory scheme as one “justified by an interest in equal- 
izing the relative ability of individuals and groups to 
influence the outcome of elections.” (Petition 12, quoting 
Buckley, 424 U.S. at 49). The record is plain that Ken- 
tucky’s interest is in eliminating corruption in politics, by 
stopping the practice, for example, of selling access to 
state contracts for campaign contributions. See United 
States v. Collins, 78 F.3d 1021, 1027 (6th Cir. 1996) (former 
governor’s husband convicted of conspiracy to violate 
Hobbs Act by illegally tying appointments and contracts 
to contributors). To further this compelling interest, Ken- 
tucky provides the Participating Slates the opportunity - 


not a guarantee — of additional spending by lifting the 


9 


cap, and matching 2-for-1 any additional fundraising the 
Participating Slate may achieve. 


The trigger is a nondiscriminatory, content-neutral 
inducement, narrowly tailored to further Kentucky’s 
compelling interest in eliminating actual and perceived 
corruption in the election process. Far from imposing an 
unconstitutional burden on Gable’s right of free speech, 
the trigger facilitates a continued, vigorous exchange of 
political speech by assuring that a Participating Slate will 
be released from its voluntary spending limit once a 
_nonparticipating opponent’s contributions have exceeded 
that limit. The trigger produces more speech, not less. See 
Rosenstiel v. Rodriguez, 101 F.3d 1544 (8th Cir. 1996), cert. 
denied, 520 U.S. 1229 (1997). 


Another court has already reached this very conclu- 
sion regarding the trigger included in the Kentucky law: 


[Even if] the trigger provision chills speech to 
some degree, we find that it is a narrowly tai- 
lored means which addresses a compelling state 
interest . . . in encouraging candidates to accept 
public financing and its accompanying limita- 
tions which are designed to promote greater 
political dialogue among the candidates and 
combat corruption by reducing candidates’ 
reliance on fundraising efforts 


Wilkinson v. Jones, 876 F.Supp. 916, 928 (W.D. Ky. 1995). 


Buckley squarely approved offering inducements to 
encourage candidates to accept voluntary spending 
limits. It follows that Kentucky does not unconstitu- 
tionally hamper a nonparticipating candidate’s rights 
when it offers additional funds in reasonable proportion 
to the additional funds raised once a nonparticipating 
candidate exceeds the statutory cap. To the extent the 


10 


trigger provides for increased debate about issues of pub- 
lic concern, it promotes the free and open debate the First 
Amendment seeks to foster and protect. See Mills v. Ala- 
bama, 384 U.S. 214 (1966); see also Buckley, 424 U.S. at 
92-93 (public financing of campaigns “furthers, not 
abridges, pertinent First Amendment values”). 


The critical flaw in Gable’s reasoning is his notion 
that the benefit Kentucky confers on Participating Slates 
is indistinguishable from a burden on Nonparticipating 
Slates. But under Buckley benefits conferred upon Partici- 
pating Slates are not unconstitutional burdens upon Non- 
participating Slates. 424 U.S. at 95 (and n. 127 regarding 
the multiple laws “providing financial assistance to the 
exercise of free speech.”). Accord, Rosenstiel v. Rodriguez, 
101 F.3d 1544 (8th Cir. 1995); Vote Choice, Inc. v. DiStefano, 
4 F.3d 26, 38 (1st Cir. 1993); Weber v. Heaney, 793 F.Supp. 
1438, 1457 (D. Minn. 1992), aff'd, 995 F.2d 872 (8th Cir. 
1993); Republican National Committee v. FEC, 487 F.Supp. 
280, 285 (S.D.N.Y.) (three-judge court), aff'd mem., 445 U.S. 
955 (1980). 


From a constitutional perspective, one’s right of 
expression is not burdened because the expression of 
another — who accepts the state’s conditional offer of 
assistance — is subsidized. For example, in other cases this 
Court has held that a state is free to make childbirth a 
more attractive alternative than abortion, thereby influ- 
encing the woman's decision, so long as it imposes no 
restriction on access to abortions that was not already 
there. Maher v. Rose, 432 U.S. 464 (1977). Explaining that 
holding, the Court observed: 

Constitutional concerns are greatest when the 


State attempts to impose its will by force of law; 
the State’s power to encourage actions deemed 


11 


to be in the public interest is necessarily far 
broader. 


Id. at 475-76 (emphasis added). Later, the Court made 
clear that a state does not unconstitutionally burden the 
exercise of First Amendment rights when it merely fur- 
thers its own value judgments by providing unequal sub- 
sidization of one form of expression over another, thereby 
“encourag[ing] alternative activity deemed in the public 
interest.” Harris v. McRae, 448 U.S. 297, 315 (1980). 


Kentucky’s 2-for-1 subsidy for Participating Slates, 
matching private expenditures of Nonparticipating Slates 
above $1.8 million, does not limit in any way the quantity 
of the Nonparticipating Slate’s fundraising or spending. 
Gable remains free to spend as he likes, irrespective of 
Kentucky’s legislation. His constitutional right to expres- 
sion is no more burdened than was that of the plaintiff in 
Harris. In Harris, the Court confirmed that none of the 
unconstitutional burden cases implied any limitation on 
the authority of a state to make a value judgment favor- 
ing one expression of First Amendment rights over 
another, and to implement that value judgment with pub- 
lic funds. 448 U.S. at 314. 


The same is true here. Kentucky has placed no limit 
on any expression of First Amendment rights. It has 
made a value judgment that as a policy matter, it prefers 
limited spending to unlimited spending, and it has imple- 
mented that judgment with public subsidies. As well, it 
has acted to further a compelling state interest to fight 
corruption and to limit the influence of money in politics. 
To encourage candidates to accept those limits and sub- 
sidies, it has provided the opportunity for a Participating 
Slate to raise and spend additional funds, beyond the $1.8 


12 


million spending cap otherwise applicable to Participat- 
ing Slates, in the event that a Nonparticipating Slate 
exceeds the cap. And to insure that the logistical prob- 
lems attendant to reporting spending beyond the thresh- 
old do not render that opportunity to respond to 
spending by Nonparticipating Slates meaningless, Ken- 
tucky has established a narrowly-tailored, content-neu- 
tral window during which a candidate may not receive 
outside contributions. All of this is entirely consistent 
with Buckley. 


Just as the state in Maher had made childbirth a more 
attractive alternative, thereby influencing the woman's 
decision, but had imposed no restrictions on access to 
abortions that did not already exist, Kentucky has influ- 
enced the decision whether to participate in the public 
financing program it created, but has not restricted in any 
way the ability of a nonparticipating candidate such as 
Gable to spend as much as he likes. 


Moreover, Gable misapprehends the holding of cases 
like Memorial Hospital v. Maricopa Co., 415 U.S. 520 (1974) 
and Speiser v. Randall, 357 U.S. 513 (1958), when he sug- 
gests that Kentucky’s trigger is constitutionally flawed 
because it makes extra funding contingent on his exercise 
of protected speech. Those cases held that the state may 
not impose a fine on an individual’s decision to exercise a 
fundamental right, or condition receipt of a benefit on his 
decision to forgo that exercise. Those cases did not hold 
that a state is precluded from extending public funds to 
others, following the exercise of such rights, as a means 
of implementing the state’s permissible value judgment 
regarding campaign funding while furthering a compel- 
ling state interest in eliminating corruption. 


| 


13 


In fact, the Maher Court highlighted a point that 
Gable seems unwilling to confront. The unconstitutional 
condition cases he cites such as Memorial Hospital 
involved burdens imposed by the state that were analo- 
gous to criminal penalties. Maher, 432 U.S. at 474 note 8. 
But that is not true here. Just as Arizona was not obliged 
to “pay the bus fares” of indigent travelers to avoid 
constitutional infirmity, so Kentucky is not obliged to 
muzzle the speech of Participating Slates once a Nonpar- 
ticipating Slate exceeds the threshold. Not only is Gable 
denied no benefit otherwise available to him but for his 
exercise of First Amendment rights, see, e.g., Shapiro v. 
Thompson, 394 U.S. 618 (1969), but neither was he directly 
penalized for exercising his rights through, for example, 
the use of the state’s taxing authority. See, e.g, Speiser, 
supra. 

Nor is Gable’s reliance on cases like Miami Herald 
Pub. Co. v Tornillo, 418 U.S. 241 (1974) helpful. That case 
also involved a penalty. The statute compelling the news- 
paper to print a reply in that case was found to be a 
penalty both because it took away space from other 
speech and cost money, and because it intruded into the 
editorial process. Therefore, under the operation of the 
Florida statute, “political and electoral coverage would be 
blunted or reduced.” Id. at 257. By contrast, Kentucky 
does not require Gable to pay for his opponent's speech, 
or demand that he print it. Similarly, in Pacific Gas & 
Electric Co. v. Public Util. Comm'n, 475 U.S. 1 (1986), the 
Court struck down a statute that compelled one exercis-

Consequently, Gable’s asserted interest in running

lacks standing. The standing question involves an anal- 
ysis concerning “whether the litigant is entitled to have 
the court decide the merits of the dispute or of particular 
issues.” Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490 (1975). The standing 
inquiry has two parts: 

First, whether the plaintiff-respondents allege 

“injury in fact” that is, a sufficiently concrete 

interest in the outcome of their suit to make it a 

case or controversy subject to a federal court’s 

Art. Ill jurisdiction, and, second, whether as a 

prudential matter, the plaintiff-respondents are 

proper proponents of the particular legal rights 

on which they base their suit. 

Singleton v. Wulff, 428 U.S. 106, 112 (1976). See also Valley 
Forge Christian College v. Americans United For Separation of 
Church and State, Inc., 454 U.S. 464, 471 (1982). 


Without question, Gable at this point lacks the suffi- 
ciently concrete interest in the outcome of the suit he 
originally filed to make it a case or controversy for pur- 
poses of Article III jurisdiction. His interest is no more 
concrete than that of more than 3.7 million other Ken- 
tuckians who will not run for the nomination of their 
party for the office of governor in the upcoming election. 
As such, he plainly lacks a sufficiently concrete interest in 
the matter to confer standing. Singleton, 428 U.S. at 112. 
See also, Spector Motor Service, Inc. v. McLaughlin, 323 U.S.

101, 105 (1944) (the Court will “not to pass on questions 
of constitutionality . . . unless such adjudication is 
unavoidable.”). Gable’s failure to file papers declaring

have had to assert this facial challenge to the constitu- 
tionality of Kentucky’s campaign finance laws, and ren- 
ders his challenge moot.

B. THE 28-DAY WINDOW BANNING OUTSIDE 
CONTRIBUTIONS FOR A LIMITED PERIOD 
OF TIME IS NOT UNCONSTITUTIONAL. 


As for the ban on contributions during the 28 days 
preceding an election effected by KRS § 121A.030(5), the 
Court of Appeals struck down that portion of the law that 
barred contributions by the candidates themselves to 
their own campaigns during the 28 days preceding an 
election. It upheld the 28-day window provision only 
with respect to outside contributions. The Registry has 
not challenged that aspect of the Court of Appeals ruling. 
At the same time, the ban on outside contributions dur- 
ing this administratively critical period of time is a nar- 
rowly tailored provision plainly designed to address a 
compelling state interest, one consistent with Buckley and 
the constitution generally.

This Court, however, has acknowledged that it

“The government interest in preventing both actual cor-

Assembly was justly concerned with the erosion of public

a fully permissible concern of the state government, one 
that justifies substantial deference. 


Given that the 28-day window is a vital component 
of the public subsidy arrangement, one that is narrowly 
tailored and logically related in scope and operation to 
both the trigger provision itself and the goal of combating 
corruption, it plainly satisfies the requirements of Buckley 
and the constitution generally. As the Court of Appeals 
noted, under the 28-day window, “[clandidates will be 
forced to rearrange their fundraising by concentrating it 
in the period before the 28-day window begins . . . but 
_.. such a restriction is justified by Kentucky’s interest in 
combating corruption.” (Petition 18a-19a). Given the 
length of modern campaigns, this modest burden is 
plainly insufficient to overturn the entire legislative plan.
