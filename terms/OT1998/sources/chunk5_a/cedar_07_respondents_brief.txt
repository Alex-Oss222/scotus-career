STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. Procedural Overview 


The Iowa Department of Education (the Department) ruled, 
on December 16, 1994, that the Cedar Rapids Community School 
District (the District) was required to reimburse Garret F. for health 
care costs his parents incurred on his behalf for the 1993-1994 
school year. The Department specifically held that the services 
required were both “related services” under federal law and 
“supportive services” under lowa law. Pet. App. 52a, 54a, 63a. 
Petitioner chose to pursue its appeal in federal rather than state 
court. The United States District Court for the Northern District of 
lowa granted summary judgment in favor of Garret, holding that 
the services were not within the “medical services” exclusion of 
the IDEA and therefore were “related services” that the School 
District was required to provide. Pet. App. 8a-16a. The Court of 
Appeals for the Eighth Circuit affirmed the District Court. Pet. 
App. la-7a. The District subsequently filed a petition for writ of 
certiorari, which this Court granted. 


B. Garret F. 


Garret F. was an energetic, active child when, in 1987, he 
sustained catastrophic injuries while riding as a passenger on his 
father’s motorcycle. While Garret’s mental abilities were unaffected, 
his spinal cord was severed. Garret, as a result of the accident and 
at all times relevant, has been quadriplegic, ventilator dependent 
and uses a wheelchair. His physical impairment has continued to 
have no effect on his mind or his mental abilities and he is doing 
well in school. Without the modest care he requires, he would be 
excluded from public school without any opportunity to obtain a 
meaningful education. Ruling — lowa Department of Education. 
Pet. App. 19a, 59a. Tr. at 87-88. He would be relegated to 
homebound instruction. Jd; Tr. at 87-88; 596-97.

1994. Tr. at 513-14. See also Brief for Amicus Curiae, National 
School Board Association, at 18, 26 (18,000 students x $5,483 = 
total funding of $98,940,000).

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit 
correctly held that Garret F. was entitled to nursing services as 
related services under the IDEA. The Court of Appeals’ ruling was 
correct under the plain language of IDEA, the relevant regulations, 
and the Act's legislative history, all as interpreted by this Court in 
Irving Independent School District v. Tatro, 468 U.S. 883 (1984) 
and other cases. Despite the District's assertions to the contrary, 
the IDEA is not merely “about education.” It is about access to 
education. It specifically provides that: “all children with disabilities 
have available . . . a free appropriate public education . . . and 
related services designed to meet their unique needs. . .” 20 U.S.C. 
§ 1400(c) (1994). Without meaningful access, including the 
provision of a broad range of supportive services, the goal of a 
“free and adequate public education” would be a hollow promise. 
See Tatro, 468 U.S. at 891. 


The importance of meaningful access is illustrated by the facts 
of this case. The services are simple, straight-forward acts that can 
be provided by a nurse’s aide or an LPN, and, in fact, have been

The services can be provided at no additional cost to the District by 
the existing educational aide, who is an LPN and could provide the 
services. Without this care, however, Garret is condemned to miss out 
on school and would be kept at home in a segregated, home-based 
program. 


This Court’s decision in Tatro, 468 U.S. 883 (1984), supports a 
“bright-line” rule for cases such as this one. The bright line is drawn

bright-line test would afford recipients of funds under the IDEA clear

G. The Bright-line Rule is Practical and is Consistent with 
Principles of Administration of Justice. 


The importance of the bright-line rule is that it eliminates the 
costs, delay, and the general state of confusion that would result 
from the imposition of the multifactor test suggested by Petitioner 
which would require adjudication of varied factual circumstances 
through extensive appellate processes. See Schweiker v. Gray 
Panthers, 453 U.S. 34, 48 (1981); Weinberger v. Salfi, 422 U.S. 
749, 782-85 (1975); Honig v. Doe, 484 U.S. 310, 322 (1988) 
(explaining that administrative and judicial review under the IDEA 
are often “ponderous”). The bright-line test furthers the efficient 
and proper use of public funds earmarked for education and should 
avoid litigation over fact-specific disputes engendered by the 
multifactor test. See Skelly v. Brookfield LaGrange Park School 
District 95, 968 F. Supp. 385, 394 (N.D. Ill. 1997). 


(Cont'd)

ventilator or tracheotomy by the time they are ready to enter school. See 
OTA Report, supra, at 15, 29. Second, they include children living in hospitals 
who receive health services from hospitals not school districts. One survey 
estimates that there are 570 ventilator-dependent patients under age 18 in 
hospitals. See American Association for Respiratory Care, A Study of Chronic

AS A FURTHER ALTERNATIVE GROUND, THE 
JUDGMENT MUST BE AFFIRMED ON STATE LAW 
GROUNDS. 


This Court as a general rule will not affirm on state law 
grounds. City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 
292 (1982). However, in IDEA cases, state law provisions for 
special education that create a higher duty for school districts than 
recognized by federal law have been held to be automatically 
incorporated into, and enforceable under, the IDEA. See David D. 
v. Dartmouth Sch. Comm., 775 F.2d at 420. Thus, affirmance is 
proper under state law grounds. In the alternative, the Court could 
remand for a determination on state law grounds. City of Mesquite, 
455 U.S. at 292. 


The ALJ held that Garret F. was entitled to the services as 
“support services” under lowa law. The Department of Education 
held that the District had the responsibility under lowa Code 
Sections 256B.2 and .3 and lowa Code Section 280.8 to provide 
the services. It stated: 


Under [the] lowa statute Garret is a child requiring 
special education, and a six-year history of successful 
functioning in the regular classroom, with aids and 
services provided by his family and the school prove 
beyond a doubt that [education in regular classes] can 
be accomplished satisfactorily. Under state law, Garret 
must be provided an education program “within the 
regular school environment” and under the same state 
law; it is the District’s responsibility to do so. 


Pet. App. 55a. 


The ALJ found that the alternative of a homebound program 
would “certainly not be an education with nondisabled children to 
the ‘maximum extent appropriate’ ” within the meaning of federal 
regulations. Pet. App. 59a. The ALJ also properly noted that under 


44 


lowa law, “only when a child ‘cannot sufficiently profit from the 
work in the regular classroom’ may he be removed.” Pet. App. 
59a: lowa Code § 256B.8 (1993). The ALJ further found that the 
lowa Board of Nursing’s declaratory ruling that an RN was required 
to render the care did not relieve the District of responsibility to 
provide health care services to Garret at school. Pet App. 60a. 
Accordingly, the Court should affirm on this ground.
