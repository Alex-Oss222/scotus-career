On Writ of Certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit REPLY BRIEF FOR THE PETITIONER This case presents the question whether the Court should steer away from the plain language of the statute, its own decisions, and over a decade of nearly unanimous lower court rulings to hold that school districts have an obligation to provide continuous one-on-one nursing serv- ices, on the pretense that such services are not really “med- ical” because they are not personally administered by a physician. The “medical services” exclusion in the IDEA must be given its plain, everyday, common-sense meaning. As a result, the lower court decisions in this case must be reversed. First, there is no question that Garret needs continuous one-on-one nursing services provided by a nursing profes- sional, and that such nursing services would result in sub- stantial additional costs to the District. At the same time, there is no basis to conclude that if the District prevails in this case, Respondent would be relegated to homebound instruction. For ten years, Respondent has attended the 2 Cedar Rapids public schools, even though the intensive nursing services that he requires have not been provided by the District. Second, because the statutory language is paramount, the District will refocus on the real issue in this case: Does the term “medical services” in the IDEA include medical services as that term is commonly understood? In a number of other contexts, Congress, federal »gencies, and the courts have defined “medical” assistance, care or services as embracing many services that are not person- ally administered by a physician. Third, the District will show that a series of develop- ments since the IDEA was enacted in 1975, including administrative regulations and interpretations, this Court's Tatro decision, and lower court decisions that have been implicitly ratified by Congress, strongly support a com- mon-sense, practical view of the term “medical.” Addi- tionally, because the IDEA was enacted under the Spend- ing Clause, not under the Fourteenth Amendment, it is a contract with the States, and should not be deemed to impose requirements on school districts unless the requirements are stated “unambiguously” in the legislation. Fourth, the District will demonstrate that balancing is at the heart of the IDEA, and that a balancing test to determine whether a service is “medical” is both logical and feasible. Unfortunately, the administrative law judge did not perform a trne balancing in this case, relying instead on his erroneous view of the law as limiting “med- ical services” to those directly provided by a physician. THE TERM “MEDICAL SERVICES” AS USED IN THE IDEA MUST HAVE A PRACTICAL, COMMON- SENSE MEANING. Any consideration of the IDEA’s medical services exclu- sion must begin with the statute. The importance of the statutory language is paramount. Connecticut Nat'l Bank v. Germain, 503 U.S. 249, 253-254 (1992). “Medical services” must be given its “ordinary, contemporary, common meaning.” See Walters v. Metropolitan Educ. Enters., Inc., 117 S. Ct. 660, 664 (1997). Congress and several courts have repeatedly defined “medical” as in- cluding services beyond those actually administered by a physician, including nursing services. This traditional defi- 6 nition of “medical” reflects practical realities and common sense. As the Seventh Circuit recently said in analyzing the medical services exclusion of the IDEA, a bright-line physician/nonphysician distinction is “unrealistic.” Mor- ton Community Unit Sch. Dist. No. 709 v. J.M., 1998 U.S. App. LEXIS 17079 at *7 (7th Cir. July 27, 1998). Most medical services, “as a matter of economy and spe- cialization, are provided by nurses, nurses’ aides, para- medics, medical technicians, medical students, the patients themselves, and members of the patient’s families, rather than by physicians.” /d. Respondent and the United States’ definition of “medical services” is not “contem- porary”; it is archaic. Respondent attempts to cite several federal statutes to support an unduly narrow definition of “medical” as “lim- ited to services directly provided by a physician.” But unlike the authorities cited by the District, such as the well-established definition of “medical care” in the Inter- nal Revenue Code, 26 U.S.C. § 213(d)(1), none of these statutes cited by Respondent actually defines the term “medical.” Moreover, far from supporting Respondent, many of the statutes cited by Respondent bolster the Dis- trict’s position that “medical” encompasses services per- formed by nonphysicians. For example, 38 U.S.C. § 101(28), relating to veterans’ benefits, does not actually define “medical services” but strongly implies that such services can be performed “un- der the general direction of” physicians. Moreover, an- other provision, 38 U.S.C. § 1701(6), expressly defines “medical services” to include rehabilitative services, surgi- cal services, dental services, appliances, optometric and podiatric services, and preventive health-care services. Likewise, 42 U.S.C. § 1397cc(c)(1)(B) suggests there is a subcategory of “medical services” provided by “phy- sicians,” and correspondingly there are medical services not provided by physicians. Additionally, while 5 U.S.C. § 8103(a) (also cited by Respondent) does not define > “medical services,” the implementing regulations do, and the definition supports the District. See 20 C.F.R. § 10.400(e) (the term “medical services” includes “serv- ices and supplies provided by or under the supervision of physicians (M.D. and D.O.), surgeons, podiatrists, den- tists, clinical psychologists, optometrists, and chiroprac- tors... .”) (emphasis added). In summary, when Congress or a federal agency has defined the term “medical” in connection with “assistance,” “care” or “services,” none of the definitions has been lim- ited to services directly administered by a physician. Il. THE LEGISLATIVE HISTORY OF THE IDEA, WHILE SPARSE, SHOWS THAT CONGRESS IN- TENDED A MEANINGFUL “MEDICAL SERVICES” EXCLUSION. The legislative history of the IDEA as enacted in 1975 shows that Congress made a deliberate choice to exclude “medical services” (other than for diagnosis and evalua- tion) from the services that school districts had to pro- vide. This was no mere accident. As originally introduced in the 94th Congress, the pre- cursors to the IDEA, S. 6 and H.R. 7217, defined “re- lated services” to include “medical” services. The House and Senate Committees then conducted hearings in the spring of 1975, during which there was extensive discus- sion of the costs of special education. Before they were reported to the floor, both S. 6 and H.R. 7217 were modi- fied to exclude medical services generally. See H.R. Rep. No. 332, 94th Cong., Ist Sess., June 26, 1975, at 42-43 (related services includes medical services, “except that such medical services shall be for diagnostic and evalua- tion purposes only”); S. Rep. No. 168, 94th Cong., Ist Sess., June 2, 1975, at 62 (related services does not in- clude medical services). Ultimately, the Conference Com- mittee approved the House language. S. Conf. Rep. No. 455, 94th Cong., Ist Sess., Nov. 14, 1975, at 30. Noth- ing in this legislative history supports or even suggests a cramped interpretation of the term “medical services” to mean only services performed by a physician. III. DEVELOPMENTS SINCE THE IDEA WAS EN- ACTED STRONGLY SUPPORT THE DISTRICT'S POSITION ON EXCLUDED MEDICAL SERVICES. Since 1975, a series of developments have occurred which confirm that the “medical services” exclusion should not be limited artificially and exclusively to services which must be personally administered by a physician. These developments include: (1) regulations and administrative interpretations of the IDEA; (2) legislative amendments; (3) the Supreme Court’s decisions in Rowley and Tatro; and (4) a succession of lower court rulings, including decisions by six separate United States Courts of Appeals, that have been implicitly ratified by Congress. A. The Department of Education’s Regulations and Interpretations Do Not Favor a Bright-Line Rule. Respondent and the United States argue that deference should be given to the regulations of the Secretary of Education. However, the question is, “deference to what?” As previously discussed, the regulations have never defined excluded medical services. Nor has the De- partment prior to this case ever adopted a bright-line in- terpretation of the medical services exclusion in the con- text of continuous one-on-one nursing. See generally J.M., 1998 U.S. App. LEXIS 17079 at *8 (characterizing the government’s statements in this case about its own inter- pretations of the medical services exclusion as “inac- curate”). As promulgated in 1977, the regulations contained (and still contain to this day) a narrow definition of included medical services as “services provided by a licensed physi- cian to determine a child’s medically related [disability] that results in the child’s need for special education and related services.” 42 Fed. Reg. 42479 (1977). Given the concerns about the impending costs of complying with the Act, it is logical to conclude that the Department wished 9 to limit strictly the diagnostic and evaluative medical services that would be covered. The Department did not need to provide a definition of excluded medical services because “medical services” already had a common mean- ing. Had the Department wished to attach a restrictive meaning of excluded medical services, it could have sim- ply adopted a regulation to that effect, just as it did for included diagnostic/evaluative medical services. In January 1981, the Secretary of Education issued a notice of interpretation of these regulations which sup- ports this view. 46 Fed. Reg. 4912 (1981). In that notice, the Secretary interpreted the IDEA and its regula- tions as requiring the provision of clean intermittent cath- eterization (“CIC”) to disabled students during the school day. However, the result is less significant than the Secre- tary’s reasoning. Since CIC is not required to be provided by physicians, the Secretary could have made the required “interpreta- tion” very quickly if she believed that the existing regula- tions established a “bright-line” rule limiting excluded medical services to physician-provided services. But the Secretary did not follow that approach, and in fact, did not even mention the included medical services regulation.~ Rather, she made a far lengthier and more balanced argument which emphasized that CIC was “a relatively simple procedure” that could be performed by a school nurse or another responsible person, “none of whom need to be licensed to perform that service.” /d. The Secretary also said at the same time that school districts did not need to provide “routine medical services, such as laboratory analysis,” even though such services are generally not administered by physicians. A _ bright-line interpretation of excluded medical services was clearly not contemplated in January of 1981. Even more importantly, in May 1981, the Department indefinitely postponed its CIC interpretation, stating that the existing regulations were “ambiguous” concerning the differentiation between related services and medical serv- 10 ices. 46 Fed. Reg. 25614 (1981). It is inconceivable that the Department would have withdrawn the CIC inter- pretation because the regulations were “ambiguous,” if it believed the regulations established a bright-line physi- cian/nonphysician rule for excluded medical services. Such a bright-line rule would clearly have required school districts to provide CIC. The Secretary's efforts to revise the regulations in 1982 do not alter this analysis. At most, the Secretary stated at that time that there were “problems ._. . determining whether certain health-related services are to be provided.” 47 Fed. Reg. 23837 (1982). The Secretary did not say there had been or should be a “bright-line” rule govern- ing excluded “medical services.” While the Secretary's attempt to narrow sharply the definition of “related serv- ices” ran into congressional opposition and was later withdrawn, there is no indication that anyone believed continuous one-on-one nursing services would be a covered service or that only physician services would be excluded. Yet again as recently as 1996, the Department could not “express a view as to whether one-on-one nursing serv- ices are a required related service.” Pet. App. 67a. The United States argues that this recent interpretation from the Department merely “acknowledged that courts have reached differing conclusions” and that the letter “reaf- firmed the Secretary's longstanding interpretation of IDEA that services provided by a nurse are not excluded medi- cal services.” However, the 1996 interpretation at no time mentions a longstanding bright-line distinction, nor does it ever say that services provided by a nurse are not ex- cluded medical services. Rather, the determination as to whether [one-on-one nursing services] are a required related service for an in- dividual disabled student must be made on a case- by-case basis. {[Pet. App. 67a] Again in 1996, the Department commented: If the services are not for diagnostic and evaluative purposes and could be provided by another qualified service,” not for diagnostic and ev purposes must be made on a case-by-case basis. {Letter to . Disab, Educ. L. Rep. 531, 532 (Nov. 13, 1996)] (emphasis added). The Department could have answered these questions simply by saying that if necessary services need not be provided by a physician, they are “related services.” It did not do so, thereby demonstrating the fallacy of the argument that the Department has consistently and clearly applied a physician/nonphysician distinction to defining “medical services.” ! It is well-settled that inconsistent agency interpretations are entitled to considerably less deference. INS v. Cardoza- Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421, 446 n.30 (1987); General Elec. Co. v. Gilbert, 429 U.S. 125, 142-43 (1976). Moreover, an agency interpretation that conflicts with the plain Jan- guage of the statute is entitled to no deference at all. Public Employees Retirement Sys. of Ohio v. Betts, 492 U.S. 158, 171 (1989); Bowsher v. Merck & Co., Inc., 460 U.S. 824, 837 (1983). B. This Court’s Decisions Do Not Favor a Bright-Line Rule. The question whether a school district was required to provide CIC under the IDEA reached this Court in Irving Independent Sch. Dist. v. Tatro, 468 U.S. 883 (1984). Some language in Tatro can be read as favoring a bright- line rule. But those statements were not necessary to the Court’s decision, and they go against much of the Court's reasoning. For the most part, this Court emphasized in Tatro that CIC was not “beyond the range of [schools’} competence,” was “difficult to distinguish from” services traditionally provided by school nurses to nondisabled children, and was a service of a kind “routinely provided Amber Tatro. This balanced, nuanced approach was con- sistent with the Court's earlier decision in Board of Educ. of Hendrick Hudson Central Sch. Dist. v. Rowley, 458 U.S. 176 (1982). D. Lower Court Decisions Do Not Favor a Bright-Line Rule. The lower courts have overwhelmingly read Tatro as adopting a balanced approach to the “medical services” exclusion. If Tatro endorsed a bright-line physician/non- physician rule, it is difficult to explain why lower courts, almost without exception, have read Tatro otherwise. USING A MULTI-FACTOR TEST FOR “MEDICAL SERVICES” IS CONSISTENT WITH DETERMIN A- TIONS MADE IN OTHER CONTEXTS UNDER THE IDEA. A. Balancing Pervades the IDEA. The District has proposed, consistent with numerous lower court decisions, to use a multi-factor test to deter- mine if services are excluded “medical services,” including examining the nature and extent of the services and the burden on the district. Respondent and his amici have argued this is an onerous test that will lead to inconsistent provision of services across the country and unnecessary litigation. However, the IDEA is premised upon the man- date that decisions are to be based on individualized de- terminations on a case-by-case basis, and many multi- factored and balancing tests are utilized under the IDEA. The nature and extent of required services are ex- amined in making determinations for Medicaid assistance and for private insurance coverage. These determinations go well beyond merely identifying whether the service must be performed by physicians. Medicaid, which is expressly defined as “medical assistance,” may include “pri- 16 vate duty nursing.” 42 U.S.C. § 1396d(r), incorporating services in § 1396d(a). In this case, Garret’s private in- surer determined that his needed one-to-one attendant services qualified as “medically necessary services,” thus entitling him to payment for a nurse to accompany him to -_school. TR 168. Similarly, several courts have examined the nature and extent of necessary services to make a determination of whether the requested services were “medical” in nature. See § ILC, supra. This balancing test is no more difficult to apply than other IDEA balancing tests. For example, the eligibility of a child for services is made only after an individualized consideration of testing and evaluation materials which must consider the nature and extent of the child’s deviation from his or her nondisabled peers. 20 U.S.C. § 1412 (5)(C). Likewise, the courts apply balancing tests in determining the appropriate placement for a child and whether the “nature and severity of a disability is such that education in regular classes with the use of supple- mentary aids and services cannot be achieved satisfac- torily.” Id. § 1412(5)(B) (emphasis added). A.W. v. Northwest R-1 Sch. Dist., 813 F.2d 158 (8th Cir.), cert. denied, 484 U.S. 847 (1987); Schuldt v. Mankato Ind. Sch. Dist.. 937 F.2d 1357 (8th Cir. 1991); Roncker v. Walter, 700 F.2d 1058 (6th Cir.), cert. denied, 464 U.S. 864 (1983); Daniel R.R. v. State Bd. of Educ., 874 F.2d 1036 (5th Cir, 1989). Balancing tests are also used in making disciplinary decisions for children with disabilities. Light v. Parkway C-2 Sch. Dist., 41 F.3d 1223 (8th Cir. 1994), cert. denied, 115 S.Ct. 2557 (1995). These de- terminations may lead to different decisions, including level of services, for similarly situated children across the country. But what is lost in consistency is gained in flexibility and practicality. B. Respondent’s Criticisms of the Balancing Test Are Misplaced. Respondent argues that adoption of a bright-line physi- cian/nonphysician distinction “eliminates the costs, delay, and the general state of confusion that would result from 17 the imposition of the multifactor test . . . which would require adjudication of varied factual circumstances through extensive appellate processes.” However, the test proposed by the District is exactly the type of balancing test currently used in making eligibility, placement and service decisions under the IDEA. 20 U.S.C. § 1414(a)(5). The ease of applying the balancing test was demonstrated in the IEP team meeting for Garret in which determinations were made as to the services Garret needed, that Garret required continuous care by an appropriately licensed practitioner, that existing school nurses could not provide the services, and that his care, if not properly performed, could be life-threatening. The bright-line physician/nonphysician test, while seem- ingly simple, in fact does not lead to uniformity or con- sistency, nor is it a viable test over time. “In recent years, nonphysician clinicians have provided increasing amounts of health care that, in the past, has been provided princi- pally by physicians.” Richard A. Cooper, Roles of Non- physician Clinicians as Autonomous Providers of Patient Care, 280 JAMA 795 (1998). “Physicians are no longer seen as the sole provider” of “physician services.” Richard A. Cooper, Perspectives on the Physician Workforce t the Year 2020, 274 JAMA 1534, 1541 (1995). saniied State and federal regulations that determine what func- tions nonphysicians are permitted to perform have seen sweeping changes in recent years. In 1995 alone, state legislatures enacted more than 300 laws regarding scope of practice and reimbursement for nonphysicians, “virtu- ally all” of which “increase{d] the authority of nonphysi- cians in areas long reserved to physicians.” Catherine M. Dower, et al., Changing Nature of Physician Licen- sure: Implications for Medical Education in California, 168 Western J. of Med. 422, 424 (1998). Insurers, including the federal govern- ment through Medicare, increasingly treat nonphysicians as covered “providers.” Under the Balanced Budget Act, beginning this year nurse practitioners, clinical nurse spe- cialists and physician assistants are treated as Medicare- covered providers. C. The Government’s Criticisms of the Balancing Test Are Misplaced. The United States argues that the five-factor test pro- posed by the District is contradicted by examples of “re- lated Services” contained in the statute. Yet the United States fails to recognize that “psychological services” and “physical and occupational therapy,” unlike medical serv- ices, are expressly required as related services. Thus, the continuous and extensive nature of those services is not a consideration in determining the student's entitlement to them. Congress may well have listed certain services as required in order that they would not be viewed as medical services; Congress did not list private-duty type nursing. Similarly, the United States misreads the District’s con- tention that consideration should be given to whether a school nurse can provide the services. The Department's own regulations define related services to include “school health services” which are “services provided by a quali- fied school nurse or other qualified person.” 34 C.F.R. §§ 300.16(a) and 300.16(b)(11). In specifically requir- ing “school” health services and not just “health services,” it was contemplated that some health services are not 19 required school health services, and in defining the serv- ices as provided by a “school” nurse, it must mean there was some recognition that school nurses are not private- duty nurses. One-on-one private duty-type nursing is not a service traditionally or generally provided by school nurses who provide services to several hundred students (TR 441-42, 470, 477-480), and certainly there is noth- ing in the record when the regulations were promulgated to indicate that the Secretary considered such intensive nursing services to be school health services. Therefore, school nurses may be required to provide certain intermit- tent health services required by children with disabilities, such as tube feedings and CIC (currently provided by District nurses) (TR 333-37, 380-83), since they can be provided in the context of their total school nursing duties. It is a far stretch to rule that districts must hire a nurse full-time for a single student. D. The District Should Prevail Under a Balancing Test. Respondent attempts to argue that he would prevail even under a balancing test. However, as has been pre- viously pointed out, the ALJ in this case did not apply a true balancing test, but simply said that Garret was not as “fragile” as the students in some of the other lower court cases. Pet. App. 52a. When a true balanc- ing is performed, all the relevant factors weigh in favor of classifying the services as “medical.” Garret cannot be left without a trained attendant with him at all times to make nursing assessments. TR 44-45, 427, EX 91 at 388. The Iowa Board of Nursing has specifically ruled that a licensed nurse (minimally an LPN) is re- quired to provide the services because of the nature and complexity of the care. The nurse may not see to the needs of any other children in the building or in any other buildings while attending to Garret. TR 469-470. Without the services provided by the LPN, Garret could be in a life-threatening situation at any time. Hiring a special LPN for Garret would cost over $30,000. Since neither of the lower federal courts addressed the state law issues, they should be considered on remand. Moreover, for all practical purposes, the relevant Iowa law is the same as the IDEA. If Petitioner prevails on the IDEA issues, it is entirely likely that the lower courts will find for Petitioner on the state-law issues. The ALJ relied upon language in the Iowa statute which defines “special education” as including “corrective and supporting services.” This language mirrors the defi- nition of “related services” in the IDEA. Compare § 256B.2(3) of the Iowa Code with 20 U.S.C. § 1401 (a)(17). The Iowa Department of Education has also adopted the federal definition of “related services,” in- cluding the specific exclusion of medical services. 281 Iowa Admin. Code § 41.94(2)(d) (1995). Furthermore, the Iowa Supreme Court has already ruled that variations in language between the federal and state laws do not grant greater rights of entitlement to special education services under state law.
