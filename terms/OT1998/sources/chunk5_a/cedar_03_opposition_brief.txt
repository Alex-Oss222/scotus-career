STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


A. Course of Proceedings 


The Petitioner, Cedar Rapids Community School District 
(the District) did file suit in the United States District Court for 
the Northern District of lowa which sought to reverse the 
December 16, 1994, ruling by the lowa Department of 
Education that the District was required under both state and 
federal law to . 2imburse Charlene F. for the costs of Garret F.’s 
healthcare costs while attending the District’s schools. The 
Department of Education ruled that the health services needed 
by Garret F. to allow him to participate in a free appropriate 
public education constituted “related services” under federal law 
and “support services” under lowa law. The Department of 
Education's ruling was carefully reasoned and findings of facts 
were made that whether the “bright line” test of Tatro was 
followed or the standards of the cases Detsel, Bevin H. and 
Shannon M.' were applied, the services were related services 
under federal law and support services under Iowa law and thus 
must be provided by the District. 


The District chose to appeal this decision to the United 
States District Court for the Northern District of Iowa rather 
than state court. 20 U.S.C. § 1415(e). Both parties moved for 
summary judgment. On March 22, 1996, the United States 
District Court for the Northern District of lowa, Honorable

REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 
L 


THE JUDGMENT IN FAVOR OF GARRET F. WOULD 
BE AFFIRMED EVEN IF THE EIGHTH CIRCUIT’S USE 
OF AND APPLICATION OF TATRO WAS IN ERROR. 


The Petition for Writ of Certiorari should be denied for 
several reasons. First, the decision of the Administrative Law 
Judge was based both on state and federal law. It was also 
based on a series of fact findings that the needed services are 
“related services” under federal law whether or not the “bright 
line” rule was employed or not. The well-established rule in the 
Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals is that the Court can affirm on 
any grounds supported in the record even if not addressed or 
relied upon by the Federal District Court. See, e.g., Smith v. 
Copeland, 87 F.3d 265, 267-68 (8th Cir. 1996); O’Hagan v. 
United States, 86 F.3d 776, 779 n.2 (8th Cir. 1996); and Phillips 
v. Marist Society of Washington Prov., 80 F.3d 274, 275 (8th 
Cir. 1996). 


This well-established rule applies in the United States 
Supreme Court as well. United States v. Carignan, 342 U.S. 
36, 38-39, 72 S. Ct. 97, 96 L. Ed. 48, 50-51 (1951); Thigpen 
v. Roberts, 468 U.S. 27, 28, 104 S. Ct. 2916, 82 L. Ed. 2d 23, 
28 (1984). In Thigpen, the Supreme Court stated: 


Although the court below and the petition 
for certiorari addressed only the double 
jeopardy issue, we may affirm on any ground 
that the law and the record permit and that 
will not expand the relief granted below. 


468 U.S. at 29-30 (citing United States v. New York Telephone 
Co., 434 U.S. 159, 166 n.8, 98 S. Ct. 364, 54 L. Ed. 2d 376 
(1977)). 


In New York Telephone Co., the Supreme Court stated: 


We have the discretion to consider [issues 
not even encompassed with the question 
posed in the Petition for Certiorari] because 
the prevailing party may defend the judgment 
on any ground which the law and the record 
permit that would not expand the relief it 
has been granted. 


434 U.S. at 166 n.8, 54 L. Ed. 2d at 385 (citing Langnes v. 
Green, 282 U.S. 531, 538-39, 51 S. Ct. 243, 75 L. Ed. 520 
(1931)). 


Judgments, such as the present one, which are sustainable 
on non-federal grounds should result in a denial of a Writ of 
Certiorari. See, e.g., Jankovich v. Indiana Toll Road 
Commission, 379 U.S. 487, 488-89, 13 L. Ed. 2d 439, 441- 
42, 85 S. Ct. 593 (1965); Wilson v. Loew's Inc., 355 U.S. 597, 
598, 2 L. Ed. 2d 519, 521, 78 S. Ct. 526 (1958). 


A case which compels denial of certiorari in this case is SS 
Monrosa v. Carbon Black Export Inc., 359 U.S. 180, 3 L. Ed. 
2d 723, 79 S. Ct. 710, reh. den., 359 U.S. 999, 3 L. Ed. 2d 
986, 79 S. Ct. 1115 (1959). 


9 


The SS Monrosa case involved a libel in admiralty in the 
United States District Court for the Southern District of Texas. 
359 U.S. at 181, 3 L. Ed. 2d at 724. 


The Court enunciated an important rule which applies in 
this case: 


While this Court decides questions of public 
importance, it decides them in the context of 
meaningful litigation. Its function in 
resolving conflicts among the Courts of 
Appeals is judicial, not simply administrative 
or managerial. Resolution here of the extent 
to which these bill of lading provisions may 
be given effect by our courts can await a day 
when the issue is posed less abstractly. 


359 U.S. 184, 3 L. Ed. 2d at 726. 


In this case, it is clear that the administrative law judge 
found in favor of Garret F. on both state and federal grounds. 
The state grounds were pursuant to lowa Code § 256B.2 (1993) 
and relevant lowa Administrative Code provisions. Apx. C. The 
District Court adopted the “reasoning of the ALJ” and ruled in 
favor of Garret F. on federal grounds both under a Tatro and 
non-TJatro approach. 


The Eighth Circuit ruled in favor of Garret F. on the grounds 
of Tatro only. The non-Tatro federal grounds and the state court 
grounds remain in support of the Circuit’s ruling and serve as 
justification to deny certiorari in this case.

Il. 


THE EIGHTH CIRCUIT RULING CLEARLY 
FOLLOWS AND APPLIES TATRO AND DOES NOT 
CREATE AN ILLOGICAL DEFINITION OF MEDICAL 
SERVICES. 


Contrary to the assertion of the District, the Court of 
Appeals’ ruling does nothing more than apply and enforce the 
plain language of Tatro and 20 U.S.C. § 1401(a)(17). 


The Eighth Circuit has not “shifted” any responsibility in 
this case. In contrast, it is requiring the District to use its 
substantial resources to provide needed related services under 
federal law for Garret F. to receive a free, appropriate public 
education. The District's attempt to plead poverty is not a matter 
of record in this case and should not be countenanced. 


It should also be noted that in Detsel v. Sullivan (Detsel II), 
895 F.2d 58, 59 (2nd Cir. 1990) the funding issue the District 
relies on was mooted for many, if not most, students when the 
United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit ruled 
Melissa Detsel was entitled to Medicaid payment for private- 
duty nursing while attending public school. 


Respondent does concede that the Eighth Circuit's ruling, 
which follows Tatro, is in conflict with rulings of the Sixth and 
Ninth Circuits which failed to follow Tatro. It is also in conflict 
with a District Court decision in New York which was affirmed 
per curiam by the Second Circuit, Detsel v. Board of Education 
of Auburn, 820 F.2d 587 (2nd Cir. 1987). 


Further, in contrast to the assertion on page 7 of the Petition, 
the record clearly establishes that the services here do not fall 
outside the range of traditional school nursing services. In fact, 


1] 


virtually all of the services are currently being provided to other 
students within the District. While the Petitioner “demands this 
Court’s immediate review” the fact of the matter is that other 
school districts are providing similar services at public expense. 
Tr. at 341-43; 364-65. 


The purpose of the IDEA “was to rectify discrimination 
suffered by children with disabilities at the hands of public 
schools.” Conner v. Branstad, 839 F. Supp. 1346, 1358 n.22 
(S.D. Ia. 1993). Thus, the IDEA must “be broadly applied and 
liberally construed in favor of providing free and appropriate 
education to handicapped students.” S-/] v. Turlington, 635 F.2d 
342, 347 (Sth Cir. 1981), cert. denied, 454 U.S. 1030. 


Under Federal law, special education and related services 
are to be provided to the maximum extent appropriate with 
children who are non-disabled. 20 U.S.C. § 1412 (1993); 34 
C.F.R. § 300.550. lowa law provides that: 


to the maximum extent possible, children 
requiring special education shall attend 
regular classes and shal! be educated with 
children who do not require special 
education. Whenever possible, hindrances to 
learning and to the normal functioning of 
children requiring special education within 
the regular school environment shall be 
overcome by the provision of special aids 
and services rather than by separate programs 
for those in need of special education. 
Special classes, separate school or other 
removal of children requiring special 
education from the regular educational 
environment, shall occur only when, and to 
the extent that the nature or severity of the 


12 


educational handicap is such that education 
and regular classes, even with the use of 
supplementary aids and services, cannot be 
accomplished satisfactorily. 


Iowa Code § 256B.2(3) (1993). 


In Independent School District v. Tatro, 468 U.S. 883, 104 
S. Ct. 3371, 82 L. Ed. 2d 664 (1984) the Supreme Court applied 
a two part inquiry to determine whether a given service is a 
related service under the IDEA: (1) is the supportive service 
necessary to enable a handicapped child to benefit from special 
education; (2) is the service excluded by the act as a “medical 
service” aside from any diagnostic or evaluation purpose. 468 
U.S. at 890, 104 S.Ct. at 3375. There is no question that both 
elements are met here and the services needed are related services 
under the IDEA. 


Under the bright line rule and holding of Tatro, in this case 
the services are related services and not medical services. In 
Tatro, the Court addressed and rejected a school district’s claim 
that it had no responsibility to administer intermittent 
catheterization because it constituted a medical service. 104 S. 
Ct. at 3374. The United States Supreme Court determined that 
catheterization was a related service because it enabled a 
handicapped child to remain at school during the day and, thus, 
have meaningful access to education. Jd. at 3377. The United 
States Supreme Court also determined that catheterization did 
not fall under the medical treatment exclusion of the statute or 
the Federal Department of Education's Rules. /d. It affirmed 
the Department’s interpretation through ruling making that 
services of a school nurse would be required as a related service 
while treatment by a physician could be excluded. Dr. 
Reinertson’s uncontroverted testimony that none of the services 
Garret needs require a physician means they are related services 
under Tatro. 


13 


In dicta, which later cases improperly extrapolated, the Court 
noted that a distinction between nursing services and treatment 
by a physician as a related service was reasonable. Part of the 
reasonableness was concluded to be a consideration of 
differentiation of costs between nursing and physician services 
and the fact that school nurses “have long been a part of the 
education system.” Jd. at 3378. 


The Supreme Court did not say that cost was a factor to be 
considered by courts. It merely stated that Congress and the 
Department of Education may have used expense, as well as 
limitations of educators’ expertise in medical matters, for making 
a distinction between nursing services and physician treatment 
as a related service. The Supreme Court expressly rejected the 
school’s argument that services provided in accordance with a 
physician’s prescription and general supervision fell within the 
medical treatment exclusion. It noted that nurses in the school 
were authorized to dispense medications and administer 
emergency injections to non-disabled students in accordance 
with a physician's prescription. /d. Thus, in Tatro, the Supreme 
Court affirmed a Congressional and administrative distinction 
between nursing services and medical treatment as a related 
service under the IDEA. 


In contrast, the District's position is based on decisions 
from within the Second, Sixth and Ninth circuits. The logical 
problem with decisions such as these was well stated in Macomb 
Co. School District v. Joshua S., 715 F. Supp. 824 (E.D. Mich. 
1989): 


The difficulty the Court has with both 
Detsel and Bevin are their failure to adhere 
to certain principles developed in Tatro. Due 
to this failure, the decisions are not entirely 
clear as to whether the disputed services were 


14 


rejected as not representing “supportive 
services” under th. & {\HCA, or whether they 
fell within the “medical services” exclusion. 
Rather, Detsel concludes that while the 
disputed services did not actually meet the 
Statutory and regulatory definition of medical 
services, given the regulation specifically 
addresses physician-performed services, 
denying the services “is in keeping with [the 
regulation’s) spirit.” 637 F.Supp. at 1022. 
The Court, however, believes that this 
conclusion ignores the spirit of the EAHCA 
itself. As Tatro repeatedly stressed, the reason 
for mandating the provision of supportive 
services under the EAHCA, is to guarantee 
handicapped students an opportunity to gain 
an education. If granting such an opportunity 
entails furnishing medically-related services 
short of requiring a licensed physician, we 
believe such services are the student's right. 
Moreover, the EAHCA, its legislative 
history, and its regulations are void of any 
suggestion that states are free to decide, on 
the basis of the cost and effort required, 
which related services fall within the medical 
services exclusion. The Court therefore 
rejects Detsel’s conclusion that services 
provided by a nurse, no matter how 
comprehensive, are medical services entitled 
to exclusion under the EAHCA. 


Bevin reaches its result by holding that 
related services are only those that are “within 
reason,” and that the proper determination 
involves a balancing of factors. Yet, Bevin's 


15 


reliance on Dept. of Educ., State of Hawaii 
v. Katherine D., 727 F.2d 809 (9th Cir. 
1984), for this proposition is misplaced. In 
holding that a school district need only 
provide services within reason, Katherine D. 
was responding to the question of whether 
the EAHCA must provide a handicapped 
student “the best possible education.” 727 
F.2d at 813. This inquiry is entirely separate 
from the inquiry concerning the services 
necessary to provide a child “with 
meaningful access to education. ...” Tatro, 
468 U.S. at 891 (emphasis supplied). 
Certainly, the issue of the level of education 
required under the EAHCA will raise 
questions of balance. Yet, once a district 
problem of access must be examined within 
the framework supplied in Tatro. For this 
reason, we find Bevin of marginal 


precedential value. 


It also should be noted that the dicta relied on by Bevin H. 
and Detsel “unduly expensive and beyond the range of their 
competence” is a two-pronged dicta, the later of which appears 
to have been overlooked by the District. In this case the evidence 
established that the services required are within the competence 
of the school nurses. 


The District's statement that a “consensus” exists (page 12) 
omits any serious discussion of cases which find similar services 
to be related services: Joshua S.; Tokaricik v. Forest Hill School 
District, 665 F.2d 443 (3rd Cir.), cert. denied, 73 L. Ed. 2d 
1383 (1981) (catheterization found to be a related service); Dept. 
of Education v. Katherine D., 727 F.2d 809, 813 (9th Cir. 1984) 


16 


(tracheotomy care held to be a related service); Digest of 
Response, 20 IDELR at 174-75 (declaring the training on 
reversing a pattern associated with aspirating during feeding 
would be the responsibility of the education community); 
Special School Dist. No. 6, St. Paul, Minn., 23 IDELR 119, 
131 (1994) (both parties assume that tracheal suctioning must 
be provided by the district as a related service within the 
framework of providing a free, appropriate public education in 
the least restrictive environment, and the crux of the dispute is 
where and by whom people should be suctioned). 


Another case bearing on the Jatro bright line rule is Max 
M. v. Thompson, 592 F. Supp. 1437, 1445 (N.D. Ill. 1984) 
(which held that psychotherapy, a recognized “related service” 
was not excluded even though in that case it was provided by a 
licensed physician). Psychotherapy and similar psychological 
services that other professionals can provide are required under 
§ 1401(a)(17) and do not become non-reimbursable by virtue 
of their being performed by a psychiatrist. Jd. 


The logic of the district’s cost-based approach collapses 
when it is considered that the “related services” identified in the 
Act includes such costly services as speech pathology and 
audiology, psychological services, physical and occupational 
therapy, recreation, medical and counseling services, except that 
such medical services shall be for diagnostic and evaluation 
purposes only. 20 U.S.C. § 1401(a)(17). 


With respect to Rowley, it should be noted that the history 
of the IDEA is discussed including the Congressional Findings: 
Congress found that of the roughly 8 million handicapped 
children in the United States at the time of enactment, | million 
were “excluded entirely from the public school system” and 
more than half were receiving an inappropriate education. 458 
U.S. at 189, 73 L. Ed. 2d at 701. That is precisely the case here

on the unrefuted record. In other words, if the services are not

The District's arguments concerning Rowley, a 1982 
Supreme Court case, are not persuasive. First, Rowley preceded 
Tatro by two years and the opportunity Garrett F. seeks in this 
case is merely to get in the front door of the school and survive 
for the school day. He is not seeking some ultimate level of 
educational experience. The whole purpose of the IDEA is to 
make public education available to handicapped children and 
to make such access meaningful. Tatro, 468 U.S. at 891, 82 L. 
Ed. 2d at 672; Rowley, 458 U.S. at 192. “A service that enables 
a handicapped child to remain at school during the day is an 
important means of providing the child with the meaningful 
access to education that Congress envisioned.” Tatro, 468 U.S. 
at 891, 82 L. Ed 2d at 672. 


The District’s argument at page 12 of its Petition on “shades 
of difference in the reasoning of these opinions” is not supported 
by the record in this case. For example, the District states “others 
emphasize the legal burden of being responsible for continuous, 
life-sustaining care.” The District specifically disavowed this as 
a substantial basis for its position and placed it in terms of the 
liability for using a non-licensed provider, not liability for 
providing the services. (Tr. at 568-71). The third distinction

nursing services’ traditionally provided by schools” is also 
without merit since as shown above, virtually all of the services 
have been provided and are being provided to other students in 
the District. 


That leaves “a focus on the school’s financial burden” as 
the only “shade of reasoning” left for the District. As set forth 
above, this ground fails based on the factual record in this case. 


The Districts’ citation to, Appendix D, a letter from the 
OSEP, adds little to the anaiysis other than the OSEP itself was 
unable to lay down a guideline applicable to all cases after 
considering the issue for two and a half years. 


Finally, the District’s argument on adverse policy 
consequences is not well-founded. The District again makes a 
number of unsupported assumptions in its cost arguments. First, 
it states that there are a “number of seriously disabled children”
