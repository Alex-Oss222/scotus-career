STATEMENT OF THE CASE A. Garret F.’s Condition and Needs. Respondent Garret F. is a ventilator-dependent child enrolled in the Cedar Rapids Community School District. In 1987, when Garret was four years old, he was seriously injured when his blanket caught in the drive mechanism of the motorcycle on which he was riding, jerking his head. Garret suffered a complete transection of the spine and, as a result, is quadriplegic and completely paralyzed be- low his neck. Garret depends on a ventilator for breath- ing. He has normal mental capacity, is able to speak, and uses a puff and sip straw to control a wheelchair. Pet. App. 19a-20a. 4 It is undisputed that Garret requires the following health services while at school: * urinary bladder catheterization (usually once a day); * suctioning of his tracheostomy as needed; * change of positioning; * ambu bag administration in cases of ventilator mal- functioning (this consists of manual pumping of an air bag attached to his tracheostomy tube); * ventilator setting checks, observations and assess- ments to determine if Garret is in respiratory distress; * assessment to determine if Garret is experiencing autonomic hyperreflexia; and * blood pressure monitoring, urinary catheterization and bowel disimpaction in the event of an episode of autonomic hyperreflexia." Pet. App. 20a. These observation and monitoring serv- ices must be provided continuously by trained personnel because Garret could die if his breathing were interrupted for just a few minutes. Unlike every other student in the District who receives free health-related services from the District during the day, Garret F. requires “constant as- sessment and monitoring.” TR at 338; EX 94 at 364-378. In dicta, the ALJ noted that even if the IDEA did not define “medical services” so narrowly as to include only services directly administered by a physician, and instead imposed a balancing test as several lower courts had found, the District still had to provide the requested one- on-one continuous services for Garret. The District then filed an action in federal district court to review the ALJ’s decision under 20 U.S.C. § 1415(e), requesting declaratory relief that “it is not required under either [the IDEA] or the Iowa Special Education laws” to provide the continuous one-on-one nursing services to Garret. On cross-motions for summary judgment, the dis- trict court granted summary judgment for Garret and his mother. The court ruled as a matter of law that the “medical services” exclusion in the Act “is limited to the services of a physician or hospital.” Pet. App. 15a. SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (the “IDEA”) does not require Cedar Rapids Community School District to hire a specially-trained nurse to attend continuously and exclusively to Garret F.’s health-care needs while he is at school. To require local school dis- tricts to provide intensive, continuous one-on-one nursing 6 The district court’s opinion incluudes a “But see” citation to Neely v. Rutherford County School, 68 F.3d 965 (6th Cir. 1995), one of the cases that used a practical, balancing approach to deter- mine the scope of the “medical services” exclusion. Pet. App. 15a. Contrary to the suggestion of the Solicitor General at the petition stage, this confirms that the district court did not apply a balancin’ approach or find that Garrett F. would have prevailed under such an approach. certain “related services” as part of their mandate to pro- vide special education, those services are enumerated, they do not include intensive nursing services, and they specifically exclude “medical services” except for evalua- tion and diagnosis. 20 U.S.C. § 1401(a)(17). The term “medical” in the IDEA should have a plain, common-sense meaning, as it does when used in other federal statutes and in other contexts. “Medical” services do not have to be directly administered by a licensed physician, but can be furnished by other competent health care providers. Congress has considered and clarified the definition of “related services” several times when it amended the IDEA, and has made specific additions to the list of related services. However, Congress has never altered the original “medical services” exclusion, even though Congress was undoubtedly aware of several federal court precedents excluding one-on-one nursing services from the scope of the IDEA services. Until this case, the pronouncements by the Department of Education also favored a common-sense definition of “medical services,” not a formalistic definition turning on whether the person providing the service happened to be a licensed physician. As recently as 1996, two interpretive letters rejected a bright-line physician/nonphysician dis- tinction and stated that private duty-type nursing services could be excluded from the coverage of the Act. This Court’s prior decisions in Board of Education of Hendrick Hudson Central School Dist. v. Rowley, 458 U.S. 176 (1982), and Irving Independent School Dist. v. Tatro, 468 U.S. 883 (1984), also lead to the conclusion that Iecal school districts are not required to provide continuous one-on-one nursing services as part of their federal special education mandate. Rowley establishes two In Tatro, this Court held that school districts can be required to provide intermittent health-care related serv- ices, such as clean intermittent catheterization (“CIC”). CIC needed to be administered only once a day, could be administered by an existing school nurse, and was similar to services already provided by school nurses. In short, the same practical, flexible approach of Tatro that favored a finding that CIC is included in “related services” favors a finding of exclusion of continuous one-on-one nursing services here. Ultimately, the question of whether health care services must be provided by a school district should not be de- cided by a mechanical physician /nonphysician test. Rather, the outcome should depend upon a series of factors, such as whether the care is continuous or intermittent, whether existing school health personnel can provide the service. the cost of the service, and the potential consequences if the service is not properly performed. Indeed, until the lower courts’ decisions in this case, the vast majority of federal courts had followed just this approach. This multi- factor test enhances the goal of the IDEA, which is to ensure that all disabled children receive a free appropriate public education by allocating scarce resources to educa- tion and education-related services, while utilizing other resources for medical services. 12 I. THE PLAIN LANGUAGE OF THE IDEA AND ITS LEGISLATIVE HISTORY CALL FOR ALL MEDI- CAL SERVICES, NOT JUST THOSE DIRECTLY PROVIDED BY A PHYSICIAN, TO BE EXCLUDED FROM THE SCHOOL DISTRICT'S RESPONSIBIL- ITY. The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, 20 U.S.C. § 1400, et seq. (the “IDEA” or “Act”)’ serves the laudable goal of assuring that every disabled child will receive a free appropriate public education including “spe- cial education and related services” suitable to him or her. Id. § 1400(c). “Related services” are defined as “trans- portation, and such developmental, corrective, and other supportive services .. . as may be required to assist a child with a disability to benefit from special education. ...” Td. §1401(a)(17). The Act requires that these services be free of charge to the student. In return, the federal government provides assistance to the States, cur- rently paying approximately seven percent of the costs of educating disabled children, leaving the States and local school districts to shoulder the rest of the costs. Cham- bers, Parrish, Lieberman & Wolman, “What Are We Spend- ing on Special Education in the U.S.?” Center for Special Education Finance (funded by Office for Special Educa- tion Programs, Department of Education), Brief No. 8, February 1998. However, consistent with the notion that the IDEA is about education, and is not a broader law requiring school districts to meet every daytime life-sustaining need of every student within their boundaries, the Act expressly excludes “medical services” from the “related services” 7 When first enacted in 1975, the Act was known as the Education for All Handicapped Children Act. See Pub. L. 94-142, 84 Stat. 175. For convenience, this brief will refer to the Act as the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act or the “IDEA,” the name it has had since 1990. 13 which must be provided by school districts. 20 U.S.C. § 1401(a)(17). School districts only have to provide and pay for medical services “for diagnostic and evaluation purposes.” Id. Garret, a 15-year-old ventilator-dependent child, re- quires the continuous assistance of medically trained per- sonnel to attend to his medical needs. Without this assist- ance, Garret could suffer respiratory distress and be in a life-threatening medical condition. By any common-sense definition of “medical services,” the intensive, one-on-one full-time nursing care that Garret needs to stay alive is a “medical” service. These same nursing services needed at both home and at school have been paid for by private health insurance as “medical services.” J.A. at 12; TR at 129. Nonetheless, the District has been ordered to pay for these services at school because the courts below concluded that “medical services” means only those serv- ices “directly administered by a physician.” Pet. App. 6a-7a. Neither the express language of the Act, the legislative history of the Act, the Department of Education’s regu- lations or interpretations, this Court’s prior decisions, the majority of other courts’ decisions, nor any other reliable source of statutory interpretation supports this unduly narrow definition of excluded medical services. To the contrary, the only logical interpretation of the Act favors a practical, common-sense application of the “medical services” exclusion so as to exempt school distiicts from having to pay for continuous one-on-one nursing services. A. The Express Language of the IDEA Favors a Common-Sense Definition of Medical Services. In interpreting a statute, the cardinal rule is first to examine the plain and ordinary meaning of the words of the statute. Connecticut Nat'l Bank v. Germain, 503 U.S. 249, 253-254 (1992); United States v. Ron Pair Enters., 489 U.S. 235, 242 (1989). The language in the IDEA is clear: it expressly excludes all “medical services,” except 14 those that are for diagnostic and evaluation purposes. As this Court has frequently reiterated, “{i]n the absence of an indication to the contrary, words in a statute are as- sumed to bear their ‘ordinary, contemporary, common meaning.” Walters v. Metropolitan Educationai Enter- prises, 117 S. Ct. 660, 664 (1997) (quoting Pioneer Investment Services Co. v. Brunswick Associates Ltd. Partnership, 507 U.S. 380, 388 (1993)). The term “medical services” is simply not understood in common parlance to mean only those services provided by a physi- cian, but is instead understood to embrace a broad range of services related to diagnosing, curing, mitigating, or preventing a disease or other health-related condition. The phrase typically has this same meaning when used in other legislation. See, e.g., 26 U.S.C. § 213(d) (definition of “medical care” in the Internal Revenue Code). Had Congress intended to exclude only those medical services directly administered by physicians, it could have sim- ply said “except physician services” or “except doctor's services.” Congress was well aware of what “medical” meant when it adopted the Act in 1975. In just the previous decade, Congress had enacted sweeping Medicaid legislation as Title XIX of the Social Security Act which expressly de- fined “medical assistance” as including an ambit of medi- cal services going well beyond those administered directly by licensed physicians. 42 U.S.C. § 1396d(a). The In- ternal Revenue Code had long defined “medical care” as amounts paid “for the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treat- ment, or prevention of disease, or for the purpose of affecting the structure or function of the body... .” 26 U.S.C. §213(d)(1). No one has ever seriously con- tended that the Tax Code’s medical expense deduction is limited to amounts paid to physicians. The Federal Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act, 21 U.S.C. § 301, ef seq., em- ploys a similar definition for determining what drugs and “medical devices” are subject to the regulation of the Act. See 21 U.S.C. § 321. 15 Likewise, there is a large body of precedent in other contexts holding that “medical” services are not only ren- dered by physicians. See, e.g., Moore v. Metropolitan Life Ins. Co., 307 N.E.2d 554 (N.Y. 1973) (“medical services . . . Obviously may be performed by any of a number of professionals or paramedical personnel”); Warren Tuck- ing Co. v. Chandler, 277 S.E.2d 488, 492 (Va. 1981) (ordinarily nursing services are among the “medical” benefits that ersployers and workmen’s compensation car- riers must provide); State ex rel. Multimedica, Inc. v. Snowden, 647 N.E.2d 1374, 1378 (Ohio 1995) (“medi- cal” refers to the science and art of the investigation, pre- vention, cure and alleviation of disease); State v. Robin- son, 735 P.2d 801, 809 n.9 (Ariz. 1987) (“medical” providers include psychologists ). B. THIS COURTS PRIOR DECISIONS RE THAT THE IDEA, INCLUDING THE MEDICAL SERVICES EXCLUSION, BE INTERPRETED IN A PRACTICAL, COMMON-SENSE MANNER, AN AP- PROACH FOLLOWED IN MOST SUBSEQUENT LOWER COURT DECISIONS. A. This Court’s Rowley Decision. This Court's decision in Board of Education of Hendrick Hudson Central School Dist. vy. Rowley, 458 U.S. 176 (1982), provides the starting-point for examining judicial 28 interpretations of the IDEA. In Rowley, this Court’s first decision under the IDEA, this Court considered whether school districts had to provide every service that was needed to give a disabled child an opportunity “com- mensurate with the opportunity provided other children.” Id. at 198 (quoting the district court). The parents in Rowley sought to require the school district to provide a sign-language interpreter for their partially deaf child, ar- guing an interpreter was needed to equalize their daugh- ter’s opportunities with those of nondisabled children even though she was performing well in school. Id. at 185-86. The Court rejected the parents’ argument, holding that the Act did not require any particular level of educational benefits. /d. at 202-203. The Court expressly rejected the notion that school districts were obligated by the Act to “maximize each child’s potential,” regardless of burden or cost. Id. at 204 n.26. Instead, the facts in each case had to be weighed individually. /d. at 202-203. Accordingly, it reversed the lower court rulings that required Amy Rowley to be provided with a sign language interpreter while attending school. Rowley repeatedly emphasizes Congress’s desire to af- ford states flexibility in meeting the requirements of the IDEA. Congress did not impose “a burden of unspecified proportions and weight” when it enacted the IDEA. 458 U.S. at 190 n.11. The IDEA does not “guarantee any particular level of education,” and imposes no clear obli- gation upon recipient States beyond the requirement that handicapped children receive some form of specialized education. .. .” /d. at 192, 195. One cannot square these kinds of statements, and Rowley’s rejection of bright- line rules, with a bright-line rule that school districts pro- vide nursing services regardless of burden or cost. B. This Court’s Tatro Decision. Practical considerations also guided this Court in Irving Independent School Dist. v. Tatro, 468 U.S. 883 (1984), its only prior decision addressing the “related 29 services” and “medical services” definitions. In Tatro, this Court generally adopted a flexible approach in ruling for 4 school-age child with spina bifida who needed clean intermittent catheterization (“CIC”) every three or four hours. The CIC procedure, according to the Court, was “a simple one that may be performed in a few minutes by a layperson with less than an hour's training.” /d. at 885. Indeed, the Court noted that Amber Tatro would soon be able to perform the procedury herself. It was also undis- puted that state law did not require even a nurse to per- form this simple procedure. /d. at 887. Under these cir- cumstances, this Court held that the school district should provide the service. This Court’s reasoning had two com- ponents: (1) CIC is analogous to the type of services that school nurses have traditionally provided to nondisabled students, such as dispensing oral medications and giving emergency injections. In other words, CIC “services [are] of a kind that are routinely provided to the non-handicapped.” /d. at 893-894. (2) While one could conclude that Congress wanted “to spare schools from an obligation to provide a service that might well prove unduly expensive and beyond the range of their competence,” it was also logical to conclude that “Congress intended to impose the obligation fon ~ ig to provide school nursing services.” /d. at 892- In short, this Court found that CIC, a simple procedure that needed to be performed only once during the school day by a school nurse who was not thereby precluded from performing services for other children, was a “re- lated” service analogous to traditional school nursing serv- ices (like administering insulin shots for a diabetic child) and not an excluded “medical service.” ™ 11 The Cedar Rapids Community School District currently pro- vides intermittent catheterization for approximately five students. TR at 287. By way of contrast, the District does not provide con- tinuous nursing services for any student. 30 As in Rowley, this Court in Tatro largely followed a balanced, nuanced approach, carefully considering the burdens on the school district. Jd. at 893-894. Some lan- guage in Tatro does support a bright-line physician/ nonphysician rule, such as the Court’s statement that the Secretary of Education “limit{ed] the ‘medical services’ exclusion to the services of a physician or hospital,” /d. at 893. See Haekyoung Suh, Note, “The Need for Con- sistency in Interpreting the Related Services Provision Under the Individuals With Disabilities Education Act,” 48 Rutgers L.J. 1321, 1325-26 (1996) (discussing the two sides to Tatro). Yet even here, the Court noted in the same sentence that physician services are “far more expensive” than school nursing services. /d. at 893. That was true when considering the time of a school nurse to administer a simple procedure once a day. Continuous, one-to-one nursing services of the sort at issue here are in fact much more expensive than an office visit to a physician. Significantly, in Tatro the parents, not the school dis- trict, were advocating a flexible approach. The school dis- trict argued for a formalistic interpretation of the IDEA that characterized CIC as a medical service because it had to be prescribed by a physician, even though it only needed to be administered once a day. C. Lower Court Decisions on the “Medical Services” Exclusion, Until This Case, Have Almost Without Exception Favored a Practical Approach. Until the decisions in this case, almost all lower courts, as well as state administrative hearing officers, had read Tatro as endorsing a pragmatic and balanced definition of “medical services.” Indeed, at least five previously re- ported federal court decisions concluded that continuous 31 one-on-one nursing services were an excluded “medical service” under the IDEA. This suggests that this opinion in Tatro clearly favored such an approach, and that such an approach makes sense given the intensive nature of the services involved. In Clovis Unified School Dist. v. California Office of Admin. Hearings, 903 F.2d 635, 644 (9th Cir. 1990), the Ninth Circuit addressed whether a child’s residential placement was an excluded “medical service” and con- cluded that it was. As the court stated: [Tatro] held only that services which must be pro- vided by a licensed physician, other than those which are diagnostic or evaluative, are excluded and that school nursing services of a simple nature are not excluded. In reaching this decision the Court con- sidered the extent and nature of the services per- formed, not solely the status of the person performing the services. The Ninth Circuit added, “[I]t would do havoc to the structure of the Act to exclude only the services of licensed physicians . . ., and to require the school district to pay for all other services.” Jd. The court also rejected a rule that would require school districts to pay for all services that are “supportive of a handicapped child’s education.” I/d. at 643 (emphasis added). The court noted that this test was “far too inclusive . . . in light of the Act’s explicit exclusion of medical services,” because many medical treatments are “supportive” of education. /d. The Sixth Circuit read Tatro similarly in Neely v. Rutherford County School, 68 F.3d 965 (6th Cir. 1995), cert. denied, 517 U.S. 1134 (1996), a case involving continuous one-on-one nursing services. There the court held that the tracheostomy suctioning and potential ambu bagging services required by Samantha Neely were ex- cluded “medical services.” Samantha had a rare condition which required her to breathe through a tracheostomy tube. The tube had to be suctioned and, if her breathing 32 stopped, artificial ventilation with an ambu bag would have to be provided. Given that a nurse or medical at- tendant “would have had to devote virtually all of his or her attention” to Samantha, and that “such care was necessary in order to protect” the child’s life, the court held that the services her parents sought were “medical’ and not “related.” Id. at 971-973. ; In discussing this Court's Tatro decision, the Sixth Cir- cuit noted that it was “subject to several interpretations.” Id. at 970. The Sixth Circuit concluded that “the better interpretation of Tatro” is that a school district is not required to provide every service that is “medical in na- ture,” but rather that the burden on the school district must be considered along with the nature and extent of the services. /d. at 971. Likewise, in Detsel v. Board of Educ. of the Auburn Enlarged City School Dist., 820 F.2d 587 (2nd Cir. 1987) (per curiam), aff'g 637 F. The court approved the district court’s opinion which “gave proper effect to the statuory scheme in balancing the in- terests of the parties.” Jd. at 588. The district court had found that the “extensive, therapeutic health services sought by the plaintiff on behalf of her daughter more closely resemble the medical services specifically excluded by § 1401(17) of the {IDEA].” 637 F. Supp. at 1027. As the district court explained: [T]}he Tatro decision does not require the provision of all health services, regardless of their magnitude, if performed by one other than a physician. The Su- preme Court held only that school nursing services of a simple nature are not excludable as therapeutic “medical services.” [{/d.] 33 Most recently, in Fulginiti v. Roxbury Tp. THE NATURE AND EXTENT OF THE SERVICES GARRET REQUIRES, ALONG WITH THE BURDEN ON THE DISTRICT, DEMONSTRATE THAT THE SERVICES HERE CONSTITUTE EXCLUDED MEDICAL SERVICES. Instead of mechanically following a bright-line physi- cian/nonphysician test, courts should consider several fac- tors in determining whether the services are excluded medi- cal services or included school health services. These fac- tors include the nature and complexity of the care, whether the care is continuous or intermittent, whether existing school health personnel may provide the services, the cost of the services, and the potential repercussions if the services are not properly performed. These can be broadly 35 summarized as the nature and extent of the care and the burden on the district. This individualized consideration of needed services for each child fits within the general mandate of the Act that programs for disabled children be developed on an in- dividualized basis. The Act requires that there be individ- ualized consideration of each student’s disability, needs, goals and objectives, along with individualized determina- tion of necessary educational, support and related services. 20 U.S.C. § 1401(a)(20). Throughout the Act, there is an emphasis upon recognizing each child’s unique needs and necessary services, by using the expertise of the mem- bers of the individualized education program (“IEP”) team. The IEP team in Cedar Rapids determined that Garret required care that was beyond the scope of services which could be provided by the Cedar Rapids school nurses. EX 91 at 364-378; J.A. at 14-16. The team made a de- tailed listing of Garret’s health care needs, of the specific interventions required, and of the frequency, and then determined that Garret needed “continuous care by an appropriately licensed practitioner” because Garret “re- quires continuous monitoring and assessment: highly technical and extensive nature of care, life-threatening prospect of inadequate care, mastery of numerous com- petencies.” EX 91 at 374. All of the participating mem- bers of the team, including two school nurses and the school nurse manager, agreed with this assessment, with the exception of Garret’s mother, Charlene F. In fact, if this Court deter- mines that continuous, one-on-one nursing or other trained attendant services which cannot be performed by a school nurse with other responsibilities are inherently burden- some to a district, the test becomes merely a factual find- ing.
