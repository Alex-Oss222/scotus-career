STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


The Plaintiffs/Appellants in these three consolidated 
actions are the wives and minor children of servicemen 
who have suffered severe birth defects as a result of ex- 
posure, without warning, to, drugs, pesticides, and unrea- 
sonably dangerous and toxic products employed by the 
Defendant United States of America during the course of 
the children’s fathers’ military service. The birth defects 
are extremely severe and rare (one occurrence per 26,000 
births, according to the Encyclopedia of Birth Defects, 
1992 Ed.). Two of the minor Plaintiffs suffer from 
Goldenhars Syndrome, which includes asymmetric devel- 
opment of the face and body, imperfect ear development, 
asymmetric body development, internal fistulas, and nu- 
merous other problems. 


The Complaints allege that the Defendant United States 
negligently exposed the Plaintiff mothers and children to 
the hazardous combination of products without warning, 
as well as exposing the father serving in the military dur- 
ing the Gulf War. Hence, the Complaints squarely allege 
direct injury to the non-service Plaintiffs as a result of the 
Defendant’s conduct. 


All of the appellants submitted their claims under the 
Federal Tort Claims Act (FTCA), 28 U.S.C. § 1346(b). 
2671-2680, to the Office of the Judge Advocate General 
at Fort Meade, Maryland. All of their claims were de- 
nied in February, 1996. Pet. App. C, 34-35a (the claim 
denial letter was virtually identical for all Petitioners, Pet. 
App. C is applicable to all). The Petitioners filed their 
Complaints in the United States Court for the District of 
Maryland in August, 1966 pursuant to the FTCA, 
§ 1346(b) and 10 U.S.C. § 2733 (Military Claims Act). 


The Complaints allege that the wives and children suf- 
fered injuries in the form of birth defects caused by their 
exposure to hazardous drugs, inoculations, pesticides and 
other toxic substances employed by the Respondent Gov-

ernment during the term of the fathers’ military services 
at the time of the Persian Gulf War. The claims were 
consolidated before Judge Joseph H. Young, Senior 
District Judge. Prior to any discovery, the Government 
moved to dismiss the Complaints for lack of subject mat- 
ter jurisdiction under the FTCA as arising out of an 
injury to service members incident to military service, as 
protected discretionary acts, as combatant activities and 
as barred because the claim arose in a foreign country. 


The Petitioners opposed these contentions on the 
grounds that they alleged, as civilians, direct injury to 
themselves arising out of the conduct of the Defendant 
United States and that such claims are cognizable under 
the FTCA, notwithstanding the doctrine of Feres v. 
United States, 340 U.S. 135 (1940), which provides for 
the Government’s sovereign immunity for injuries to serv- 
icemen arising out of activity incident to service. They 
also argued that the discretionary function exception is 
not applicable where negligence occurred at the non-policy 
operational level, in implementation of high level policy 
decisions, and where there is no room for independent 
judgment due to an already established policy, such as 
safety. The Petitioners urged that it is premature prior 
to discovery to determine where, by whom and under 
what constraints such decisions as caused the Plainitffs’ 
injuries were made. 


The trial court granted the Government’s Motion to 
Dismiss on the grounds that the court lacked subject mat- 
ter jurisdiction, Pet. App. B, 3la, Minns v. United States, 
974 F. Supp. 500. The Court of Appeals for the Fourth 
Circuit affirmed his decision on substantially the same 
grounds. Pet. App. A, 2a-16a, Minns v. United States, 
155 F.3d 445 (4th Cir. 1998). For the first time, the 
Fourth Circuit adopted the “genesis” test for evaluating 
that the Feres doctrine bars such derivative genetic injury 
claims. The panel held that even if the wives and children 
did suffer direct injury, their injuries had their “genesis”

REASONS FOR ALLOWANCE OF THE WRIT 


The Petitioners believe that the United States Court of 
Appeals has decided an important question of federal 
law that has not been, but should be settled by this Court. 
The issue whether the spouses and children of Gulf War, 
indeed all military personnel, are barred as a matter of 
law from any recovery for injuries they suffered as a result 
of the government’s culpable conduct is important for 
many reasons. 


First, it is fundamentally unfair that only these, of all 
citizens, should be deprived of any recourse for such Sig- 
nificant, indeed catastrophic injuries. Second, the magni- 
tude of the problem is extensive, potentially affecting 
thousands of spouses and children for their entire lives 
at a cost of millions of dollars. Third, unless fully and 
finally resolved by the Court, these identical issues will 
be raised again and again in the courts below, and by 
petition to this Court. See Clark v. United States, 116 
F.3d 476 (Sth Cir. 1997), aff'g 974 F. Supp. 895 (E.D. 
Tex. 1996), cert. denied, 118 S. Ct. 625 (1997). The 
allegations of Clark, as evidenced by the rather perfunc- 
tory analysis of the issues by the District Judge in Texas, 
were more narrow and limited than those raised in these 
Complaints, and the arguments raised, as addressed by 
that court, apparently were more limited than here. Com- 
pare the detailed analysis of the Maryland District Judge 
and the panel of the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals 
(Pet. App. A and B) with the short shrift given to Clark’s 
arguments by the Texas District Court, 974 F. Supp. 895, 
and the affirmation without comment of the Fifth Circuit 
Court of Appeals. This Court is the appropriate forum 
for defining the scope and limits of the Feres doctrine 
under circumstances such as these and for providing 
appropriate guidance as to the factors to be weighed, and 
the relative significance of each such factor, particularly 
the absence of any remedy. 


8 


Finally, the Court should note that this problem of 
birth defects related to the Gulf War has received wide- 
spread publicity, and has caused great concern, and even 
some unrest, among the general public, and certainly 
those affected by this misfortune. The absence of any 
recourse can only contribute to this ongoing sense of 
disaffection. 


I. FERES DOCTRINE 


The FTCA provides, in general, that the United States 
shall be liable for tort claims in a manner similar to that 
of a “private individual under like circumstances,” 28 
U.S.C. § 2674. Nonetheless, this overbroad construction 
of the Feres doctrine places these Petitioners in the anom- 
alous position of having no remedy or recourse because 
they have the misfortune to be the wives and children of 
military personnel. Even the military member of the 
family is entitled to some form of statutory compensa- 
tion for any injury he might sustain in the line of duty, 
even if he cannot sue the government in tort. It is incom- 
prehensible that virtually the only class of wrongfully 
damaged individuals in this society denied any right to 
seek damages for their injuries is comprised of those who 
suffer most—and deserve more-—the spouses and deformed 
minor children of service personnel injured as a conse- 
quence of that service. 


The courts below erred in holding that the Feres doc- 
trine bars claims by civilians, without other remedy, for 
their own direct injuries arising out of their own direct 
exposure to toxic agents, even if caused by the culpable 
conduct of the Government. The appropriate standard 
for determining whether a claim is barred under Feres 
is a three-prong balancing test. Feres, 340 U.S. at 143. 
The balance weighs heavily for the Petitioners in that: 
(1) they have no other remedies; (2) they have no dis- 
tinctive relationship with the United States Government; 
and (3) the effect upon military discipline and morale 


9 


would be minimal at worst, particularly with respect to 
post-war warnings. 


It is clear that under Feres and its progeny, e.g., Stencel 
Aero Engineering Corp. v. United States, 431 US. 
666 (1977), that the civilian family member of the serv- 
ice person may not maintain a claim for direct injuries 
to the service person incident to that service, e.g., De Font 
v. United States, 453 F.2d 1239 (1st Cir.), cert. denied, 
407 U.S. 910 (1972) (denying claim of wife for wrong- 
ful death of husband where husband would have been 
barred by Feres had he survived). However, it is equally 
clear that Feres does not bar the claims of civilian Plain- 
tiffs for their own independent injuries, even where mili- 
tary family members would be barred by Feres from pur- 
suing their own claims arising out of the same incident, 
e.g. Orken v. United States, 239 F.2d 850 (6th Cir. 
1956). In Orken the court permitted claims of civilian 
family members of an officer killed when an Air Force 
plane crashed into their living quarters, while barring 
the related claim of the officer himself even though it 
arose out of the same incident. Indeed, in Orken, the 
civilian and military injuries were similar and arose out 
of a common occurrence, with the only significant dis- 
_ tinction the military or civilian status of the injured parties. 


The Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit recog- 
nized this latter principle in Romero v. U.S., 954 F.2d 
223 (4th Cir. 1992), proceedings after remand, 806 
F. Supp. 569 (E.D. Va. 1992), aff'd without published 
opinion, 2 F.3d 1149 (4th Cir. 1993). There, the Court 
held that the claims of negligent prenatal care adminis- 
tered to the pregnant active duty military mother by Gov- 
ernment medical employees was not barred by Feres 
because “we believe the precedent governing injuries by 
the military to civilians applies here. The civilian child 
was injured, not a military patient,” 954 F.2d 223. Sim- 
ilar results were reached in other cases involving injury to 
a military child in the medical malpractice context. Del 


10 


Rio v. United States, 833 F.2d 282 (11th Cir. 1987); 
Atkinson v. United States, 825 F.2d 202 (9th Cir. 1987), 
cert. denied, 485 U.S. 987 (1988). 


The Del Rio case is particularly instructive because in 
that case the pregnant mother was also injured in the 
course of the negligent treatment that led to the loss of 
her twin children. The claims of the children were based 
upon the allegations that the military mother did not 
receive proper prenatal treatment due to a pre-existing 
condition. Nonetheless, although her claims were barred, 
those of her children were not. The Fourth Circuit Panel 
in Romero expressly found the reasoning of the Ninth 
Circuit in Atkinson “persuasive when the Plaintiff is a 
civilian child.” 954 F.2d 227 n.6. Here, both the Peti- 
tioner mothers and children are civilians, and their claims 
must be allowed to proceed. 


II. DISCRETIONARY FUNCTION EXCEPTION 


The discretionary function exception, 28 U.S.C. § 2680(a) 
is also not applicable where the Appellants have alleged 
that their injuries occurred through non-conforming 
batches of inoculant, the failure of the Government to 
follow its own policies and standards with respect to 
warnings and the use of pesticides, and where pesticides 
were sprayed in disregard of directions and instructions 
for safe use. See Berkovitz v. United States, 486 U.S. 
531 (1988). The appeals court below concluded that: 


Even if the military used investigational, worn out or 
defective drugs that had been stored too long, some- 
one in the military nevertheless made the decision 
to use them, and that decision, with all its alleged 
flaws, amounted to a judgment that the risk of using 
these drugs was less than the risk of exposing un- 
protected soldiers to potential biological and chemical 
attack. 


Pet. App. A, 13a. 


il 


That conclusion, without facts and context, is too ex- 
treme. For example, in the event that there were in effect 
policies and procedures against, or to prevent such admin- 
istration and use of defective drugs, or the reckless admin- 
istration of pesticides, and the innocent wives and children 
of the exposed fathers were injured as a result of pure 
negligence or recklessness in violation of those policies 
and procedures, without any knowledge or consideration 
of the risks and potential benefits of such use, there must 
be a judicial remedy. 


These allegations, if established, would place this case 
within the parameters of Dube v. Pittsburgh Corning, 
870 F.2d 790, 799 (ist Cir. 1989) in which the Navy’s 
negligent failure to warn of asbestos hazards was not a 
discretionary policy decision protected by the discretion- 
ary function exception. The Dube case would particularly 
apply were the plaintiffs to establish that the Department 
of Defense had established a policy, by its agreement 
with the FDA, to warn of the risks of its use of investiga- 
tive drugs, and that lower level staff, who did not have 
discretion in the matter, breached that policy. Similarly, 
in Griffin v. United States, 500 F.2d 1059 at 1066 (3rd 
Cir. 1974) the court held that: 


Where the conduct of Government employees in im- 
plementing agency scientific evaluation and not the 
formulation of policy, we do not believe that the con- 
duct is immunized from judicial review as a ‘discre- 
tionary function’. 

The purchase and use of immunizing agents and pesti- 
cides without testing and quality control, and improper 
storage, implicates this principle of safety, as does the 
manner and use of pesticides without following the manu- 
facturers’ directions. See Mover v. Martin Marietta Corp., 
481 F.2d 585 (Sth Cir. 1973) (refusing to apply discre- 
tionary exception to procurement decision regarding 
faulty ejection seat mechanism in B-57, but not to deci- 
sion to procure B-57).
