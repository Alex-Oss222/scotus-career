

D. The Costs of Providing Continuous One-On-One 
Nursing Services to Garret Are Significantly Be- 
yond the Educational Resources the District Is 
Required to Expend. 


Congress has also recognized that the federal financial 
contribution to the education of disabled students is lim- 
ited, and therefore the burdens imposed on local school 
districts must have some limits as well. In 1994, the fed- 
eral government's share of funding for special education 
services was only $413 for each child served. Eighteenth 
Annual Report to Congress on the Implementation of the 
IDEA, U.S. Department of Education (1996). In Iowa. 
none of this funding goes directly to local school districts. 


At the time of the 1994 administrative hearing in this 
case, the District received approximately $12,780 in state 
and local income and property taxes on account of Gar- 
ret’s enrollment, an amount that is supposed to provide 
sufficient resources for educational and related services 
for someone of Garret’s level of disability. TR at 513- 
514. The District expends funds not only for the regular 
educational and activity programs for Garret, but also for 
his educational associate, his special transportation, and 
his computer equipment. Pet. App. 34a-36a. Thus. even 
without taking into account Garret’s need for continuous 
nursing services, the funding for his needs is inadequate. 


20 


Hiring a nurse to care for Garret from the time he left 
home on the bus until he returned home would have cost 
between $28,630 and $39,810. J.A. at 15. There was no 
dispute in the record that nurses hired in Cedar Rapids are 
registered nurses carrying a bachelor of science degree. 
J.A. at 17. The variation in estimates was based upon 
whether a nurse with no experience could be hired or a 
typical nurse employed in the District with ten years ex- 
perience. This cost estimate was consistent with cost esti- 
mates of one-on-one nursing services in other cases where 
courts have declined to impose the costs on the school dis- 
trict. In Ellison v. Board of Educ. of Three Villages Cen- 
tral School Dist., 597 N.Y.S. 2d 483 (App. Div. 1993), it 
was estimated that the cost of one-on-one nursing would 
be $25,000 to $40,000. In Granite School Dist. v. Shan- 
non M., 787 F. Supp. 1020 (D. Utah 1992), it was esti- 
mated to be approximately $30,000, and in Fulginiti v. 
Roxbury Tp. Public Schools, 921 F. Supp. 1320 (D.N.J. 
1996), aff'd, without published opinion, 116 F.3d 468 
(3rd Cir. 1997), the costs were estimated to be $56,000. 


If Congress had intended that school districts were to 
provide for costly private nursing services, such as Garret 
requires, it would have specifically stated so in its defi- 
nition of “related services,” just as it did for other poten- 
tially expensive services, such as physical and occupational 
therapy. Instead, Congress chose to specifically exclude 
“medical services” unless for diagnostic or evaluation pur- 
poses. If public school entities are to use their limited 
resources to fund such expensive individualized nursing 
services, then that should be required by specific Congres- 
sional action rather than by a narrow interpretation of 
the term “medical.” This Court has repeatedly stated that 
if Congress desires to condition states’ receipt of federal 
funds, it must do so unambiguously. Pennhurst State 
School and Hospital v. Halderman, 451 U.S. 1, 17 (1981); 
South Dakota v. Dole, 483 U.S. 203, 207 (1987); New 
York v. United States, 505 U.S. 144, 172 (1992). See

Gebser v. Lago Vista Independent School Dist., §. Ct. No. 
96-1866 (June 22, 1998), slip op. at 10-12 (Title IX 
scheme, imposing conditions on receipt of federal funds, 
requires careful scrutiny to ensure recipients had clear 
notice of nature and scope of conditions). Accord Com- 
monwealth of Virginia, Dept. of Ed. v. Riley, 106 F.3d 
559, 560-61 (4th Cir. 1997) (applying this principle to 
the IDEA). Indeed, in Board of Education of Hendrick 
Hudson Central Sci:ool Dist. v. Rowley, 458 U.S. 176, 
190 n.11 (1982), discussed below, this Court described 
this proposition as “fundamental.” This proposition should 
be especially true when, as here, Congress is paying only 
about seven percent of the costs of the program. 


Il. THE DEPARTMENT OF EDUCATION’S REGU- 
LATIONS AND INTERPRETATIONS DO NOT SUP- 
PORT AN UNDULY RESTRICTIVE DEFINITION 
OF THE MEDICAL SERVICES EXCLUSION. 


The Department of Education regulations implementing 
the IDEA do not address the question at issue in this case: 
what medical services are excluded from a schcol district's 
responsibility under 20 U.S.C. § 1401(a)(17). The regu- 
lations do define covered medical services for which a dis- 
trict is responsible, see 34 C.F.R. § 300.16(b)(4), but 
that is a different question. There is no basis for inferring 
that it is the Department’s considered view that any type 
of service that does not fit within its definition of a 
covered medical service is not a medical service at all, 
and therefore not subject to the medical services exclusion. 
In fact, as detailed below, the available evidence confirms 
that such an inference is not the Department’s position. 


The Department’s regulations, which date back to 1977 
with relatively minor modification, define the term “related 
services” as follows: 


The term “related services” means transportation and 
such developmental, corrective, and other supportive 
services aS are required to assist a child with a dis-

ability to benefit from special education, and includes 
speech pathology and audiology, psychological serv- 
ices, physical and occupational therapy, recreation, 
including therapeutic recreation, early identification 
and assessment of disabilities in children, counseling 
services, including rehabilitation counseling, and med- 
ical services for diagnostic or evaluation purposes. 
The term also includes school health services, social 
work services in schools, and parent counseling and 
training. [34 C.F.R. § 300.16(a)] 


“School health services” are separately defined in 
§ 300.16(b) (11) as “services provided by a qualified 
school nurse or other qualified person.” Lastly, 34 C.F.R. 
§ 300.16(b)(4) defines covered “medical services” as 
“services provided by a licensed physician to determine a 
child’s medically related disability that results in the child's 
need for special education and related services.” There is 
no definition of excluded medical services.® 


Thus, the regulations leave the critical question in this 
case—the definition of excluded medical services—unan- 
swered. As the Solicitor General acknowledged in the 
brief filed at the petition stage, “the regulations expressly 
define only those medical services that are owed to handi- 
capped children. . . .”. SG Br. 3 n.2 (emphasis in orig- 
inal). Simply because the Department of Education has 
concluded that the covered medical services of diagnosis 
and evaluation are to be provided by a physician does not 
mean that the only excluded medical services are those 
similarly provided by a physician. It may make sense, in 
considering the covered services of diagnosis and evalua- 
tion, to conclude that such services should be provided by
