## Event

Minnesota v. Carter and Minnesota v. Johns, No. 97-1147. Merits decision, December 1, 1998. Argued October 6, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Minnesota, reviewing Carter, 569 N.W.2d 169, and Johns, 569 N.W.2d 180. The state opinions issued September 11, 1997; judgment entry was September 29 for Carter and October 1 for Johns. The petition was filed December 29, 1997 and granted March 9, 1998.

Both respondents were convicted after the trial court rejected their suppression claims. Carter's intermediate court rejected personal interest without deciding observation; Johns's assumed an interest and sustained the observation. The Minnesota Supreme Court reversed both judgments on the residential-search issue, and Johns's companion opinion adopts Carter's reasoning. The questions are whether each respondent had his own legitimate Fourth Amendment interest in the apartment and whether the officer's actual unaided observation through its drawn blinds was a search. The State's descriptions of exclusive illegal purpose and a public, common, noncurtilage vantage are contested premises, not additional findings.

The state reasoning expressly applies federal precedent without a plain independently sufficient state ground; section 1257 review is available under Long as retained in Evans. No independent Minnesota disposition is found. Thompson's separate guilty plea, Carter's sentencing branch and Johns's jury branch are outside these federal questions.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The two reviewed judgments are vacated to the extent they dispose of the federal suppression claims through the observation determination, and the cases are remanded for lawful further proceedings. Each respondent's personal Fourth Amendment interest is recognized. The Court adopts no conclusive search application.

## Holdings

### I. Each respondent has a personal interest in the shared domestic privacy

**Operative rule:** Invited occupants who use a resident's deliberately screened home for a private activity possess their own Fourth Amendment interest against police acquisition of interior information; brief duration, commercial purpose and illegality, separately or together, do not automatically defeat that interest. Carter and Johns each have that interest on the actual host-authorized joint occupancy here; permission merely to enter, another person's rights or a desire to conceal crime alone does not establish it.

**Authority:** Part I of Breyer's opinion has five direct joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The proposition controls despite their disagreement over the ultimate disposition. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas do not join it.

**Controlling explanation:** Rakas requires a person's own protected interest and rejects a claim derived solely from someone else's rights. Jones, as preserved by Rakas, shows that ownership is unnecessary. Olson recognizes privacy shared with a guest. Fields applies those principles both to independent control and, separately, to expressly permitted sharing of domestic privacy without an overnight arrangement. Its protection of Crawley's recurring closed-room use did not make recurrence indispensable to every shared-privacy claim.

The resident deliberately admitted Carter and Johns to the screened living interior, joined their private undertaking and allowed that use for approximately two and one-half hours. Their interest rests on that actual sharing, not her title alone. The State stresses the brief compensated transaction and absence of an established prior relationship or continuing control. Those facts bear on the relationship, but do not erase the personally shared private occupancy actually shown. Payment informs the arrangement without turning the domestic interior into a public business area. Criminal activity neither supplies the interest nor forfeits one otherwise established.

Neither respondent needs an accepted key finding for this application. Carter's disputed proffer is not adjudicated and is not attributed to Johns. The Court does not protect every casual visitor, unauthorized occupant, person permitted merely to enter, or activity plainly exposed to ordinary public observation. Recognition of personal privacy leaves the distinct question whether the officer's particular observation invaded it.

**Precedent treatment:** Rakas retains the personal-right requirement and exclusion of mere presence. Jones remains relevant to protected use without ownership. Olson's shared-home principle is applied beyond its overnight facts through Fields. Fields's relationship inquiry extends beyond Crawley's recurring use to the particular host-authorized sharing here; its independent-control branch and rejection of automatic protection for every visitor remain intact.

### II. Conditional suppression rules remain distinct from the observation question

**Operative rule:** If an antecedent intrusion is held unlawful in properly available further proceedings, a later warrant is an independent source only when both the decision to seek it and its issuance are genuinely independent of the illegality. A warrant's arrival does not retroactively authorize the earlier intrusion, and consent, attenuation, inevitable discovery and other asserted grounds require their own factual and procedural predicates; neither automatic exclusion nor automatic admission follows.

**Authority:** Part III of Breyer's opinion has all eight Associate Justices' direct joins. Stone-Zsela does not join this additional remedial rationale. These conditional rules govern the remand if their triggering conditions arise; they do not find that this observation was unlawful or that any particular later item must be excluded.

**Controlling explanation:** Fields and Murray require genuine independence of both the decision to seek a warrant and its issuance. Karo preserves a supported untainted-warrant ground; Pinson and Myers separate correction of an antecedent search premise from final exclusion and from revival of unavailable arguments. The trial memorandum here treated the observation as central to the stop and warrants. Its rejection of the particular Franks attack on descriptions of the investigation and surveillance does not establish an independent source.

The stop, door opening, handgun discovery, arrests, apartment and vehicle warrants, physical evidence and statements require their actual personal-right and causal inquiries. The prosecutor's concession that an invalid arrest would bring down statements and physical evidence retains that condition. It does not concede that every defect in the observation automatically excludes every later item for both respondents. The ninety-minute surveillance gap is not erased.

Consent requires actual authority, scope, voluntariness and any applicable taint analysis. Leon affords no protection for knowing or reckless deception, abandoned judicial neutrality, an affidavit wholly lacking reasonable probable-cause indicia or a facially deficient warrant; the conduct of obtaining and supplying officers and proper execution scope matter. Evans remains confined to qualifying court-clerical error with objectively reasonable reliance, not a general exception for deliberate police investigation. Attenuation and inevitable discovery need their actual predicates. Ordinary preservation rules remain operative, and no new statement, Miranda or Franks claim is created.

The Court orders neither an evidence-by-evidence exclusion nor unconditional admission or reinstatement. The remaining claims may be resolved only to the extent properly available under ordinary procedure.

**Precedent treatment:** Fields and Murray's genuine-independence rule is applied without expansion. Karo's supported untainted-warrant possibility remains available. Pinson and Myers retain the distinction between antecedent error and final suppression. Leon and Evans retain their stated institutional, factual and procedural limits; Franks's false-affidavit inquiry does not substitute for independent source.

### III. The judgment does not establish a controlling search application

**Judgment without controlling search rationale:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer vacate the reviewed judgments to the extent they dispose of the federal suppression claims through the observation determination and remand for lawful further proceedings. O'Connor would require renewed actual-vantage analysis. The other four accept the judgment only on that point and would conclusively find no search on their qualified actual-view ground. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg would affirm because they find a search on the supported minimum.

The five-Justice personal-interest rule and eight-Associate conditional remedial rules control their subjects. No majority decides whether this observation was a search. O'Connor's reservation and the four-Justice conclusive no-search rule are not combined under Marks: leaving the legal application open does not supply a logical subset of a rule conclusively resolving it. The five votes for the operative judgment do not create a fifth vote for no search or for O'Connor's rationale.

The mandate removes the adverse federal suppression judgments and permits ordinary lawful further proceedings. It neither declares the record insufficient nor holds that further actual-vantage analysis is necessary. If a later disposition depends on rejecting an appellate factual characterization, the governing factual-review and preservation objections must be addressed. If a constitutional answer instead rests on an express assumption of the disputed additions, it must explain why the conclusion follows on that assumption. These conditional source safeguards create no new hearing, photographic reconstruction, discovery, burden shift or factual finding. Neither an actual search nor actual nonsearch application is imposed as Court law.

## Precedent Treatment

- Rakas v. Illinois: retains the requirement that each respondent establish his own protected relationship; neither mere presence nor Thompson's rights alone suffices.
- Jones v. United States: applies the surviving relationship-based privacy principle without requiring ownership or reviving automatic standing.
- Minnesota v. Olson: applies shared residential privacy beyond its overnight facts through the specific relationship established here.
- United States v. Fields / Crawley: extends the shared-privacy application beyond recurring closed-room use to this actual host-authorized private occupancy; its independent-control branch and exclusion of mere presence remain. Its limited-sharing and imperfect-screening rules continue, without importing either nonmajority actual-search application.
- Murray v. United States: applies genuine independence of both seeking and issuing a later warrant if antecedent illegality is established.
- United States v. Karo: preserves a supported untainted-warrant basis without finding one here.
- United States v. Pinson and United States v. Myers: retain protection of otherwise concealed domestic information and the separation between an antecedent search ruling, preservation and final suppression. Their technological holdings do not decide this unaided observation.
- United States v. Leon: retains the four stated limits on objective warrant reliance, including the relevant obtaining/supplying officers and proper execution scope.
- Arizona v. Evans: retains Long-based review and its bounded qualifying court-clerical-error rule; neither an independent state ground nor a police-investigation reliance exception is created.
- Franks v. Delaware: remains the separate false-affidavit inquiry. Rejection of the particular misstatement attack does not establish genuine independent source.
- Ornelas v. United States: preserves the existing distinction between historical facts and ultimate legal review within its decided scope; this mandate creates no new review standard or permission to replace facts. The competing uses of Ciraolo, Riley and Dunn to assess the actual vantage remain separate applications, not a new Court conclusion about this view or every shared yard.

## Law After Decision

Actual host-authorized sharing of a screened domestic interior can establish each invited occupant's personal Fourth Amendment interest without an overnight arrangement, prior recurring visits or accepted independent-control evidence. Brief duration, commercial purpose and illegality do not automatically defeat that sharing. Mere permission to enter, unauthorized presence, borrowed rights and ordinary public exposure remain outside the rule.

The Court has not held this observation to be either a search or no search. The four-Justice natural-view application, O'Connor's renewed-analysis rationale and the four-Justice affirmative-search application remain distinct. The operative judgment controls the parties, while the directly supported privacy and conditional-remedy rules govern their subjects. Existing Fields rules against automatic publication from limited sharing and automatic cure from a later warrant remain in force. No new general curtilage rule, visibility distance, duration threshold, factual-review burden or unaided-observation immunity is adopted.

## Separate Writings

**Breyer, Part II, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** These four would conclusively hold that this observation was not a search. The trial findings and testimony establish an unenclosed area actually used by residents and nonresidents, including access beside the window for bicycles, and a view obtainable by natural sight without touching the window, lighting it or using equipment. The initial position near the projecting brickwork already disclosed the table and packaging; it was a window-side position, not a proven sidewalk view. The later move gave a closer view of the same activity.

They assume in respondents' favor the disputed climbing and crouching. Crossing shrubs not shown to enclose the space and adjusting posture do not, by themselves, turn the actual natural view from this ordinarily used position into a search. The approximate 12–18-inch finding is no constitutional minimum, and uncertain gap geometry, the exact crossing of one entrance walk and the asserted failure to seek rehearing are unnecessary to this answer. They do not find the disputed acts occurred or discredit the appellate account through photographs.

Fields's limited-sharing rule remains controlling: cotenant visibility alone is not publication. Their answer rests on the fuller actual-use record, including ordinary nonresident use, and the natural view. Pinson and Myers protect otherwise concealed information and do not require disregarding what this opening actually exposed. Drawn blinds, sustained proximity, the nonordinary entrance route and the disputed bodily movements are serious contrary facts, but the four regard the actual access and unenhanced view as sufficient together. Their rationale would not resolve opening or moving blinds, crossing a protected enclosure, intruding into the dwelling or using equipment to obtain otherwise unavailable information. It creates no common-yard immunity, fixed distance or time rule, or exterior-location exception.

They accept vacatur and ordinary remand as sufficient removal of the adverse judgments while publishing that conclusive view. They do not join O'Connor's reason for further actual-vantage analysis. They accept that the absence of a Court search holding leaves lawful further consideration available; they order neither automatic admission nor unconditional conviction reinstatement. Breyer also joins the Court's affirmative personal-interest holding. The other three retain the contrary privacy position expressed in O'Connor's Part A.

**O'Connor, concurring in the judgment and dissenting in part.** In Part A, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, O'Connor would reject the accepted invitation-duration-task basis for personal privacy. Fields protects actual independent control and, separately, developed recurring residential sharing. Permission for this brief compensated task does not establish either relationship in their view. Their conclusion rests on the supported combination, not a rule that illegal activity forfeits rights, that commerce is inherently unprotected, that only an owner has privacy or that an overnight visit is indispensable. A temporary rental carrying control or genuinely shared residential or social relationship could differ.

Carter's counsel's key affidavit cannot be treated as nonexistent or as an accepted control finding. A properly usable finding of authorized independent access could change the application of Fields's control branch. Its procedural status, weight and actual permission implications are not decided; no new hearing or revived waiver is ordered. Johns has no corresponding proffer. Breyer does not join this personal-interest disagreement.

In Part B, O'Connor alone would resolve the actual-view question through renewed source-accurate analysis once the Court recognizes personal privacy. Ordinary access, the standing natural view, bicycle storage, nonresident use and lack of enclosure support the lawful-view route; screening, prolonged proximity and the position outside ordinary entrance passage support the search route. Her Fields plurality and Riley qualification preserve the claimant's burden without equating limited sharing with publication. If the constitutional answer requires rejecting an appellate factual characterization, factual-review and preservation objections must actually be addressed. If the same answer follows while assuming the disputed additions, that ground can be stated instead. Ornelas and Fields do not authorize invention of historical facts. She orders no new hearing or photographic reconstruction and imposes no new public-access burden. This is her reason for the judgment, not a Court finding that the record is insufficient or renewed analysis necessary.

**Souter, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Part A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Ginsburg, would affirm both reviewed judgments. Each respondent has his own interest in the deliberately shared domestic privacy. An officer conducts a search by deliberately obtaining screened home-interior information from a residential window-side vantage outside ordinary public observation. The close, sustained examination through drawn blinds from the nonordinary entrance approach satisfies that rule on the trial-level minimum. No finding of climbing, crouching, a state offense or a universal shared-yard curtilage classification is required.

The grass's common uses establish access, not ordinary exposure of this screened interior to the precise examination. The abutment position already required the close window-side approach; neither it nor the informant's unexplained outside view supplies a lawful sidewalk source. Fields rejects equating limited residential sharing or imperfect blinds with publication. Katz protects privacy without physical penetration. Pinson and Myers explain the protection of concealed home information, but their technological holdings did not already decide this naked-eye observation. The contrary actual-use and natural-visibility argument is substantial; these Justices find it insufficient to convert this inspection into ordinary public observation.

The conclusion preserves unaided observation from a street, walkway, common approach, open window or other position genuinely available to ordinary public observation. It requires no fence or posted sign whenever the actual residential vantage is otherwise shown not to be such a position. Neither approximately fifteen minutes nor approximately a foot is a fixed constitutional threshold. The disputed appellate additions are unnecessary to the conclusion, so this ground does not decide the factual-correction and rehearing disputes.

In Part B, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg additionally conclude that no warrant, pre-view probable cause, consent or supported exception justified the search. An investigatory tip and modest asserted intrusion supply no reasonable-suspicion exception for protected home information. Stone-Zsela does not join that additional rationale. All four would leave the actual state suppression judgments in force, subject to properly available independent issues; none orders acquittal or a new blanket exclusion of every later item. The three Associates additionally join the Court's conditional remedial rules. The separate writing does not turn the prosecutor's invalid-arrest condition into unconditional exclusion.

## Procedure After Action

The cases return to the Minnesota Supreme Court for ordinary lawful further proceedings on the reviewed federal suppression claims. The adopted personal-interest rule governs both respondents. The mandate permits lawful consideration of observation and remaining properly available grounds without a Court search holding; it does not declare the record insufficient or require renewed analysis as a controlling legal conclusion.

Trial findings, reproduced testimony, appellate characterization and party assertions retain their distinct roles. If a further disposition depends on rejecting an appellate factual characterization, the governing factual-review and preservation objections must be addressed; an answer that instead assumes the disputed additions must explain why its conclusion follows on that assumption. No new hearing, photographic reconstruction, discovery, public-access burden or factual finding is ordered. The subjective-expectation objection is not adjudicated because subjective privacy is assumed. No binding curtilage concession, criminal-purpose forfeiture, state privacy offense or independent state-law judgment is found. A genuinely available independent state ground remains a matter for lawful state proceedings, not an outcome directed here.

The stop, gun discovery, arrests, warrants, physical evidence and statements retain their actual separate issues. The State's lack of probable cause before observation and the prosecutor's conditional invalid-arrest concession are not converted into an all-evidence order. Later warrants are not presumed independent from a viewing the trial court treated as central to their basis. Ordinary preservation rules control any remaining claim, and no waived or unpresented issue is revived automatically. No admission, exclusion, acquittal, new trial, release or unconditional conviction reinstatement is ordered. No further Supreme Court event or retained jurisdiction is set.
