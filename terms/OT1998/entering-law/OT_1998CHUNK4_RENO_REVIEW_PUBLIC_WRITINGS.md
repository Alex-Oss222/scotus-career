#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Thomas, concurs in part and in the judgment. They join the statutory text, structure, and claim-channel analysis. They do not rely on legislative history and do not join the Court's formulation of the separate constitutional objection. In their view, a district court retains §1331 jurisdiction for the limited question whether the exclusion of original review is itself unconstitutional and therefore ineffective. That question cannot be rejected on the merits while every prerequisite to entertaining it is reserved; they find no such obstacle here.

They also decline to assume that compliance causes only insignificant harm. If the operator is right on its underlying claim, entry by union officials and disclosure of business information can have unrecoverable consequences. But that premise still does not require original pre-enforcement district-court review. The operator may comply and obtain review, or refuse and obtain judicial review before noncompliance penalties are imposed in the constitutionally material sense. Ordinary unrecoverable compliance costs therefore do not themselves establish a due-process violation. Ex parte Young addressed a different dilemma: compliance foreclosed review, while resistance exposed the challenger to severe penalties that practically prevented access. That foreclosure is absent here. Their distinct constitutional formulation is noncontrolling; their partial join does not turn it into the Court's seven-Justice explanation or add two votes to it.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Breyer, concurring in part and in the judgment.** He joins Parts II–III but rejects Part I's Rule 16 exclusion. Paragraph (a)(1)(C)'s three disjunctive categories perform different work; its defense-preparation category is not confined to the Government's evidence in chief. Information material to a genuine selective-prosecution defense can therefore fall within the Rule, subject to its independent exclusions and protections. Here the missing relevant comparison defeats materiality, so he agrees that the order and dependent dismissal fail. Kimberlin and Klinger preserve grounded circumstantial proof, but disproportionate access and a less intrusive proposed stage cannot supply an absent predicate. He does not resolve a necessary work-product exception, promise enforceable access to all state records or eliminate a distinct lawful source of discovery.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He joins Part II and would expressly vacate the unsupported-order dismissal, but would remand for a staged, limited inquiry under an attainable, evidence-based threshold. The selected 24-defendant sample does not identify comparably eligible non-Black offenders treated differently, supply the denominator or itself prove discriminatory purpose. Kimberlin forbids categorical direct-proof demands; it does not compel his proposed charging procedure or erase effect, purpose and relevant comparison.

His proposed remand would assess the comparison in its offense, eligibility, place and time context with available purpose evidence. These are reliability considerations, not a mandatory checklist or a demand for every listed form of proof. Reliable jurisdiction-specific data, concrete indications of comparably eligible offenders and a supported reason missing information lies with the prosecutor can justify narrow production. A specifically justified alternative evidentiary basis must be accepted where comparator names cannot reasonably be obtained. If the threshold is met, inquiry would begin with limited comparable data or interrogatories, appropriate redaction, privilege review and protective orders, expanding only if the response warrants it. The existing broad order would not return on this sample alone. Ordinary prosecution would resume unless lawful new proceedings justify another remedy. His proposal establishes neither automatic discovery, racial discrimination nor acquittal, and his directed staged remand is not the Court's mandate.

**Stevens, dissenting.** He would affirm the reviewed judgment. Respondents established neither the selective-prosecution defense nor a right to discovery under Rule 16 or inherent authority. That lack of entitlement does not, in his view, eliminate the district judge's bounded discretion to inquire into a disturbing evidentiary pattern. The defined all-Black sample, Reed's firsthand state/federal experience, the substantial federal/state penalty difference and the judge's consideration of the developed response justified inquiry appreciably short of merits proof. Relayed observations and firsthand experience should not be conflated, and the absence of a Government admissibility objection below does not establish a universal hearsay rule.

Wayte's constitutional limits, Williams's bounded judicial protection and the distinction between failed party entitlement and retained judicial function support his discretionary ground. Wade's screening requirement and the mismatch between general crack cases and these federal charges remain serious limits; they do not make every inquiry an abuse of discretion. He acknowledges that the order may have been broader than necessary, but does not replace his affirmance with a criteria-only remand or unconditional access to protected files. He supplies no representative denominator, merits discrimination finding or shifted ultimate burden, and would not give the Government a new unconditional opportunity to refuse, obtain review and then comply after losing.

### Reno v. Catholic Social Services, Inc., No. 91-1826

Merits disposition, 1993-06-18

Argued 1993-01-11; decided 1993-06-18. On writ of certiorari to Ninth Circuit, 956 F.2d 914: consolidated CSS and LULAC legalization litigation; relief included extensions of the statutory application period.

The question presented is whether §1255a(f) excludes these district-court challenges, which claims are ripe, and whether the undifferentiated application-period extensions can stand.

The INS adopted rules concerning departures from the country and reentry documents in administering the legalization program’s residence and physical-presence requirements. The classes included persons who had not submitted accepted applications during the statutory period. The administrative front-desking practice could prevent an application from being accepted at all, leaving no formal denial or administrative record to review. The present record does not identify particular class members with the precision needed to establish that this practice was applied to them.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas.

Vacated and remanded, 6–3 in the judgment.

On consolidated class relief and application-period extensions, Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas supported the judgment. White, Blackmun, Stevens dissented.

Souter delivered the Opinion of the Court on the review channel, concrete application, and remand, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas. O’Connor concurred only in the judgment: she considered the claims ripe but the extension unjustified. Stevens dissented, joined by White and Blackmun; they considered the policy challenges ripe and would sustain the class relief.

Section1255a(f) channels review of individual legalization determinations but does not categorically eliminate district-court review of a challenge to generally applicable regulations where no individual determination supplies an effective review route. A regulatory label alone establishes neither ripeness nor a right to district-court reconsideration of an individual denial.

Souter’s Opinion of the Court, supported on this holding by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, controls by a majority of 5 Justices.

The Court holds that the object of the challenge matters to the statutory channel. McNary distinguishes a systemic challenge from review of an individual application determination under a related legalization statute. Section1255a(f) likewise does not clearly prohibit every challenge to a regulation. But an applicant whose claim depends on a formal denial ordinarily must follow the limited administrative and judicial path Congress specified, including review connected to a deportation order. A plaintiff cannot avoid that structure merely by calling an individual eligibility dispute a policy challenge. Conversely, refusal to accept papers can prevent an application record and formal denial from ever arising. Reading that refusal as necessarily foreclosing all review would require an indication Congress has not supplied. Michigan Academy’s presumption favors a review-preserving construction in that circumstance; it does not prove the facts of any claimant’s case. The Court leaves eligibility, substantive validity of the challenged rules and the ultimate remedy outside this threshold disposition.

McNary, 498 U.S.479 — systemic-review distinction applied; individual denials remain distinct. Bowen v. Michigan Academy, 476 U.S.667 — presumption of review applied, not converted into a factual burden. Section1255a(f)(2) — late-filing denial bar preserved; no general permission to evade it by never filing.

An actual refusal to accept a timely application under the challenged rule supplies concrete application sufficient for a ripe district-court challenge where front-desking blocks the individual-review route. Mere awareness of an adverse rule is insufficient on this record; further facts may establish that the front-desking policy substantially caused a comparable inability to apply even without a personal front-desk rejection.

Souter’s Opinion of the Court, supported on this holding by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, controls by a majority of 5 Justices.

The Court holds that a benefits regulation does not necessarily injure every person who reads it. Abbott Laboratories asks whether the controversy is fit for decision and what hardship postponement creates. Here some persons might not otherwise qualify or might have used designated assistance that bypassed front-desking. The existing record does not identify particular class members actually refused acceptance with sufficient precision. Those who were refused encountered the regulation directly and lost the administrative record needed for ordinary review. The Government cannot create that absence and then insist on a formal denial as the only judicial doorway. The Court nevertheless does not equate every decision not to apply with a government refusal. Further evidence might show that the policy substantially caused nonapplication in a sufficiently concrete way; that possibility remains open. The district courts must establish the affected claimants and adequate class representation before choosing relief. No present finding establishes legalization eligibility for every class member.

Abbott Laboratories, 387 U.S.136 — fitness and hardship applied to concrete effects. Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation, 497 U.S.871 — concrete application and immediately operative burdens distinguished. McNary, 498 U.S.479 — effective-review concern applied to refused filings.

The decision adds the review-channel distinction and concrete-application requirement with an express indirect-barrier reservation. Vacatur rejects the present undifferentiated relief; no majority decides every equitable-tolling issue or automatically extends the statutory period. Processing an actually timely tender is distinct from extending a deadline.

O’Connor concurs in the judgment. She regards the request to extend an already expired application period as ripe: closure has occurred, and its effect is not contingent. But §1255a(a)(1)(A) requires a timely application. Promulgating an erroneous benefits regulation, without more, is not the affirmative misconduct needed to justify this broad equitable extension. Irwin, Hansen and Pangilinan preserve statutory conditions. She agrees that actual front-desking may support processing a timely application and further inquiry; she does not adopt the Court’s ripeness analysis or a categorical total-review bar.

Stevens, joined by White and Blackmun, dissents. The rules concretely discouraged eligible persons from applying during a finite statutory opportunity. Under Abbott and McNary, the policy challenges and class relief should not depend on proving an individual futile visit to the front desk. They would preserve an effective opportunity to apply while leaving individual eligibility for administrative determination.
