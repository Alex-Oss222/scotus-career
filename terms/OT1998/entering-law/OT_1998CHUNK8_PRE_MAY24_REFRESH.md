# clinton_v_goldsmith_merits_1999-05-17.md

## Event

Clinton v. Goldsmith, No. 98-347. Merits decision, May 17, 1999; argued March 22, 1999. On certiorari to the Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces, 48 M.J. 84. The question is whether the All Writs Act permits that court to enjoin an administrative drop from the Air Force rolls outside its review of court-martial findings and sentence. Goldsmith's sentence imposed confinement and forfeiture of pay, not dismissal; the later drop action was separate.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the judgment granting the drop-from-rolls injunction and vacates that injunction.

## Judgment & Remedy

Reversed; injunction vacated, 9–0. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The injunction must be dissolved. The court-martial findings and sentence remain undisturbed. Goldsmith may pursue any independently available administrative or judicial route, subject to its own jurisdictional and remedial requirements; no entitlement to relief or adequacy determination is entered.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Entire opinion |

## Holdings

#### A writ cannot enlarge military appellate jurisdiction

**Holding and operative rule:** The All Writs Act permits the military appellate court to protect jurisdiction independently conferred over court-martial findings and sentences. It does not authorize an injunction against a separate executive personnel action outside that review merely because the action follows a court-martial conviction.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion, joined by all nine Justices, controls the complete rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a useful remedy cannot supply the jurisdiction it is supposed to protect. Judicial Council v. McBryde establishes that an auxiliary writ must aid existing or potential appellate jurisdiction; Your Home Visiting Nurse Services v. Shalala likewise separates authority to review from the asserted wrong. Here the final sentence imposed confinement and forfeiture. Dropping Goldsmith from the rolls was not part of that sentence and would not amend its findings or punishment. The absence of dismissal from the sentence did not adjudicate an entitlement against every separate personnel action. The constitutional importance of Goldsmith's objections therefore cannot bring this act within CAAF's statutory subject matter. The Court preserves writ authority genuinely needed to protect or enforce a lawful military appellate judgment. It decides neither the removal's constitutional validity nor the medication claim or the availability and adequacy of any particular outside remedy.

## Precedent Treatment

- Judicial Council v. McBryde is applied: protection of existing or potential appellate jurisdiction supplies no general supervision of unrelated administrative acts.
- Your Home Visiting Nurse Services v. Shalala is applied for the distinction between a lawful review channel and the underlying merits; its statutory setting and preserved limits remain intact.

## Law After Decision

The existing rule that auxiliary writs create no independent jurisdiction now governs this separate military personnel action. Constitutional challenges and properly authorized proceedings elsewhere remain open; no rule on their merits or adequacy is established.

## Separate Writings



## Procedure After Action

The military appellate injunction is dissolved. The Court directs no new sentence, administrative outcome or further Supreme Court proceeding. A challenge elsewhere proceeds only under that forum's independent authority.

## Source Notes

The reported chronology, sentence and lower-court posture appear in the [official United States Reports, volume 526, pages 529–540](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/526bv.pdf). The question is stated in substance. This decision resolves the injunction's statutory basis and expresses no constitutional judgment on the administrative drop.

# florida_v_white_merits_1999-05-17.md

## Event

Florida v. White, No. 98-223. Merits decision, May 17, 1999; argued March 23, 1999. On certiorari to the Supreme Court of Florida, 710 So. 2d 949. The appeal concerns suppression in a possession prosecution. Police seized White's readily movable car from a public workplace lot on probable cause that he had used it for earlier cocaine deliveries. They had no stated probable cause that drugs were then inside; cocaine was found during the subsequent inventory. The question is whether the forfeiture seizure required a warrant or an additional emergency.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Florida Supreme Court's judgment and remands.

## Judgment & Remedy

Reversed and remanded, 6–3. Supporting: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. Opposing: Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Ginsburg. Reversal rejects the federal warrant/additional-emergency ground for suppressing the fruits of this seizure. It does not unconditionally reinstate the conviction, order admission of every item, or enter a forfeiture award. Ordinary properly available proceedings and independent grounds remain for the state courts.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer | Entire opinion |
| Concurrence | Souter | Breyer | Joins the Court in full; explains the limited vehicle rule |
| Dissent | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Ginsburg | Would affirm the seizure-based suppression judgment |

## Holdings

#### Seizure of a forfeitable automobile in a public place

**Holding and operative rule:** Officers with probable cause that a readily movable automobile itself is forfeitable may seize it from a public place without a warrant or an additional case-specific emergency. The rule rests on the automobile's characteristics and the public-place seizure, not merely on a legislature's decision to label property contraband.

**Authority:** Thomas's opinion is joined in full by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer; these six Justices control the rule, application and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the established automobile rationale reaches custody of the automobile that probable cause identifies as the forfeitable object. Pennsylvania v. Labron supplies mobility and the reduced privacy associated with regulated vehicles without requiring a further showing that process was impracticable. Wyoming v. Houghton makes the object supported by probable cause important to the exception's scope; here that object is White's own car. The seizure occurred in public and required no additional invasion of a home. United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property requires prior process for the real-property deprivation it decided while expressly distinguishing movable property; its land-specific rule does not decide this case. G. M. Leasing Corp. v. United States supports distinguishing public seizures from intrusions into protected premises. The dissent correctly identifies a possessory interest and the retrospective purpose of forfeiture, but those differences do not remove this vehicle's mobility or the direct probable-cause connection. Earlier opportunity to obtain a warrant therefore does not itself defeat the exception.

The Court does not decide whether excessive delay can make probable cause stale. The holding authorizes no entry into a home or protected curtilage, and does not adjudicate inventory policy, prolonged retention, notice, ultimate forfeiture or a distinct state-law protection.

## Precedent Treatment

- Pennsylvania v. Labron is extended from a vehicle evidence search to seizure of the readily movable vehicle itself when probable cause identifies it as forfeitable and it is taken in public.
- Wyoming v. Houghton is applied for attention to the object and scope supported by probable cause; it did not itself decide forfeiture seizure.
- United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property is distinguished: its real-property process requirement and movable-property reservation retain their force.
- G. M. Leasing Corp. v. United States supports the public-place/protected-premises distinction; its revenue-enforcement holding is not converted into unlimited forfeiture authority.
- Knowles v. Iowa remains intact: an inventory or adjacent enforcement power cannot itself furnish authority for the antecedent seizure.

## Law After Decision

The automobile exception permits this public seizure of the forfeitable vehicle without a separate urgency showing. It supplies no general warrant exception for all property designated contraband, and leaves distinct seizure, search, retention, hearing and final-forfeiture questions separate.

## Separate Writings

**Souter, joined by Breyer, concurring:** Both join the Court's complete rule. They emphasize that statutory designation alone cannot permit warrantless seizure of any property a legislature calls contraband. The automobile and public-place limits do the constitutional work. G. M. Leasing involved traditional revenue enforcement and does not validate unlimited forfeiture practice.

**Stevens, joined by Stone-Zsela and Ginsburg, dissenting:** The Fourth Amendment protects possession as well as privacy. A contemporary search for evidence and permanent forfeiture custody based on past use are different intrusions; regulation of a car does not erase its owner's possessory interest. Officers had a reasonable opportunity to obtain a warrant and identified no concrete urgency. The later inventory cannot cure that antecedent seizure. G. M. Leasing involved an antecedent tax assessment with the force of a judgment, unlike the officers' own retrospective assessment here. The dissent would affirm suppression on the reviewed ground while preserving prosecution based on untainted evidence and forfeiture through lawful process. Its rule leaves genuine roadside exigency, a proper Labron evidence search, possession of contraband itself, lawful temporary securing while a warrant is diligently sought, and ordinary regulatory impoundment distinct.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the state courts for proceedings consistent with the federal seizure ruling. No acquittal, final forfeiture, particular new hearing or later Supreme Court event is directed.

## Source Notes

The chronology and lower-court posture are reported in the [official United States Reports, volume 526, pages 559–573](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/526bv.pdf). The decision addresses the federal warrant question, without deciding the complete statutory notice or forfeiture procedure.

# hunt_v_cromartie_merits_1999-05-17.md

## Event

Hunt v. Cromartie, No. 98-85. Merits decision, May 17, 1999; argued January 20, 1999. On direct appeal from a three-judge District Court, 34 F. Supp. 2d 1029. The court entered summary judgment invalidating North Carolina's Twelfth Congressional District before discovery or an evidentiary hearing. The State disputed the court's inference that race, rather than partisan voting patterns, predominated. The appeal requires the Court to identify the governing constitutional injury before determining which factual disputes matter. The State used a replacement plan for the 1998 elections with district-court approval. Section 1.1 of 1998 North Carolina Session Laws chapter 2 governs that plan's use in 1998 and 2000 subject to this Court's reversal of the judgment declaring the prior G.S. 163–201(a) unconstitutional. That conditional operation leaves a live controversy; the Court does not decide the replacement map's validity or whether this vacatur satisfies the statutory condition.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court vacates the summary judgment and dependent injunction and remands under Johnson v. Miller.

## Judgment & Remedy

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting | Opposing or differently aligned |
|---|---|---|---|
| Existing summary judgment and injunction | Vacated, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | No opposition to vacatur |
| Governing remand | Reject predominance alone as a constitutional deprivation; consider only actually presented independently cognizable claims under their own elements and ordinary procedure, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas would preserve the predominance claim for factfinding |

No trial is required solely to choose between racial and political motives for the design-only theory. The Court does not finally validate the map, supply a new claim, deem an unknown claim forfeited, or independently decide the State's own summary-judgment motion.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer | Common vacatur and ordinary summary-judgment protections |
| Opinion of the Court, Parts II–III | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Breyer | Constitutional rule and governing remand |
| Concurrence in part and in the vacatur, dissenting from the remand limitation | Kennedy | O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Joins Part I; would retain the predominance claim |

## Holdings

#### I. Predominance alone cannot support this injunction

**Holding and operative rule:** A districting injunction cannot rest solely on a finding that race predominated in forming a common territorial electorate, even if ordinary districting criteria were subordinated. A plaintiff must establish a separately cognizable denial of equal participation or treatment; remand evaluates any such actually presented claim under its own elements and ordinary procedural rules.

**Authority:** Part II and the governing remand in Part III are joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These five direct joins control.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that factual disagreement matters only when the governing law makes the fact material. Shaw v. Reno and Johnson v. Miller distinguish racial composition of a shared electorate from unequal civic status, racial eligibility or representation legally limited to one race. Predominance may exist without establishing any of those deprivations. The district court therefore could not invalidate the district merely by resolving the design's motivation against the State. A trial on the same legally insufficient theory would not cure that error. Coalition for Economic Equity v. Wilson illustrates a concrete burden on political access; it does not turn racial motivation alone into that burden. An independently cognizable injury may be noneconomic and need not arise from a second act or later electoral loss, but its own elements must be established. The Court neither invents such a claim nor rules it forfeited because its preservation is not established here. It leaves narrow tailoring, statutory vote dilution, the applicable scrutiny for an actually supported independent injury and the district's validity against other claims undecided.

#### II. Summary judgment cannot resolve a genuine material inference against the nonmovant

**Holding and operative rule:** When an actually cognizable claim makes motive material, a supported genuine dispute between racial and political explanations cannot be resolved against the nonmoving party on summary judgment. Circumstantial proof can support summary judgment in an appropriate case; neither district shape nor racial and partisan correlation is automatically conclusive.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part I at this ordinary procedural level, without agreeing on which constitutional claim may proceed.

**Controlling explanation:** The State offered a supported political explanation for the boundaries. The court could not treat the contrary motive inference as a trial finding on this summary record. Johnson's evidentiary treatment separates proof from legal consequence. Under the Court's substantive holding, no motive trial follows on a design-only theory; under any actually preserved independent claim, ordinary materiality and evidentiary rules govern. The Court does not treat new reply-brief material as evidence that was before the district court or decide the denial of the State's cross-motion as a separate component.

## Precedent Treatment

- Shaw v. Reno is applied: territorial racial composition alone does not establish the asserted unequal-treatment injury.
- Johnson v. Miller controls both the substantive insufficiency of predominance alone and the distinction between evidentiary dispute and legal materiality; its genuine-injury protections and limits remain.
- Coalition for Economic Equity v. Wilson is distinguished as an actual political-access burden, not authority to infer such a burden from motive or constituency composition.

## Law After Decision

The Court applies the existing Shaw/Johnson rule and requires a conforming remand. Predominance alone does not sustain an injunction or require a trial. Ordinary summary-judgment rules remain available for facts material to a properly presented independent claim; the four-Justice proposed predominance trigger is not governing law.

## Separate Writings

**Kennedy, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas:** These Justices agree that the existing summary judgment and injunction cannot stand. They maintain their position that predominant racial assignment subordinating ordinary districting criteria should trigger strict scrutiny, and would retain that claim for factfinding. Mere racial awareness, a numerical racial percentage and an unusual shape are not automatic invalidity even under their rule; the State's supported political explanation precludes the present adverse summary judgment. Justification and fit would follow only if the proposed trigger were proved. Their disagreement with the Court concerns the constitutional premise and the remand's foreclosure of the design-only theory, not a vote to affirm the district court's invalidation.

## Procedure After Action

The district court must proceed consistently with the Court's rejection of the design-only theory and its preserved-claim limits. No replacement map, new election, mandatory motive trial or later Supreme Court event is ordered. The Court orders neither automatic restoration of the predecessor map nor displacement of the replacement map and does not invalidate the completed 1998 elections.

## Source Notes

The argument date, district-court posture and contested evidence are reported in the [official United States Reports, volume 526, pages 541–558](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/526bv.pdf). The constitutional rule and remand are governed by Shaw and Johnson; no independent claim or preservation finding is supplied beyond the adjudicated record. The replacement-plan condition is set out in [1998 North Carolina Session Laws chapter 2, §1.1](https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/SessionLaws/HTML/1997-1998/SL1998-2.html); its application to this judgment is left open.

# ruhrgas_ag_v_marathon_oil_co_merits_1999-05-17.md

## Event

Ruhrgas AG v. Marathon Oil Co., No. 98-470. Merits decision, May 17, 1999; argued March 22, 1999. On certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 145 F.3d 211. In a removed Texas action concerning a Norwegian gas venture, the District Court dismissed for lack of personal jurisdiction without resolving three asserted removal grounds. The en banc Court of Appeals required subject-matter jurisdiction to be addressed first. The question is whether that order is invariably required.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the en banc judgment and remands for further appellate proceedings.

## Judgment & Remedy

Reversed and remanded, 9–0. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The District Court was permitted to choose the personal-jurisdiction inquiry. The Fifth Circuit must review the dismissal under the correct sequencing rule; the Court does not affirm the correctness of the disputed contacts or the ultimate personal-jurisdiction conclusion. No removal ground or state-court remand is decided.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Entire opinion |

## Holdings

#### A court may choose a straightforward nonmerits jurisdictional ground

**Holding and operative rule:** A federal court may dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction before resolving subject-matter jurisdiction when personal jurisdiction is straightforward and presents no complex state-law question while the subject-matter issue is difficult. Ordinarily an easily resolved subject-matter question should come first; the choice must respect applicable statutory remand commands and review bars and must decide no contested merits.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion, joined by all nine Justices, controls the rule, application and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that declining authority over a defendant does not assume authority to decide liability. Citizens for a Better Environment v. Steel Co. forbids assumed Article III power for contested merits, and NAACP, Los Angeles Branch v. Jones applies that boundary to constitutional claims. Neither exhausts the order among nonmerits thresholds. Personal jurisdiction protects a defendant's interest in being subject to the court's authority; its waivability does not make it an optional prerequisite to a binding merits judgment. Here choosing that straightforward ground avoids an unnecessary difficult subject-matter determination without deciding the tort claims. The State's forum interest and the possible consequences of jurisdictional determinations remain relevant to the choice, but do not establish an absolute order. If subject matter is as readily resolved and personal jurisdiction raises difficult state law, comity ordinarily favors deciding remand first. Murphy Brothers keeps removal timing distinct from actual jurisdiction, and El Paso Natural Gas gives effect to a particular statutory priority rather than a universal hierarchy. An established absence of subject-matter jurisdiction retains its statutory consequence; an unresolved difficult issue is not the same thing. The Court leaves the correctness of the actual personal-jurisdiction dismissal to the Court of Appeals and decides none of the three asserted removal grounds.

## Precedent Treatment

- Citizens for a Better Environment v. Steel Co. is applied within its express scope: jurisdiction must precede contested merits, but its nonmerits-sequencing reservation permits today's rule.
- NAACP, Los Angeles Branch v. Jones is applied on the same merits boundary; its prohibition on assumed standing is unchanged.
- Murphy Brothers, Inc. v. Michetti Pipe Stringing, Inc. is distinguished as removal timing and formal process, which establish no subject-matter jurisdiction or universal sequence.
- El Paso Natural Gas Co. v. Neztsosie is distinguished as a statute-specific priority over prudential exhaustion, not a general order between jurisdictional grounds.
- Insurance Corp. of Ireland v. Compagnie des Bauxites de Guinee supplies the distinction between the individual interest protected by personal jurisdiction and institutional subject-matter limits; the distinction does not establish an invariable order.

## Law After Decision

Federal courts may use the bounded personal-jurisdiction-first route in removed as well as originally filed actions. The rule adds discretion among nonmerits thresholds while preserving ordinary comity, actual statutory commands and the prohibition on deciding contested merits without jurisdiction. It establishes no universal preclusion rule or actual jurisdiction over these claims.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Fifth Circuit for further proceedings, including review of the personal-jurisdiction dismissal under the permitted sequence. No merits determination, state-court remand, new discovery or later Supreme Court event is commanded.

## Event

Maine Green Party v. Maine, First Circuit No. 98-1309. Merits decision, May 20, 1999, on review of the First Circuit's March 2, 1999 judgment, 173 F.3d 1, affirming summary judgment for Maine. The question concerns the five-percent presidential-vote condition for retaining statewide party recognition and the asserted right to organize locally. No Supreme Court argument date is established.

The party qualified through Jonathan Carter's 6.4% gubernatorial vote in 1994 and lost recognition after Ralph Nader received less than five percent in the 1996 presidential election. The initial papers challenged the condition and asserted organizational autonomy; the parties dispute whether the later exclusive-local theory introduced a materially different factual premise. An affidavit described efforts toward national organization. The First Circuit majority affirmed on preservation; Campbell concurred on the merits.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The First Circuit's judgment is affirmed. No injunction, recognition order or further merits remand is entered.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg.

The judgment rejecting this challenge remains in force. Four affirm on preservation; the Chief concurs on the facial merits and leaves concrete as-applied exclusion claims open. The four dissenters would vacate and remand for ordinary examination, without ordering recognition, an injunction or a new election.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lead opinion announcing the judgment | Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Supports affirmance | Procedural preservation ground only; no constitutional merits holding. |

No opinion commands a majority rationale for affirmance. The Chief and the four dissenters directly agree on the bounded preservation proposition stated below; their distinct judgments and writings remain separate.

## Holdings

### A refined legal argument does not itself forfeit the original associational claim

**Holding and operative rule:** A party that initially challenged the presidential-vote condition and asserted its right to choose its organizational structure does not lose that legal organizational-autonomy objection merely by sharpening it later. That conclusion neither proves an exclusive local philosophy nor permits new summary-judgment facts to be assumed; the national-organizing affidavit and the factual basis of the as-applied theory retain their ordinary evidentiary significance.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's concurrence and Stevens's dissent, joined by Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, directly adopt this same limited proposition. Those five votes control the legal-argument distinction, without supplying a majority rationale for the judgment or a command to reopen this case.

**Controlling explanation:** Yee permits a different legal argument supporting a properly presented federal claim while preserving distinct limits on facts and the question presented. Here the original challenge identified the same presidential-vote condition and the asserted freedom to organize as the members chose. The later legal formulation therefore is not wholly new merely because it gives that associational objection greater precision. The contrary position correctly identifies a possible difference between inability to build a national party and a philosophical refusal to do so. That difference prevents a court from assuming the asserted philosophy; it does not itself erase the originally presented legal objection. Thomas v. Arn permits reasonable, nonjurisdictional presentation rules, but its failure-to-object setting does not automatically decide an argument raised in objections. The district court's silence establishes neither a new fact nor an express waiver ruling. This conclusion makes no factual finding about exclusive-local intent and resolves no constitutional merits.

### The affirmance has no controlling constitutional rationale

**Judgment without controlling rationale:** Five Justices affirm, but the four-Justice preservation ground and the Chief's contextual merits ground are not logical subsets of one another. Marks yields no common narrower rationale. The judgment binds the parties; it establishes no general five-percent safe harbor, no duty to participate nationally, and no Court holding approving every alternative ballot route or rejecting every concrete as-applied exclusion claim.

## Precedent Treatment

- Yee v. City of Escondido is applied by the five Justices supporting the preserved-legal-argument proposition; its separate factual and granted-question limits remain.
- Thomas v. Arn remains a rule allowing nonjurisdictional presentation requirements, not a mandatory forfeiture rule for every theory raised in objections.
- Marks v. United States supplies no common affirmance rationale because the procedural and merits grounds are independent.
- Anderson v. Celebrezze retains contextual review of actual burden, precise interests and fit; no majority adopts a new retention-rule application.
- Burdick v. Takushi retains its calibrated framework and complete-system limits; no new constitutional application commands five here.
- Norman v. Reed retains its actual-access and separate-electorate protections without a majority retention-rule application.
- Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party retains its specific nonfusion holding; no majority extends it to validate this condition.
- National Committee of the Reform Party v. Democratic National Committee retains its support-sensitive financing scope and supplies no general retention safe harbor.
- Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation retains its petition-advocacy protections; no majority changes them in this case.

## Law After Decision

The reviewed judgment stands. The bounded legal-argument preservation proposition has five direct supporters, but the Court establishes no constitutional safe harbor for presidential-vote retention and no unconditional right to statewide party privileges based on local success. Existing contextual ballot-access scrutiny remains unchanged. Concrete as-applied exclusion claims require their own properly available proceedings and supported record; this judgment orders no new proceeding.

## Separate Writings

**Breyer, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, announcing the judgment:** These Justices would affirm because an as-applied case based on practical difficulty building a national organization differs materially from one based on an exclusive philosophical commitment to local politics. Yee protects new legal arguments, not assumed new facts. The affidavit describing national organization efforts makes the change consequential. The State's undeveloped objection and the district court's unexplained adoption do not prevent enforcement of ordinary presentation requirements. Arn supports reasonable nonjurisdictional management but does not make every omitted theory jurisdictionally barred. They reach no constitutional merits, approve no universal presidential-support proxy and find no alternative route necessarily adequate.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in the judgment:** The initial organizational-autonomy argument and Eu and Tashjian citations preserve the legal objection, while the affidavit prevents an unsupported finding of an exclusively local philosophy. Applying Anderson–Burdick and Timmons to this record, he would reject the facial challenge because statewide administrative privileges may depend on demonstrated statewide support and workable alternative access remains available. The party can organize, endorse and campaign; its 1994 qualification through a nonparty gubernatorial candidate demonstrates an operative route. The record does not establish practically prohibitive requalification or alternative access. This is not automatic deference to the statewide label, a duty to nominate a presidential candidate, or approval of every retention and qualification rule. A developed record of compelled abandonment of genuinely local organization without a workable ballot alternative would require a different burden-and-fit inquiry.

The routes remain distinct: section 301 governs retention, with caucus, convention and certification conditions; section 302 permits formation through a consenting qualifying nonparty candidate, together with enrollment, caucuses in all sixteen counties and a convention; section 303 provides petition qualification. Its approved form, verified and certified signatures and legal addresses equal to five percent of the last gubernatorial vote, and filing before 5 p.m. on the 180th day before a primary are not another presidential-vote condition. Section 302's March 20 caucus-notice date is separate. None receives blanket constitutional approval.

**Stevens, joined by Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, dissenting:** They agree that the original legal associational objection remains available, but would return the case for ordinary consideration of its actual factual basis, electoral burdens, precise state interests and feasible alternatives. Yee permits that legal refinement; the affidavit presents an issue to examine rather than a categorical loss of the claim. Norman and Anderson–Burdick require attention to real organizing burdens. Timmons's nonfusion holding and Reform Party's funding rule do not decide this retention condition, and nominal statutory access does not establish its actual cost. They neither find exclusive-local intent nor declare the condition unconstitutional, and they require no recognition, injunction or new election.

Ordinary mandate follows affirmance. The reviewed summary judgment remains effective. No Supreme Court protection is inferred from the earlier agreed injunction described in the state proceedings, and no new filing, deadline, election, compulsory hearing or continuing injunction is ordered.
