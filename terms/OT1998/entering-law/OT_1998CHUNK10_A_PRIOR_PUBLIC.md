### Lane v. Peña, No. 95-365

Decision, June 20, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 15, 1996; decided June 20, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit. The question is whether the Rehabilitation Act unequivocally waives federal sovereign immunity from compensatory damages for discrimination in this federally conducted academy program. The District Court found a violation and ordered reinstatement, then rejected damages after Dorsey; the Court of Appeals summarily affirmed. The Government did not contest the injunction.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

**Opposing:** Stevens and Breyer.

The discrimination determination and reinstatement remain undisturbed. No damages calculation or new trial is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Souter delivered the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg. Justice Stevens dissented, joined in full by Justice Breyer.

#### Holdings

**Federal program coverage does not supply the missing consent to compensatory damages.** Sections 504(a) and 505 of the Rehabilitation Act, read with §1003 of the Rehabilitation Act Amendments of 1986, do not unequivocally waive the United States' immunity from Lane's claim for compensatory damages arising from this federally conducted academy program. The damages judgment is affirmed; the existing discrimination determination and reinstatement remain undisturbed.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg; seven Justices adopt the rule, explanation and limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress imposed §504(a)'s nondiscrimination duty on programs conducted by Executive agencies and the Postal Service. Section 505(a)(2), however, supplies Title VI remedies to persons aggrieved by recipients or federal providers of financial assistance. That assistance-related formulation does not unequivocally expose every federal program operator to compensatory damages. Nordic Village requires enacted consent to the particular money judgment; Ohio requires an incorporated remedial provision's limits to receive independent effect. A substantive prohibition and an affirmative obligation to pay from the Treasury are distinct.

The combined-enactment argument does not cure that difference. Section 505(a)(1) separately supplies Title VII remedies for §501 federal employment complaints, while §505(b) permits a reasonable attorney's fee as costs, in the court's discretion, to a prevailing party other than the United States. Neither channel converts this academy enrollment claim into a federal employment case or a general damages waiver. Section 1003 removes specified State immunity and compares remedies against States with remedies against other public or private entities. Its public-entity reference can encompass nonfederal recipients; its comparison does not independently grant every remedy against every sovereign. The Court need not choose among all possible readings of that State-directed comparison to find the claimed federal consent unclear.

Franklin permits compensation for proved intentional discrimination in the established Title IX action, subject to recipient responsibility, causation and compensable loss; it did not supply consent to this federal Treasury award. Their satisfaction is not decided because waiver fails. Meyer likewise keeps waiver, cause of action and entitlement to recovery distinct. Legislative purpose and the desirability of compensation cannot supply missing enacted clarity. This conclusion neither licenses discrimination nor removes independently authorized prospective relief. It decides no differently situated federal assistance-provider claim, fee application, new officer action or implied damages cause.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Nordic Village, Inc., 503 U.S. 30 (1992):** Applies the requirement of unequivocal enacted consent to the particular affirmative monetary remedy; a plausible narrower statutory operation defeats the asserted waiver. The original §106(c) holding and its limits remain unchanged.
- **United States Department of Energy v. Ohio, 503 U.S. 607 (1992):** Applies the distinction between federal substantive or adjudicatory coverage and the limits of incorporated monetary-remedy provisions; no pollution-remedy question is reopened.
- **Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, 503 U.S. 60 (1992):** Preserves appropriate relief in the established Title IX action and distinguishes that remedial inquiry from federal sovereign consent. Compensation still requires proved intentional discrimination, recipient responsibility, causation and compensable loss; their satisfaction is not decided here.
- **FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471 (1994):** Preserves the separate inquiries into waiver, substantive cause and recovery, including the effect of a genuinely broad sue-and-be-sued clause. No such clause supplies this academy claim's waiver; no agency Bivens question is decided.

#### Law After Decision

The existing requirement of unequivocal consent to the particular federal monetary remedy remains unchanged. These Rehabilitation Act provisions do not authorize Lane's compensatory claim against the United States for this federally conducted academy program. Section 504's substantive duty, independently authorized prospective relief, the separate discretionary fee provision and differently worded waivers retain their own force.

#### Separate Writings

Justice Stevens dissents, joined by Justice Breyer. In their view, Congress's express extension of §504 to federal programs carries the remedial setting in which that prohibition already operated. Section 505's differentiated enforcement channels codify particular means of enforcement without withdrawing ordinary damages for the newly express federal obligation; the 1986 recognition of legal and equitable remedies reinforces that reading. They accept that consent must come from enacted law and that Franklin alone cannot waive immunity, but find the combined provisions sufficient and distinguish Nordic Village's ambiguous bankruptcy formulation. Treating §505's assistance language as a restriction on the separate federal-program obligation gives the remedial scheme an unwarranted gap. They would reverse the categorical damages bar and remand for litigation of entitlement, amount and any remaining lawful defense. They would neither award damages automatically nor reopen the injunction.

#### Source Notes

The applicable text is Rehabilitation Act §§504 and 505, 29 U.S.C. §§794 and 794a, in the 1994 Code, and §1003 of the Rehabilitation Act Amendments of 1986, 100 Stat. 1845, codified at 42 U.S.C. §2000d-7. Section 1003 applies to violations occurring in whole or part after October 21, 1986. The lower proceedings include 867 F. Supp. 1050 and the D.C. Circuit's summary affirmance following Dorsey, 41 F.3d 1551. The academy's separation decision supplies the damages controversy; the unchallenged reinstatement is outside the reviewed dispute.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The ordinary mandate returns the case with the damages rejection affirmed and the reinstatement order undisturbed. This decision adds no new claim and resolves no fee application.

### Kyles v. Whitley, No. 93-7927

Merits decision, April 19, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 7, 1994; decided April 19, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, reviewing 5 F.3d 806 (October 14, 1993), which affirmed denial of federal habeas relief. The question is whether the established suppressed favorable evidence, viewed collectively, is material to Kyles’s conviction under Brady and Bagley.

Dolores Dye was killed and her automobile taken on September 20, 1984. Kyles’s first trial ended in a deadlock; the second produced a conviction and death sentence. No reason for the deadlock beyond disagreement is established. The prosecution used eyewitness identifications, the murder weapon found behind a stove at the apartment where Kyles stayed, ammunition and a holster, the victim’s purse and papers found in garbage, and pet food of brands she had bought. A print linked Kyles to a receipt in the car, but processing destroyed its readable date. The defense presented a theory that Joseph “Beanie” Wallace was implicated and framed Kyles. Adverse credibility findings and evidence supporting guilt remain part of the record.

The established suppression concerns six contemporaneous eyewitness statements, Beanie’s recorded September 22 and written September 23 accounts, prosecutor Strider’s interview notes, a garbage-retrieval memorandum, and the parking-lot plate list. Smallwood’s earlier sequence differed from his trial observation account; Williams’s early description differed from Kyles’s height and build. Beanie’s accounts bear on corroboration, the police investigation, his possible access, and the provenance of physical evidence. Some lawful uses arise through police examination and preparation without calling Beanie or admitting every statement for its truth. The seventeen-plate list omitted Kyles’s plate, but its completeness is disputed. The Court finds neither Beanie’s guilt nor planting, witness perjury, or a complete list.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the denial of relief on the conviction-level Brady claim, 6–3, and remands for a conditional writ. Louisiana must retry Kyles within a reasonable time fixed by the district court or release him under the writ.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Collective suppression undermines confidence in this conviction

**Controlling proposition:** Suppressed favorable evidence is material when its collective lawful uses, considered in the whole record and in the defense’s preparation and presentation, create a reasonable probability of a different result sufficient to undermine confidence in the verdict. No item must independently satisfy that standard, and the defendant need not show acquittal more likely than conviction or that the remaining evidence is legally insufficient; the established suppression here meets the standard.

**Authority:** Justice Souter’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes for the rule, application and conviction-level relief. The dissent accepts collective assessment but disputes materiality on this record.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the undisclosed evidence changes the relationship among the prosecution’s apparently reinforcing proofs. Brady requires disclosure of material favorable evidence, and Bagley supplies a probability sufficient to undermine confidence rather than a demand for probable acquittal. Earlier identification accounts permit substantial impeachment; Beanie’s differing accounts support corroboration and examination of the investigation; and the garbage material bears on the provenance of tangible evidence. These uses reinforce one another. They do not all depend on calling Beanie or accepting every statement as true. The remaining identifications, ammunition, holster, pet food and credibility objections remain significant. But counting that evidence as sufficient does not answer how the defense could have challenged its apparent independence after disclosure. The plate list contributes only within its disputed completeness. The Court finds no planting, perjury or factual innocence. It resolves the established cumulative suppression and leaves later evidence and independently unadmitted constitutional claims outside this decision.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83: applies the duty to disclose material favorable evidence to the established suppression.
- United States v. Bagley, 473 U.S. 667: applies reasonable-probability materiality to the combined defense counterfactual, including lawful impeachment and preparation uses.
- Schlup v. Delo, January 23, 1995: distinguishes its gateway showing; it supplies no additional innocence prerequisite for this Brady claim.

##### The prosecutor’s responsibility includes the investigating team

**Controlling proposition:** A prosecutor must learn of favorable evidence known to police acting on the government’s behalf in the case and disclose it when Brady requires disclosure; personal ignorance does not remove that evidence from the materiality inquiry. This case-specific responsibility does not impute all knowledge of every government office or establish unrestricted investigative or open-file duties.

**Authority:** Souter’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes. The dissent’s consideration of the police-held material does not supply additional votes for an affirmative organizational rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the prosecution cannot avoid its disclosure responsibility by separating the trial lawyer from police who worked on the case. Brady protects the fairness of the trial, and Bagley assesses the material effect of nondisclosure; that protection would fail if the same favorable information counted only when personally recalled by the prosecutor. Here the eyewitness statements, recorded accounts, plate list and investigative memorandum arose within the investigation used to prosecute Kyles. Their lawful defense uses therefore enter the same collective inquiry. The rule does not require every agency to search all government files, and it does not convert every nondisclosure into a completed constitutional violation. Materiality and the evidence’s favorable character still matter. United States v. Williams addresses a distinct federal grand-jury duty and its specified remedy conditions; its personal-knowledge limitation does not narrow trial Brady responsibility. The Court creates no open-file entitlement and excuses no lack of diligence concerning evidence already known to the defense.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brady and Bagley: apply their trial-disclosure protection to information held by police acting for this prosecution.
- United States v. Williams, May 4, 1992: distinguishes its confined grand-jury disclosure and dismissal rules; those limits remain effective within their own setting.

##### A completed material Brady violation requires no second duplicative harmlessness showing

**Controlling proposition:** Once this suppression is found material under Brady and Bagley, a second prejudice or harmlessness test may not defeat relief merely by reassessing whether the same nondisclosure affected the verdict. This holding rests on the prejudice built into the completed Brady violation and establishes no universal harmlessness rule for other habeas claims.

**Authority:** Souter’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes for this claim-specific ground. The dissent finds no material violation and adopts no independent harmlessness ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a second duplicative prejudice inquiry cannot rescue a conviction after material suppression has already undermined confidence in its verdict. Bagley makes the requisite effect part of the constitutional violation itself. The Court has found that effect through the combined disclosure counterfactual, not merely identified an error whose effect remains unexamined. Brecht retains Chapman for its specified preserved protected-silence claim, and O’Neal extends that treatment to its specified personal-intent category; neither decision supplies a general habeas rule displacing Brady’s own materiality requirement. Nor does this decision replace Bagley with Chapman. The State’s surviving evidence has already been considered, and it does not restore confidence after the lawful combined uses are taken into account. Ordinary habeas gates and independently presented claims retain their own rules. For this established violation, the appropriate relief is a conditional new-trial opportunity, not an additional weaker review of the same prejudice or a declaration of innocence.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bagley: treats materiality as a defining element of the suppression violation, not an initial screen before a second duplicative prejudice test.
- Brecht v. Abrahamson, April 21, 1993, and O’Neal v. McAninch, February 21, 1995: preserve their actual category-specific holdings and distinguish them from claim-defining Brady materiality.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Brady and Bagley govern collective materiality and the trial prosecution’s responsibility. Williams remains confined to its distinct grand-jury setting. Schlup remains a gateway rule. Brecht and O’Neal retain their category-specific holdings; this decision neither adopts a general habeas Kotteakos rule nor extends Chapman universally. No precedent is overruled.

Courts must assess the established favorable suppression collectively, including legitimate effects on preparation, corroboration, impeachment and examination of the investigation. Prosecutors bear case-specific responsibility for investigating-police information. A completed material Brady violation carries no second duplicative harmlessness hurdle. The holdings neither require open-file discovery nor treat every nondisclosure as structural error, and they do not excuse defense lack of diligence about known evidence.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia, joined by Justices Kennedy and Thomas, dissents. The three accept cumulative reasonable-probability review and consider the police-held material. They find that the supported additional uses do not sufficiently weaken the surviving identifications, physical evidence and adverse credibility findings to undermine confidence in this verdict. They treat the jury’s knowledge of a framing theory as relevant but not conclusive, and Beanie’s absence as a limitation on some uses rather than a bar to every investigative use. Their disagreement concerns the combined application: they reject the majority’s assessment that the new corroboration and provenance challenges materially change the evidentiary picture. They do not demand proof of innocence, impose a materiality threshold on each item, or hold that mere legal sufficiency defeats Brady. Having found no material violation, they would affirm denial of this claim without adopting a second harmlessness ground. Their application is noncontrolling.

Justice Stevens joins the Court’s application of Bagley without withdrawing his earlier request-sensitive criticism of that standard. The Court does not reject that criticism or adopt a new restrictive pretrial-disclosure theory; no additional writing is filed here.

The [filed record and briefs](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0539) support the established suppression and adversarial submissions; disputed later evidence and material not securely identified as a party’s filing supply no additional finding. [Bagley, 473 U.S. 667](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep473/usrep473667/usrep473667.pdf) supplies the reasonable-probability standard. The case returns through the Fifth Circuit for the district court to issue a conditional writ. The district court fixes a reasonable period for Louisiana to retry Kyles; failure to retry within that period requires release under the writ. No immediate unconditional release or acquittal is ordered, and the Court does not predict the result of any new trial.

### Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, No. 91-1671

Merits decision, 1993-06-01

#### Chronology and Posture

Judgment under review: Ninth Circuit, 948 F.2d 607 (1991).

Question: Whether §502(a)(3)’s appropriate-equitable-relief provision authorizes the requested compensatory monetary recovery from an admitted nonfiduciary alleged knowingly to have participated in a fiduciary breach.

Plan participants alleged that the actuary knowingly participated in conduct causing pension losses. Review follows dismissal; allegations of breach, participation, knowledge and causal loss are not findings. Hewitt’s nonfiduciary status is fixed. The restitution ruling is unchallenged, and additional parties and claims were dismissed without a preserved appeal. The Ninth Circuit’s separate revival of the state malpractice claim is not under review. Hewitt expressly disclaimed the alternative argument that §502(a)(3) supplies no cause of action against a nonfiduciary at all.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The reviewed compensatory award is not appropriate equitable relief

**Controlling proposition:** Section 502(a)(3)’s appropriate equitable relief encompasses traditionally equitable categories of relief, not compensatory damages merely because a court of equity could award them in trust litigation. The requested monetary compensation from this admitted nonfiduciary for the plan’s losses is therefore unavailable under that provision; the Court does not bar every monetary equitable remedy or decide the antecedent, expressly disclaimed cause-of-action question.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court; Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (5 Justices). The named Justices form a direct majority for this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted remedy limitation must retain independent force. Curtis and Terry distinguish compensation for loss, ordinarily legal relief, from traditionally equitable categories. Reading equitable relief to include everything an equity court could award in a trust controversy would deprive that limitation of its work and disregard ERISA’s use of different remedial language elsewhere. Firestone directs consultation of trust law where it fits the statute; it does not authorize replacing the statute’s express limit with the full remedial jurisdiction of historical equity. Clews establishes that trust courts could award money, but that fact does not determine the category Congress selected here. Russell reinforces respect for ERISA’s differentiated enforcement structure without itself settling this question. The participants seek compensation rather than a preserved restitution remedy. The Court rejects that claim while leaving the disclaimed antecedent action question, unreviewed remedies and the separate malpractice disposition untouched; no allegations become findings of liability.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Curtis v. Loether, 415 U.S. 189: traditional legal compensation distinction applied.
- Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers v. Terry, 494 U.S. 558: remedy classification applied; trust forum alone does not decide the statutory category.
- Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch, 489 U.S. 101: trust-law guidance preserved subject to express statutory limits.
- Clews v. Jamieson, 182 U.S. 461: equity’s monetary powers acknowledged but distinguished from the statutory category.
- Massachusetts Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Russell, 473 U.S. 134: enforcement-structure caution applied; its reserved question is now resolved only within this holding.
- United States v. Burke, May26,1992: its fractured judgment supplies no controlling general remedial-range test.
- Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, February26,1992: appropriate-remedy presumption distinguished because Congress expressly limited this provision to equitable relief.

#### Current-Law Effect

The availability of this compensatory nonfiduciary remedy remained open. After this decision, five Justices reject it under §502(a)(3)’s equitable-relief limit. The Court does not decide whether a cause against the nonfiduciary otherwise exists, does not prohibit every equitable monetary remedy and does not reopen restitution. Stone’s bounded statutory-coverage and restoration proposals remain noncontrolling. Burke’s actual fractured authority and Franklin’s cause/remedy distinction remain unchanged.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Dissent — White; joined by Stevens, O'Connor:** White, joined by Stevens and O’Connor, reads equitable relief in the particular trust-law setting that ERISA incorporates. Firestone makes that background central, and Clews illustrates the traditional power of equity to restore trust losses through a monetary award, including against a knowing participant. The dissent regards the majority’s general legal-versus-equitable categories as an insufficient account of this particular remedy. It argues that the equitable limitation still matters because punitive relief and remedies arising only from the later merger of law and equity were not traditional trust restoration. Russell does not decide the different §502(a)(3) issue. The express redress language and §502(l)’s treatment of knowing participants further support a claim against a participating nonfiduciary without making every professional a fiduciary. This position would remove the categorical dismissal, not establish liability or an amount of recovery. It does not control.

**Dissent — Stone-Zsela; no other joins:** Stone-Zsela would permit traditional monetary restoration for losses caused by actual knowing participation in an ERISA fiduciary’s breach. Firestone supports consulting trust law, and Clews identifies this restorative remedy as equitable; the argument is not that every award once obtainable incidentally in equity becomes available. Russell’s respect for the statutory enforcement structure remains, but does not settle the distinct redress provision presented here. Section 502(a)(3)’s express authority to redress the fiduciary violation supplies the bounded cause; §502(l) offers context without creating an independent private action. Hewitt’s disclaimer is a presentation limit, not a jurisdictional flaw. On remand the participants would still need to prove breach, actual knowing participation and causal loss, and the court would determine the proper amount and recipient within equitable limits. No professional-negligence or punitive claim is authorized. Fixed nonfiduciary status, unchallenged restitution, unappealed parties and claims, and the separate malpractice disposition remain closed. His conclusion on statutory coverage is a dissenting proposition, not an answer supplied by the Court.

### Johnson v. De Grandy; De Grandy v. Johnson; United States v. Florida, Nos. 92-519, 92-593 and 92-767, respectively

Merits decision, June 30, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 4, 1993; decided June 30, 1994. On direct appeals under 28 U.S.C. §1253 from the three-judge District Court for the Northern District of Florida, De Grandy v. Wetherell, 815 F. Supp. 1550. The trial ended and rulings were announced July 1, 1992; House and Senate judgments were entered July 2, the House judgment was supplemented July 6, and the explanatory opinion issued July 17.

The questions are whether Florida's preliminary validation precludes the private Section 2 claims or bars the United States' independent enforcement action, and whether the tried Dade-area House and Senate arrangements deny equal electoral opportunity under Section 2. State officials challenge House liability and the substituted House plan and defend the Senate result; the private Hispanic challengers and the United States seek additional Senate relief. The Black claim and proposed alternative bear on the lower court's asserted remedial conflict, without a separate Black appeal being decided here.

Florida adopted SJR 2-G on April 10, 1992, creating 120 single-member House districts and forty single-member Senate districts. The May 13 state validation, 597 So.2d 276, occurred within a thirty-day timetable and preserved later factual Section 2 challenges. Federal complaints were amended to challenge the new plan. The United States was not a party to the validation. The June 25 Hillsborough adjustment, 601 So.2d 543, responded to a Section 5 objection and did not adjudicate the Dade claims. Dade was not among Florida's five covered counties.

The District Court found continuing effects of official discrimination, substantial polarized voting, and cohesion within Hispanic and Black groups but no general cohesion between them. The House comparison is nine effective Hispanic districts among eighteen primarily-Dade districts against 50 percent Hispanic voting-age population, or nine of twenty districts touching Dade against about 47 percent. These are distinct descriptive frames. Experts identified neighborhood splitting in District 116, concentration in District 114 and fragmentation involving Districts 102 and 109. Those descriptions are not a finding that comparable minority and other neighborhoods were subjected to inconsistent districting standards.

The initial De Grandy House proposal listed eleven Hispanic districts with voting-age percentages of at least 64.5 percent. The imposed Modified De Grandy Plan 268 instead included District 109 at 63.74 percent and District 110 at 62.56 percent. The State's separate ten-district and eleven-district remedial proposals were different plans; the latter included two approximately 61-percent Miami Beach districts whose effectiveness was disputed because of recent noncitizen arrivals. The Court assumes the disputed first Gingles condition favorably and decides no citizenship method, universal percentage minimum or precise remedial-plan effectiveness.

The seven-district Senate area includes substantial Dade voters: Hispanic and Black voting-age shares are 44.8 and 15.8 percent, with three effective Hispanic-majority districts and one Black-majority district, approximately 42.9 and 14.3 percent of seven. Another district supplies Black electoral opportunity with crossover support. In the distinct five wholly-Dade districts, Hispanic and Black voting-age shares are 53.9 and 13.5 percent, with three Hispanic supermajorities and one Black majority. The broader South Florida account includes a Broward/Palm Beach Black-majority district outside the Dade seven. The July 2 Senate judgment stated no violation; the July 17 explanation described violations but conflicting proposed remedies. The Court reviews the operative judgment on the grounds below.

Congressional proceedings, the Escambia settlement, dismissed claims outside the tried area, dismissed constitutional claims and preclearance decisions remain undisturbed. No constitutional challenge to these district lines is decided.

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision: Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. No recusal or nonparticipation is established.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the House Section 2 liability and dependent remedial judgment and affirms the Senate judgment, 7–2 on each component. The State's procedural objections are rejected, and no additional districting relief is ordered.

| House Section 2 liability and dependent substitution of Modified De Grandy Plan 268; State's appeal, No. 92-519 | Reversed, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Thomas and Scalia would vacate and direct dismissal on statutory noncoverage | Remove the dependent House remedy through lawful election administration; no new statutory liability trial or Supreme Court replacement map |
| Senate judgment leaving the State's plan operative; appealed requests for further relief in Nos. 92-593 and 92-767 | Affirmed, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Thomas and Scalia would vacate and direct dismissal on statutory noncoverage | No additional Senate district ordered; affirmance rests on absent proved violation, not irremediability |

The Court rejects the asserted preclusion and jurisdictional obstacles through the seven-Justice procedural opinion. Thomas and Scalia publish no procedural join and rest their alternative mandate on statutory noncoverage, not preclusion or want of jurisdiction. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The state validation does not preclude the reserved factual claims

**Controlling proposition:** Under 28 U.S.C. §1738, Florida's validation receives its actual state-law preclusive scope, including its express preservation of later factual Section 2 challenges; it therefore does not bar these private claims. The availability of a return to state court does not require exhaustion of that optional procedure before this preserved federal litigation.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part I-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg; seven Justices directly adopt this limited rule. Thomas and Scalia do not join the opinion, and their different statutory disposition supplies no additional published joinder.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that respecting a state judgment requires respecting its limits as well as its conclusions. Marrese requires the federal court to begin with the preclusive effect supplied by state law. Allen preserves ordinary preclusion principles without making every federal-rights claim immune from them. Florida's constitutional validation took place on a thirty-day timetable and expressly preserved subsequent factual challenges under Section 2. The private plaintiffs now present the kind of challenge that remained available; their amended complaints concern the new plan. The State's interest in finality cannot convert that limited validation into a completed adjudication of the reserved claims. Growe requires legal effect for an operative state plan, but does not insulate it from a properly pleaded federal defect. Nor does permission to seek further state review make that route mandatory. The Court creates no general Voting Rights Act exception to Section 1738 and decides no preclusion question beyond the judgment's established reservation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Marrese v. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, 470 U.S. 373: applies the state-law-first rule for the preclusive effect of a state judgment; no additional federal preclusion is imposed.
- Allen v. McCurry, 449 U.S. 90: preserves ordinary preclusion and its qualifications; creates no categorical federal-forum exemption for Voting Rights Act claims.
- Growe v. Emison, February 23, 1993: follows its requirement to respect the legally operative state plan and distinguishes its obsolete-plan defect; no enlarged party-preclusion rule follows.

The holding does not decide every nonparty, privity, intervention or intertwined-claim question or permit review of a preclearance determination.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part I-B, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg; seven Justices directly adopt the original-suit distinction on these facts. Thomas and Scalia publish no join in this part.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this enforcement action falls within original federal jurisdiction. Rooker denies federal district courts appellate authority over a state judicial judgment. Feldman preserves that limit while distinguishing an independent challenge from review of a state court's determination. The United States did not participate in Florida's validation proceeding and has no adverse state judgment of its own rights to overturn. It brings its statutory claim against the enacted electoral arrangement. The fact that success could unsettle a court-approved map does not itself transform that original suit into an appeal. The Government's other federal litigation and preclearance responsibilities did not make it a party to the state proceeding. The Court need not classify every aspect of state plan validation as legislative or judicial, nor reconsider ordinary preclusion. Shaw's separate restriction on review of federal preclearance remains intact; no such review is sought here. Jurisdiction permits adjudication of the claim and does not establish its merits.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rooker v. Fidelity Trust Co., 263 U.S. 413: applies the limit on lower-federal appellate review of state judgments; this independent enforcement action does not invoke that authority.
- District of Columbia Court of Appeals v. Feldman, 460 U.S. 462: preserves the appellate/original distinction without deciding every possible relationship between nonparties and a state judgment.
- Shaw v. Reno, June 28, 1993: preserves the designated channel and nonreviewability limits for federal preclearance; those limits do not immunize a state plan from an independent statutory claim.

##### 3. Necessary voting conditions do not require maximization

**Controlling proposition:** This Section 2 fragmentation claim requires a minority sufficiently large and geographically compact to form a district majority, political cohesion, and majority bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat its preferred candidate, absent special circumstances such as an unopposed minority candidate; the claimant must also establish unequal opportunity to participate and elect chosen representatives under the totality of circumstances, without proving discriminatory purpose. Even when those necessary conditions are satisfied, Section 2 does not require every feasible additional majority-minority district; the disputed first condition is assumed here, leaving citizenship, population-denominator and coalition or influence-claim questions unresolved.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg; seven Justices directly adopt the complete necessary-versus-sufficient distinction and rejection of maximization. Thomas and Scalia's statutory noncoverage position is a different rationale and supplies no join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a possible additional district identifies potential electoral opportunity, not an automatic statutory entitlement. Gingles supplies the conditions connecting the challenged arrangement to the asserted dilution. Growe applies those conditions to single-member fragmentation and requires local voting evidence rather than addition of distinct groups' populations. Voinovich keeps the burden on challengers to establish unequal opportunity and distinguishes state districting choices from judicial relief for a proved violation. Holder likewise separates a workable comparison from proof of injury and from remedy. Those decisions retain the statutory totality inquiry. Here the Court assumes the contested first condition in the challengers' favor and accepts the findings of cohesion within each minority group and polarized voting. Neither those premises nor the feasibility of additional districts dispenses with the remaining inquiry. Congress preserved a results claim while disclaiming proportional officeholding. The Court therefore rejects both an automatic maximization requirement and a new discriminatory-purpose requirement, without selecting a citizenship measure or deciding an influence or coalition entitlement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows its causal conditions, special-circumstances qualification and independent totality inquiry; the conditions do not establish liability by themselves.
- Growe v. Emison, February 23, 1993: preserves local cohesion and majority-bloc proof for single-member fragmentation and the unresolved coalition, influence and population questions.
- Voinovich v. Quilter, March 2, 1993: follows the challengers' burden and the distinction between a State's authority to choose districts and a court's power to remedy a proved violation.
- Holder v. Hall, June 30, 1994: applies its separation of coverage, comparison, proof and remedy; a permissible comparison creates no maximization command.
- Shaw v. Reno, June 28, 1993: preserves the independent statutory elements; its constitutional injury rule adds no further element to Section 2.

##### 4. Proportional opportunity is relevant evidence, not a safe harbor

**Controlling proposition:** The relationship between a group's share of the relevant population and the share of districts in which it has an effective opportunity to elect its preferred candidates is relevant evidence within Section 2's totality inquiry, whose weight depends on the other evidence; it is neither a conclusive safe harbor nor a burden-shifting affirmative defense, and disproportionality alone likewise does not establish liability. The inquiry concerns demonstrated electoral opportunity, not nominal district labels or the race of officeholders; it guarantees no proportional election, racial floor or ceiling, statewide offset, or universal geographic or population denominator, and cannot excuse proved local packing, fragmentation or another barrier merely because opportunities exist elsewhere.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg; six Justices directly adopt this contextual rule and its explanation. Kennedy separately accepts the exact limited propositions that proportionality is relevant, that the necessary conditions alone do not establish liability, and that proportionality supplies no safe harbor; his concurrence does not join Part III's full rationale or Parts IV and V's affirmative applications. No combined rationale under Marks is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that actual opportunity matters to the statutory question. Gingles requires an appraisal of the local electoral system under the totality, and Voinovich rejects liability inferred solely from a preferred district arrangement. A relationship between effective districts and population may bear substantially on whether an asserted lack of opportunity exists. But district opportunity, election outcomes and officeholding by racial identity are different facts. The statutory permission to consider protected-class electoral success does not guarantee proportional election or make any numerical relationship decisive. History, polarization and particular barriers retain their force when the aggregate appears favorable. Thus a State cannot defend proved local packing or fragmentation simply by pointing to compensating opportunities elsewhere. Conversely, an arithmetical shortfall does not itself prove the statutory wrong. The State's conclusive-defense proposal fails, as does treating statewide measurement as indispensable to that supposed defense. The Court uses the locally tried record without announcing a general geographic rule or resolving unnecessary preservation questions under Rule 8(c).

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows its contextual inquiry and treatment of electoral success; adopts no conclusive proportional-success presumption.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: preserves the need for proof of actual unequal opportunity rather than automatic condemnation or approval of a district form.
- Holder, June 30, 1994: preserves independent totality proof after an adequate comparison; its local institutional comparison supplies neither a quota nor an evidentiary safe harbor.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the House judgment used the wrong measure of injury. The findings of continuing discriminatory effects and polarization remain serious and accepted. Feasibility is assumed favorably. Nine effective Hispanic districts accompany about half the voting-age population in the eighteen primarily-Dade districts; the distinct twenty-district frame yields nine opportunities against about 47 percent Hispanic voting-age population. Those relationships are evidence, not immunity. The Court also considers testimony about split neighborhoods in District 116, concentration in District 114, and fragmentation involving Districts 102 and 109. No finding establishes inconsistent standards for comparable minority and other neighborhoods; such a finding is not a new prerequisite to liability. The credited testimony, history and voting evidence do not establish the asserted unequal opportunity merely because two more districts are feasible. The liability announcement and insistence on eleven districts instead made that possibility obligatory. Gingles requires respect for local findings under Rule 52(a), but does not insulate the legal error defining the ultimate injury. Voinovich and Holder require a proved wrong before federal relief. The dependent remedy consequently falls without adjudicating its precise boundaries or reopening liability.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows the totality standard and Rule 52(a)'s protection of supported findings; corrects a legally infected liability determination without creating a ratio presumption or new intent requirement.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: applies the need for an established federal violation before judicial imposition of remedial districts.

**Controlling proposition:** On the tried Senate record, the asserted unequal opportunity is not established under the totality merely because an additional Hispanic district and a different additional Black district are each feasible; the judgment leaving the State's Senate plan operative is affirmed for lack of a proved violation, not because a proved violation may remain when proposed remedies conflict. ### Washington v. Glucksberg, No. 96-110

Merits decision, June 26, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 8, 1997; decided June 26, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit. Its March 6, 1996 en banc judgment, 79 F.3d 790, invalidated Washington's assisted-suicide prohibition as applied to competent terminally ill adults seeking physician assistance. The question is whether the Due Process Clause protects that asserted right against the prohibition on knowingly causing or aiding suicide. Washington seeks reversal; the physician respondents seek affirmance. Compassion in Dying was a party below and is not a party in this Court. Vacco v. Quill, concerning New York's equal-protection distinction, is separately decided today.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Ninth Circuit's class-wide constitutional invalidation is reversed. On remand it must direct entry of judgment for Washington on the asserted substantive-due-process right to physician-assisted suicide. Forced treatment, valid refusal, lawful pain relief, good-faith palliation and discriminatory administration are outside this disposition.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The asserted physician-assistance right is not fundamental

**Holding and operative rule:** The Due Process Clause does not establish a fundamental right for a competent, terminally ill adult to obtain a willing physician's intentional assistance in suicide through a lethal prescription. The asserted class-wide entitlement therefore receives no heightened protection; a materially different, more particularized claim is not foreclosed merely by this disposition.

**Authority:** The five-Justice opinion of the Court identified above directly supports both holdings and their limits. The other four Justices concur in the judgment on the separate grounds identified in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court addresses the claim actually made: a competent person approaching death seeks willing medical assistance to cause death, without demanding compulsory service or public financing. Its terminal-illness and competence limits matter. Neither an unrestricted wish to die nor a generic appeal to autonomy adequately describes it.

The text and structure of the Due Process Clause, the relevant precedents and the enduring legal treatment of assistance in suicide do not establish this entitlement as a fundamental liberty. The tradition of prohibiting such assistance bears on this claimed right; the Court does not adopt an exclusive rule requiring the most specifically imaginable historical analogue for every constitutional liberty. Cruzan explains refusal of unwanted treatment through bodily integrity, battery and informed consent. It assumed a competent person's right to refuse lifesaving nutrition and hydration and upheld Missouri's evidentiary safeguard for an incompetent patient's wishes. Ending an unwanted intervention does not itself establish permission for another person intentionally to supply lethal means.

Casey retains the protected reproductive decision, individualized viability, timely necessary life-and-health care and its complete six-step regulation standard. Mazurek requires application of that entire standard, including its purpose and residual-interference protections. Those established holdings are not reduced to a generic substantial-obstacle rule or displaced by history alone. They concern a defined reproductive liberty, however, and do not recognize every important medical decision as fundamental. The involvement of a willing professional is not itself dispositive: protected reproductive care also involves assistance. What remains unestablished here is constitutional protection for this distinct intentional lethal act. The refusal and reproductive precedents therefore do not require the asserted extension.

**Limits:** The Court does not decide forced treatment, interference with competent refusal or valid directives, denial of lawful pain care, punishment of good-faith palliation merely because death is foreseen, or discriminatory administration. The reserved claim must be materially different from the class entitlement rejected here; the reservation neither recognizes that entitlement nor creates a new pending action.

##### II. Washington's prohibition rationally serves its protective interests

**Holding and operative rule:** Because the asserted assistance entitlement is not fundamental, Washington's prohibition is valid against this substantive-due-process challenge if it is rationally related to legitimate state interests. It meets that standard by protecting life, preventing suicide, guarding vulnerable people against error and pressure, maintaining medical responsibility and containing the risks of a legalized lethal practice; the Ninth Circuit's class-wide invalidation cannot stand.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice Opinion of the Court supplies direct majority support. This rationality determination completes the rejection of the claim; the absence of a fundamental right alone is not treated as sufficient to dispose of every constitutional objection.

**Controlling explanation:** The State may protect human life without grading the value of particular lives. Terminal illness does not eliminate the risks of mistaken diagnosis, depression, coercion, family pressure, unequal care or indifference toward vulnerable persons. A physician's involvement does not guarantee a reliable determination that a request is informed, responsible and voluntary. Washington may also preserve the professional duty to care and guard against expansion from the proposed class into voluntary or involuntary euthanasia.

The physicians answer that competent adults near death can choose deliberately and that safeguards could confine the practice. The Court does not find that any named person lacked capacity or that any physician acted improperly. The issue is whether the State can reasonably address risks attending authorization of the practice as a class. Difficulty assessing voluntariness, predicting terminal decline and enforcing the proposed boundary supplies the required connection. Rational review does not require certainty that every risk will occur, a finding that every request is involuntary, or proof that prohibition is the best available policy. Nor must the State adopt a less restrictive scheme solely because one can be proposed.

The legal availability of treatment refusal and good-faith pain relief preserves their distinct functions; the challenged prohibition does not require unwanted intervention or forbid palliation because a shorter life is foreseeable. That permission is not a guarantee that every patient's suffering is relieved. States remain free to authorize and regulate assisted death within applicable constitutional limits. This judgment sustains Washington's chosen protective response to the claim before the Court without imposing a national legislative policy.

**Remedy and scope:** The Ninth Circuit must direct entry of judgment for Washington on the asserted substantive-due-process right to physician-assisted suicide. The remand does not authorize lethal prescriptions, command a new patient-specific hearing or adjudicate a materially different claim merely because it could arise.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Department of Health: preserves its bodily-integrity and informed-consent foundation, its assumed competent-refusal premise concerning nutrition and hydration, and its approval of Missouri's clear-and-convincing safeguard for an incompetent patient's wishes. The Court declines to extend that premise to intentional lethal assistance.
- Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey: leaves the protected reproductive choice, individualized viability, required life-and-health protection and complete six-step standard intact. Its established liberty is distinguished from the asserted assisted-suicide entitlement; neither its constitutional foundation nor any regulatory component is overruled.
- Mazurek v. Armstrong: preserves its requirement to apply the full Casey standard, including independent purpose review and the severe- and meaningful-interference inquiries that remain below a substantial obstacle. That abortion-regulation rule is not transplanted into a universal medical-liberty test.

#### Law After Decision

The asserted class-wide entitlement to willing physician assistance in suicide is not a fundamental right, and Washington's prohibition survives rational review against that claim. These are the controlling additions effective June 26, 1997. Existing refusal and reproductive precedents retain their scope. No exclusive history-only method governing all fundamental liberties, universal medical-regulation standard, constitutional pain-care test or prohibition on legislative authorization of assisted death is established. A materially different particularized claim remains subject to its own facts and governing law.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, concurring.** She joins the Court's opinion but does not decide whether a competent person facing imminent death and great suffering has a cognizable interest in controlling those circumstances. Washington permits qualified physicians to alleviate suffering with medication even to unconsciousness and despite foreseeable hastening of death. Given that legal availability, the risks of erroneous judgments about terminal condition, competence and voluntariness sufficiently support the prohibition even if the narrower interest is assumed. Her Cruzan concurrence supports States' initial development of safeguards and democratic consideration. This neither guarantees effective delivery of pain care, eliminates suffering associated with sedation, requires legislative exhaustion nor creates a freestanding constitutional pain-care standard. Ginsburg adopts substantially these separate reasons. Breyer joins the separate analysis except insofar as it joins the principal opinions.

**Stevens, concurring in the judgment.** No person has complete autonomy to end life regardless of others' interests, and the State may maintain a general prohibition. That conclusion does not decide a competent individual's well-supported claim concerning the manner of an unavoidable imminent death. His Cruzan dissent and Casey's defined protected choice support attention to dignity, intolerable suffering and genuine justification. Preservation of life does not automatically outweigh every such interest. Mistake, coercion and professional-integrity concerns justify the general rule but may carry different weight in an exceptional case. Palliation does not logically eliminate every form of suffering or the distinction between conscious relief and terminal sedation. He also questions whether intent and causation invariably distinguish treatment withdrawal, sedation and lethal prescriptions; those overlaps do not establish this class-wide entitlement. He finds no individual entitlement proved here and orders no additional hearing.

**Souter, concurring in the judgment.** Harlan's Poe analysis asks whether the restraint is arbitrary or purposeless in light of the relative importance and fit of the individual and state interests. Traditions retained and abandoned inform that inquiry; neither longstanding prohibition alone nor an abstract autonomy formula resolves it. Responsible terminally ill patients seeking medical counsel and assistance present an important interest connected to bodily integrity, treatment refusal and defined reproductive choice. Casey's complete framework is not a universal medical test. He accepts the competent and voluntary premises and provisionally accepts the professional premises for this claim without inventing incapacity or misconduct.

Protection against mistaken or involuntary suicide and expansion into euthanasia supplies the sufficient justification. Family pressure alone would not settle the matter, because proposed physician screening is intended to answer it. The harder problem is reliably assessing a knowing, responsible decision and ensuring that physicians preserve the asserted boundaries. The disputed evidence concerning safeguards and the Dutch experience establishes a serious empirical uncertainty, not an incontrovertible foreign-law conclusion. Courts cannot presently displace the State's protective judgment with adequate assurance; legislatures can investigate and develop experience.

His conclusion rejects this entitlement now without permanently foreclosing recognition, demanding legislative experiments, imposing a judicial moratorium or assuming inevitable logical expansion of every protected right. Legislative foot-dragging and a materially different individual record remain for another case. Incidental difficulty does not automatically require compelling justification, and not every liberty claim demands elaborate balancing: the importance of the interest and the burden determine the inquiry. His view does not become the Court's general standard.

**Ginsburg, concurring in the judgment.** She adopts substantially O'Connor's separate reasons about the unresolved imminent-death and suffering interest and the legal availability of palliation. She does not join the principal opinion or a declaration sustaining every future application. Her agreement creates no additional independent theory.

**Breyer, concurring in the judgment.** The potential liberty combines personal control over the manner of death, willing medical assistance and avoidance of unnecessary severe physical suffering. Describing it merely as any right to suicide misses the strongest claim without establishing that claim as fundamental. Poe, Cruzan and the defined personal-liberty authorities support serious consideration. Because the law does not forbid the pain treatment central to that interest, the present case need not decide its status; the stated protective interests suffice here. Delivery failures are not the same as a legal prohibition. He recognizes sedation and does not find every person's suffering treatable. A prohibition of necessary palliation would present a materially different case. These reasons supplement his qualified O'Connor join; they do not adopt the principal opinion or one universal liberty method for all judgment supporters.

#### Source Notes

The lower-court judgment is reported at 79 F.3d 790. The state-law operation, public procedural history and party status are reflected in the record recitations at 521 U.S. 702.
