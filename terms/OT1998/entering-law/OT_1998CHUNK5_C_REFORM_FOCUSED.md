#### Libertarian Party of Florida v. Smith

**Docket or dockets:** 96-1867.

**Decided:** 1998-09-21.

##### This objective support criterion is constitutional at the demonstrated burden

**Holding:** A State may allocate this limited party-expense benefit according to an objective five-percent registered-support criterion where lawfully collected fees, continued candidate access, unrestricted private political spending and the demonstrated record establish no severe associational burden, and the measure reasonably serves stable public participation. The holding permits demonstrated-support financing; it does not permit preserving named incumbents or suppressing political viewpoints as independent objectives.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens). Stevens's Part II is joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight direct rationale votes, including the stated limitations.

**Material application:** Anderson v. Celebrezze requires comparing the character and magnitude of actual injury with the State's precise interests and their need for the burden. Burdick v. Takushi applies that inquiry to the complete electoral setting: severe burdens demand narrowly fitted compelling justification; reasonable, nondiscriminatory burdens ordinarily may rest on important regulatory interests. A funding label does not excuse examination of the classification, but a comparative benefit alone does not establish severe restriction.

Buckley v. Valeo, Part III, permits demonstrated-support conditions on election funding and distinguishes withholding a subsidy from excluding a candidacy. Here the objective, potentially changing registration threshold reasonably measures stable party support. Supporting established public participation without subsidizing every splinter organization is a legitimate allocation interest. Wilson's individual ballot qualification does not establish an automatic entitlement to party-wide financial assistance. Norman v. Reed protects independently qualified candidacies; this provision does not remove one from the ballot.

The strongest contrary argument is that selective aid magnifies existing parties' advantages after rivals qualify. Such an allocation remains reviewable for substantial suppression, viewpoint favoritism or discriminatory administration. This record establishes no such burden beyond the limited benefit's existence. Florida's registration measure differs from Buckley's vote criteria, expenditure ceilings and post-election payments; those features are not imported here. The Court does not adopt unqualified rhetoric about discouraging minor parties, a general rational-basis exemption for funding, or validity for every five-percent threshold.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Anderson applies the actual-burden and precise-interest inquiry to this financial classification.
- Burdick preserves burden-sensitive scrutiny; its write-in presumption is not transferred to all subsidies.
- Buckley supplies demonstrated-support funding authority while retaining constitutional limits and political fluidity; the schemes are not identical.
- Norman preserves qualified-component ballot protection without creating automatic party-expense funding.
- Arkansas Educational Television Commission v. Forbes preserves review of genuine support criteria and viewpoint discrimination; its forum classification is not an election-finance rule.
- National Endowment for the Arts v. Finley supplies no universal subsidy immunity; its arts-program holding and divided spending rationales retain their actual scope.

##### Affirmance supplies no new payment or allocation entitlement

**Holding:** Because the presented federal challenge fails, the judgment sustaining §99.103(1)'s limited allocation is affirmed without a rebate, refund or judicially prescribed formula. The lawful collection of candidate fees, access to the ballot and a materially different source-of-funds exaction remain outside the adjudicated challenge.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens). Stevens's Part III is joined by all seven other Associates; eight direct rationale votes.

**Material application:** The challengers sought relief against discriminatory operation of the allocation, while conceding lawful fee collection. The Court's bounded constitutional conclusion supplies no basis for enjoining this scheme or directing new payments. No state-law refund claim, expenditure ceiling or future financing system is decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Buckley remains a source of lawful financing distinctions, not a command to adopt its formula or award money in this case.

Related questions in Libertarian Party of Florida v. Smith, 1998-09-21, dockets 96-1867, appear under Federal Courts — Federal review of coextensive state constitutional reasoning.

## Source: state/holdings/federal-courts.md ? Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992)

#### Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-1424.
**Decided:** June 12, 1992.

##### What evidence of Article III injury, causation and redressability is required at summary judgment

**Holding:** A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction must establish a concrete and particularized, actual or imminent injury, a causal connection fairly traceable to the defendant rather than independent third parties, and a likelihood that the requested relief will redress the injury. At summary judgment, the plaintiff must supply specific facts by affidavit or other evidence on the challenged elements; favorable reasonable inferences remain available, but allegations cannot substitute for the missing evidence.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II of Scalia's opinion of the Court, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; five Justices adopt the elements and litigation-stage evidentiary requirements. Stone concurs in the judgment without joining this section.

**Material application:** Earlier survival of the pleading stage does not establish the specific facts needed at summary judgment. The Court resolves this action on insufficient threatened personal injury.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The general redressability element controls. Its application to absent funding agencies and uncertain foreign-project responses appears only in the three-Justice Part III-B and is not controlling. Favorable reasonable inferences remain available; the decision does not replace Rule 56 or the separate APA programmatic-review holding of National Wildlife Federation.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The merits declaration, mandatory rulemaking relief and dependent §1540(g)(4) fee award are vacated, and the action is remanded for jurisdictional dismissal. Six Justices support dismissal for lack of standing; seven support reversal, with Stevens relying on a noncontrolling statutory ground. No independent fee-entitlement rule or substitute award is adopted.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490 (1975): applied for the plaintiff's personal stake and burden to establish standing; its statutory-rights discussion remains subject to concrete injury.
- Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737 (1984): applied for the constitutional elements and the limits on generalized judicial supervision.
- Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights Organization, 426 U.S. 26 (1976): applied for fair traceability and likely redress; no new majority application concerning these absent agencies follows.
- Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation, 497 U.S. 871 (1990): applied for the evidentiary distinction between pleading and summary judgment; its separate APA programmatic-review holding is not enlarged.
- Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317 (1986), and Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242 (1986): applied to require evidence of challenged essential elements while preserving genuine material disputes and reasonable inferences.

##### Whether past visits, indefinite return intentions and general wildlife interests establish imminent personal injury

**Holding:** Aesthetic, recreational and professional interests in observing wildlife can support standing, but these plaintiffs must show threatened interference with their own concrete use. Prior visits and an indefinite intention to return, without concrete plans or another supported basis for imminent renewed exposure, do not establish standing for these prospective claims; an ecosystem, animal or professional connection that supplies only general interest in distant species does not cure that defect.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part III-A of Scalia's opinion of the Court, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls with five votes. Stone's separate concurrence supplies a sixth vote for the record-specific personal-injury conclusion without a formal opinion join.

**Material application:** The affidavits describe visits to Egyptian and Sri Lankan project areas but no sufficiently definite return undertaking or continuing activity showing imminent exposure. General ecosystem, animal and professional connections supply no missing evidence of these members' own threatened use.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No purchased-ticket or particular-document prerequisite is imposed. Actual use of an affected ecosystem and demonstrated injury transmitted across geographic distance remain undecided. Aesthetic, recreational and professional harm remains cognizable when it affects the claimant's own concrete activity.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Jurisdictional dismissal leaves the statute's geographic reach unresolved and does not preclude a later action supported by sufficient standing facts.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Sierra Club v. Morton, 405 U.S. 727 (1972): applied; aesthetic injury is cognizable, but an organization's special interest cannot replace injury to a member.
- Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Commission, 432 U.S. 333 (1977): applied to require an injured member for this associational claim; no other associational requirement is changed.
- Los Angeles v. Lyons, 461 U.S. 95 (1983): applied for the distinction between past exposure and a present or imminent injury supporting prospective relief.
- Whitmore v. Arkansas, 495 U.S. 149 (1990): applied for actual or imminent rather than conjectural injury.
- Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation, 497 U.S. 871 (1990): applied to specific affected use; no categorical bar to geographically transmitted injury is established.
- Japan Whaling Association v. American Cetacean Society, 478 U.S. 221 (1986): distinguished because its whale-watching and studying members alleged an adverse effect on their own activities; concrete professional or ecological injury remains possible.

##### Whether ESA citizen-suit permission and an asserted procedural right replace threatened concrete injury

**Holding:** ESA §11(g)'s authorization for any person to sue does not give Article III standing to a plaintiff asserting only the public's common interest in lawful administration. A procedural right may protect a plaintiff's threatened concrete interest without requiring proof that the procedure will certainly change the ultimate agency decision, but the procedural label cannot replace that personal interest; these respondents have not shown the required connection.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part IV of Scalia's opinion of the Court, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; five Justices adopt the citizen-suit and concrete-interest rule. Stone does not join this section.

**Material application:** Section 11(g), 16 U.S.C. §1540(g), permits enforcement but identifies no concrete injury to these members independent of the unsupported future use. The public's common interest in lawful administration cannot supply the missing personal connection.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Congress may recognize concrete factual injuries previously inadequate in law; the outer reach of that power is not settled. Procedural protections tied to a threatened concrete interest remain cognizable without certainty of a changed ultimate decision. Neither §7(a)(2)'s geographic reach nor the binding effect of a revised rule on absent agencies is decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The action is dismissed for want of Article III standing; no new consultation rule or cancellation of any foreign project is ordered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Fairchild v. Hughes, 258 U.S. 126 (1922): applied to reject a general right to compel lawful administration as a sufficient personal injury.
- Massachusetts v. Mellon, 262 U.S. 447 (1923), and Ex parte Levitt, 302 U.S. 633 (1937): applied for the distinction between direct injury and an undifferentiated public grievance.
- United States v. Richardson, 418 U.S. 166 (1974), and Schlesinger v. Reservists Committee to Stop the War, 418 U.S. 208 (1974): applied to generally shared governmental grievances without distinctive concrete harm.
- Valley Forge Christian College v. Americans United for Separation of Church and State, Inc., 454 U.S. 464 (1982), and Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737 (1984): applied; a claimed right to lawful government conduct alone does not satisfy Article III.
- Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490 (1975), and Sierra Club v. Morton, 405 U.S. 727 (1972): preserved for Congress's power to recognize injuries and broaden protected interests while retaining the plaintiff's own concrete injury.
- Trafficante v. Metropolitan Life Insurance Co., 409 U.S. 205 (1972), and Hardin v. Kentucky Utilities Co., 390 U.S. 1 (1968): distinguished as statutory recognition of concrete personal interests, not authorization of a wholly abstract grievance.

##### Equal opportunity to compete is a concrete injury

**Holding:** An association may challenge a governmental barrier preventing members who are able and ready to bid from competing on equal terms, without proving that a member would have won a contract, when a member has individual standing, the interest is germane to the association’s purpose, and neither the claim nor the requested relief requires individual-member participation. The unchallenged allegations accepted here establish the required connection; genuinely disputed elements remain subject to stage-appropriate proof.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, Equal opportunity to compete is a concrete injury portion; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Turner v. Fouche, 396 U.S. 346 — equal eligibility applied.
- Bakke, 438 U.S. 265 — opportunity injury distinguished from guaranteed selection.
- Warth, 422 U.S. 490 — concrete causal connection retained.
- Hunt, 432 U.S. 333 — association requirements retained.
- Lujan, June 12, 1992 — concrete injury and stage-specific proof preserved.

##### The replacement continues the controversy

**Holding:** Repeal does not moot this dispute because the successor’s sheltered-market method continues to exclude the association’s members from particular competitions on the challenged basis. Changes in groups, percentages and methods do not remove that injury; a replacement actually ending it could produce a different result.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, The replacement continues the controversy portion; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Stage-appropriate proof remains necessary for genuinely disputed elements. No equal-protection merits rule, guaranteed contract or categorical rule about every replacement enactment is established.

##### Whether individual Members may challenge abstract institutional dilution after fully effective votes

**Holding:** Individual Members of Congress whose votes were counted and given full effect lack Article III standing to challenge the Line Item Veto Act on the asserted abstract dilution of Congress's institutional power and alteration of future votes. ##### The Commission lacks independent certiorari authority for this FECA enforcement action

**Holding:** Section 437d(a)(6)'s authority to litigate and appeal civil enforcement actions through the Commission's general counsel does not displace 28 U.S.C. §518(a)'s allocation of Supreme Court representation in this FECA corporate-contribution action. **Holding:** Even assuming that the Solicitor General's May 26, 1994 authorization fully adopted the Commission's January 18 petition, it cannot relate back to validate this unauthorized invocation after the period in which an authorized petition could be filed had expired. **Operative remedy or transition:** The writ is dismissed for want of jurisdiction. The D.C. Circuit judgment remains in place without Supreme Court endorsement of its constitutional merits. ## Exact register entry: Concrete personal interests and procedural citizen-suit standing

### Concrete personal interests and procedural citizen-suit standing

**Governing question:** Article III standing for a citizen-suit plaintiff alleging a deprivation of procedure, including ESA §11(g) enforcement.

**Current rule:** A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction must establish a concrete and particularized, actual or imminent injury; a causal connection fairly traceable to the defendant's challenged conduct rather than to independent third parties' own decisions; and a likelihood, not mere speculation, that the requested relief will redress the injury. Authorization for any person to sue does not supply standing for only the public's undifferentiated interest in lawful administration. A procedural right must protect the plaintiff's own threatened concrete interest; when that connection exists, the plaintiff need not prove that the required procedure will certainly alter the ultimate agency decision. Aesthetic, recreational or professional wildlife interests may qualify, but past visits and indefinite return intentions without concrete plans or another supported basis for imminent renewed exposure do not establish threatened interference with personal use. At summary judgment the plaintiff must support challenged standing elements with specific facts by affidavit or other evidence, with favorable reasonable inferences available; allegations cannot replace missing evidence.

**Authority by component:**
- Stage-specific evidentiary requirement and existing injury, traceability and redressability elements: Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, “1. Article III elements must be supported at the litigation stage”, June 12, 1992; Scalia Part II, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas (five).
- Prospective personal-use boundary: Lujan, “2. Indefinite return intentions and general species interests do not establish threatened personal injury here,” June 12, 1992; Scalia Part III-A, same five-Justice opinion; Stone separately supports the record-specific conclusion.
- Citizen-suit and procedural-right boundary: Lujan, “3. Citizen-suit authorization does not dispense with a threatened concrete interest,” June 12, 1992; Scalia Part IV, same five Justices.

**Present operation effective:** Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, June 12, 1992, for the identified citizen-suit and procedural-right clarification; ordinary Article III elements and evidentiary burdens are retained.

## Exact register entry: Presented questions, subsidiary arguments and exceptional Supreme Court review

### Presented questions, subsidiary arguments and exceptional Supreme Court review

**Governing question:** When an issue outside the stated certiorari question may be considered.

**Current rule:** Rule 14.1(a) creates a strong prudential presumption against deciding questions neither stated in the petition nor fairly included, subject to exceptional circumstances. A distinct intervention denial is not fairly included merely because reversing it would permit reaching a settlement-vacatur question; discussion in the petition’s body and merits briefing does not alone suffice. A consistently asserted Teague defense may be subsidiary where it limits application of the very constitutional rule and habeas relief challenged by the petition. Geographic tailoring is fairly included in a challenge to exclusion from designated speech zones. An exceptional extension requires its own supported basis; adversarial briefing, preservation, logical priority or usefulness alone does not enlarge the questions. The duty to notice jurisdictional defects remains separate. A respondent may defend the existing favorable judgment on a record-supported alternative rejected below when doing so neither enlarges its own rights nor reduces the adversary’s rights under that judgment. The Court may also elect to consider a deliberately disavowed legal argument supporting the same preserved claim when it falls within the question presented and can be resolved on the established record without a new factual theory or additional findings. That discretion imposes no duty to consider abandoned grounds and does not erase limits on distinct claims, relief, factual stipulations or missing facts.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Souter, concurring in the judgment, joined by Ginsburg.** Official injury is not categorically excluded by Article III: Coleman and authorized institutional representation prevent that shortcut. Whether this injury is personal rather than official, or concrete rather than abstract, is fairly debatable. Souter instead gives decisive weight to Allen's separation-of-powers foundation and Valley Forge's restraint against unnecessary constitutional adjudication. The Members seek judicial intervention in an unauthorized internal and interbranch contest, while a beneficiary deprived of an actual spending or tax benefit may present a conventional controversy satisfying injury, causation and redressability. These conditions make the present claim constitutionally insufficient. This is neither prudential abstention, an absolute political-question bar, nor a universal requirement that another plaintiff exist.

**Stevens, dissenting.** A Member's opportunity to vote on the law's ultimate text is an individual constitutional function. The Act's present alteration of that function is concrete without waiting for a particular cancellation; Coleman supports protection of official voting power, and Bender's prohibition on unauthorized representation does not defeat a Member's own injury. Stevens finds the claim ripe and would affirm the declaration on Article I grounds. The Act permits the President, after a bill becomes law, to cancel in whole a defined discretionary dollar amount, new-direct-spending item or limited tax benefit under its conditions. Chadha requires bicameralism and presentment for repeal. Giving cancellation a different name or intelligible standards does not turn removal of enacted legal force into execution; Field's contingent execution and ordinary impoundment do not answer the distinct spending-obligation and tax effects. His ground is allocation of lawmaking authority, not a finding that the delegation's standards are insufficiently intelligible. He adds no private presidential-reservation theory and decides no later cancellation remedy.

The cancellation permission requires determinations that the action reduces the deficit, does not impair essential Government functions and does not harm the national interest; the special message must be sent within five calendar days excluding Sundays. Cancellation takes effect upon both Houses' receipt; enacted disapproval nullifies it and restores the provision from its original effective date. A disapproval bill cannot itself be canceled. Disapproval requires legislation, and the special procedures do not abolish ordinary later legislation. The Act has no force or effect on or after January 1, 2005. None of these limitations, in Stevens's view, changes the Article I character of a repeal after enactment.

**Breyer, dissenting.** The systematic impairment of the Members' existing constitutional work is sufficiently concrete. Coleman does not limit official injury to one decisive voting event, and express congressional authorization removes a prudential barrier while reducing separation-of-powers concerns; it does not enlarge Article III. The prospect of a later private suit cannot erase present injury if that injury exists. Breyer finds standing and ripeness and would proceed to the merits. He expressly reserves the Act's constitutionality and does not join Stevens's merits conclusion or vote to sustain the Act.

## Current-term standing component from Commerce / Glavin

### I. A supported threat to personal representation permits Glavin review

**Holding and operative rule:** A voter may challenge an announced census method before the final apportionment when summary-judgment evidence sufficiently supports a concrete threat to that voter's representation, causally attributable to the method and likely redressable by relief against responsible officials. Hofmeister's Indiana representation claim meets that standard; neither statutory authorization alone nor an unsupported prediction would suffice.

**Authority:** Souter's Part I, joined by all eight other Justices, controls.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Weber's comparison of Bureau projections and measured differential undercounts identifies a particular marginal Indiana seat at risk. Wetrogan's updated projections and Thompson's objections concerning redesigned methods, uncertainty and overseas personnel qualify the forecast but do not eliminate the supported threat attributable to sampling. *Lujan* requires evidence appropriate to summary judgment, not merely pleading a possible injury; it does not require certainty of the eventual allocation. *Franklin* connects representation injury to relief directed at census officials without requiring a completed census before prevention may be sought. Section 209 makes the plan reviewable and provides expedition, but does not manufacture injury. The Court adopts no finding of conceded or certain seat loss and need not decide county-funding, intrastate-districting or feared invalid-census/reversion theories. Each would require its own personal and causal showing.

## Current-term standing component from Commerce / Glavin

### II. The House has not established its own institutional injury

**Holding and operative rule:** Statutory authorization for the House to sue does not establish Article III injury where the asserted harm is disagreement with the lawfulness of a future census methodology, without withheld information or a demonstrated present impairment of institutional power. This House action must be dismissed without prejudice, and its judgment and injunction vacated.

**Authority:** Souter's Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg, supplies six controlling rationale votes. Stone-Zsela supplies a seventh judgment vote only. Stevens and Breyer dissent from this component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the authorized House is a different plaintiff from the individual legislators in *Raines*, which distinguishes authorization from the existence of a concrete constitutional injury. The House will receive an apportionment report; the dispute concerns the legality of its preparation, not the withholding of particular information for an established use. *Akins* protects actual deprivation of statutorily required electoral information and does not convert every methodology dispute into informational injury. The House retains 435 seats, while the composition theory concerns later electorates and membership without present nullification of its votes or demonstrated impairment of concrete work. The House's continuing identity and statutory authorization therefore do not cure the missing injury. The Court adopts no categorical bar on institutional suits and reserves actual information refusals, subpoena enforcement, property injury and complete vote nullification for their own records. Standing in Glavin cannot be transferred to this separate plaintiff.
