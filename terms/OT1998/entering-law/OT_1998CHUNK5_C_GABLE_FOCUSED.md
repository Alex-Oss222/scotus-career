## Source: state/holdings/first-amendment.md ? Day v. Holahan

#### Day v. Holahan

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-672.
**Decided:** May 15, 1995.

##### Authority of the responsive-financing judgment

**Authority of the judgment:** The section 10A.25(13) responsive-financing invalidation is affirmed 5–4: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support the judgment. Kennedy's plurality, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, treats the adverse proportional legal trigger as an expressive burden; Stone-Zsela's judgment concurrence rests on insufficient programmatic connection on this record and rejects categorical invalidity. Neither framework controls, and no controlling rationale is established under Marks. The judgment binds the parties; the three independently majority-supported holdings below do not supply a unified financing rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The invalidated section 10A.25(13) mechanism increases candidate limits by independent expenditures opposing the candidate plus expenditures for the candidate's major-party opponents, excluding an association's communications solely to its dues-paying members. The provision itself has no $100 threshold; that figure describes the lower court's operational reporting scenario. The Board must notify every candidate in the race within 48 hours after receipt of the report or notice under section 10A.20(3), (6) or (6b), and within three days after notice must pay half the expenditure only to a subsidy-eligible candidate who has raised twice the minimum match, from an appropriation from the general fund. The lower court's electoral-result-eligibility concern describes implementation; it is neither a statutory payment exception nor a finding that every subsidy is immediately payable.

##### The special immediate-notice obligation falls with this trigger

**Holding:** Section 10A.20, subdivision 6b's additional immediate affidavit and service obligation cannot be enforced independently of the invalid responsive-financing mechanism on this scheme's demonstrated function and legislative relationship. Under Minnesota section 645.20, the special mechanism is essentially and inseparably connected with the invalid provision such that the legislature cannot be presumed to have enacted it alone; ordinary campaign reports remain enforceable within their separate terms.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct votes. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissent on this component.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The additional obligation is a twenty-four-hour affidavit and service on the candidates and campaign treasurers in the affected race. The holding rests on essential legislative connection under section 645.20, not physical impossibility of reporting. The independent vagueness and overbreadth theories are not reached.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Buckley, 424 U.S. 1: preserves distinct disclosure interests and associational protections; no categorical disclosure invalidation follows.
- Gibson, 372 U.S. 539: applies the need for a substantial relation between information demanded and the legitimate subject; does not eliminate ordinary reports.
- Brown v. Socialist Workers, 459 U.S. 87: recognizes genuine disclosure interests with protection for demonstrated associational harms; no new general exemption is created.

##### This $100 intermediary-contribution ceiling lacks sufficient fit

**Holding:** Minnesota's $100 annual ceiling on contributions received by the covered political committees and political funds is unconstitutional on the established burden and insufficiently fitted corruption/circumvention justification. Contributions remain distinct from independent expenditures: realistic routes around valid candidate limits may be regulated, but a broadly restrictive ceiling must preserve meaningful collective political association and fit an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism; no permanent dollar minimum follows.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes, at this stated functional application.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 10A.27(12) covers aggregate annual contributions from individuals, political committees and political funds, but excludes a candidate's principal campaign committee and a political party unit defined in section 10A.275 from its covered recipients. Higher limits, coordination controls, candidate transfers and demonstrated conduit restrictions remain open. The record's substantial pooling burden does not establish a permanent constitutional dollar floor or equate contributions with independent expenditures.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Buckley: applies contribution/expenditure distinction, corruption fit and effective collective advocacy; its historical dollar figure is no minimum.
- California Medical, 453 U.S. 182: preserves candidate-transfer anticircumvention regulation; its plurality is not a categorical answer for every committee.
- Citizens Against Rent Control, 454 U.S. 290: applies protection of pooling for collective political expression; does not immunize a candidate-contribution conduit.
- NCPAC, 470 U.S. 480: preserves independent advocacy at its scope; contributions are not silently equated with expenditures.

##### MCCL receives protection on its actual nonprofit characteristics

**Holding:** Minnesota may not prohibit MCCL's independent expenditures solely because its incidental business activity and absence of a formal policy against significant corporate contributions fall outside section 211B.15, subdivision 15's literal safe harbor. On the undisputed minimal-business-income and insignificant-corporate-funding record, its relevant political-association characteristics support as-applied MCFL protection; material commercial accumulation, economic claimants or corporate/union conduit funding requires a different assessment.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by all eight Associates: nine direct votes on this as-applied holding. No general overruling or endorsement beyond Austin's surviving scope is adopted.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The section 211B.15(15) safe harbor requires no business activity, no economic shareholders or claimants, no establishment by a business corporation or union, and a policy against significant contributions from them. Protection rests on MCCL's actual minimal business income and undisputed insignificant corporate funding. That checklist cannot foreclose this as-applied protection; materially changed operations or corporate or union financing may require reassessment. Direct corporate candidate contributions and ordinary business-corporation treasury restrictions are not decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- MCFL, 479 U.S. 238: applies protection to actual political-resource, structure and funding characteristics; descriptive facts do not foreclose this narrow as-applied claim.
- Austin, 494 U.S. 652: distinguishes materially different commercially accumulated corporate wealth; its surviving corporate-treasury rule is not overruled.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the Eighth Circuit on all four components. Maintain the permanent restraints on section 10A.25(13)'s responsive financing, section 10A.20(6b)'s linked special notice and section 10A.27(12)'s $100 annual ceiling, and MCCL's bounded as-applied protection under section 211B.15(15). Ordinary reports and otherwise lawful corporate and contribution regulation remain; no damages, general public-financing prohibition or facial excision of corporate restrictions follows.

## Source: state/holdings/first-amendment.md ? Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. Federal Election Commission, 518 U.S. 604 (1996)

#### Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. Federal Election Commission, 518 U.S. 604 (1996)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-489.
**Decided:** June 26, 1996.

##### Whether the Senate party-expenditure ceiling may reach this independent advertisement

**Holding:** A political party's own campaign advertisement that is genuinely independent of a candidate may not be subjected to the Senate-election expenditure ceiling in 2 U.S.C. §441a(d)(3) merely because its speaker is an official political party. Party identity and a continuing electoral relationship do not, without more, justify applying that ceiling to this independent expression; the adverse enforcement judgment must therefore be vacated.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s Part I, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; six direct votes.

**Material application:** The party’s $15,000 pre-nomination advertisement was genuinely independent. Its prior assignment of the whole $103,248 adjusted election allowance explains statutory excess, not constitutional validity. Legitimate corruption and circumvention concerns do not establish an actual candidate connection from party identity alone.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No majority adopts the complete narrower expenditure framework or its prudential reservation rationale (O’Connor, Souter, Breyer), or Kennedy/Scalia/Thomas’s broader coordinated-party-expression protection. No Marks combination is used. Coordinated expression, direct candidate cash, earmarks, republication, sham independence and actual conduits are neither generally immunized nor upheld by implication. No universal bribery-proof, express-advocacy formula, constitutional dollar floor, vagueness or administrative-deference rule.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Adverse enforcement/penalty mandate vacated and remanded, 6–3. The broader Senate-election counterclaim receives no majority constitutional determination; no fixed penalty, damages, refund or compelled new factual trial.

##### Whether party identity supplies the connection required by the alternative contribution theory

**Holding:** For the government's theory that this advertisement is a contribution under §441a(a)(7)(B)(i), party status alone cannot substitute for expenditure made in cooperation, consultation or concert with, or at the request or suggestion of, a candidate, the candidate's authorized political committees or their agents. On the accepted independence premise, that theory cannot preserve the adverse enforcement judgment under §441a(a)(2).

**Proposition-level authority:** The same six-Justice Part I coalition.

**Material application:** A categorical agency characterization is not a factual finding of consultation; competing assertions about novelty and noncontest establish neither forfeiture nor concession. Transfers among national, State, district, local and subordinate same-party committees are exempt from §441a(a)(1) and (2). Candidate-material dissemination, distribution or republication under (a)(7)(B)(ii), and original-source attribution for earmarked/intermediary contributions under (a)(8), have distinct predicates absent here.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The constitutionality of a transaction with an actual connection, all interactions with (d)(1)’s notwithstanding clause, presidential allowances and other House limits are not decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1:** Its distinction between independent expenditures and candidate contributions supplies the present independent-advertisement rule. Its treatment of the party provision's Fifth Amendment challenge did not decide this First Amendment question. The general distinction remains in force.
- **FEC v. National Conservative Political Action Committee, 470 U.S. 480:** Its protection of genuinely independent political advocacy supports the result; it supplies no categorical immunity for actual contribution channels.
- **Day v. Holahan, May 15, 1995:** Its controlling protection of meaningful intermediary association and preservation of realistic conduit regulation inform the required distinction. Its recipient category excluded political-party units, and its responsive-financing judgment had no unified controlling rationale. Neither is converted into an earlier decision of this party-spending question.
- **FEC v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life, Inc., 479 U.S. 238:** Its protection of political expression and its nonprofit setting remain distinct; the Court does not rest today's judgment on a universal express-advocacy formula or extend the nonprofit exception to every entity.

## Source: state/holdings/election-law.md ? Kentucky Right to Life, Inc. v. Stengel

#### Kentucky Right to Life, Inc. v. Stengel

**Docket or dockets:** 97-60; Sixth Circuit No. 95-6581.

**Decided:** 1998-09-14.

##### Nonprofit status does not require a categorical exemption for corporate candidate gifts

**Holding:** Kentucky may apply §121.025's direct candidate-contribution restriction to KRL's general corporate treasury without creating a categorical exemption merely because KRL is an ideological nonprofit. The rejection of that facial exemption does not authorize restrictions on protected independent advocacy or determine whether KRL satisfies an independent-expenditure funding condition.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part III of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices.

**Material application:** The Court holds that a payment to a candidate remains different from an association's own expression. NRWC supports prophylactic treatment of the corporate contribution channel, and MCFL expressly distinguishes direct contributions from the independent spending it protects. The destination of the payment and the risk of candidate obligation supply the relevant ground; the State need not first prove that this corporation completed a corrupt transaction. KRLPAC supplies a separate voluntary path for candidate-directed support, while lawful independent advocacy remains distinct. Day protects an association according to its actual resources and structure; its nonprofit holding expressly left direct corporate gifts undecided. The contrary categorical exemption would erase that distinction. The Court does not find KRL to be a commercial treasury or conduit, decide its funding eligibility, or endorse every restriction that can be attached to incorporation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** NRWC applies to the contribution channel; MCFL's independent-expression protection remains intact. Day's nonprofit holding is distinguished according to the transaction it decided. Austin is neither overruled nor enlarged beyond its surviving corporate-treasury scope.

##### The paid candidate-advertisement identification rule survives this facial challenge

**Holding:** The surviving facial challenge to §121.190(1)'s payer-identification requirement fails in the paid candidate-finance and organizational setting presented. The rule requires the payer's name and address, introduced by the paid-for-by designation, on newspaper and magazine advertising, posters, circulars, billboards, handbills, sample ballots and paid television or radio announcements expressly advocating election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate, slate or group of candidates; ordinary issue advocacy is not covered merely because it mentions a candidate.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part IV of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight Justices. Thomas dissents.

**Material application:** The Court holds that identifying the financer of organized paid candidate advocacy serves source-tracing and enforcement interests different from general curiosity about an ordinary issue leafleteer's identity. The address helps identify the legal payer and connect an advertisement to financial transactions; it is a burden requiring justification, not dispensable statutory language. Buckley's disclosure interests and McIntyre's express reservation of materially different campaign-finance and organizational settings permit this bounded facial judgment. The on-message command is more intrusive than filing a report, but the actual financer-identification function supplies the necessary connection here without limiting the amount or content of the independent expenditure. The State cannot presume corruption or coordination merely from an advertisement's independence or political identity. McIntyre continues to protect ordinary anonymous issue leaflets, and materially different individual applications remain open. So does a claim supported by a reasonable probability of threats or reprisals. No actual retaliation, coordination, general membership-disclosure authority or requirement of permission before publication is found.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** McIntyre is distinguished on its reserved candidate-finance and organizational context without diminishing its individual-leaflet rule. Buckley supplies the distinct finance-disclosure interests. Colorado Republican preserves independence and forbids identity-based substitution for an actual candidate connection. Brown v. Socialist Workers preserves protection against demonstrated associational harms without creating a new blanket exemption.

##### The preserved challenges to the two direct $1,000 limits fail

**Holding:** The preserved facial challenge to §121.150(6)'s $1,000 giving and acceptance limit in any one election is rejected under the direct-contribution distinction. The separate challenge to §121A.050(1)'s $1,000 slate limit in any one election is likewise rejected, preserving that section's knowing-acceptance and knowing-giving terms and the giving prohibition's express exception for independent expenditures as statutorily defined.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part V of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; the coalition covers both distinct provisions.

**Material application:** The Court holds that the surviving direct-transfer challenges can be resolved under Buckley's contribution rule without treating contribution limits as constitutionally unlimited. A direct payment to the candidate presents a corruption and circumvention concern different from financing one's own advocacy. The lower court reported that counsel had proposed the $1,000 figure and essentially conceded its constitutionality. That reported position confines the preserved controversy; it is not a general unseen waiver or a reason to accept an incorrect legal rule. The direct-transfer character and bounded claim supply the judgment. The Court does not move the slate provision's knowledge requirement or independent-expenditure exception into a different section. Nor does it turn a per-election limit into an annual one, establish $1,000 as a constitutional minimum or safe harbor for every office, or authorize limits meant to equalize political influence. Future demonstrated cumulative or prohibitory burdens remain distinct.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Buckley's direct-contribution distinction and fitted anticorruption rationale apply; its historical figure is not a constitutional floor. Day preserves scrutiny of meaningful association and cumulative burdens without equating contributions with independent expenditures.

##### The annual all-intermediary aggregate requires the inquiry the lower court bypassed

**Holding:** A $1,500 ceiling on one person's annual contributions to all permanent committees and contributing organizations together must be assessed for meaningful collective political association and fit to an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism. Because the lower judgment sustained §121.150(10) without giving that inquiry its required force, the judgment on this component is vacated and remanded for application of Day to the existing record and ordinary remaining remedial questions.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part VI of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer: six Justices. Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Ginsburg dissent from this remand.

**Material application:** The Court holds that the ability to regulate a real candidate-funding conduit does not answer the fit of an aggregate that exhausts one allowance across unrelated recipients. Day requires meaningful opportunity to pool resources for political expression. California Medical permits prevention of circumvention through candidate-transfer channels, but its four-Justice low-protection reasoning is not a controlling categorical answer to every intermediary contribution. KRLPAC's candidate transfers identify a legitimate concern; they do not establish the fit of the law's complete combined-recipient reach. The lower court must consider actual recipient functions, the aggregate's breadth, the supported burden and the separate opportunity for direct candidate gifts under the proper standard. A higher figure than Day's $100 does not end the inquiry, and a general reference to smaller state campaigns does not supply it. Conversely, the Court finds neither every application invalid nor the State's evidentiary showing absent. This is a legal-standard remand, not a compulsory new trial, discovery order, new burden of proof, constitutional dollar minimum or direction to exempt all independent recipients automatically.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Day applies its meaningful-association and fit rule beyond the particular $100 ceiling while retaining its limits. California Medical preserves genuine conduit regulation without converting plurality reasoning into law. Citizens Against Rent Control protects collective expression; NCPAC preserves independent advocacy without immunizing candidate contributions.

##### Both gubernatorial receipt branches survive KRLPAC's facial challenge

**Holding:** KRLPAC's facial challenge to both §121A.030(4) receipt limits is rejected: the Fund participant's permanent-committee receipts are limited to 25 percent of qualifying contributions received, up to $150,000 per election, while the nonparticipant is subject to the separate $150,000 ceiling. Direct candidate receipts present a source-specific dependency concern, and this facial record does not demonstrate obstruction of effective advocacy or association; concrete later burdens under either branch remain open.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part VII of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: six Justices. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would vacate and remand both branches.

**Material application:** The Court holds that candidate receipts from one organized financing channel differ from the annual pool across independent recipients addressed in Part VI. Buckley's contribution distinction permits a fitted response to dependence on that channel without requiring proof of a completed corrupt bargain. The percentage branch limits the share measured against the participant's own qualifying contributions; it remains operative at receipts below the maximum. The nonparticipant branch uses its separate fixed ceiling. The district court reported no indication that the provision had actually limited PAC contributions or was likely to do so on this record. That reported conclusion supports this facial judgment; it is not a statutory exception or an irrebuttable fact about every race. The contrary concern that earlier donors may exhaust a later association's opportunity is substantial but does not establish facial invalidity here. Neither the maximum-fundraising example nor the number of committees needed to reach a maximum is a premise of this judgment. The Court does not rely on consent to unknown funding conditions, disfavor of PAC views, or equalization. Later proof of ordinary fundraising disabled by the percentage at low receipts, selective foreclosure or prohibitory cumulative effects requires separate consideration.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Buckley's direct-contribution rationale applies at this source-specific and facial level. Day preserves meaningful association and rejects a permanent dollar safe harbor; its different annual-intermediary and responsive-financing components do not resolve these candidate-receipt rules. Colorado Republican preserves independent advocacy without converting direct candidate receipts into independent spending.

## Source: state/holdings/federal-courts.md ? Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992)

#### Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-1424.
**Decided:** June 12, 1992.

##### What evidence of Article III injury, causation and redressability is required at summary judgment

**Holding:** A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction must establish a concrete and particularized, actual or imminent injury, a causal connection fairly traceable to the defendant rather than independent third parties, and a likelihood that the requested relief will redress the injury. At summary judgment, the plaintiff must supply specific facts by affidavit or other evidence on the challenged elements; favorable reasonable inferences remain available, but allegations cannot substitute for the missing evidence.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II of Scalia's opinion of the Court, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; five Justices adopt the elements and litigation-stage evidentiary requirements. Stone concurs in the judgment without joining this section.

**Material application:** Earlier survival of the pleading stage does not establish the specific facts needed at summary judgment. The Court resolves this action on insufficient threatened personal injury.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The general redressability element controls. Its application to absent funding agencies and uncertain foreign-project responses appears only in the three-Justice Part III-B and is not controlling. Favorable reasonable inferences remain available; the decision does not replace Rule 56 or the separate APA programmatic-review holding of National Wildlife Federation.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The merits declaration, mandatory rulemaking relief and dependent §1540(g)(4) fee award are vacated, and the action is remanded for jurisdictional dismissal. Six Justices support dismissal for lack of standing; seven support reversal, with Stevens relying on a noncontrolling statutory ground. No independent fee-entitlement rule or substitute award is adopted.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490 (1975): applied for the plaintiff's personal stake and burden to establish standing; its statutory-rights discussion remains subject to concrete injury.
- Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737 (1984): applied for the constitutional elements and the limits on generalized judicial supervision.
- Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights Organization, 426 U.S. 26 (1976): applied for fair traceability and likely redress; no new majority application concerning these absent agencies follows.
- Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation, 497 U.S. 871 (1990): applied for the evidentiary distinction between pleading and summary judgment; its separate APA programmatic-review holding is not enlarged.
- Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317 (1986), and Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242 (1986): applied to require evidence of challenged essential elements while preserving genuine material disputes and reasonable inferences.

##### Whether past visits, indefinite return intentions and general wildlife interests establish imminent personal injury

**Holding:** Aesthetic, recreational and professional interests in observing wildlife can support standing, but these plaintiffs must show threatened interference with their own concrete use. Prior visits and an indefinite intention to return, without concrete plans or another supported basis for imminent renewed exposure, do not establish standing for these prospective claims; an ecosystem, animal or professional connection that supplies only general interest in distant species does not cure that defect.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part III-A of Scalia's opinion of the Court, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls with five votes. Stone's separate concurrence supplies a sixth vote for the record-specific personal-injury conclusion without a formal opinion join.

**Material application:** The affidavits describe visits to Egyptian and Sri Lankan project areas but no sufficiently definite return undertaking or continuing activity showing imminent exposure. General ecosystem, animal and professional connections supply no missing evidence of these members' own threatened use.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No purchased-ticket or particular-document prerequisite is imposed. Actual use of an affected ecosystem and demonstrated injury transmitted across geographic distance remain undecided. Aesthetic, recreational and professional harm remains cognizable when it affects the claimant's own concrete activity.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Jurisdictional dismissal leaves the statute's geographic reach unresolved and does not preclude a later action supported by sufficient standing facts.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Sierra Club v. Morton, 405 U.S. 727 (1972): applied; aesthetic injury is cognizable, but an organization's special interest cannot replace injury to a member.
- Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Commission, 432 U.S. 333 (1977): applied to require an injured member for this associational claim; no other associational requirement is changed.
- Los Angeles v. Lyons, 461 U.S. 95 (1983): applied for the distinction between past exposure and a present or imminent injury supporting prospective relief.
- Whitmore v. Arkansas, 495 U.S. 149 (1990): applied for actual or imminent rather than conjectural injury.
- Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation, 497 U.S. 871 (1990): applied to specific affected use; no categorical bar to geographically transmitted injury is established.
- Japan Whaling Association v. American Cetacean Society, 478 U.S. 221 (1986): distinguished because its whale-watching and studying members alleged an adverse effect on their own activities; concrete professional or ecological injury remains possible.

##### Whether ESA citizen-suit permission and an asserted procedural right replace threatened concrete injury

**Holding:** ESA §11(g)'s authorization for any person to sue does not give Article III standing to a plaintiff asserting only the public's common interest in lawful administration. A procedural right may protect a plaintiff's threatened concrete interest without requiring proof that the procedure will certainly change the ultimate agency decision, but the procedural label cannot replace that personal interest; these respondents have not shown the required connection.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part IV of Scalia's opinion of the Court, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; five Justices adopt the citizen-suit and concrete-interest rule. Stone does not join this section.

**Material application:** Section 11(g), 16 U.S.C. §1540(g), permits enforcement but identifies no concrete injury to these members independent of the unsupported future use. The public's common interest in lawful administration cannot supply the missing personal connection.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Congress may recognize concrete factual injuries previously inadequate in law; the outer reach of that power is not settled. Procedural protections tied to a threatened concrete interest remain cognizable without certainty of a changed ultimate decision. Neither §7(a)(2)'s geographic reach nor the binding effect of a revised rule on absent agencies is decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The action is dismissed for want of Article III standing; no new consultation rule or cancellation of any foreign project is ordered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Fairchild v. Hughes, 258 U.S. 126 (1922): applied to reject a general right to compel lawful administration as a sufficient personal injury.
- Massachusetts v. Mellon, 262 U.S. 447 (1923), and Ex parte Levitt, 302 U.S. 633 (1937): applied for the distinction between direct injury and an undifferentiated public grievance.
- United States v. Richardson, 418 U.S. 166 (1974), and Schlesinger v. Reservists Committee to Stop the War, 418 U.S. 208 (1974): applied to generally shared governmental grievances without distinctive concrete harm.
- Valley Forge Christian College v. Americans United for Separation of Church and State, Inc., 454 U.S. 464 (1982), and Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737 (1984): applied; a claimed right to lawful government conduct alone does not satisfy Article III.
- Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490 (1975), and Sierra Club v. Morton, 405 U.S. 727 (1972): preserved for Congress's power to recognize injuries and broaden protected interests while retaining the plaintiff's own concrete injury.
- Trafficante v. Metropolitan Life Insurance Co., 409 U.S. 205 (1972), and Hardin v. Kentucky Utilities Co., 390 U.S. 1 (1968): distinguished as statutory recognition of concrete personal interests, not authorization of a wholly abstract grievance.

## Exact register entry: Concrete personal interests and procedural citizen-suit standing

### Concrete personal interests and procedural citizen-suit standing

**Governing question:** Article III standing for a citizen-suit plaintiff alleging a deprivation of procedure, including ESA §11(g) enforcement.

**Current rule:** A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction must establish a concrete and particularized, actual or imminent injury; a causal connection fairly traceable to the defendant's challenged conduct rather than to independent third parties' own decisions; and a likelihood, not mere speculation, that the requested relief will redress the injury. Authorization for any person to sue does not supply standing for only the public's undifferentiated interest in lawful administration. A procedural right must protect the plaintiff's own threatened concrete interest; when that connection exists, the plaintiff need not prove that the required procedure will certainly alter the ultimate agency decision. Aesthetic, recreational or professional wildlife interests may qualify, but past visits and indefinite return intentions without concrete plans or another supported basis for imminent renewed exposure do not establish threatened interference with personal use. At summary judgment the plaintiff must support challenged standing elements with specific facts by affidavit or other evidence, with favorable reasonable inferences available; allegations cannot replace missing evidence.

**Authority by component:**
- Stage-specific evidentiary requirement and existing injury, traceability and redressability elements: Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, “1. Article III elements must be supported at the litigation stage”, June 12, 1992; Scalia Part II, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas (five).
- Prospective personal-use boundary: Lujan, “2. Indefinite return intentions and general species interests do not establish threatened personal injury here,” June 12, 1992; Scalia Part III-A, same five-Justice opinion; Stone separately supports the record-specific conclusion.
- Citizen-suit and procedural-right boundary: Lujan, “3. Citizen-suit authorization does not dispense with a threatened concrete interest,” June 12, 1992; Scalia Part IV, same five Justices.

**Present operation effective:** Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, June 12, 1992, for the identified citizen-suit and procedural-right clarification; ordinary Article III elements and evidentiary burdens are retained.

**Exceptions and limits:** No purchased-ticket or particular-document requirement is adopted. The redressability application to absent agencies, binding force of the challenged regulation upon those agencies, effect of particular funding changes and ESA §7(a)(2)'s foreign reach are not controlling holdings.

**Holdings navigation:** Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992) — Standing and personal injury.

## Exact register entry: Independent political-party campaign expression and candidate connections

### Independent political-party campaign expression and candidate connections

**Governing question:** Application of the Senate party-expenditure ceiling and the alternative candidate-contribution theory to a genuinely independent party advertisement.

**Current rule:** Section 441a(d)(3)'s Senate-election expenditure ceiling cannot be applied to a party's own genuinely independent campaign advertisement merely because the speaker is an official political party. Party identity or a continuing electoral relationship also cannot replace §441a(a)(7)(B)(i)'s required cooperation, consultation, concert, request or suggestion by a candidate, authorized political committee or agent for the asserted contribution theory. Actual independence defeats that theory; realistic corruption and circumvention concerns do not alone establish the statutory candidate connection.

**Authority by component:**

- Independent-expression protection: Independent party expression; distinct contribution predicate: The alternative contribution theory, 1996-06-26; O’Connor Part I, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer (six).

**Present operation effective:** Colorado Republican, 1996-06-26.

**Exceptions and limits:** The general contribution/expenditure distinction survives. **Common authority.** Stevens's Court opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg throughout. Those six directly support Parts I, III-A and III-B. ### I. Registered-voter condition

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado may not require otherwise qualified resident-adult initiative circulators to be registered voters on this record. Excluding otherwise qualified speakers because they are unregistered substantially burdens protected petition circulation, while the retained identification and enforcement mechanisms serve traceability more closely.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a person need not register to vote to engage in the protected political conversation involved in circulating a petition. Meyer v. Grant protects that interaction and the available pool of speakers. Colorado's condition excludes at least 400,000 otherwise qualified but unregistered voters. Easy enrollment does not eliminate that reduction or show that registration adequately measures residence, availability for process or honesty.

The State's strongest argument treats registration as an administrable qualification in a legally operative election process. Anderson and the governing Burdick rule permit appropriately fitted administration, but require attention to the actual burden and interest. Here the sworn identifying and residential-address affidavit, signature review, subpoenas and targeted fraud sanctions address traceability without the registration exclusion. Timmons's different candidate-nomination setting does not make this speaker restriction minor. The Court invalidates the registration condition, not the age floor, circulation period or residence rule whose validity is not presented. An affidavit cannot preserve the invalid condition merely by requiring a registration recital.

### II. Personal name displayed during solicitation

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado may not compel every circulator to display a personal name on a badge during protected face-to-face petition advocacy when the retained post-circulation identification and enforcement measures serve the asserted interests with materially less speech burden. The personal-name component is invalid; standalone paid-or-volunteer and employer-information requirements are not adjudicated.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that compelled personal attribution at the expressive encounter requires a justification fitted to that burden. McIntyre protects individual political advocacy against inadequately justified identification demands; Meyer protects petition circulation, and Riley prevents payment from stripping a solicitor of speech protection. The trial record shows deterrence from the personal badge, particularly when the subject is unpopular. That is a burden on the actual political conversation.

Even assuming fraud prevention and detection are compelling interests, Colorado has not shown the necessary fit. The identifying affidavit, signature assurances, review, protests and fraud penalties allow officials to identify and investigate circulators without exposing their names to everyone approached. The later public affidavit differs in timing and accessibility from a worn badge; the Court creates no anonymity from lawful investigation. A witness's interest in reporting misconduct does not establish the needed additional benefit of this blanket personal-name display. This holding does not decide standalone paid-status, sponsor or employer attribution, nor invalidate lawful post-circulation verification.

### III-A. Final individual-circulator reports

**Holding and operative rule.** The challenged final-report requirements publishing each paid circulator's identity, address and individual compensation fail the required substantial relationship to Colorado's asserted informational and enforcement interests on this record. Disclosure of initiative proponents and aggregate spending remains distinct; the decision creates no categorical exemption from initiative-finance disclosure.

**Controlling explanation.** Buckley v. Valeo permits campaign-finance disclosure substantially related to important governmental interests. That inquiry requires attention to what the information reveals. Proponent identity and aggregate expenditures reveal who finances an initiative and the extent of paid efforts. The individual messenger is not, for that reason, a financing source or a candidate contributor. Colorado has not demonstrated the added benefit necessary to justify publishing every paid circulator's identity and compensation, without a threshold separating consequential financing from any paid messenger.

The State argues that payee information permits tracing and checking spending beyond aggregate totals. That is a legitimate subject of inquiry, but the retained sponsor, aggregate, amount-per-signature and verification mechanisms make the asserted incremental need insufficient here. Final reporting is less immediate than a badge; it still requires its own disclosure justification. Kentucky Right to Life's payer-identification rule concerns a different financing function and does not answer this demand for every messenger's information. A narrower individual-reporting rule supported under the governing scrutiny remains open.

### III-B. Monthly individual-circulator reports

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado's challenged monthly requirements publishing each paid circulator's identity, residential and business addresses and individual amounts paid or owed are likewise invalid on this record. The Court decides that individual-information package, not the independent validity of every other monthly-report field.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court separately considers publication during the circulation campaign. Timely information can expose abuse and inform voters before qualification; that argument gives monthly reporting work that a final report cannot always perform. But timing does not cure the insufficient relationship between identifying each messenger and the State's demonstrated financing or enforcement need. It also exposes the individual while the political effort is underway. The retained sponsor and aggregate disclosures and enforcement mechanisms remain material alternatives. The decision does not hold that referendum disclosure has no informational value or that every individual challenge requires proof of reprisals.

The Tenth Circuit invalidated the monthly subsection as a whole. The Court affirms the reviewed invalidity of individual circulator disclosures and leaves the independent validity of its other prescriptions, including proponents and measure identity, undecided. It neither orders those fields conclusively reinstated nor independently declares them unconstitutional. Any ordinary remedial or severability implementation must respect the existing judgment and the scope actually decided.

**Common limits and remedy.** The reviewed registration, personal-name and individual-disclosure invalidations stand. Age, circulation timing, verification affidavits, fraud and signature-review mechanisms are outside the reviewed invalidations and remain governed by their own terms. This decision does not invalidate standalone paid-status or sponsor attribution, create a replacement initiative code, grant damages, destroy public records, authorize fraud or decide every future disclosure rule. Unreviewed requirements receive no automatic new constitutional endorsement.

Kennedy's plurality, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, treats the programmed amount-linked adverse benefit as a legal burden on the independent speaker, even without a spending ceiling.
