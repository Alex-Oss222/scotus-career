## Event

Martin v. Hadix, No. 98-262. Argued March 30, 1999; decided June 21, 1999. Certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, 143 F.3d 246. The question is whether the PLRA's hourly attorney-fee ceiling governs services performed before and after its April 26, 1996 enactment in prisoner actions already pending on that date.

The Glover litigation began in 1977 and Hadix in 1980; both had reached a continuing remedial and monitoring stage. Glover's stipulated fee procedure dates to November 1985, and Hadix's monitoring-fee order to November 1987 after an April 4, 1985 consent decree. The requests include January 1–June 30, 1996 services. The established rate was $150 per hour; 150 percent of the identified $75 Criminal Justice Act rate yields a $112.50 hourly ceiling. These arrangements establish no immutable contractual rate for all future services.

The district court separated services by date. The Sixth Circuit preserved the earlier-work exemption and reversed application of the ceiling to later work, thus exempting both periods in these pending cases. Prison officials seek application to awards made after enactment, including earlier work; respondents defend the both-periods exemption. The temporal review does not reopen the underlying prison decrees, every fee-entitlement determination or all PLRA restrictions.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms in part, reverses in part and remands for fee proceedings under a service-date distinction.

The judgment is affirmed in part, reversed in part and remanded. The district court must allocate compensation by the dates of actual services, retaining the former compensation regime for completed pre-enactment work and applying the new hourly ceiling to later work. Actual compensable hours, amounts and independently applicable statutory predicates remain for ordinary lower proceedings. No final award, mandatory hearing, decree alteration, new prevailing-party finding or reopening of a completed fee judgment is ordered. Unaddressed constitutional objections remain undecided.

## Holdings

### 1. The enacted fee language supplies no command repricing completed services

**Holding and operative rule:** The general action-and-award language in original 42 U.S.C. §1997e(d) does not clearly direct application of its new hourly ceiling to legal services completed before April 26, 1996. The separate express transition for prospective relief in PLRA §802 does not itself establish the fee amendment's treatment of every pending action; each provision's operation must be examined.

**Authority:** Thomas's Part I, directly joined by all nine Justices. The statement does not resolve whether the undertaking of representation or each service controls later work; that disagreement is addressed separately.

**Controlling explanation:** Kaiser and Landgraf make congressional temporal direction the starting question. Section 1997e(d) defines covered actions and limits awards, but its reference to an award does not alone identify the activity whose legal treatment changes. Section 802 expressly addresses prospective relief granted before, on or after enactment; §803's fee amendment contains no equivalent instruction. Lindh permits ordinary structural inference without a prescribed verbal formula, yet does not make every adjacent express transition an automatic direction for a provision with a different function. Neither the text nor that comparison commands a reduced hourly return for services already performed. The remaining temporal application therefore requires attention to the concrete services and the change the cap would make. This conclusion rejects neither all fee changes in pending cases nor Congress's authority to prescribe a different temporal rule within constitutional limits.

### 2. The new ceiling does not govern pre-enactment services

**Holding and operative rule:** The hourly ceiling in original §1997e(d)(3) does not govern legal services completed before the PLRA's April 26, 1996 enactment, even if the fee request or award follows enactment. Those services remain subject to the compensation law governing when they were performed.

**Authority:** Thomas's Part II, directly joined by all nine Justices at this concrete level of application; broader methodological agreement is not inferred.

**Controlling explanation:** The hourly compensation provision addresses the legal work for which reimbursement is sought. Reducing the permissible rate for completed work would change the legal compensation consequences of services already supplied. The later act of issuing an award does not transform those completed services into future conduct. Landgraf protects completed events absent sufficient temporal direction; its application here turns on actual legal work, not the old prison conduct or the mere pendency of litigation. Bradley remains valid in its actual litigation-fee setting, but does not establish that every later fee statute may reprice completed services. The statutory incentive and the earlier fee arrangements support this treatment without establishing an immutable contract for future rates. Requests spanning January 1 through June 30, 1996 must therefore separate earlier services from later ones. The Court does not determine compensable hours, prevailing-party status, an amount, or the treatment of a vested final fee judgment.

### 3. Later services are subject to the ceiling despite the older action

**Holding and operative rule:** Original §1997e(d)(3)'s hourly ceiling governs services performed after enactment in these already-pending prisoner actions. On remand, the district court must allocate compensation by when the legal work was actually performed, retaining the prior regime for completed pre-enactment work and applying the enacted ceiling to later work.

**Authority:** Thomas's Part III, directly joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. Stevens and Ginsburg dissent from this component. No Marks reconstruction supplies the rule.

**Controlling explanation:** A fee statute regulating compensation for later services operates on that later work even though counsel entered the litigation before enactment. The original representation, decree and monitoring-fee orders do not establish an unalterable statutory rate for all work yet to be performed. Counsel's continuing professional obligations deserve consideration, but do not convert every later service into a completed pre-enactment act. This conclusion assumes neither that counsel could withdraw as of right nor that any withdrawal occurred. The new hourly ceiling supplies the compensation rule for work after its enactment; the earlier filing date alone does not remove that work from the statute. Landgraf requires examining the provision's actual operation, and Lindh's provision-specific habeas construction supplies no universal exemption for pending proceedings. The Court accordingly reverses the Sixth Circuit's exemption of later services. It neither changes the underlying decrees nor decides the unaddressed constitutional objections, privately contracted fee entitlements, other distinct fee restrictions, or a statute carrying materially different express temporal direction.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Landgraf v. USI Film Products:** Applies the concrete inquiry into temporal direction and changed consequences for completed services. The existing general-framework holding retains its prior force and original coalition; this decision does not turn service-date agreement into new assent to every methodological proposition.
- **Rivers v. Roadway Express:** Preserves provision-specific construction; a statute's general remedial purpose does not supply retrospective reach for a particular amendment.
- **Kaiser Aluminum & Chemical Corp. v. Bonjorno:** Applies the priority of enacted temporal direction; its fee discussion does not establish an automatic rule choosing either action filing or fee-award date.
- **Bradley v. School Board of Richmond:** Retains its actual fee-legislation result; distinguishes it from using a new hourly ceiling to reduce compensation for already completed work. It is not overruled.
- **Lindh v. Murphy:** Preserves ordinary structural construction and its particular AEDPA application-date rule. The PLRA provisions' different functions prevent automatic transfer of that result to all fees in old prisoner actions.
- **Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States ex rel. Schumer:** Preserves examination of the real legal change rather than a jurisdictional, procedural or collateral label. No new rule about its distinct private-enforcement facts is announced.

## Law After Decision

Before this decision the disputed pending-case application of the original PLRA hourly ceiling remained open. Effective June 21, 1999, the hourly-ceiling temporal rule requires service-date allocation in this setting: prior law for completed pre-enactment services and the new ceiling for later services, even in an older action. The original text's independent fee-entitlement and monetary-judgment provisions retain their own predicates and are not newly construed. No cited precedent is overruled; no final fee award is reopened.

## Separate Writings

**Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, concurring.** Their agreement rests on identification of the activity regulated. Section 1988's fee mechanism supplies an incentive for legal work, and the new hourly ceiling alters compensation for that work. Isolating the later word award would miss the provision's operation. The cap therefore cannot reprice already performed services absent the necessary temporal direction but governs services performed after enactment. Their Landgraf method calls for the relevant activity and sufficient textual direction, rather than a free-standing assessment of expectations. They join the concrete Court opinion because it does not require adoption of the broader methodology. Bradley's actual fee result remains intact, and the concurrence neither freezes future rates under prior monitoring orders nor decides every fee statute by its award date.

**Ginsburg, joined by Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They agree that the statute does not require repricing completed services and join their protection, but would also retain prior compensation treatment for later work in these existing representations. The relevant completed undertaking is representation in long-running, court-supervised institutional litigation, carrying continuing professional responsibilities. Its consequences cannot be reduced to a series of freely chosen new engagements after enactment. The absence of §802's express pending-matters transition in the fee amendment reinforces that reading, although the difference alone is not dispositive. Their application of Landgraf and Lindh would affirm the Sixth Circuit's exemption for both periods. They announce no immutable contractual right to every future rate and decide no constitutional invalidity, entitlement to an award or change to the prison decrees.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for fee proceedings consistent with the service-date distinction. Compensation for pre-enactment services is determined under the law governing that work; the new hourly ceiling applies to services performed after enactment. The lower court must make the actual allocation and determine otherwise applicable compensation predicates and amounts. The underlying decrees and unreviewed matters remain outside the mandate, including the constitutional objections not reached below. The Court orders no particular fee amount, new evidentiary hearing or alteration of the prison remedies.
