## Event

Minnesota v. Mille Lacs Band of Chippewa Indians, No. 97-1337. Argued December 2, 1998; decided March 24, 1999. On certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, whose judgment, 124 F.3d 904, recognized continuing treaty hunting, fishing and gathering rights subject to lawful conservation regulation.

Minnesota asks whether the 1850 Executive Order, 1855 Treaty, statehood or later events terminated the rights reserved on the ceded territory in the 1837 Treaty during the President's pleasure. The Court considers each asserted terminating act separately.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

## Public Action

The judgment is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Disposition:** Affirmed, 6–3, preserving recognition of the surviving treaty rights and the declaratory and injunctive framework within the questions reviewed.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, each relying on the three independent extinction grounds described in the dissents.

The treaty rights remain subject to lawful, necessary nondiscriminatory conservation measures. No particular regulation, harvest allocation, private-land-access claim or right outside the ceded territory is decided.

## Holdings

### I. The combined removal order did not terminate the reserved use rights

**Holding and operative rule.** An unauthorized compelled-removal order does not terminate reserved treaty use rights when its revocation and removal commands are inseparable parts of the same purpose and operation. The 1850 order therefore did not extinguish these rights; the Court does not decide the full extent of an independently exercised presidential power under the treaty's pleasure clause.

**Authority.** Stevens's Part I is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, counting the author within that six-Justice coalition. All six join the bounded combined-order rationale and its reservation of independent presidential revocation authority.

**Controlling explanation.** The Removal Act's consent structure did not authorize the compelled removal described by this record. That unlawful removal command could not cancel the separately reserved use rights. The order connected its revocation command to that same removal purpose and operation; treating revocation as a standalone measure would create a materially different executive action rather than preserve an independently chosen lawful termination. Minnesota's strongest argument is that the treaty separately permitted revocation during the President's pleasure. Even assuming an independently lawful revocation could occur, that possibility does not make this combined order severable or validate its unauthorized removal command. The Court decides this order's supported relationship and operation. It adopts neither a prohibition of every independent presidential revocation nor a rule that subsequent nonenforcement restores rights already lawfully extinguished.

### II. The 1855 land cession did not surrender these independent use rights

**Holding and operative rule.** A later land cession does not extinguish separately retained treaty use rights unless the agreement, read in its setting and as the tribal parties reasonably understood it, clearly relinquishes those rights. The 1855 agreement did not surrender the Band's retained hunting, fishing and gathering privileges.

**Authority.** Stevens's Part II has the six-Justice coalition of Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation.** Dion requires clear evidence that the disputed right was considered and relinquished, without imposing an invariable formula of express words. Menominee and Fishing Vessel protect retained rights against casual implication. Here the supported negotiations concerned a land purchase and did not address surrender of the independent use privileges. The broad cession language must be read in that setting. Klamath's reservation-dependent exclusive rights do not make every land transfer a surrender of a separately retained use right. Bourland likewise distinguishes regulation based on a power to exclude from personal treaty uses that remain after the relevant land change. The Court therefore rejects Minnesota's claim that comprehensive conveyancing words alone settle this distinct right. No cession by a nonsignatory Band or surrender under another agreement is inferred.

### III. Statehood did not itself extinguish the treaty rights

**Holding and operative rule.** Minnesota's admission to the Union did not itself terminate otherwise surviving federal treaty use rights. Those rights can coexist with lawful, necessary nondiscriminatory conservation regulation; the treaty's presidential-pleasure condition does not make state admission the terminating event.

**Authority.** Stevens's Part III has the same six-Justice coalition.

**Controlling explanation.** The Supremacy Clause preserves enforceable federal treaty rights after state admission. Winans, Tulee and Fishing Vessel demonstrate the compatibility of reserved uses with lawful conservation authority. Race Horse addressed a differently conditioned right on unoccupied lands. Its treaty-specific construction does not establish that this pleasure-qualified reservation ended at statehood. To the extent Race Horse treats equal footing alone as incompatible with surviving treaty use rights, the Court rejects that proposition. Minnesota's resource-management interest remains substantial, but it can operate through the governing conservation standards rather than an inference of wholesale extinction. The Court does not decide every concrete regulation or displace Race Horse's separate construction of its own treaty condition.

### IV. The affirmance preserves the rights at their actual scope

**Holding and operative rule.** Recognition of the surviving rights within the ceded territory remains in force against the presented extinction claims, subject to the applicable treaty and conservation standards. Survival does not itself decide private access, unrelated criminal conduct, numerical allocations or rights beyond the treaty's territory.

**Authority.** Stevens's Part IV has the same six-Justice coalition.

**Controlling explanation.** None of the presented terminating acts extinguished the rights, and no supported later event supplies another termination premise. That resolves the reviewed judgment. It does not require reopening every resource-management ruling in the larger proceedings, granting an unrestricted harvest or conferring a veto over every state rule. The declaration and related injunctive framework therefore remain in force within their lawful scope, without a new allocation, title transfer or retrospective award.

## Precedent Treatment

- Dion is applied: clear abrogation is required, but no mandatory form of express words is imposed.
- Menominee and Fishing Vessel are applied: independently reserved rights are not extinguished by casual implication.
- Klamath is distinguished: reservation-dependent exclusive rights do not determine the fate of these retained use privileges.
- Bourland is distinguished and preserved: exclusion-derived nonmember regulation and retained personal uses remain different rights.
- Hagen and Yankton retain their instrument-specific inquiry into actual federal displacement; land or ownership change alone does not decide every retained right.
- Race Horse is limited: its broader equal-footing incompatibility premise cannot terminate otherwise surviving rights merely at statehood; its distinct treaty-condition construction is not decided here.
- Winans and Tulee are applied with Fishing Vessel: treaty uses and lawful conservation regulation can coexist.

## Law After Decision

The Band's reserved uses survive the three asserted extinction grounds. The 1850-order, 1855, statehood and bounded-remedy holdings each control through the same six direct joins. No combination of separate opinions is needed to identify the governing rules. Race Horse no longer supports extinction solely from equal-footing incompatibility at statehood.

## Separate Writings

The common dissent of Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would find the rights extinguished on three independent grounds. The treaty's revocation authority gives the revocation command independent operation even if removal was unauthorized. The 1855 comprehensive cession, in their view, reaches the retained use interest; negotiation silence does not overcome that text. Race Horse's temporary-right analysis also applies to a privilege conditioned on presidential pleasure. They reject the Court's removal-purpose, land-purchase-context and conservation-coexistence answers, while confining their 1855 ground to the agreement's parties and rights and declining to nullify every tribal right at statehood.

Thomas separately questions whether survival alone establishes that Minnesota's regulatory authority is limited to conservation necessity. He considers the treaty's pleasure-qualified privilege distinct from the perpetual usual-and-accustomed-place language in other fishing cases and would require a treaty-specific examination of regulatory scope. His objection decides no particular state regulation and receives no additional join.

## Procedure After Action

Supreme Court review of the presented extinction claims is complete. The judgment recognizing the rights and its related framework remain in force, subject to applicable treaty and conservation standards. The Court directs no new allocation, hearing, deadline, compensation or adjudication of unpresented management claims.
