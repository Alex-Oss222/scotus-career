### Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, No. 90-1424

Merits decision, June 12, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 3, 1991; decided June 12, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Eighth Circuit, [911 F.2d 117](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/911/911.F2d.117.89-5386.89-5192.html), which affirmed the District of Minnesota's summary judgment, declaratory and mandatory rulemaking relief, and dependent attorney-fee award after an earlier remand on standing.

The questions are whether the conservation organizations have Article III standing to challenge the geographic limit on consultation under the Endangered Species Act (ESA) and, if so, whether §7(a)(2) reaches agency actions abroad. The Secretary seeks reversal; respondents seek to preserve the declaration and revised-regulation order. The Court resolves standing and does not reach the statute's geographic scope.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Article III elements must be supported at the litigation stage

**Controlling proposition:** A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction must establish a concrete and particularized, actual or imminent injury, a causal connection fairly traceable to the defendant rather than independent third parties, and a likelihood that the requested relief will redress the injury. At summary judgment, the plaintiff must supply specific facts by affidavit or other evidence on the challenged elements; favorable reasonable inferences remain available, but allegations cannot substitute for the missing evidence.

**Authority:** Part II of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls with five votes. Stone-Zsela concurs separately in the judgment on the personal-injury ground and does not join this section. The general redressability element is controlling; its application to these absent agencies is not.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that standing is part of the plaintiff's case and must be supported as the litigation progresses. Warth v. Seldin and Allen v. Wright require a personal injury connected to the challenged conduct; Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights Organization requires likely redress through the requested judgment. Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation supplies the distinction between allegations sufficient at pleading and evidence required at summary judgment. Respondents therefore cannot rely on the earlier decision allowing their allegations to survive dismissal. They must produce specific facts supporting their own threatened injury. Rule 56 preserves favorable reasonable inferences from evidence; it does not authorize a court to presume facts necessary to establish jurisdiction merely because the claim otherwise fails. Where independent third parties determine whether the plaintiff will be harmed or helped, respondents must support the necessary causal connection. The Court resolves this action for want of personal injury and does not adopt the separate opinion's application of redressability to the absent financing agencies.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490 (1975): applied for the plaintiff's personal stake and burden to establish standing; its statutory-rights discussion remains subject to concrete injury.
- Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737 (1984): applied for the constitutional elements and the limits on generalized judicial supervision.
- Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights Organization, 426 U.S. 26 (1976): applied for fair traceability and likely redress; no new majority application concerning these absent agencies follows.
- Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation, 497 U.S. 871 (1990): applied for the evidentiary distinction between pleading and summary judgment; its separate APA programmatic-review holding is not enlarged.
- Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317 (1986): applied to require evidence of challenged essential elements while preserving genuine material disputes and reasonable inferences.
- Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242 (1986): applied to require evidence of challenged essential elements while preserving genuine material disputes and reasonable inferences.

##### 2. Indefinite return intentions and general species interests do not establish threatened personal injury here

**Controlling proposition:** Aesthetic, recreational and professional interests in observing wildlife can support standing, but these plaintiffs must show threatened interference with their own concrete use. Prior visits and an indefinite intention to return, without concrete plans or another supported basis for imminent renewed exposure, do not establish standing for these prospective claims; an ecosystem, animal or professional connection that supplies only general interest in distant species does not cure that defect.

**Authority:** Part III-A of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls with five votes. Stone-Zsela's separate concurrence supplies a sixth vote for this record-specific personal-injury conclusion without joining the Court's writing. No purchased-ticket or particular-document prerequisite is adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a recognized interest must be threatened in the plaintiff's own life. Sierra Club v. Morton recognizes aesthetic and recreational injury while requiring a member of the plaintiff organization to be among those harmed. The affidavits identify actual visits to the Egyptian and Sri Lankan areas and an interest in observing their wildlife. They provide no sufficiently definite return undertaking or continuing activity showing imminent exposure to the threatened loss. Los Angeles v. Lyons and Whitmore v. Arkansas require more than past exposure followed by a conjectural future injury. The Court accepts reasonable inferences from the supplied facts, but an unspecified intention cannot itself establish when personal use will recur. The proposed ecosystem and professional theories fail for the same evidentiary reason: they broaden the class of interested persons without showing a concrete effect on these members. National Wildlife Federation requires an affected personal use, not a general environmental concern. The Court does not decide a case involving actual use of an affected ecosystem or demonstrated injury transmitted across geographic distance.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Sierra Club v. Morton, 405 U.S. 727 (1972): applied; aesthetic injury is cognizable, but an organization's special interest cannot replace injury to a member.
- Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Commission, 432 U.S. 333 (1977): applied to require an injured member for this associational claim; no other associational requirement is changed.
- Los Angeles v. Lyons, 461 U.S. 95 (1983): applied for the distinction between past exposure and a present or imminent injury supporting prospective relief.
- Whitmore v. Arkansas, 495 U.S. 149 (1990): applied for actual or imminent rather than conjectural injury.
- Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation, 497 U.S. 871 (1990): applied to specific affected use; no categorical bar to geographically transmitted injury is established.
- Japan Whaling Association v. American Cetacean Society, 478 U.S. 221 (1986): distinguished because its whale-watching and studying members alleged an adverse effect on their own activities; concrete professional or ecological injury remains possible.

##### 3. Citizen-suit authorization does not dispense with a threatened concrete interest

**Controlling proposition:** ESA §11(g)'s authorization for any person to sue does not give Article III standing to a plaintiff asserting only the public's common interest in lawful administration. A procedural right may protect a plaintiff's threatened concrete interest without requiring proof that the procedure will certainly change the ultimate agency decision, but the procedural label cannot replace that personal interest; these respondents have not shown the required connection.

**Authority:** Part IV of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls with five votes. Stone-Zsela does not join this section or its broader congressional-power analysis; he separately concludes only that the claimed consultation deprivation fails to connect to sufficiently imminent interference with these members' concrete use.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that statutory permission to bring suit and constitutional injury remain distinct. Fairchild v. Hughes and the generalized-grievance decisions refuse federal jurisdiction based solely on every citizen's interest in lawful government. Section 11(g) authorizes enforcement but does not itself demonstrate that every violation injures every person. Respondents cannot establish the missing personal connection by treating consultation as an abstract entitlement available to the public at large. This conclusion does not exclude procedural injuries as a class. A person whose concrete interest a required procedure protects need not prove with certainty that performing the procedure will change the final agency choice. Sierra Club preserves the requirement of personal injury while Warth permits Congress to recognize legally protected injuries. Congress may recognize concrete factual injuries previously inadequate in law; the Court does not settle the outer reach of that power. Here, however, the citizen-suit provision does not identify a concrete injury to these members independently of the unsupported future use already examined. The Court leaves the statute's geographic reach undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Fairchild v. Hughes, 258 U.S. 126 (1922): applied to reject a general right to compel lawful administration as a sufficient personal injury.
- Massachusetts v. Mellon, 262 U.S. 447 (1923): applied for the distinction between direct injury and an undifferentiated public grievance.
- Ex parte Levitt, 302 U.S. 633 (1937): applied for the distinction between direct injury and an undifferentiated public grievance.
- United States v. Richardson, 418 U.S. 166 (1974): applied to generally shared governmental grievances without distinctive concrete harm.
- Schlesinger v. Reservists Committee to Stop the War, 418 U.S. 208 (1974): applied to generally shared governmental grievances without distinctive concrete harm.
- Valley Forge Christian College v. Americans United for Separation of Church and State, Inc., 454 U.S. 464 (1982): applied; a claimed right to lawful government conduct alone does not satisfy Article III.
- Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737 (1984): applied; a claimed right to lawful government conduct alone does not satisfy Article III.
- Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490 (1975): preserved for Congress's power to recognize injuries and broaden protected interests while retaining the plaintiff's own concrete injury.
- Sierra Club v. Morton, 405 U.S. 727 (1972): preserved for Congress's power to recognize injuries and broaden protected interests while retaining the plaintiff's own concrete injury.
- Trafficante v. Metropolitan Life Insurance Co., 409 U.S. 205 (1972): distinguished as statutory recognition of concrete personal interests, not authorization of a wholly abstract grievance.
- Hardin v. Kentucky Utilities Co., 390 U.S. 1 (1968): distinguished as statutory recognition of concrete personal interests, not authorization of a wholly abstract grievance.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 12, 1992, respondents' standing fails for insufficient threatened personal injury. Ordinary evidentiary burdens apply at each litigation stage. The ESA citizen-suit provision does not turn an undifferentiated demand for consultation into concrete injury. Procedural protections tied to a personal interest remain cognizable without certainty that the final agency choice will change. The Court applies and clarifies existing Article III doctrine on this foreign-project record and imposes no documentary prerequisite.

The Court applies Sierra Club's personal-injury requirement, National Wildlife Federation's evidentiary requirements, and the generalized-grievance authorities described above. No earlier precedent is overruled. The Court does not decide whether the Secretary's regulation binds absent agencies, whether particular funding changes would alter the projects, or whether §7(a)(2) reaches actions abroad. Each Court holding has five supporters independently of the judgment-only agreements. Part III-B's redressability application has three votes and Stevens's statutory ground has one; neither controls or changes precedent.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia's Part III-B, joined by White and Thomas, would independently reject redressability because the action agencies are absent and their response, and the foreign projects' response, to a revised rule is not adequately established. Three Justices adopt that application; it does not control.

Kennedy, joined by Souter, reserves that redressability question. Their concurrence leaves room for factually demonstrated ecosystem or professional injuries and for Congress to define injuries and causal chains, while requiring Congress at least to identify the injury and relate it to the persons entitled to sue.

Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment because the past visits and indefinite return intentions do not show imminent renewed personal use. A definite undertaking or an established return practice could supply the necessary evidence; purchased tickets or a particular travel document are unnecessary. He reserves disputed redressability, the outer constitutional scope of congressionally created procedural rights, and ESA geographic coverage. Actual use of an affected ecosystem presents a materially different record; multiple contributors alone would not defeat such a claim.

Stevens concurs in reversal while finding standing. In his view, the personal aesthetic and professional interest suffers when the threatened species and habitat are destroyed, and consultation can provide meaningful relief. He reads §7(a)(2)'s domestic consultation and exemption structure, alongside the Act's express international provisions, to exclude foreign actions. That construction receives no majority and is not the Court's law.

Blackmun, joined by O'Connor, dissents from ending the action on summary judgment. The prior visits, professional interests and intended return permit favorable inferences about future exposure, and the agencies' involvement and potential project influence create a factual issue about redress. Their procedural-injury discussion concerns Congress's capacity to protect concrete interests through required processes; it does not supply a controlling exception to Article III.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Court reverses and remands. Supreme Court review is complete. The case returns through the Eighth Circuit for vacatur of the merits declaration, mandatory rulemaking relief and dependent fee award, and dismissal for want of Article III jurisdiction. No substitute fee amount or independent fee-entitlement rule is adopted.

The Court requires no cancellation of a foreign project, new regulation, consultation, factfinding remand or particular later filing. The jurisdictional dismissal does not resolve the statute's geographic reach or preclude a later action supported by sufficient standing facts.

**End of entry: Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, merits decision, June 12, 1992.**

### Franklin v. Massachusetts, No. 91-1502

Merits decision, June 26, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 21, 1992. On direct appeal from the three-judge District Court for Massachusetts, [785 F. Supp. 230 (1992)](https://static.case.law/f-supp/785/html/0230-01.html), which set aside the inclusion of overseas federal employees in state apportionment counts and ordered corrective relief. Massachusetts and its voters challenge that inclusion, the Secretary's residence methodology and the explanation for using home-of-record data after cancellation of a proposed survey. The questions concern standing, review of the Secretary's completed decision under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), constitutional and statutory counting authority, and review for arbitrary or capricious administrative action.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the District Court's judgment and dissolves its corrective relief.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Operative judgment and constitutional claim requiring exclusion of overseas personnel | Reversed; constitutional exclusion claim rejected, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No Justice opposes | Corrective relief dissolved; no replacement allocation or injunction against the President |
| Review of the Secretary's completed action and statutory and APA merits | Review available; statutory objections and arbitrary-or-capricious challenge rejected by five Justices | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter | White, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas oppose APA review and would reach reversal on that threshold ground; they do not vote that the methodology violates the APA | No further factual proceedings or new allocation required |

#### Opinion Topology

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–III | Stone-Zsela | White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Supports reversal and rejection of the constitutional exclusion claim | Posture, standing for the inclusion injury and constitutional counting rule; nine Justices |
| Opinion of the Court, Parts IV–V | Stone-Zsela | Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter | Supports reversal after APA review and rejection of statutory and administrative objections | Final agency action in Part IV; statutory and administrative merits in Part V; five Justices |

| Concurrence | Scalia | No other Justice | Supports standing and the constitutional merits | Applies Montana's express declaratory-redress holding while leaving coercive presidential relief unresolved |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Standing for the continuing apportionment injury

**Controlling proposition:** A State and its voters have standing to contest inclusion of an identified population in the operative apportionment when that inclusion costs the State a seat and authoritative relief concerning the responsible census determination would likely correct the allocation through the prescribed statutory sequence. Likely redress here does not require an injunction against the President or a general assumption that persons outside the litigation follow judicial advice.

**Authority:** Parts I–III of Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight Associate Justices, control. This holding concerns the proved inclusion injury; it does not find that any particular alternative data source would independently give Massachusetts a seat.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this continuing representational injury can be judicially redressed. Massachusetts would receive an additional seat if the challenged overseas population were excluded. The injury therefore rests on the operative allocation, not an abstract objection to governmental procedure. This Court's Montana decision establishes that authoritative declaratory adjudication can probably correct an ongoing apportionment through the prescribed governmental sequence. Lujan requires a concrete injury, causal connection and likely relief at the pertinent evidentiary stage; its absent-agency application did not command a majority and does not supply a categorical bar here. The statutory sequence and the Government's litigation position support the likelihood of correction following an authoritative determination concerning the census. The Court need not presume a power to command presidential discretion. Nor does it assume that a different residence dataset would necessarily produce another seat: that separate causal proposition has not been established. The inclusion injury and its connected legal challenges suffice for the review undertaken here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States Department of Commerce v. Montana, March 31, 1992, this Court: applied for continuing representational injury and likely declaratory redress through the allocation process; the express unanimous holding remains controlling and its reservation of coercive presidential relief remains intact.
- Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, June 12, 1992, this Court: applied for injury, causation, likely redress and evidentiary demands; the noncontrolling application concerning absent funding agencies is not promoted into law. Its rule for procedure connected to concrete injury does not require certainty that a new method changes the result.

##### The Secretary's completed census determination is reviewable

**Controlling proposition:** The Secretary's completed census determination supplies final agency action under APA §704 even though the President later transmits the resulting apportionment statement. The census statutes neither preclude review nor commit the counting decision entirely to unreviewable discretion: the duty to count the proper population supplies law to apply, while leaving substantial methodological choice to the Secretary.

**Authority:** Part IV of Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter, controls with five votes. White, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas disagree with this rule while agreeing with reversal on their own reviewability ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the official responsible for the count cannot escape review merely because another official transmits its apportionment consequence. Section 141 assigns the census and its completed population figures to the Secretary. Section 2a directs transmission of numbers ascertained under that census and application of the prescribed formula; it does not confer a new presidential choice of the population to count. Abbott Laboratories supports review of a completed administrative determination producing concrete consequences. Waterman involved a statutory presidential judgment over foreign air routes; that independent decision differs from the prescribed transmission here. Possible correction and general executive supervision do not make every completed agency decision tentative. Japan Whaling confirms that broad administrative discretion remains judicially reviewable when governing statutory commands supply a legal standard. The Secretary retains latitude over census form and method, but must count the proper population under the governing law. Review of that completed administrative choice is sufficient. The Court therefore leaves the President's own APA status and susceptibility to coercive relief undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136 (1967): applied for reviewability and practical completion of an agency determination with concrete consequences; the statutory transmittal here does not postpone finality.
- Chicago & Southern Air Lines, Inc. v. Waterman Steamship Corp., 333 U.S. 103 (1948): distinguished because Congress there required an independent presidential foreign-affairs judgment; its rule for that decision structure is preserved.
- Japan Whaling Assn. v. American Cetacean Society, 478 U.S. 221 (1986): applied for review against statutory limits despite broad administrative judgment; methodological latitude is not absence of law to apply.

##### Temporarily absent residents may be included in the constitutional count

**Controlling proposition:** The constitutional command to count persons in each State permits inclusion of federal personnel temporarily overseas whose enduring ties support residence in a particular State. It does not require a literal census-day physical presence rule, and it does not authorize attribution to a State without a supportable residence connection.

**Authority:** Part III of Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight Associate Justices, controls with nine votes. The constitutional rule does not itself resolve the separate statutory finality or APA explanation questions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that temporary government service abroad does not necessarily sever residence in a State. The constitutional text requires an enumeration of the population belonging to each State, but does not define residence exclusively by bodily presence on census day. The first census legislation treated persons temporarily absent from their usual abode as belonging there. That early implementation gives concrete content to the counting command. The challenged personnel retain identified state connections, and the Secretary may reasonably treat their absence on federal assignment as temporary for this purpose. Wesberry and Karcher enforce equality in drawing districts within a State; they do not dictate this answer to who belongs in an interstate population count. Montana likewise recognizes that interstate apportionment must respect the constitutional structure rather than import every intrastate rule unchanged. Home-of-record information is imperfect, but that does not make every supported attribution constitutionally forbidden. The Court decides this record and rejects mandatory exclusion; unsupported connections and different methodological records remain open.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States Department of Commerce v. Montana, March 31, 1992, this Court: applied for the constitutional structure of interstate apportionment and the distinction from intrastate districting; no new tolerance for unequal districts is adopted.
- Wesberry v. Sanders, 376 U.S. 1 (1964): distinguished as an intrastate congressional district-equality rule; its equal-population command remains effective in its own setting.
- Karcher v. Daggett, 462 U.S. 725 (1983): distinguished on the same interstate/intrastate ground; the present residence decision does not excuse avoidable inequality among a State's districts.

##### The statutory and administrative objections fail on this record

**Controlling proposition:** Section 141 permits the supported inclusion of temporarily overseas personnel with enduring state ties, and the Secretary's comparative choice of supplemented home-of-record information is not arbitrary or capricious on this record. The agency must supply a contemporaneous, reasonably discernible explanation connecting its available information and residence objective to its choice; imperfection alone does not require rejection of every available dataset.

**Authority:** Part V of Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter, controls with five votes. The other four Justices do not join the statutory or APA merits determination.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Secretary made a permissible comparative choice and adequately explained it. State Farm requires examination of relevant considerations and a rational connection between the record and the decision. Bowman Transportation permits an explanation whose path is reasonably discernible even if its expression is imperfect. Cancellation of the proposed survey is a serious objection because improved residence information helped motivate the change. But the contemporaneous record also contains assurances of supplementary personnel information and continuing reasons to recognize temporarily absent residents. Home of record can identify an enduring connection; legal residence may instead reflect taxation, and last duty station may identify employment rather than home. The Secretary could weigh those comparative defects under the statutory counting responsibility. The Court relies on the recorded reasons, not later declarations or a rationale invented by counsel. It does not demand a perfect dataset unavailable to the agency. Nor does it excuse persistence when an agency's only factual premise collapses. Different methodological challenges require their own records.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Assn. v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co., 463 U.S. 29 (1983): applied for consideration of relevant factors, reasoned explanation and review on the agency's actual rationale; no exemption from review accompanies census discretion.
- Bowman Transportation, Inc. v. Arkansas-Best Freight System, Inc., 419 U.S. 281 (1974): applied for a reasonably discernible decisional path despite imperfect exposition; a court may not substitute a new rationale.

**Limits and questions not reached.** No replacement allocation, judicially selected dataset, injunction against the President, or general APA status for the President is decided. No claim that another residence method necessarily restores a seat is found proved. The statutory and administrative holdings are record-specific applications; no earlier Supreme Court precedent is overruled.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 26, 1992, the Secretary's completed census decision is reviewable under APA §704 despite subsequent presidential transmission; the counting obligations supply law for a court to apply. Supported temporarily absent residents may be included in the constitutional count. The statutory and APA merits uphold this inclusion and the home-of-record choice on the contemporaneous explanation. Montana supplies the standing premise, and Lujan applies at the level of its controlling propositions. Waterman remains effective for its distinct structure requiring an independent presidential decision; State Farm and Bowman govern the actual administrative record. The Court creates no presidential-immunity or presidential-APA holding, new evidentiary burden, replacement formula or tolerance for unequal districts.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O'Connor, joined by White, Scalia and Thomas, views the Secretary's report as an intermediate step because the President transmits the operative apportionment and may seek corrections; they would also exclude the President from the APA's agency definition. They concur in the constitutional rule and reversal but oppose review of the Secretary under §704. Scalia additionally explains that this Court's express Montana standing decision, which he joined, governs likely declaratory redress for the proved inclusion injury; it does not decide whether a court may coerce the President. These positions do not displace the five-Justice APA holdings.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The District Court judgment is reversed and its corrective relief dissolved. This judgment orders no replacement allocation, further factual proceedings or injunction against the President. Supreme Court review of these claims ends without a merits remand.

**End of entry: Franklin v. Massachusetts, merits decision, June 26, 1992.**

### Raines v. Byrd, No. 96-1671

Merits decision, June 26, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued May 27, 1997; decided June 26, 1997. On direct appeal under 2 U.S.C. §692(b) from the judgment of a single District Judge for the District of Columbia, reported at 956 F. Supp. 25. The questions are whether six Members of Congress have Article III standing and, only if they do, whether the Line Item Veto Act violates Article I's lawmaking requirements or nondelegation principles.

The Act was enacted April 9, 1996, effective January 1, 1997; the Members sued January 2. The District Court found standing and ripeness and declared the Act unconstitutional on April 10; appeal followed April 11. Four Senators and two Representatives had voted against the Act. Their votes were counted, no cancellation had injured them personally, and neither House authorized them to represent Congress. The congressional amici defended the Act's validity without taking a standing position. Senator Hatfield subsequently retired; five plaintiffs remained Members.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of Article III standing, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

**Opposing:** Stevens and Breyer.

Stevens would affirm the constitutional declaration. Breyer would recognize jurisdiction and proceed to the merits, which he reserves. Souter's judgment support does not join the principal injury analysis.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Vacatur and jurisdictional dismissal | Entire opinion |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Souter | Ginsburg | Same disposition, separate Article III ground | Entire concurrence; Ginsburg also joins the Court opinion |
| Dissent | Stevens | Author alone | Would affirm the District Court | Standing, ripeness and Article I merits |
| Dissent | Breyer | Author alone | Would reject jurisdictional dismissal | Standing and ripeness; merits expressly reserved |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. These individual legislators lack a concrete personal injury

**Holding and operative rule:** Individual Members of Congress whose votes were counted and given full effect lack Article III standing to challenge the Line Item Veto Act on the asserted abstract dilution of Congress's institutional power and alteration of future votes. Express statutory permission to sue removes the applicable prudential obstacle but cannot supply the missing concrete personal injury.

**Authority:** The six-Justice opinion of the Court identified above adopts this rule, its explanation, limits and remedy. Souter supplies a seventh judgment vote on a separate ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Lujan requires a plaintiff's own concrete injury, with causation and redressability; legislative permission cannot replace that constitutional stake. These Members cast fully effective votes against the Act and lost. They retain office and their voting entitlement, apart from Hatfield's subsequent retirement, and identify no cancellation injuring them personally. Powell concerned exclusion from a seat, an individual deprivation absent here. Coleman concerned complete nullification of votes sufficient to determine a legislative outcome; it does not turn every change in the practical force of future votes into that injury.

Akins establishes that numerical sharing does not make a concrete deprivation abstract. Its voters were denied particular information useful to their own electoral participation. These Members instead assert an institutional allocation of power. Their strongest argument is that cancellation authority presently deprives them of voting on the ultimate legal text. But no vote of theirs was overridden or denied legal effect; the asserted change belongs to the legislative institution, which has not authorized their representation. Available legislative remedies and a later concrete cancellation controversy reinforce this bounded conclusion. They create no legislative-exhaustion requirement and do not guarantee any future plaintiff standing. The Court decides no universal rule against official injury or legislator litigation.

##### II. The jurisdictional defect requires vacatur and dismissal

**Holding and operative rule:** Because these plaintiffs lack Article III standing, the District Court's constitutional declaration must be vacated and the complaint dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. The Court does not decide the Act's constitutionality, nondelegation, Presentment or the independent ripeness question.

**Authority:** The same six-Justice Court opinion controls the jurisdictional disposition and its limits; Souter separately agrees with vacatur and dismissal.

**Controlling explanation:** Steel Co. requires resolution of disputed Article III power before disputed constitutional merits in this setting. Congress's special direct-review and expedition provisions supply a route for review, not jurisdiction over an uninjured plaintiff's constitutional complaint. Vacating the declaration removes a merits judgment the District Court lacked power to enter; it does not sustain the Act on its merits. Personal exclusion, lost salary, complete nullification of decisive votes, an authorized institutional suit meeting Article III, and an actual cancellation causing concrete injury remain distinct questions. The Court neither adjudicates any such future controversy nor adds an independent holding on traceability.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Lujan supplies the concrete-personal-injury requirement and the distinction between statutory permission and constitutional injury; its rule is applied without enlarging its noncontrolling redressability analysis.
- Akins preserves standing for concrete injury shared by many; its informational deprivation is distinguished from the institutional dilution alleged here.
- Powell remains authority for personal exclusion from office, a circumstance not presented.
- Coleman remains authority for complete nullification of decisive votes; it is distinguished from votes counted and lost.
- Steel Co. supplies the requirement to decide disputed Article III power before the disputed merits in this setting.
- Bender and Allen support the distinction between an individual's legally cognizable stake and unauthorized institutional litigation and the separation-of-powers limits on jurisdiction. No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

These individual legislators cannot invoke Article III on this institutional-dilution claim. Congress's authorization and expedition provisions do not change that conclusion. The Act's constitutional validity remains undecided, and concrete personal or properly authorized institutional injuries remain subject to their own jurisdictional analysis.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Souter, concurring in the judgment, joined by Ginsburg.** Official injury is not categorically excluded by Article III: Coleman and authorized institutional representation prevent that shortcut. Whether this injury is personal rather than official, or concrete rather than abstract, is fairly debatable. Souter instead gives decisive weight to Allen's separation-of-powers foundation and Valley Forge's restraint against unnecessary constitutional adjudication. The Members seek judicial intervention in an unauthorized internal and interbranch contest, while a beneficiary deprived of an actual spending or tax benefit may present a conventional controversy satisfying injury, causation and redressability. These conditions make the present claim constitutionally insufficient. This is neither prudential abstention, an absolute political-question bar, nor a universal requirement that another plaintiff exist.

**Stevens, dissenting.** A Member's opportunity to vote on the law's ultimate text is an individual constitutional function. The Act's present alteration of that function is concrete without waiting for a particular cancellation; Coleman supports protection of official voting power, and Bender's prohibition on unauthorized representation does not defeat a Member's own injury. Stevens finds the claim ripe and would affirm the declaration on Article I grounds. The Act permits the President, after a bill becomes law, to cancel in whole a defined discretionary dollar amount, new-direct-spending item or limited tax benefit under its conditions. Chadha requires bicameralism and presentment for repeal. Giving cancellation a different name or intelligible standards does not turn removal of enacted legal force into execution; Field's contingent execution and ordinary impoundment do not answer the distinct spending-obligation and tax effects. His ground is allocation of lawmaking authority, not a finding that the delegation's standards are insufficiently intelligible. He adds no private presidential-reservation theory and decides no later cancellation remedy.

The cancellation permission requires determinations that the action reduces the deficit, does not impair essential Government functions and does not harm the national interest; the special message must be sent within five calendar days excluding Sundays. Cancellation takes effect upon both Houses' receipt; enacted disapproval nullifies it and restores the provision from its original effective date. A disapproval bill cannot itself be canceled. Disapproval requires legislation, and the special procedures do not abolish ordinary later legislation. The Act has no force or effect on or after January 1, 2005. None of these limitations, in Stevens's view, changes the Article I character of a repeal after enactment.

**Breyer, dissenting.** The systematic impairment of the Members' existing constitutional work is sufficiently concrete. Coleman does not limit official injury to one decisive voting event, and express congressional authorization removes a prudential barrier while reducing separation-of-powers concerns; it does not enlarge Article III. The prospect of a later private suit cannot erase present injury if that injury exists. Breyer finds standing and ripeness and would proceed to the merits. He expressly reserves the Act's constitutionality and does not join Stevens's merits conclusion or vote to sustain the Act.
