# Humana Inc. v. Forsyth — 1999-01-20

## Event
**Humana Inc. v. Forsyth, No. 97-303.** Argued November 30, 1998; decided January 20, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 114 F.3d 1467. The question is whether the McCarran-Ferguson Act bars the Co-Payors' surviving federal RICO action concerning insurer fraud also prohibited by Nevada insurance law.

The Co-Payors allege concealed hospital discounts and charges of 20 percent of undiscounted bills despite a represented 80/20 allocation, during 1985–1988. The Court of Appeals permitted their overpayment claim while retaining rejection of the Premium Payors' separate RICO branch and limiting injury to overpayments actually proved. Those distinct limitations remain outside this review.

## Participation
All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action
The Ninth Circuit's judgment is affirmed on the McCarran-Ferguson question insofar as it permits the surviving Co-Payor RICO overpayment claim to proceed.

## Judgment & Remedy
**Affirmed, 9–0, on the reviewed question.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Co-Payor action may proceed subject to proof of every RICO element, causation, injury and authorized remedy, with ordinary defenses intact. The Premium Payor branch is not revived. The actual-overpayment injury limit remains; a lawful statutory multiplier is a separate remedial matter. The related conspiracy-amendment instruction follows the surviving branch without establishing conspiracy or guaranteeing amendment success. The Court awards no liability, premium recovery or treble-damages amount and directs no automatic new trial.

## Holdings
#### Compatible federal fraud remedies do not impair the actual state insurance scheme

**Operative rule.** A generally applicable federal law impairs protected state insurance law when its operation contradicts the state law or frustrates an actual state policy or the administration of the state scheme; literal contradiction is not the only possible impairment. An additional private action or greater federal remedy does not itself establish impairment, and the Co-Payors' RICO claim may proceed because it targets fraud Nevada forbids while leaving the State's scheme intact and displacing no demonstrated exclusive remedial arrangement.

**Authority.** Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court has all nine Justices' direct joins and controls the rule, application and reservations.

**Controlling explanation.** The parties do not dispute here that Nevada enacted the relevant laws to regulate the business of insurance or that RICO does not specifically relate to that business. Those predicates frame the impairment question. Fabe protects policy performance and administration necessary to implement it, so the inquiry must examine the actual state policy that federal law would frustrate. Barnett Bank separately addresses an insurance-specific federal exception; RICO has no such subject matter and receives no exemption by that analogy.

The insurers correctly observe that enforcement machinery and remedial choices can form part of protected state policy. A common prohibition of fraud therefore does not by itself establish compatibility. Here, however, Nevada prohibits the alleged deception, and its regulatory authority and remedies are not established as an exclusive arrangement displaced by this Co-Payor action. RICO neither invalidates the state command nor replaces its regulator or defeats an identified policy forbidding this action or remedy. Larger federal damages alone do not establish the missing interference.

The allegations span 1985–1988. The 1987 addition of an express private remedy in Nevada Revised Statutes §686A.310 is not applied retrospectively, and the Court does not decide whether an earlier statutory action was implied. Even assuming no statutory private remedy for earlier conduct, that absence does not itself establish a policy making administrative sanctions exclusive or excluding independent federal relief. The result rests on the actual nonexclusive arrangement, not invented earlier private rights or a comprehensive state-remedy inventory.

A federal claim requiring conduct the State forbids, nullifying a state-approved practice or displacing an exclusive remedial structure established by enacted law would present impairment. No such operation, conflicting allocation of a finite insolvency estate or displacement of an administrative judgment is established here. The Court decides neither hypothetical exclusive schemes nor unrelated insurance conduct, criminal RICO or other federal laws. ERISA, antitrust, the rejected Premium Payor branch and ordinary RICO prerequisites remain unadjudicated in this review.

## Precedent Treatment
- **United States Department of Treasury v. Fabe:** Applied to preserve actual state policy-performance rules and necessary administration against conflicting nonspecific federal law. It does not insulate every insurer-related act or remedy from federal law.
- **Barnett Bank of Marion County, N.A. v. Nelson:** Preserved. Its insurance-specific federal exception remains separate from the interference question and does not extend to RICO by analogy; the actual relationship between the statutes must still be examined.

## Law After Decision
The McCarran-Ferguson inquiry compares the operation of the federal claim with the State's actual protected policy and administration. Neither overlap nor greater federal recovery alone bars the federal claim; actual conflict or frustration can do so even without a literal contradiction. On the relationship established here, the surviving Co-Payor RICO claim complements Nevada's fraud prohibition without displacing an exclusive state mechanism. No retrospective Nevada cause of action or broader RICO entitlement is created.

## Procedure After Action
The case returns to the remaining proceedings contemplated by the Ninth Circuit's judgment, with the Co-Payor claim and related amendment instruction subject to ordinary RICO requirements and defenses. Rejected Premium Payor claims and the proved-overpayment injury limitation remain unchanged. Further liability and any authorized remedy require ordinary adjudication.
