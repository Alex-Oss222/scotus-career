### Monge v. California, No. 97-6146

Merits decision, June 26, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 28, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of California, reviewing 16 Cal. 4th 826, 941 P.2d 1121 (1997). The question is whether the Double Jeopardy Clause bars another attempt to establish the serious-prior-felony allegation after the first proof failed to establish its necessary personal-conduct qualification.

The jury convicted Monge of using a minor to sell marijuana, selling or transporting marijuana, and possessing marijuana for sale. The information gave notice of a serious-prior-felony allegation and a distinct prior-prison-term allegation. Monge waived a jury on those allegations. At the bifurcated bench proceeding, the State introduced a prison record establishing an assault conviction and imprisonment, but not necessarily the required personal use of a dangerous or deadly weapon or personal infliction of great bodily injury. The prosecutor's assertion about use of a stick was not evidence of that missing fact. The trial court found both allegations true and imposed eleven years: a selected five-year term doubled to ten, plus one year for the separate prison-term allegation. A three-year term on count two was stayed and a two-year term on count three ran concurrently.

The State conceded on appeal that the first record did not prove the serious-prior qualification beyond a reasonable doubt. The Court of Appeal found insufficiency and barred retrial. The California Supreme Court reversed that double-jeopardy ruling, permitting another proof proceeding. The seven-year unenhanced maximum on count one, fourteen-year qualifying-prior maximum, selected five-year term and independent one-year enhancement remain distinct.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Stevens, Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg.

The judgment rejecting the federal double-jeopardy bar is affirmed. California may, but is not required to, attempt renewed proof of the serious-prior allegation under applicable state law and safeguards. The unsupported first finding is not reinstated; neither qualifying personal-conduct alternative is found true, no burden is reduced and no enhanced sentence is directed. The drug convictions and independent prior-prison-term enhancement are not invalidated. Independent defenses and applicable sentencing constraints remain.

The dissenters would reverse permission to retry either qualifying alternative for the same failed serious-prior allegation and remand for lawful sentencing without its doubling effect, while preserving the separate enhancement and convictions. Neither disposition fixes an aggregate sentence, release date or the treatment of stayed or concurrent counts.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Thomas delivers the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Breyer. They join Parts I–II in full: the classification of this proceeding as a noncapital determination of recidivist status, the conclusion that jeopardy does not attach, and the bounded remedy permitting renewed proof.

Justice Scalia files a dissent, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, addressing the additional personal-conduct qualification's constitutional function as an element and the resulting finality of acquittal. Justice Stevens files an additional dissent on the finality of this failed trial-like aggravating adjudication even if it is treated as genuine noncapital sentencing.

Stevens joins Scalia's case-confined element ground and separately gives a broader finality ground. His join adopts no general rule for every fact that increases a punishment maximum; the common dissent addresses this additional personal-conduct qualification and the completed failed adjudication. Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg do not join an extension of Bullington to genuine noncapital sentencing merely because trial safeguards were afforded. No combination of dissenting rationales creates a Court holding.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. This noncapital recidivist-status determination does not receive offense-acquittal finality

**Holding and operative rule:** The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar renewed proof of the serious character of Monge's prior conviction in this noncapital sentencing proceeding after the first proof was insufficient. Here the required personal-conduct qualification determines recidivist status for punishment of the current offense, rather than adjudicating a new offense or a capital acquittal-like determination; neither the availability of trial safeguards nor the increase in the punishment ceiling alone makes this particular determination an offense acquittal.

**Authority:** Part I of Thomas's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Breyer; five direct joins to this classification and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** DiFrancesco distinguishes ordinary sentencing determinations from final acquittals of offenses. Bullington and Rumsey preserve acquittal-like protection for their capital adjudications; the Court does not erase that exception or make a statutory label conclusive. The present allegation concerns the serious character of an existing conviction as a basis for punishment of the current drug offense. Renewed proof neither prosecutes the old assault again nor reopens the current drug verdicts.

The strongest contrary argument is that the generic conviction does not establish the required personal use or infliction, and that this additional fact authorizes a higher maximum after a completed criminal-burden hearing. The Court accepts those premises. It nevertheless classifies this inquiry into the prior conviction's qualifying character as noncapital recidivist sentencing, not a new offense adjudication. California's jury, confrontation, evidence and reasonable-doubt safeguards govern the proceeding; their provision does not itself create federal acquittal finality. Legislative characterization alone could not remove protection from an actual offense.

Caspari left these direct-review merits open. Almendarez-Torres construed the federal reentry statute to make its prior conviction an aggravated-offense element; it adopted neither a general constitutional recidivism exception nor a general maximum-increase rule. Its statutory construction does not decide California's different classification. Witte and Watts address sentencing within conviction-authorized punishment and do not independently answer the increased-ceiling question here. The Court therefore decides this particular classification directly rather than treating those cases as foreclosing the argument.

The holding supplies no comprehensive classification of all maximum-increasing facts, mandatory minima, unchanged-range findings or prior-conviction procedures. It decides no independent indictment, jury or due-process claim, changes no actual jury waiver, and permits no relabeling of an actual offense to evade jeopardy.

##### II. Permission to prove the allegation again neither repairs the first record nor directs an enhancement

**Holding and operative rule:** The California judgment permitting another proof proceeding is affirmed because the disputed adjudication does not receive offense-acquittal protection, not because the State's first evidence was sufficient or its failure excusable. Any renewed proceeding must satisfy applicable law and safeguards; no true finding, automatic doubling, aggregate sentence, or invalidation of the independent prior-prison-term enhancement or drug convictions follows from this judgment.

**Authority:** Part II of Thomas's opinion; the same five Justices join the complete remedy and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The State conceded that the original prison record failed to prove personal weapon use or personal infliction of great bodily injury beyond a reasonable doubt. A valid generic assault conviction remains distinct from the unproved serious-felony qualification. The Court makes no new finding about the assault and does not treat the prosecutor's assertion as evidence.

Burks, as applied in Hinds, bars a second prosecution opportunity to supply missing proof after actual insufficiency in a constitutionally protected offense adjudication. That consequence is mandatory when its predicate exists. The present judgment rests on nonattachment to this recidivist-status determination; it creates no trial-error exception, proof-repair permission after an offense acquittal, or authority to reopen a protected capital acquittal. Watts's different-burden evidentiary-use rule supplies no such permission either.

The State may attempt renewed proof only under governing state law. The Court neither requires it to do so nor predicts success, and it does not decide whether another independent defense or sentencing constraint limits that course. Count one's seven-year unenhanced maximum is not a fixed lawful aggregate sentence; the selected term, doubled term, independent year, stayed count and concurrent count retain their distinct treatment. Those matters remain for lawful proceedings below.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. DiFrancesco:** Applied to distinguish ordinary sentence reconsideration from final offense acquittal, without making a sentencing label dispositive.
- **Bullington v. Missouri; Arizona v. Rumsey:** Preserved for acquittal-like capital adjudications; the Court declines to extend their protection to this noncapital recidivist-status determination.
- **Caspari v. Bohlen:** Its habeas new-rule holding remains intact; its reservation of the direct-review merits is answered only for the proceeding now presented.
- **Almendarez-Torres v. United States:** Its statutory aggravated-reentry element holding remains controlling. It supplies neither a general constitutional recidivism exception nor an already controlling constitutional maximum-increase rule; no part is overruled.
- **Witte v. United States; United States v. Watts / United States v. Putra:** Retain their bounds concerning ordinary sentencing within conviction-based authority and their independent constitutional reservations. They do not independently decide this increased-maximum classification or permit retrial after an offense acquittal.
- **McMillan v. Pennsylvania:** Its unchanged-maximum setting does not resolve this different ceiling increase; its limited holding is undisturbed.
- **Burks v. United States; United States v. Hinds:** Preserve acquittal and the bar on renewed proof after actual insufficiency in a protected offense adjudication. The result here depends on classification and nonattachment, not a new exception to that consequence.
- **United States v. Dixon; Grady v. Corbin:** Dixon's retained Grady rule remains unchanged. Those same-offense rules do not themselves classify the disputed enhancement proceeding as a prosecution to which jeopardy attaches.

No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

The previously unresolved direct-review issue is resolved for this noncapital serious-prior qualification: failed proof does not receive offense-acquittal finality, so the Double Jeopardy Clause does not forbid another lawful proof proceeding. The State still must prove the qualifying facts under governing safeguards, and the initial unsupported finding is not restored. Actual offense acquittals, acquittal-like capital determinations, the independent convictions and enhancement, and unpresented jury, indictment and due-process questions retain their separate rules. No universal constitutional recidivism exception or rule for every maximum-increasing fact is adopted. The dissenters' element-function and additional sentencing-finality grounds are noncontrolling.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Scalia, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, dissenting.** The additional personal-conduct fact was not established by the old conviction and was indispensable to authority for the greater punishment maximum. It therefore performs the constitutional work of an offense element. Calling it the serious character of a prior conviction does not remove that function. Ordinary reliance on criminal history within already authorized punishment is different from adjudicating a new fact needed to enlarge the available range. McMillan's unchanged ceiling and Witte's and Watts's ordinary sentencing uses do not answer this case. Almendarez-Torres resolved its federal statute without a controlling constitutional exception, and California's classification now requires deciding the constitutional question within the preserved double-jeopardy claim.

California afforded a completed reasonable-doubt adjudication and conceded that its proof failed. Once the allegation is recognized as an element for this finality inquiry, Burks and Hinds bar another opportunity to supply the missing prosecution evidence. Either personal weapon use or personal infliction of great bodily injury was an alternative way to qualify the same failed allegation; switching alternatives cannot avoid the bar. These Justices would reverse and require lawful sentencing without its doubling effect. They do not invalidate the generic assault conviction, the drug convictions or the separate prison-term enhancement, prescribe an aggregate term, or adjudicate a separate indictment or jury defect. This is an element-function ground, not a holding that trial procedures alone extend Bullington to genuine noncapital sentencing.

**Stevens, additionally dissenting.** He joins that confined element conclusion but also considers the State's failed aggravating adjudication final even if the proceeding remains genuine sentencing. The State chose a trial-like determination, had a complete opportunity to prove the prescribed allegation beyond a reasonable doubt, and failed to establish the condition producing additional punishment. Bullington's acquittal-finality principle and Burks's prohibition on repeated attempts to repair insufficient prosecution proof should not turn on whether the possible penalty is death. His Caspari position likewise would protect a completed status adjudication without making a changed maximum a necessary condition. State procedural generosity alone creates no federal right, but the adjudication's substantive punitive consequence, criminal burden and completed proof failure together justify protection. He would reverse renewed-proof permission on this additional ground and order the same bounded sentencing remand. Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg do not join this extension to genuine noncapital sentencing.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the California Supreme Court for further proceedings consistent with the affirmed rejection of the federal double-jeopardy bar. A renewed serious-prior proof proceeding is permitted only as otherwise lawful; it is not commanded. The unsupported first serious-prior finding and doubled term are not reinstated by this decision, and no substitute sentence or release order is entered. Supreme Court review of this event is complete; the remaining state proceedings retain their independent legal constraints.
