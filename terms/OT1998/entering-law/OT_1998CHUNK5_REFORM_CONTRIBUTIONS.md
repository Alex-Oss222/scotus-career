## Source: state/holdings/first-amendment.md - Day v. Holahan

#### Day v. Holahan

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-672.
**Decided:** May 15, 1995.

##### Authority of the responsive-financing judgment

**Authority of the judgment:** The section 10A.25(13) responsive-financing invalidation is affirmed 5–4: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support the judgment. Kennedy's plurality, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, treats the adverse proportional legal trigger as an expressive burden; Stone-Zsela's judgment concurrence rests on insufficient programmatic connection on this record and rejects categorical invalidity. Neither framework controls, and no controlling rationale is established under Marks. The judgment binds the parties; the three independently majority-supported holdings below do not supply a unified financing rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The invalidated section 10A.25(13) mechanism increases candidate limits by independent expenditures opposing the candidate plus expenditures for the candidate's major-party opponents, excluding an association's communications solely to its dues-paying members. The provision itself has no $100 threshold; that figure describes the lower court's operational reporting scenario. The Board must notify every candidate in the race within 48 hours after receipt of the report or notice under section 10A.20(3), (6) or (6b), and within three days after notice must pay half the expenditure only to a subsidy-eligible candidate who has raised twice the minimum match, from an appropriation from the general fund. The lower court's electoral-result-eligibility concern describes implementation; it is neither a statutory payment exception nor a finding that every subsidy is immediately payable.

##### The special immediate-notice obligation falls with this trigger

**Holding:** Section 10A.20, subdivision 6b's additional immediate affidavit and service obligation cannot be enforced independently of the invalid responsive-financing mechanism on this scheme's demonstrated function and legislative relationship. Under Minnesota section 645.20, the special mechanism is essentially and inseparably connected with the invalid provision such that the legislature cannot be presumed to have enacted it alone; ordinary campaign reports remain enforceable within their separate terms.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct votes. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissent on this component.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The additional obligation is a twenty-four-hour affidavit and service on the candidates and campaign treasurers in the affected race. The holding rests on essential legislative connection under section 645.20, not physical impossibility of reporting. The independent vagueness and overbreadth theories are not reached.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Buckley, 424 U.S. 1: preserves distinct disclosure interests and associational protections; no categorical disclosure invalidation follows.
- Gibson, 372 U.S. 539: applies the need for a substantial relation between information demanded and the legitimate subject; does not eliminate ordinary reports.
- Brown v. Socialist Workers, 459 U.S. 87: recognizes genuine disclosure interests with protection for demonstrated associational harms; no new general exemption is created.

##### This $100 intermediary-contribution ceiling lacks sufficient fit

**Holding:** Minnesota's $100 annual ceiling on contributions received by the covered political committees and political funds is unconstitutional on the established burden and insufficiently fitted corruption/circumvention justification. Contributions remain distinct from independent expenditures: realistic routes around valid candidate limits may be regulated, but a broadly restrictive ceiling must preserve meaningful collective political association and fit an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism; no permanent dollar minimum follows.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes, at this stated functional application.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 10A.27(12) covers aggregate annual contributions from individuals, political committees and political funds, but excludes a candidate's principal campaign committee and a political party unit defined in section 10A.275 from its covered recipients. Higher limits, coordination controls, candidate transfers and demonstrated conduit restrictions remain open. The record's substantial pooling burden does not establish a permanent constitutional dollar floor or equate contributions with independent expenditures.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Buckley: applies contribution/expenditure distinction, corruption fit and effective collective advocacy; its historical dollar figure is no minimum.
- California Medical, 453 U.S. 182: preserves candidate-transfer anticircumvention regulation; its plurality is not a categorical answer for every committee.
- Citizens Against Rent Control, 454 U.S. 290: applies protection of pooling for collective political expression; does not immunize a candidate-contribution conduit.
- NCPAC, 470 U.S. 480: preserves independent advocacy at its scope; contributions are not silently equated with expenditures.

##### MCCL receives protection on its actual nonprofit characteristics

**Holding:** Minnesota may not prohibit MCCL's independent expenditures solely because its incidental business activity and absence of a formal policy against significant corporate contributions fall outside section 211B.15, subdivision 15's literal safe harbor. On the undisputed minimal-business-income and insignificant-corporate-funding record, its relevant political-association characteristics support as-applied MCFL protection; material commercial accumulation, economic claimants or corporate/union conduit funding requires a different assessment.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by all eight Associates: nine direct votes on this as-applied holding. No general overruling or endorsement beyond Austin's surviving scope is adopted.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The section 211B.15(15) safe harbor requires no business activity, no economic shareholders or claimants, no establishment by a business corporation or union, and a policy against significant contributions from them. Protection rests on MCCL's actual minimal business income and undisputed insignificant corporate funding. That checklist cannot foreclose this as-applied protection; materially changed operations or corporate or union financing may require reassessment. Direct corporate candidate contributions and ordinary business-corporation treasury restrictions are not decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- MCFL, 479 U.S. 238: applies protection to actual political-resource, structure and funding characteristics; descriptive facts do not foreclose this narrow as-applied claim.
- Austin, 494 U.S. 652: distinguishes materially different commercially accumulated corporate wealth; its surviving corporate-treasury rule is not overruled.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the Eighth Circuit on all four components. Maintain the permanent restraints on section 10A.25(13)'s responsive financing, section 10A.20(6b)'s linked special notice and section 10A.27(12)'s $100 annual ceiling, and MCCL's bounded as-applied protection under section 211B.15(15). Ordinary reports and otherwise lawful corporate and contribution regulation remain; no damages, general public-financing prohibition or facial excision of corporate restrictions follows.

## Source: state/holdings/first-amendment.md - Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. Federal Election Commission, 518 U.S. 604 (1996)

#### Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. Federal Election Commission, 518 U.S. 604 (1996)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-489.
**Decided:** June 26, 1996.

##### Whether the Senate party-expenditure ceiling may reach this independent advertisement

**Holding:** A political party's own campaign advertisement that is genuinely independent of a candidate may not be subjected to the Senate-election expenditure ceiling in 2 U.S.C. §441a(d)(3) merely because its speaker is an official political party. Party identity and a continuing electoral relationship do not, without more, justify applying that ceiling to this independent expression; the adverse enforcement judgment must therefore be vacated.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s Part I, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; six direct votes.

**Material application:** The party’s $15,000 pre-nomination advertisement was genuinely independent. Its prior assignment of the whole $103,248 adjusted election allowance explains statutory excess, not constitutional validity. Legitimate corruption and circumvention concerns do not establish an actual candidate connection from party identity alone.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No majority adopts the complete narrower expenditure framework or its prudential reservation rationale (O’Connor, Souter, Breyer), or Kennedy/Scalia/Thomas’s broader coordinated-party-expression protection. No Marks combination is used. Coordinated expression, direct candidate cash, earmarks, republication, sham independence and actual conduits are neither generally immunized nor upheld by implication. No universal bribery-proof, express-advocacy formula, constitutional dollar floor, vagueness or administrative-deference rule.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Adverse enforcement/penalty mandate vacated and remanded, 6–3. The broader Senate-election counterclaim receives no majority constitutional determination; no fixed penalty, damages, refund or compelled new factual trial.

##### Whether party identity supplies the connection required by the alternative contribution theory

**Holding:** For the government's theory that this advertisement is a contribution under §441a(a)(7)(B)(i), party status alone cannot substitute for expenditure made in cooperation, consultation or concert with, or at the request or suggestion of, a candidate, the candidate's authorized political committees or their agents. On the accepted independence premise, that theory cannot preserve the adverse enforcement judgment under §441a(a)(2).

**Proposition-level authority:** The same six-Justice Part I coalition.

**Material application:** A categorical agency characterization is not a factual finding of consultation; competing assertions about novelty and noncontest establish neither forfeiture nor concession. Transfers among national, State, district, local and subordinate same-party committees are exempt from §441a(a)(1) and (2). Candidate-material dissemination, distribution or republication under (a)(7)(B)(ii), and original-source attribution for earmarked/intermediary contributions under (a)(8), have distinct predicates absent here.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The constitutionality of a transaction with an actual connection, all interactions with (d)(1)’s notwithstanding clause, presidential allowances and other House limits are not decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1:** Its distinction between independent expenditures and candidate contributions supplies the present independent-advertisement rule. Its treatment of the party provision's Fifth Amendment challenge did not decide this First Amendment question. The general distinction remains in force.
- **FEC v. National Conservative Political Action Committee, 470 U.S. 480:** Its protection of genuinely independent political advocacy supports the result; it supplies no categorical immunity for actual contribution channels.
- **Day v. Holahan, May 15, 1995:** Its controlling protection of meaningful intermediary association and preservation of realistic conduit regulation inform the required distinction. Its recipient category excluded political-party units, and its responsive-financing judgment had no unified controlling rationale. Neither is converted into an earlier decision of this party-spending question.
- **FEC v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life, Inc., 479 U.S. 238:** Its protection of political expression and its nonprofit setting remain distinct; the Court does not rest today's judgment on a universal express-advocacy formula or extend the nonprofit exception to every entity.

## Source: state/holdings/election-law.md - Kentucky Right to Life, Inc. v. Stengel

#### Kentucky Right to Life, Inc. v. Stengel

**Docket or dockets:** 97-60; Sixth Circuit No. 95-6581.

**Decided:** 1998-09-14.

##### Nonprofit status does not require a categorical exemption for corporate candidate gifts

**Holding:** Kentucky may apply §121.025's direct candidate-contribution restriction to KRL's general corporate treasury without creating a categorical exemption merely because KRL is an ideological nonprofit. The rejection of that facial exemption does not authorize restrictions on protected independent advocacy or determine whether KRL satisfies an independent-expenditure funding condition.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part III of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices.

**Material application:** The Court holds that a payment to a candidate remains different from an association's own expression. NRWC supports prophylactic treatment of the corporate contribution channel, and MCFL expressly distinguishes direct contributions from the independent spending it protects. The destination of the payment and the risk of candidate obligation supply the relevant ground; the State need not first prove that this corporation completed a corrupt transaction. KRLPAC supplies a separate voluntary path for candidate-directed support, while lawful independent advocacy remains distinct. Day protects an association according to its actual resources and structure; its nonprofit holding expressly left direct corporate gifts undecided. The contrary categorical exemption would erase that distinction. The Court does not find KRL to be a commercial treasury or conduit, decide its funding eligibility, or endorse every restriction that can be attached to incorporation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** NRWC applies to the contribution channel; MCFL's independent-expression protection remains intact. Day's nonprofit holding is distinguished according to the transaction it decided. Austin is neither overruled nor enlarged beyond its surviving corporate-treasury scope.

##### The paid candidate-advertisement identification rule survives this facial challenge

**Holding:** The surviving facial challenge to §121.190(1)'s payer-identification requirement fails in the paid candidate-finance and organizational setting presented. The rule requires the payer's name and address, introduced by the paid-for-by designation, on newspaper and magazine advertising, posters, circulars, billboards, handbills, sample ballots and paid television or radio announcements expressly advocating election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate, slate or group of candidates; ordinary issue advocacy is not covered merely because it mentions a candidate.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part IV of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight Justices. Thomas dissents.

**Material application:** The Court holds that identifying the financer of organized paid candidate advocacy serves source-tracing and enforcement interests different from general curiosity about an ordinary issue leafleteer's identity. The address helps identify the legal payer and connect an advertisement to financial transactions; it is a burden requiring justification, not dispensable statutory language. Buckley's disclosure interests and McIntyre's express reservation of materially different campaign-finance and organizational settings permit this bounded facial judgment. The on-message command is more intrusive than filing a report, but the actual financer-identification function supplies the necessary connection here without limiting the amount or content of the independent expenditure. The State cannot presume corruption or coordination merely from an advertisement's independence or political identity. McIntyre continues to protect ordinary anonymous issue leaflets, and materially different individual applications remain open. So does a claim supported by a reasonable probability of threats or reprisals. No actual retaliation, coordination, general membership-disclosure authority or requirement of permission before publication is found.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** McIntyre is distinguished on its reserved candidate-finance and organizational context without diminishing its individual-leaflet rule. Buckley supplies the distinct finance-disclosure interests. Colorado Republican preserves independence and forbids identity-based substitution for an actual candidate connection. Brown v. Socialist Workers preserves protection against demonstrated associational harms without creating a new blanket exemption.

##### The preserved challenges to the two direct $1,000 limits fail

**Holding:** The preserved facial challenge to §121.150(6)'s $1,000 giving and acceptance limit in any one election is rejected under the direct-contribution distinction. The separate challenge to §121A.050(1)'s $1,000 slate limit in any one election is likewise rejected, preserving that section's knowing-acceptance and knowing-giving terms and the giving prohibition's express exception for independent expenditures as statutorily defined.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part V of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; the coalition covers both distinct provisions.

**Material application:** The Court holds that the surviving direct-transfer challenges can be resolved under Buckley's contribution rule without treating contribution limits as constitutionally unlimited. A direct payment to the candidate presents a corruption and circumvention concern different from financing one's own advocacy. The lower court reported that counsel had proposed the $1,000 figure and essentially conceded its constitutionality. That reported position confines the preserved controversy; it is not a general unseen waiver or a reason to accept an incorrect legal rule. The direct-transfer character and bounded claim supply the judgment. The Court does not move the slate provision's knowledge requirement or independent-expenditure exception into a different section. Nor does it turn a per-election limit into an annual one, establish $1,000 as a constitutional minimum or safe harbor for every office, or authorize limits meant to equalize political influence. Future demonstrated cumulative or prohibitory burdens remain distinct.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Buckley's direct-contribution distinction and fitted anticorruption rationale apply; its historical figure is not a constitutional floor. Day preserves scrutiny of meaningful association and cumulative burdens without equating contributions with independent expenditures.

##### The annual all-intermediary aggregate requires the inquiry the lower court bypassed

**Holding:** A $1,500 ceiling on one person's annual contributions to all permanent committees and contributing organizations together must be assessed for meaningful collective political association and fit to an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism. Because the lower judgment sustained §121.150(10) without giving that inquiry its required force, the judgment on this component is vacated and remanded for application of Day to the existing record and ordinary remaining remedial questions.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part VI of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer: six Justices. Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Ginsburg dissent from this remand.

**Material application:** The Court holds that the ability to regulate a real candidate-funding conduit does not answer the fit of an aggregate that exhausts one allowance across unrelated recipients. Day requires meaningful opportunity to pool resources for political expression. California Medical permits prevention of circumvention through candidate-transfer channels, but its four-Justice low-protection reasoning is not a controlling categorical answer to every intermediary contribution. KRLPAC's candidate transfers identify a legitimate concern; they do not establish the fit of the law's complete combined-recipient reach. The lower court must consider actual recipient functions, the aggregate's breadth, the supported burden and the separate opportunity for direct candidate gifts under the proper standard. A higher figure than Day's $100 does not end the inquiry, and a general reference to smaller state campaigns does not supply it. Conversely, the Court finds neither every application invalid nor the State's evidentiary showing absent. This is a legal-standard remand, not a compulsory new trial, discovery order, new burden of proof, constitutional dollar minimum or direction to exempt all independent recipients automatically.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Day applies its meaningful-association and fit rule beyond the particular $100 ceiling while retaining its limits. California Medical preserves genuine conduit regulation without converting plurality reasoning into law. Citizens Against Rent Control protects collective expression; NCPAC preserves independent advocacy without immunizing candidate contributions.

##### Both gubernatorial receipt branches survive KRLPAC's facial challenge

**Holding:** KRLPAC's facial challenge to both §121A.030(4) receipt limits is rejected: the Fund participant's permanent-committee receipts are limited to 25 percent of qualifying contributions received, up to $150,000 per election, while the nonparticipant is subject to the separate $150,000 ceiling. Direct candidate receipts present a source-specific dependency concern, and this facial record does not demonstrate obstruction of effective advocacy or association; concrete later burdens under either branch remain open.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part VII of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: six Justices. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would vacate and remand both branches.

**Material application:** The Court holds that candidate receipts from one organized financing channel differ from the annual pool across independent recipients addressed in Part VI. Buckley's contribution distinction permits a fitted response to dependence on that channel without requiring proof of a completed corrupt bargain. The percentage branch limits the share measured against the participant's own qualifying contributions; it remains operative at receipts below the maximum. The nonparticipant branch uses its separate fixed ceiling. The district court reported no indication that the provision had actually limited PAC contributions or was likely to do so on this record. That reported conclusion supports this facial judgment; it is not a statutory exception or an irrebuttable fact about every race. The contrary concern that earlier donors may exhaust a later association's opportunity is substantial but does not establish facial invalidity here. Neither the maximum-fundraising example nor the number of committees needed to reach a maximum is a premise of this judgment. The Court does not rely on consent to unknown funding conditions, disfavor of PAC views, or equalization. Later proof of ordinary fundraising disabled by the percentage at low receipts, selective foreclosure or prohibitory cumulative effects requires separate consideration.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Buckley's direct-contribution rationale applies at this source-specific and facial level. Day preserves meaningful association and rejects a permanent dollar safe harbor; its different annual-intermediary and responsive-financing components do not resolve these candidate-receipt rules. Colorado Republican preserves independent advocacy without converting direct candidate receipts into independent spending.

#### Kentucky Right to Life, Inc. v. Stengel

**Docket or dockets:** 97-60; Sixth Circuit No. 95-6581.

**Decided:** 1998-09-14.

##### The removed applications no longer present a live constitutional dispute

**Holding:** The obsolete §121.180(6)(a) definition-based reporting and §121.035(2) corporate-employee claims, §121.025 corporate independent/issue-spending claim, §121.190(1) issue-only identification claim, and KRL/Zoeller and independent-spending branches of the §121A.030(4) gubernatorial claim are moot because the amendments removed the challenged applications and no surviving injury or supported recurrence remains. The theoretical power to reenact does not preserve those disputes; a credible threat, continuing enforcement or legally available retrospective relief would require separate analysis.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part I of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices.

**Material application:** The Court holds that the claims must be assessed individually against the law that now operates. The revised permanent-committee definition requires a primary purpose of expressly advocating election or defeat of identified candidates, slates or political parties and regular operation throughout the year; it excludes campaign committees, political issues committees, inaugural committees and party executive committees. Independent expenditures no longer fall within contributions. Section 121.035(3)'s permission for contributions concerning constitutional amendments, ballot public questions and issues of public importance is distinct from its nonprofit independent-expenditure permission, which requires that the nonprofit not derive a substantial portion of its revenue from for-profit corporations. Nothing here decides every nonprofit's eligibility under that condition. The changed text, removed applications and reported enforcement history support mootness, without a surviving damages claim or adverse current application. Arizonans requires a live claim throughout review. The mere possibility of reenactment is insufficient, but an express announcement is not the exclusive way a recurrence concern could be established. No merits conclusion about an obsolete restriction follows from dismissing its challenge.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Arizonans applies the requirement of a continuing controversy and the separation of live relief from dead constitutional questions. City of Mesquite's voluntary-cessation principle remains available on supported recurrence facts; it does not make bare legislative power sufficient or announced reenactment indispensable.

##### Mootness requires only the appropriate nonmerits treatment of the affected judgments

**Holding:** Existing dismissals for want of a controversy remain nonmerits dismissals; underlying merits adjudications of the now-moot applications, including the former issue-advertisement application, are vacated and those claims are to be dismissed as moot. The relief is confined to those applications and does not erase still-live judgments or confer constitutional merits jurisdiction.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part II of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight rationale votes. Stone-Zsela joins only this limited remedial judgment.

**Material application:** The Court holds that jurisdiction and equitable treatment of a judgment are different questions. Bancorp permits a just disposition under §2106 when ordinary merits review becomes unavailable, while denying any automatic entitlement to vacatur. Here the legislature changed the law as the losing challengers pursued review; they did not settle or voluntarily abandon that review. Leaving obsolete constitutional merits determinations in place would retain decisions that cannot now receive the review sought. Arizonans supports clearing such merits rulings while preserving the nonmerits character of jurisdictional dismissals. Those equities justify the limited cleanup, but supply no reason to disturb the independently reviewed live claims. The Court neither approves the repealed scheme nor treats the original merits rulings as void when entered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Bancorp supplies equitable §2106 authority and retains its settlement-specific limits. Arizonans applies corrective disposition without merits jurisdiction. Munsingwear supports judgment clearing when review becomes unavailable, subject to the equitable inquiry rather than an automatic rule.

#### Chronology and Posture

The State seeks to sustain the arrangements; the challengers seek retained relief. The lower appeals were Nos. 94-2387, 94-2388, 94-2390 and 94-2587; they are not additional Supreme Court dockets.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No recusal or nonparticipation is established.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Financing component: no Opinion of the Court

The responsive-financing judgment has five votes to affirm invalidation but no five-Justice financing framework. Kennedy's four-Justice plurality regards this adverse, proportional legal trigger as a burden; Stone-Zsela agrees to the judgment on the insufficient programmatic connection shown here and rejects categorical invalidity. Their shared judgment does not establish that either complete framework controls. The lower judgment invalidating this arrangement binds the parties. The reporting, contribution and nonprofit holdings below each have direct majorities.

##### The special immediate-notice obligation falls with this trigger

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 10A.20, subdivision 6b's additional immediate affidavit and service obligation cannot be enforced independently of the invalid responsive-financing mechanism on this scheme's demonstrated function and legislative relationship. Under Minnesota section 645.20, the special mechanism is essentially and inseparably connected with the invalid provision such that the legislature cannot be presumed to have enacted it alone; ordinary campaign reports remain enforceable within their separate terms.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct votes. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissent on this component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the special notice mechanism falls with the responsive program it implements. Buckley recognizes genuine informational, anticorruption and enforcement interests for disclosure, together with protection against serious associational burdens. Minnesota already requires ordinary expenditure reports. Subdivision 6b adds a twenty-four-hour affidavit and service on the candidates and campaign treasurers in the affected race, allowing the Board to administer the amount-linked response. Section 645.20 generally preserves valid provisions, but excepts those essentially and inseparably connected with invalid provisions when independent enactment cannot be presumed. That exception applies to this specially linked mechanism. The State answers that prompt information may still be useful. General usefulness does not establish an independently intended statutory function for this additional obligation once its material implementing purpose disappears. This is a legislative-relationship conclusion, not a finding that notices are physically impossible. Ordinary reports remain intact. The Court does not reach the preserved independent vagueness and overbreadth theories.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Buckley, 424 U.S. 1: preserves distinct disclosure interests and associational protections; no categorical disclosure invalidation follows.
- Gibson, 372 U.S. 539: applies the need for a substantial relation between information demanded and the legitimate subject; does not eliminate ordinary reports.
- Brown v. Socialist Workers, 459 U.S. 87: recognizes genuine disclosure interests with protection for demonstrated associational harms; no new general exemption is created.

##### This $100 intermediary-contribution ceiling lacks sufficient fit

**Holding and operative rule:** Minnesota's $100 annual ceiling on contributions received by the covered political committees and political funds is unconstitutional on the established burden and insufficiently fitted corruption/circumvention justification. Contributions remain distinct from independent expenditures: realistic routes around valid candidate limits may be regulated, but a broadly restrictive ceiling must preserve meaningful collective political association and fit an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism; no permanent dollar minimum follows.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes, at this stated functional application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this unusually low, broadly applied intermediary ceiling cannot stand on the showing made. Buckley distinguishes contributions from expenditures and permits contribution regulation fitted to corruption and its appearance while preserving meaningful association. California Medical sustains regulation of transfers to a multicandidate committee that makes candidate contributions; its fractured reasoning does not settle every independent-advocacy committee's case. The State therefore has a legitimate circumvention route to investigate, and the absence of legislative power in a committee is not dispositive. But Minnesota has not fitted this across-the-board ceiling to an identified route of improper dependence or evasion. Between one-quarter and one-third of MCCL-CSPC's contributions exceeded $100 in the relevant cycle, demonstrating a substantial practical pooling burden. A general reference to special-interest money does not justify it. Buckley's historical amount supplies no constitutional floor or permanent safe harbor. The Court leaves higher limits, coordination controls, candidate transfers and demonstrated conduit restrictions open.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Buckley: applies contribution/expenditure distinction, corruption fit and effective collective advocacy; its historical dollar figure is no minimum.
- California Medical, 453 U.S. 182: preserves candidate-transfer anticircumvention regulation; its plurality is not a categorical answer for every committee.
- Citizens Against Rent Control, 454 U.S. 290: applies protection of pooling for collective political expression; does not immunize a candidate-contribution conduit.
- NCPAC, 470 U.S. 480: preserves independent advocacy at its scope; contributions are not silently equated with expenditures.

##### MCCL receives protection on its actual nonprofit characteristics

**Holding and operative rule:** Minnesota may not prohibit MCCL's independent expenditures solely because its incidental business activity and absence of a formal policy against significant corporate contributions fall outside section 211B.15, subdivision 15's literal safe harbor. On the undisputed minimal-business-income and insignificant-corporate-funding record, its relevant political-association characteristics support as-applied MCFL protection; material commercial accumulation, economic claimants or corporate/union conduit funding requires a different assessment.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by all eight Associates: nine direct votes on this as-applied holding. No general overruling or endorsement beyond Austin's surviving scope is adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the State must examine MCCL's actual constitutionally relevant characteristics rather than mechanically deny protection because it misses a descriptive checklist. MCFL protects independent spending by a voluntary political organization whose resources reflect political support rather than commercial accumulation and whose structure and funding avoid economic claimants and corporate conduits. Austin preserves regulation of materially different corporate treasury wealth; a nonprofit label alone does not establish an exemption. MCCL's incidental business operations generated minimal income related to its political work, and the State did not dispute that corporate contributions were insignificant. Failure to adopt the statutory policy against significant contributions does not itself establish material corporate financing. The State's strongest argument is administrative certainty and prevention of conduits. Objective safe-harbor conditions can provide certainty, but cannot foreclose as-applied protection where the actual organization lacks the regulatory predicate. Changed activity, business revenue, corporate or union financing may justify reassessment. Direct corporate candidate contributions and ordinary business-corporation treasury restrictions are not decided.

**Precedent treatment:**
- MCFL, 479 U.S. 238: applies protection to actual political-resource, structure and funding characteristics; descriptive facts do not foreclose this narrow as-applied claim.
- Austin, 494 U.S. 652: distinguishes materially different commercially accumulated corporate wealth; its surviving corporate-treasury rule is not overruled.

#### Precedent Treatment

Buckley retains separate contribution, expenditure, disclosure and public-financing treatment. California Medical retains its candidate-transfer circumvention holding and fragmented independent-spending limits; no categorical immunity for intermediary contributions is recognized. MCFL supports the narrow actual-characteristics application; Austin is not overruled. NCPAC remains independent-expression authority rather than a universal ban on public financing. Gibson and Brown retain tailored disclosure/association principles. McIntyre's anonymous-individual-leaflet rule is not extended to decide these organizational arrangements.

#### Law After Decision

Effective May 15, these statutory responsive-financing and linked notice arrangements remain unenforceable, the $100 covered-committee/fund ceiling is invalid, and MCCL receives the established as-applied independent-spending protection. No five-Justice responsive-financing framework is established. The reporting, intermediary-contribution fit and functional MCCL applications have direct controlling majorities as stated. Ordinary disclosure, valid contribution/conduit rules, voluntary public financing and Austin's materially different corporate context remain intact.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy's plurality, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, treats the programmed amount-linked adverse benefit as a legal burden on the independent speaker, even without a spending ceiling. Buckley and NCPAC protect independent advocacy; an ordinarily permissible voluntary grant differs from selecting adverse independent advocacy as the proportional trigger for enlarged spending authority and public funds. The plurality does not recognize a right to escape private rebuttal or prohibit ordinary public grants. It regards Minnesota's asserted participation justification as insufficient on the established record and rejects equalizing influence as a sufficient suppression rationale.

Stone-Zsela concurs in the financing judgment alone. He rejects both categorical rules: public funding of Speaker B ordinarily does not burden Speaker A, yet an independent-expenditure trigger is not automatically valid. His inquiry examines the actual legal burden, whether candidate participation is genuinely voluntary, whether the independent speaker remains legally free to spend, whether the response is bounded rather than indefinitely escalating, whether the State identifies a legitimate program objective beyond equalizing influence, and the demonstrated relationship between trigger and objective. The established over-$100 operational scenario increases an affected candidate's allowance and may yield a half-amount subsidy, subject to statutory eligibility and twice-minimum-match requirements. Participation was already about 96 percent in 1990 and 97 percent among legislative candidates in 1992; these different populations cannot be compared as a common measured trend. This record failed to substantiate the claimed need to preserve participation. Electoral-result eligibility also creates the implementation difficulty the lower opinion identified in relation to statutory payment commands; he does not invent an automatic immediate subsidy or new statutory primary-election exemption. The defect is this particular automatic, potentially escalating response's insufficient programmatic connection, not public funds answering private speech. His framework leaves room for benefits adjusted to genuine electoral circumstances, without financing primarily to neutralize disfavored advocacy. It has one vote and is noncontrolling.

His Court opinion confines special notice invalidity to its material function implementing the invalid mechanism, preserves ordinary informational/anticorruption/enforcement disclosure, and applies Minnesota's actual severability qualification. The contribution opinion rejects the lower court's categorical suggestion that intermediaries pose no corruption concern simply because they lack governmental power. It identifies legally permitted candidate relationships, realistic corruption or circumvention routes, practical magnitude and meaningful pooled advocacy as relevant inquiry subjects without a mandatory universal dollar minimum; the common holding is this ceiling's deficient fit. The nonprofit opinion preserves an objective safe harbor while allowing as-applied protection for an actual voluntary political association outside its literal conditions. Economic claimants, substantial commercial activity, material corporate or union funding and whether advocacy resources reflect voluntary political support are relevant. Minor incidental revenue or a missing formal policy alone is not conclusive; commercial financing or control can require different treatment. These applications do not overrule Austin.

Stevens, joined by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, dissents from financing and derivative reporting results. The independent speaker remains uncapped and retains its resources; a voluntary public program facilitates additional advocacy rather than confiscating speech. They reserve materially coercive or punitive applications and do not permit equalization as a basis for direct suppression. Because the trigger should survive, its notice mechanism cannot be invalidated solely as an orphaned implementation provision. They would remand preserved independent vagueness and overbreadth objections rather than affirm disclosure in all respects. They join the contribution and MCCL applications, preserving realistic intermediary corruption controls and regulation of materially different business wealth.

### Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. Federal Election Commission, No. 95-489

Merits decision, June 26, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 15, 1996; decided June 26, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, 59 F.3d 1015, under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1).

The question is whether the First Amendment permits applying the federal party-expenditure limit to an expenditure made independently of a candidate. The Party also seeks broader declaratory relief concerning the Senate-election spending ceiling. That counterclaim raises a separate question about the decision's permissible reach.

In April 1986, before either party nominated its Senate candidate, the state committee paid $15,000 for a radio advertisement criticizing Democratic candidate Timothy Wirth's record. The committee had assigned its entire $103,248 adjusted allowance for the Senate contest to the National Republican Senatorial Committee. The District Court entered summary judgment for the Party and dismissed its constitutional counterclaim as moot. The Tenth Circuit reversed and directed judgment for the FEC and determination of an appropriate civil penalty. No penalty amount had been fixed.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Ginsburg.

The direction to enter judgment for the FEC and assess a penalty for this independent advertisement cannot stand. No damages, refund, fixed penalty or new factual trial is ordered. The six supporting Justices agree on that relief but divide over the broader constitutional counterclaim. The Court grants no facial declaration concerning coordinated party expression and does not affirm the counterclaim's mootness dismissal as a substantive rejection of that claim. Its further treatment remains for the proceedings below, subject to the controlling protection of this advertisement. The dissent would affirm enforcement and leave the amount of a penalty to the lower court.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Principal opinion, Part I | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer | Opinion of the Court on the limited independent-advertisement and statutory-predicate propositions | First Amendment protection of this genuinely independent advertisement; party status cannot itself supply the candidate-connection predicate for the alternative contribution theory |
| Principal opinion, Parts II–III | O'Connor | Souter, Breyer | Plurality explanation supporting vacatur and remand | Complete independent-expenditure framework and proposed separate consideration of the remaining constitutional counterclaim |

| Dissent | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Ginsburg | Would affirm enforcement, with penalty assessment below | Institution-specific contribution-channel, corruption and circumvention grounds; no equalization ground or finding of actual coordination |

No majority joins the complete narrower framework or the broader coordinated-expression rule. Part I controls through its six express joins. The Court does not derive an additional controlling rationale by combining the other writings.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Independent party expression

**Controlling proposition:** A political party's own campaign advertisement that is genuinely independent of a candidate may not be subjected to the Senate-election expenditure ceiling in 2 U.S.C. §441a(d)(3) merely because its speaker is an official political party. Party identity and a continuing electoral relationship do not, without more, justify applying that ceiling to this independent expression; the adverse enforcement judgment must therefore be vacated.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion, with the six-Justice coalition identified above. This is their expressly shared sufficient ground for protecting the independent advertisement, not a six-Justice adoption of every further explanation or reservation.

**Controlling explanation:** Paying for its own political message directly exercises the Party's speech and its members' association. Buckley distinguishes independent expression from resources furnished to a candidate; NCPAC protects genuinely independent advocacy against expenditure limits. Those principles do not disappear when the independent speaker is a political party. The committee's general purpose of electing candidates does not establish that a candidate directed, requested or helped arrange this advertisement.

The government relies on the continuing party–candidate relationship and the risk that donors will use parties to evade candidate limits. Corruption and realistic circumvention are legitimate concerns. But those concerns do not justify treating this independently arranged message as a candidate contribution solely from the speaker's identity. Day likewise preserves both meaningful collective advocacy and regulation of real contribution conduits; its intermediary holding did not decide the separately excluded party category, so the present conclusion rests on this transaction and the independent-expression authorities.

The advertisement preceded nomination, and the record supplies no actual candidate connection overcoming its independence. The fact that the adjusted party allowance had already been assigned explains why the FEC sought enforcement; it does not cure the constitutional defect. The Court requires neither proof of a completed bribe in every financing case nor immunity for every party payment. Whether coordinated party-authored expression receives the additional protection urged by three Justices is not resolved by a majority.

##### The alternative contribution theory

**Controlling proposition:** For the government's theory that this advertisement is a contribution under §441a(a)(7)(B)(i), party status alone cannot substitute for expenditure made in cooperation, consultation or concert with, or at the request or suggestion of, a candidate, the candidate's authorized political committees or their agents. On the accepted independence premise, that theory cannot preserve the adverse enforcement judgment under §441a(a)(2).

**Authority:** Part I's same six-Justice coalition. The proposition concerns the required connection and this alternative theory; it does not decide the constitutionality of every transaction in which the connection is present.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress stated a candidate-connection predicate. The agency's categorical description of party activity does not make that predicate a factual finding about this advertisement. Nor does the government's assertion that its theory was uncontested establish an actual concession. The Party's claim that the theory was newly raised supplies no independent forfeiture holding.

The separate contribution provisions therefore do not automatically disappear when the expenditure ceiling cannot be applied. They remain subject to their own definitions and qualifications, including the exemption from §441a(a)(1) and (2) for transfers among national, State, district, local and subordinate committees of the same political party. Financing the dissemination, distribution or republication of candidate-prepared materials under §441a(a)(7)(B)(ii) and attributing earmarked or intermediary-directed contributions to their original source under §441a(a)(8) present different predicates. None is established for this advertisement. The decision does not settle every interaction between those provisions and §441a(d)(1)'s notwithstanding clause.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

The Court establishes no general exemption for coordinated party spending, direct candidate transfers, candidate cash, earmarked donations, republication, sham independence or demonstrated donor conduits. It also sustains none of those applications by implication. The broader Senate-election counterclaim remains without a majority constitutional resolution; only three Justices would invalidate the ceiling for party-authored expression even when coordinated, and only three join the complete prudential rationale for reserving that issue.

The presidential allowance in §441a(d)(2), other House-election limits, a permanent constitutional dollar floor, a universal vagueness ruling and a general administrative-deference rule are not decided. The Court does not hold that the absence of a particular verbal formula always excludes election-related speech from the statute. No Justice adopts a general rejection of the contribution/expenditure distinction.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1:** Its distinction between independent expenditures and candidate contributions supplies the present independent-advertisement rule. Its treatment of the party provision's Fifth Amendment challenge did not decide this First Amendment question. The general distinction remains in force.
- **FEC v. National Conservative Political Action Committee, 470 U.S. 480:** Its protection of genuinely independent political advocacy supports the result; it supplies no categorical immunity for actual contribution channels.
- **Day v. Holahan, May 15, 1995:** Its controlling protection of meaningful intermediary association and preservation of realistic conduit regulation inform the required distinction. Its recipient category excluded political-party units, and its responsive-financing judgment had no unified controlling rationale. Neither is converted into an earlier decision of this party-spending question.
- **FEC v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life, Inc., 479 U.S. 238:** Its protection of political expression and its nonprofit setting remain distinct; the Court does not rest today's judgment on a universal express-advocacy formula or extend the nonprofit exception to every entity.

#### Current Law

Section 441a(d)(3) cannot support enforcement against genuinely independent party campaign expression at the scope decided here. A party-status presumption also cannot alone supply the actual candidate connection required by the government's alternative contribution theory. The holding adds this protection to the existing contribution/expenditure distinction without repealing the separate contribution channels.

There is no controlling rule today either invalidating all coordinated party-expression limits or approving their constitutionality. Neither plurality treatment becomes law through the six votes for protecting this independent advertisement. Ordinary contribution doctrine, the distinct rules for candidate-prepared material and earmarked contributions, and their operative qualifications remain available within their own scopes.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor's Parts II–III, joined by Souter and Breyer.** These Justices apply the independent-expenditure framework while preserving a separate inquiry into actual candidate resources and preventive contribution controls. Buckley supplies the functional distinction; MCFL and NCPAC support protection of independent expression; Day preserves both collective association and realistic conduit regulation. They give legitimate predictions about corruption their due but reject replacing the advertisement's actual circumstances with a party label. They do not demand a proved bribe for every preventive rule.

They would address the remaining counterclaim separately because party-authored expression, direct payment of a candidate's bills and undifferentiated transfers may involve different constitutional and statutory questions. The relation between the party allowance and ordinary contribution limits, and any necessary severability issue, should receive proper treatment before a broader declaration. The counterclaim is not jurisdictionally barred, and the advertisement need not be retried merely to recreate a fact of coordination. Their proposed restraint is supported by these three Justices; it is not the full rationale of the six-Justice holding.

**Kennedy's concurrence, joined by Scalia and Thomas.** These Justices accept Part I's protection of the independent advertisement but find that relief insufficient. A party pursues its own political program through the candidates it supports. Its own campaign message does not become another person's speech merely because the party and candidate cooperate. Buckley's valid treatment of ordinary contributions therefore does not decide the distinct restriction on that expression. Kennedy's Austin position and Day's protection of political association support the distinction; Day's preserved contribution rule continues to govern genuine contribution channels.

The broad Senate-election counterclaim and allegation that the ceiling chilled additional spending warrant resolving that party-expression claim now, in their view. They would invalidate §441a(d)(3)'s ceiling on the Party's own Senate-campaign expression, including expression produced in cooperation with its candidate, and remand for residual statutory and remedial consequences. They do not invalidate every §441a(a)(2) recipient limit, decide presidential financing, immunize undifferentiated party transfers or candidate cash, or protect donors using earmarked or sham conduits. Thomas joins this party-specific ground within Buckley's framework; he proposes no general merger of contributions and expenditures.

**Stevens's dissent, joined by Stone-Zsela and Ginsburg.** These Justices accept that the advertisement was independently arranged. They nevertheless regard the official party's continuing selection, support and financing of its candidates as a distinctive contribution channel. Donors who may give more through party channels than directly to a candidate could evade lawful candidate limits if all party election spending were uncapped. The official nominating institution's relationship survives the fact that this particular advertisement preceded nomination. Congress may address that institutional risk without proving an actual bargain or consultation for each advertisement.

They would uphold this election-related, inflation-adjusted allowance because it leaves meaningful collective advocacy while constraining the contribution channel. Ordinary issue discussion outside the statute's relevant candidate-election coverage is not automatically included. Buckley supplies legitimate contribution and anti-circumvention interests, and Day preserves realistic conduit regulation while requiring fit: Day's poorly fitted $100 intermediary ceiling does not itself decide this different party allowance. They do not rely on equalizing influence, reducing campaign costs or disliking expensive speech, and they do not extend their conclusion to every PAC, individual, press organization or nonprofit. The dissent would affirm enforcement under §441a(d), leave the penalty amount for the lower court and reject the requested facial invalidation without adjudicating every hypothetical coordinated transaction or finding an independent §441a(a)(2) violation.

The [official 1994 text of §441a](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title2/html/USCODE-1994-title2-chap14-subchapI-sec441a.htm) supplies the distinct expenditure and contribution provisions. The agency's categorical coordination description and the parties' assertions about an alternative theory are legal contentions, not findings of actual consultation, concession or forfeiture. No fixed penalty or later remand event is established.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Tenth Circuit. Its prior instruction to enter judgment for the FEC and determine a penalty for the independent advertisement is displaced. Further proceedings must respect the six-Justice protection of that advertisement and address the remaining counterclaim and any genuinely remaining statutory or remedial questions without treating either three-Justice constitutional approach as the Court's holding. No particular subsequent filing, evidentiary hearing, payment or disposition of the broader claim is ordered or presumed.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The removed applications no longer present a live constitutional dispute

**Holding and operative rule:** The obsolete §121.180(6)(a) definition-based reporting and §121.035(2) corporate-employee claims, §121.025 corporate independent/issue-spending claim, §121.190(1) issue-only identification claim, and KRL/Zoeller and independent-spending branches of the §121A.030(4) gubernatorial claim are moot because the amendments removed the challenged applications and no surviving injury or supported recurrence remains. **Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the claims must be assessed individually against the law that now operates. The revised permanent-committee definition requires a primary purpose of expressly advocating election or defeat of identified candidates, slates or political parties and regular operation throughout the year; it excludes campaign committees, political issues committees, inaugural committees and party executive committees. Independent expenditures no longer fall within contributions. Section 121.035(3)'s permission for contributions concerning constitutional amendments, ballot public questions and issues of public importance is distinct from its nonprofit independent-expenditure permission, which requires that the nonprofit not derive a substantial portion of its revenue from for-profit corporations. The changed text, removed applications and reported enforcement history support mootness, without a surviving damages claim or adverse current application. No merits conclusion about an obsolete restriction follows from dismissing its challenge.

**Precedent treatment:** Arizonans applies the requirement of a continuing controversy and the separation of live relief from dead constitutional questions. City of Mesquite's voluntary-cessation principle remains available on supported recurrence facts; it does not make bare legislative power sufficient or announced reenactment indispensable.

##### 2. Mootness requires only the appropriate nonmerits treatment of the affected judgments

**Holding and operative rule:** Existing dismissals for want of a controversy remain nonmerits dismissals; underlying merits adjudications of the now-moot applications, including the former issue-advertisement application, are vacated and those claims are to be dismissed as moot. The relief is confined to those applications and does not erase still-live judgments or confer constitutional merits jurisdiction.

**Authority:** Part II of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight rationale votes. Stone-Zsela joins only this limited remedial judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that jurisdiction and equitable treatment of a judgment are different questions. Bancorp permits a just disposition under §2106 when ordinary merits review becomes unavailable, while denying any automatic entitlement to vacatur. Here the legislature changed the law as the losing challengers pursued review; they did not settle or voluntarily abandon that review. Leaving obsolete constitutional merits determinations in place would retain decisions that cannot now receive the review sought. Arizonans supports clearing such merits rulings while preserving the nonmerits character of jurisdictional dismissals. Those equities justify the limited cleanup, but supply no reason to disturb the independently reviewed live claims. The Court neither approves the repealed scheme nor treats the original merits rulings as void when entered.

**Precedent treatment:** Bancorp supplies equitable §2106 authority and retains its settlement-specific limits. Arizonans applies corrective disposition without merits jurisdiction. Munsingwear supports judgment clearing when review becomes unavailable, subject to the equitable inquiry rather than an automatic rule.

##### 3. Nonprofit status does not require a categorical exemption for corporate candidate gifts

**Holding and operative rule:** Kentucky may apply §121.025's direct candidate-contribution restriction to KRL's general corporate treasury without creating a categorical exemption merely because KRL is an ideological nonprofit. The rejection of that facial exemption does not authorize restrictions on protected independent advocacy or determine whether KRL satisfies an independent-expenditure funding condition.

**Authority:** Part III of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a payment to a candidate remains different from an association's own expression. NRWC supports prophylactic treatment of the corporate contribution channel, and MCFL expressly distinguishes direct contributions from the independent spending it protects. The destination of the payment and the risk of candidate obligation supply the relevant ground; the State need not first prove that this corporation completed a corrupt transaction. KRLPAC supplies a separate voluntary path for candidate-directed support, while lawful independent advocacy remains distinct. Day protects an association according to its actual resources and structure; its nonprofit holding expressly left direct corporate gifts undecided. The contrary categorical exemption would erase that distinction. The Court does not find KRL to be a commercial treasury or conduit, decide its funding eligibility, or endorse every restriction that can be attached to incorporation.

**Precedent treatment:** NRWC applies to the contribution channel; MCFL's independent-expression protection remains intact. Day's nonprofit holding is distinguished according to the transaction it decided. Austin is neither overruled nor enlarged beyond its surviving corporate-treasury scope.

##### 4. The paid candidate-advertisement identification rule survives this facial challenge

**Holding and operative rule:** The surviving facial challenge to §121.190(1)'s payer-identification requirement fails in the paid candidate-finance and organizational setting presented. The rule requires the payer's name and address, introduced by the paid-for-by designation, on newspaper and magazine advertising, posters, circulars, billboards, handbills, sample ballots and paid television or radio announcements expressly advocating election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate, slate or group of candidates; ordinary issue advocacy is not covered merely because it mentions a candidate.

**Authority:** Part IV of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight Justices. Thomas dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that identifying the financer of organized paid candidate advocacy serves source-tracing and enforcement interests different from general curiosity about an ordinary issue leafleteer's identity. The address helps identify the legal payer and connect an advertisement to financial transactions; it is a burden requiring justification, not dispensable statutory language. Buckley's disclosure interests and McIntyre's express reservation of materially different campaign-finance and organizational settings permit this bounded facial judgment. The on-message command is more intrusive than filing a report, but the actual financer-identification function supplies the necessary connection here without limiting the amount or content of the independent expenditure. The State cannot presume corruption or coordination merely from an advertisement's independence or political identity. McIntyre continues to protect ordinary anonymous issue leaflets, and materially different individual applications remain open. So does a claim supported by a reasonable probability of threats or reprisals. No actual retaliation, coordination, general membership-disclosure authority or requirement of permission before publication is found.

**Precedent treatment:** McIntyre is distinguished on its reserved candidate-finance and organizational context without diminishing its individual-leaflet rule. Buckley supplies the distinct finance-disclosure interests. Colorado Republican preserves independence and forbids identity-based substitution for an actual candidate connection. Brown v. Socialist Workers preserves protection against demonstrated associational harms without creating a new blanket exemption.

##### 5. The preserved challenges to the two direct $1,000 limits fail

**Holding and operative rule:** The preserved facial challenge to §121.150(6)'s $1,000 giving and acceptance limit in any one election is rejected under the direct-contribution distinction. The separate challenge to §121A.050(1)'s $1,000 slate limit in any one election is likewise rejected, preserving that section's knowing-acceptance and knowing-giving terms and the giving prohibition's express exception for independent expenditures as statutorily defined.

**Authority:** Part V of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; the coalition covers both distinct provisions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the surviving direct-transfer challenges can be resolved under Buckley's contribution rule without treating contribution limits as constitutionally unlimited. A direct payment to the candidate presents a corruption and circumvention concern different from financing one's own advocacy. The lower court reported that counsel had proposed the $1,000 figure and essentially conceded its constitutionality. That reported position confines the preserved controversy; it is not a general unseen waiver or a reason to accept an incorrect legal rule. The direct-transfer character and bounded claim supply the judgment. The Court does not move the slate provision's knowledge requirement or independent-expenditure exception into a different section. Nor does it turn a per-election limit into an annual one, establish $1,000 as a constitutional minimum or safe harbor for every office, or authorize limits meant to equalize political influence. Future demonstrated cumulative or prohibitory burdens remain distinct.

**Precedent treatment:** Buckley's direct-contribution distinction and fitted anticorruption rationale apply; its historical figure is not a constitutional floor. Day preserves scrutiny of meaningful association and cumulative burdens without equating contributions with independent expenditures.

##### 6. The annual all-intermediary aggregate requires the inquiry the lower court bypassed

**Holding and operative rule:** A $1,500 ceiling on one person's annual contributions to all permanent committees and contributing organizations together must be assessed for meaningful collective political association and fit to an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism. Because the lower judgment sustained §121.150(10) without giving that inquiry its required force, the judgment on this component is vacated and remanded for application of Day to the existing record and ordinary remaining remedial questions.

**Authority:** Part VI of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer: six Justices. Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Ginsburg dissent from this remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the ability to regulate a real candidate-funding conduit does not answer the fit of an aggregate that exhausts one allowance across unrelated recipients. Day requires meaningful opportunity to pool resources for political expression. California Medical permits prevention of circumvention through candidate-transfer channels, but its four-Justice low-protection reasoning is not a controlling categorical answer to every intermediary contribution. KRLPAC's candidate transfers identify a legitimate concern; they do not establish the fit of the law's complete combined-recipient reach. The lower court must consider actual recipient functions, the aggregate's breadth, the supported burden and the separate opportunity for direct candidate gifts under the proper standard. A higher figure than Day's $100 does not end the inquiry, and a general reference to smaller state campaigns does not supply it. Conversely, the Court finds neither every application invalid nor the State's evidentiary showing absent. This is a legal-standard remand, not a compulsory new trial, discovery order, new burden of proof, constitutional dollar minimum or direction to exempt all independent recipients automatically.

**Precedent treatment:** Day applies its meaningful-association and fit rule beyond the particular $100 ceiling while retaining its limits. California Medical preserves genuine conduit regulation without converting plurality reasoning into law. Citizens Against Rent Control protects collective expression; NCPAC preserves independent advocacy without immunizing candidate contributions.

##### 7. Both gubernatorial receipt branches survive KRLPAC's facial challenge

**Holding and operative rule:** KRLPAC's facial challenge to both §121A.030(4) receipt limits is rejected: the Fund participant's permanent-committee receipts are limited to 25 percent of qualifying contributions received, up to $150,000 per election, while the nonparticipant is subject to the separate $150,000 ceiling. Direct candidate receipts present a source-specific dependency concern, and this facial record does not demonstrate obstruction of effective advocacy or association; concrete later burdens under either branch remain open.

**Authority:** Part VII of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: six Justices. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would vacate and remand both branches.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that candidate receipts from one organized financing channel differ from the annual pool across independent recipients addressed in Part VI. Buckley's contribution distinction permits a fitted response to dependence on that channel without requiring proof of a completed corrupt bargain. The percentage branch limits the share measured against the participant's own qualifying contributions; it remains operative at receipts below the maximum. The nonparticipant branch uses its separate fixed ceiling. The district court reported no indication that the provision had actually limited PAC contributions or was likely to do so on this record. That reported conclusion supports this facial judgment; it is not a statutory exception or an irrebuttable fact about every race. The contrary concern that earlier donors may exhaust a later association's opportunity is substantial but does not establish facial invalidity here. Neither the maximum-fundraising example nor the number of committees needed to reach a maximum is a premise of this judgment. The Court does not rely on consent to unknown funding conditions, disfavor of PAC views, or equalization. Later proof of ordinary fundraising disabled by the percentage at low receipts, selective foreclosure or prohibitory cumulative effects requires separate consideration.

**Precedent treatment:** Buckley's direct-contribution rationale applies at this source-specific and facial level. Day preserves meaningful association and rejects a permanent dollar safe harbor; its different annual-intermediary and responsive-financing components do not resolve these candidate-receipt rules. Colorado Republican preserves independent advocacy without converting direct candidate receipts into independent spending.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Arizonans: applies continuing-controversy and corrective-disposition principles; no constitutional merits jurisdiction survives for the moot applications.
- Bancorp: applies equitable judgment-disposition authority while distinguishing its voluntary-settlement rule; mootness alone does not compel vacatur.
- Munsingwear: supports equitable clearing of unreviewable merits judgments within the limited remedy ordered.
- City of Mesquite: preserves fact-supported voluntary-cessation analysis; neither bare reenactment power nor the absence of an express announcement alone is dispositive.
- Buckley: applies distinct contribution and disclosure principles; no numerical constitutional safe harbor or equalization rationale is created.
- NRWC: applies the corporate contribution-channel distinction; no corruption finding about KRL is required or made.
- MCFL: preserves protected independent expression and its distinction from direct candidate contributions.
- Austin: remains within its surviving corporate-treasury scope; no general overruling or expansion occurs.
- McIntyre: remains controlling for ordinary anonymous issue leaflets; reserved finance and organizational settings receive separate treatment.
- Day: applies meaningful-association and fit requirements to the complete annual intermediary aggregate, preserves actual nonprofit protection, and supplies neither a dollar floor nor a unified responsive-financing rule.
- Colorado Republican: preserves genuine independence and actual connection requirements; candidate cash and realistic conduits remain distinct.
- California Medical: preserves candidate-transfer anticircumvention regulation; its plurality's low-protection rationale does not control all intermediary association.
- Citizens Against Rent Control and NCPAC: preserve collective and independent advocacy respectively without immunizing actual candidate-contribution channels.
- Brown v. Socialist Workers: preserves protection against demonstrated threats and reprisals without creating a blanket exemption here.

#### Law After Decision

Kentucky's annual $1,500 all-intermediary aggregate has not been held facially unconstitutional; its sustaining judgment must be reconsidered under Day's meaningful-association and fit inquiry. The direct corporate-gift, paid candidate-identification and two direct $1,000 judgments remain effective only within the preserved facial questions and distinct statutory terms. Both gubernatorial receipt branches survive KRLPAC's facial attack, with the percentage operative below the maximum and later concrete burdens reserved. The decision creates no universal dollar threshold, blanket nonprofit immunity, permission to regulate issue speech merely mentioning a candidate, or complete rule for public-financing conditions. Obsolete constitutional merits rulings receive no continuing force through the limited mootness disposition.

#### Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part; Stevens and Ginsburg join the annual-aggregate dissent only.** The three would affirm §121.150(10)'s annual-aggregate judgment on the preserved facial record. Buckley and California Medical permit preventive regulation of actual candidate-funding intermediaries, and KRLPAC makes candidate transfers. Day requires meaningful pooling and genuine fit, but its established $100 burden does not compel remand whenever a higher limit's practical effects have not been quantified. The valid direct candidate limit and anti-pass-through function suffice here, without declaring intermediary association less valuable or requiring proof of completed corruption. The dissent recognizes that the combined cap substantially burdens multi-organization association. It reserves developed proof of prohibitory cumulative operation, improperly covered independent advocacy and changed conditions, including inflation; its position creates no perpetual $1,500 safe harbor. Stone-Zsela alone further explains that the Fund-participant percentage connects the source ceiling to the slate's own qualifying receipts more closely than a flat maximum. It may bind at lower receipts; its facial justification rests on source-specific dependency and this record, not on a 151st donor. A small-receipt slate's later concrete fundraising disability remains open. That additional comparison is not part of the Court's holding.

**Kennedy, dissenting in part, joined by Scalia and Thomas.** These Justices would vacate and remand both gubernatorial branches for distinct fit analysis. They accept the difference between candidate cash and independent expression and the legitimacy of preventing candidate dependency. But a classwide receipt ceiling excludes later willing donors because others have already given, even though each donor obeys the base limit. Day and the contribution principles preserved in Colorado Republican require the added burden to have its own fitted justification. General hostility toward organized interests and an illustration assuming maximum fundraising and maximum gifts do not supply it, particularly for a percentage that binds earlier. They would remand without facially invalidating either branch, immunizing candidate contributions, or deciding the full constitutionality of voluntary public financing.

**Thomas, dissenting in part.** Thomas would vacate and remand the surviving identification judgment to address the statute's coverage of an individual's independent candidate handbill. Talley and the anonymous-pamphleteering tradition underlying his McIntyre position do not end when the pamphlet discusses a candidate instead of a ballot issue. Identifying organized campaign financing may justify distinct reports, but it does not automatically justify placing an individual's name and address on every protected handbill. He does not find that Zoeller actually distributed one, exempt every organization or medium, select an unverified statutory exception, or order a blanket injunction. His objection seeks proper treatment of the protected-pamphlet breadth, rather than facial invalidation of every identification duty.
