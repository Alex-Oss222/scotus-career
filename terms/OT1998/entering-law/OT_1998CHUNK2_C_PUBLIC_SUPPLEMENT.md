### Dewsnup v. Timm, No. 90-741

Merits decision, January 15, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

*Dewsnup v. Timm*, 502 U.S. 410 (1992), No. 90-741. Argued October 15, 1991; decided January 15, 1992. The Court granted review of the Tenth Circuit's judgment, 908 F.2d 588, affirming dismissal of a Chapter 7 debtor's action to reduce a real-property lien under 11 U.S.C. §§506(a) and (d). The debtor seeks avoidance of the lien above the property's established value. The bankruptcy court dismissed with prejudice, 87 B.R. 676, and the district court affirmed.

Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter participated at argument and at decision. Thomas took no part: argument preceded his October 23, 1991 oath, and no reargument is established.

#### Judgment

The Tenth Circuit judgment is affirmed.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Section 506(d) claim to reduce the lien to the property's value | Affirmed, 5-3 | White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy | Stone, Scalia, Souter would reverse and remand | The lien is not reduced; dismissal stands |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Allowed real-property liens in the presented Chapter 7 setting

**Controlling proposition:** In the Chapter 7 setting presented, §506(d) does not reduce a lien on real property to its current judicially determined value when the claim is allowed under §502 and secured by that lien. Section 506(a)'s valuation for treatment of a claim does not, on these facts, compel avoidance of the lien's excess under §506(d); other statutory settings and express exceptions remain outside the decision.

**Authority:** Blackmun's opinion, joined by White, Stevens, O'Connor and Kennedy; five of eight participating Justices control the statutory construction and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this allowed lien survives the requested Chapter 7 strip-down. Section 506's interaction is sufficiently uncertain in this setting that the established distinction between personal liability and the lien informs its construction. Long preserves a lien despite discharge, and Johnson confirms that extinguishing personal liability does not itself extinguish the creditor's claim against the property. Section 506(a) assigns secured and unsecured amounts for the claim's treatment; the Court does not read that valuation as automatically avoiding the corresponding lien under subsection (d). The debtor's adjacent-text reading has force, but it would alter the ordinary survival of an allowed lien and transfer later appreciation without a sufficiently clear instruction in this setting. Ron Pair's attention to enacted text remains governing; the disagreement concerns what these provisions mean together here. The Court confines its answer to the presented real-property lien and decides neither hypothetical applications of §506 nor reorganization-specific restrictions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Long v. Bullard*, 117 U.S. 617 — the ordinary survival of liens through personal discharge remains an interpretive premise, not immunity from every express avoidance provision.
- *Johnson v. Home State Bank*, 501 U.S. 78 — applied for the distinction between extinguished personal liability and a surviving claim against property.
- *Farrey v. Sanderfoot*, 500 U.S. 291 — lien survival informs the Code's operation subject to its actual avoidance provisions.
- *United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235 — preserved; the Court finds contextual uncertainty here rather than displacing the priority of clear statutory text.
- *United Savings Association v. Timbers of Inwood Forest Associates*, 484 U.S. 365 — §506(a)'s valuation function remains operative; it does not decide this §506(d) question.
- *Union Bank v. Wolas*, December 11, 1991 — its §547(c)(2) rule remains intact; no lien-avoidance rule is drawn from its treatment of debt maturity.

#### Current-Law Effect

Allowed, lien-backed real-property debt in the presented Chapter 7 posture is not stripped down through §506(d) merely because §506(a) values the collateral below the allowed debt. Section 506(a) continues to perform its valuation function. Other subsections, statutory exceptions and reorganization questions are reserved. No constitutional taking or future-valuation question is adjudicated.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone, joined by Scalia and Souter, would read the adjacent provisions consistently: allowance establishes the claim, while §506(a) supplies the amount secured for §506(d). They would preserve the established $39,000 secured portion and avoid only the unsupported excess, subject to preserved exceptions and estate-administration issues on remand. They distinguish Long and Johnson because survival through personal discharge does not defeat an affirmative statutory avoidance provision. Reliance interests cannot supply an unwritten exception to the enacted valuation language.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The allowed lien is not reduced under §506(d) to the judicially determined property value. The affirmance leaves the dismissal with prejudice in place and ends Supreme Court review. The Court does not adopt the Tenth Circuit's abandonment rationale or make a new finding about administration of the estate. No valuation rehearing, new abandonment finding, or retained jurisdiction is ordered.

**End of entry: Dewsnup v. Timm, merits decision, January 15, 1992.**

### Brecht v. Abrahamson, No. 91-7358

Vacatur and remand, 1993-04-21

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1992-12-01; decided 1993-04-21. Reported source: 507 U.S. 619 (1993). Seventh Circuit, 944 F.2d 1363. The case is before the Court on writ of certiorari to that court.

Question: Which harmless-error rule governs this preserved Doyle violation on federal habeas review, and what appellate disposition follows?

Brecht admitted shooting Hartman but testified that the rifle fired accidentally when he tripped. The prosecution permissibly impeached him with prewarning silence and impermissibly referred to silence after Miranda warnings and his failure to offer the account before trial. The Wisconsin Supreme Court found the Doyle error harmless under Chapman; the federal district court disagreed and granted relief. The Seventh Circuit reversed under Kotteakos, treating the references as cumulative in light of physical and circumstantial evidence. Judge Cudahy concurred on a separate Chapman harmlessness assessment. The preserved constitutional error is conceded; guilt and the harmlessness application remain distinct.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated in argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Vacate and remand for Chapman review, 5–4; no automatic writ.

| Component | Judgment and named votes | Remedy and limits |
|---|---|---|
| Doyle violation and amenability to harmless-error analysis | Constitutional trial error: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9) | No reclassification as merely nonconstitutional prophylaxis; no automatic structural reversal. |
| Governing standard and disposition | Vacate and remand under Chapman: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Souter (5); Affirm under Kotteakos: Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (4) | Seventh Circuit must apply harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt with the State bearing that burden. If it is not met, conditional relief allows retrial within a reasonable period or release from custody on this invalid conviction; no automatic writ or acquittal now. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Parts and exact joins | Judgment relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | White | I: constitutional trial error. Joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas. | Unanimous classification |
| Opinion of the Court | White | II–III: Chapman for this claim; vacatur and conditional-remedy directions. Joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Souter. | Five-Justice controlling rule |
| Concurrence | White | A: broader continuity argument. Joined by Blackmun, Souter. | Souter joins without taking a position on Powell’s merits |
| Concurrence | White | B: objection to rigid structural/trial taxonomy. Joined by Blackmun. | Noncontrolling methodological reservation |
| Concurrence | O'Connor | Entire. No other join. | Reliability and administration grounds; joins the Court |
| Dissent | Kennedy | Entire. Joined by Scalia, Thomas. | Would affirm using Kotteakos |
| Dissent | Stevens | Entire. No other join. | Would affirm using demanding whole-record Kotteakos review |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The protected-silence error remains constitutional trial error

**Controlling proposition:** Using Brecht’s silence after Miranda warnings to impeach his trial explanation violates Doyle’s due-process protection; permitted impeachment with prewarning silence is analytically separate. This violation is trial error subject to harmless-error review, not a merely nonconstitutional rule or a structural defect requiring automatic reversal.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court, I; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). The named majority adopts this proposition as the Court’s controlling rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the warning’s assurance matters. Doyle protects a defendant from being told that he may remain silent and then having that protected silence used to discredit a later explanation. That is a due-process rule of fundamental fairness, not merely another layer of prophylaxis that can be treated as nonconstitutional error. Jenkins and Fletcher permit the impeachment based on silence before the warning that occurred here; the prosecution’s broader references crossed a different line when they reached the protected period. The distinction remains material even though the prosecution also used lawful impeachment. Under Fulminante, an error in presenting the case to the factfinder can be assessed in the context of the rest of the record. This conceded Doyle violation has that character. Recognizing it therefore establishes neither structural error nor an automatic right to a new trial. The remaining inquiry concerns the effect of the constitutional error under the proper allocation of uncertainty.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Doyle, 426 U.S. 610 — applied: warning-induced silence receives due-process protection.
- Jenkins, 447 U.S. 231, and Fletcher, 455 U.S. 603 — distinguished: prewarning impeachment remains permissible in the circumstances presented.
- Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279 — applied: this evidentiary trial error permits harmlessness analysis; structural defects remain distinct.

##### Chapman governs this preserved Doyle claim on habeas

**Controlling proposition:** For this preserved Doyle trial error properly before the federal habeas court, the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the constitutional error did not contribute to the verdict. Collateral posture alone does not replace that burden with Kotteakos; the reviewing court must apply Chapman to the whole record, with relief conditional on failure of the State’s showing rather than automatic from the violation.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court, II–III; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Souter (5 Justices). The named majority adopts this proposition as the Court’s controlling rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court retains Chapman’s allocation for this constitutional violation. Section 2254 does not expressly choose between the competing harmless-error standards, and prior collateral applications in Rose and Yates do not conclusively settle that precise choice. They nevertheless support continuity with the familiar rule protecting constitutional trial rights. Kotteakos supplies a legitimate alternative model, and finality, federalism and the costs of delayed retrial weigh in its favor. For this properly presented use of protected silence, the Court concludes that uncertainty about contribution to the verdict should remain with the State responsible for the violation. Independent exhaustion, preservation and other habeas requirements continue to protect finality; Chapman itself permits a conviction to stand when harmlessness is proved. The Seventh Circuit must evaluate lawful impeachment and the physical evidence against the credibility defense under that allocation. The Court neither decides harmlessness itself nor reinstates the writ automatically, and it does not prescribe every collateral-review procedure.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Chapman, 386 U.S. 18 — applied on federal habeas to this preserved Doyle claim with its burden unchanged.
- Kotteakos, 328 U.S. 750 — not extended to replace Chapman here; its existing nonconstitutional-error operation remains.
- Rose, 478 U.S. 570, and Yates, 500 U.S. 391 — continuity supported; prior assumed use is not misdescribed as a square holding on this distinction.
- Stringer, Sochor, Espinosa and Richmond — unchanged: actual correction of capital weighing errors remains distinct.
- Sawyer and Herrera — not extended into a universal innocence requirement for review of preserved trial error.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective April 21, Chapman expressly governs this preserved Doyle claim on habeas. Kotteakos is not adopted as a general constitutional-habeas standard. Other habeas gates, structural doctrine and capital-weighing correction are unchanged.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

White, joined by Blackmun and by Souter in Part A with an express reservation of Powell’s merits, concurs that section 2254 and the Court’s constitutional practice more broadly resist giving the same error different remedial force solely because direct review has ended. White and Blackmun alone also question the reliability of a rigid trial-error/structural-defect taxonomy; that reservation does not undo their join in the classification of this Doyle error. O’Connor concurs on the importance of confidence in verdict accuracy and the administrative costs of multiplying harmless-error categories. She accepts that direct and collateral review can differ, but finds no sufficient justification for diluting Chapman here.

Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas, would affirm. Finality, comity and the limited collateral role justify asking whether the violation substantially and injuriously affected the verdict under Kotteakos. Lawful prewarning impeachment and the physical and circumstantial evidence make the improper references cumulative on this record. The dissent does not recast Doyle as nonconstitutional or treat structural defects as ordinary trial errors. It leaves open, without adopting an exception, whether an especially egregious deliberate error or a pattern of misconduct could independently undermine the proceeding’s integrity despite no substantial verdict effect. Stevens separately would affirm under Kotteakos, as anticipated by his Greer concurrence. He emphasizes a demanding independent review of the entire record, the prosecution’s obligation to explain harmlessness and the error’s actual influence on the factfinder rather than a bare sufficiency-of-evidence test. His reasoning and the principal dissent remain noncontrolling.

Supreme Court merits review complete. The Seventh Circuit’s judgment is vacated; it must perform Chapman review, including the State’s burden. No current writ, release order, acquittal or retrial deadline is entered. The alternative conditional remedy applies only if the State fails that inquiry.

### Nobelman v. American Savings Bank, No. 92-641

Merits decision, 1993-06-01

Argued 1993-04-19; decided 1993-06-01. Review of the Fifth Circuit, 968 F.2d 483 (1992), which affirmed rejection of the proposed Chapter 13 treatment.

Question: Whether §506(a) permits the debtors to modify the unsecured portion of a partially secured mortgage claim protected by §1322(b)(2).

The bank’s claim of approximately $71,335 was secured by a residence valued without objection at $23,500. The plan would pay the value-supported portion under the note but treat the balance as unsecured, with no payment on that balance. The bankruptcy court rejected the plan, and the district court and Fifth Circuit affirmed. The reviewed question proceeds on the statutory principal-residence premise; the deed’s ancillary language concerning condominium common areas, escrow, insurance proceeds and rents is not adjudicated as additional collateral.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated in argument and decision.

Affirmed, 9–0.

Proposed modification of the partially secured home-mortgage claim: Affirm: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9); no dissent. The proposed stripping treatment cannot be confirmed; other lawful plan treatment remains available.

Thomas delivered the opinion of the court. Joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter (9 total). Valuation and the protection of the holder’s mortgage rights under §1322(b)(2).

Stevens delivered the concurrence. No other Justice joins (1 total). Joins the entire Court opinion and publishes a concurrence.

When a creditor retains a secured portion of an allowed claim secured only by a security interest in real property that is the debtor’s principal residence, §1322(b)(2) protects the holder’s rights in the mortgage claim as a whole, notwithstanding §506(a) valuation and bifurcation. The debtor may not modify the deficiency portion in a way that alters those protected repayment and enforcement rights; independent statutory restrictions and the express cure authority remain effective.

Thomas’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

The Court holds that valuation and protection of the mortgage holder’s rights answer different questions. Section 506(a) permits valuation of the bank’s collateral and confirms that the claim retains a secured portion. Butner supplies the state-law rights attached to the mortgage: repayment on the agreed terms, retention of the lien, acceleration, foreclosure and recovery of a permissible deficiency. Section 1322(b)(2) protects the holder of the described claim, rather than merely the dollars assigned secured status by valuation. Reducing the unsecured balance would change the repayment rights in the same note and thus modify the protected rights. The debtors’ last-antecedent reading is grammatically possible, but the full phrase and the focus on the holder’s rights supply the better reading. Ron Pair requires attention to that enacted text, not a transfer of Dewsnup’s different Chapter 7 rule. Section 362’s stay and §1322(b)(5)’s cure authority operate independently. Wholly unsecured liens and disputed additional collateral are not decided.

Butner v. United States, 440 U.S.48: state law identifies the property and contract rights to which federal protection attaches.

United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, Inc., 489 U.S.235: the text of each Bankruptcy Code provision receives independent effect.

Dewsnup v. Timm, January 15, 1992: Chapter 7 §506(d) rule distinguished; §506(a) valuation is not displaced.


Before this decision: Dewsnup resolved a Chapter 7 lien question without deciding Chapter 13. After this decision: §506(a) still values the claim, but §1322(b)(2) protects this holder’s rights in the partially secured principal-residence mortgage. Section 1322(b)(5) permits curing a default within a reasonable time and maintaining payments while the case is pending on a claim whose last payment comes due after the final plan payment; it is not a general permission to rewrite the protected obligation. The automatic stay remains independent. The Court does not classify the deed’s ancillary provisions, decide wholly unsecured liens.

**Source notes.**

[Official report and underlying record description, 508 U.S.324](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep508/usrep508324/usrep508324.pdf); [Fifth Circuit judgment, 968 F.2d 483](https://static.case.law/f2d/968/html/0483-01.html), including footnote 3, supplies the valuation, plan and ancillary deed language. The official report at 328–332 identifies the reviewed premise, state-law rights and statutory cure qualification.

Record and statutory descriptions are supported by these sources. The public opinion summaries are paraphrases, not verbatim quotations. The additional-security issue is expressly outside this holding; there is no finding here that the deed contained no ancillary language. Its legal classification is unnecessary to the question reviewed on the stated premise. No special mandate date or subsequent lower-court outcome is reported.

The Fifth Circuit’s judgment is affirmed. This plan cannot strip the protected mortgage rights by treating the deficiency as a freely modifiable unsecured claim. The ruling does not prevent a new proposal complying with the statute, including the distinct express cure provisions, and orders no foreclosure or distribution.

Supreme Court review is complete by affirmance. The bankruptcy case remains subject to lawful plan proceedings; no new Supreme Court docket or particular replacement plan is created. This decision does not itself decide the interest-on-cure-arrearages question presented in Rake.

### BFP v. Resolution Trust Corp., No. 92-1370

Merits decision, 1994-05-23

#### Chronology and Posture

Reported at 511 U.S. 531. Argued December 7, 1993; decided May 23, 1994. On certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 974 F.2d 1144 (1992), which affirmed both the district-court/private-buyer and Bankruptcy Appellate Panel/Imperial routes on reasonably equivalent value, while rejecting a no-property-interest alternative. The question is whether a compliant noncollusive mortgage foreclosure conclusively establishes §548(a)(2)(A)'s federal value condition.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg participate at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the categorical value ruling and remands, 5–4.

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| District-court/private-buyer route | Reverse categorical value ground, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Remand for federal value inquiry and remaining statutory and recovery issues. |
| Bankruptcy Appellate Panel/Imperial route | Reverse categorical value ground, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Same bounded further proceedings; no immediate avoidance. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Ginsburg | Reverse and remand both routes | Entire opinion; both controlling propositions and all limits. |
| Dissent | Scalia | O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas | Would affirm both routes | Conclusive value for regular noncollusive real-property mortgage foreclosure only. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Regular foreclosure procedure does not conclusively establish federal value

**Controlling proposition:** A noncollusive mortgage foreclosure of real property that complies with state law does not conclusively establish reasonably equivalent value under 11 U.S.C. §548(a)(2)(A); when the statutory avoidance claim is timely and otherwise properly presented, the bankruptcy court must assess the value exchanged independently under that federal requirement. Procedural compliance remains relevant evidence, and rejection of conclusiveness establishes neither insolvency nor any other avoidance predicate, entitlement to recovery, or a defect in the debtor's property interest.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and Ginsburg; five Justices directly adopt the proposition and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress's value condition does work beyond checking foreclosure procedure. Section 101(54) expressly includes foreclosure of the debtor's equity of redemption, and §548 reaches voluntary and involuntary transfers. Treating every compliant price as equivalent would collapse the substantive value inquiry into a different state's procedural requirements. Butner v. United States respects state-created property interests where federal bankruptcy law supplies no contrary consequence; it does not eliminate the consequence Congress expressly provided here. Owen v. Owen likewise requires giving the federal avoidance provision its own operation. Dewsnup protects an allowed lien against the particular Chapter 7 strip-down it considered, while Nobelman protects specified mortgage rights in Chapter 13. Neither decided this separate avoidance question. Title stability and the effect of uncertainty on bidding are substantial concerns, but they cannot supply a conclusive exception to the enacted condition. The limited avoidance period, required statutory predicates, claimant's burden and applicable transferee protections remain. The Court reverses the categorical value ground without finding this sale avoidable or resolving recovery.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Butner v. United States, 440 U.S. 48 (1979): applies its distinction between state-defined property interests and countervailing federal bankruptcy consequences.
- Owen v. Owen, 500 U.S. 305 (1991): applies the independent operation of an enacted federal avoidance power.
- Dewsnup v. Timm, January 15, 1992: distinguishes its limited §506(d) Chapter 7 lien-survival rule; express avoidance powers remain separate.
- Nobelman v. American Savings Bank, June 1, 1993: distinguishes the §1322(b)(2) principal-residence protection; its rights and qualified cure rules remain intact.

##### Value is compared under realistically comparable forced-sale conditions

**Controlling proposition:** The §548(a)(2)(A) comparison concerns the value transferred and the total legally relevant consideration in realistically comparable forced-sale conditions, with competent evidence of property condition, enforceable interests and liens, realistic sale constraints, available purchasers, actual marketing and notice, and reliable comparable distressed transactions where pertinent; these are sources of valuation evidence, not mandatory elements or new foreclosure procedures. The avoidance claimant retains its statutory burden, and neither an unsupported appraisal above the price, procedural regularity alone, a fixed percentage, nor hindsight about a better hypothetical sale supplies the required conclusion.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and Ginsburg; five Justices directly adopt the proposition and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court requires a substantive comparison without substituting the ordinary voluntary market for foreclosure. Gelfert v. National City Bank recognizes that compelled sales occur under distinct conditions; that difference must inform evidence rather than make the actual auction price conclusive by definition. In re Bundles illustrates an inquiry into circumstances, but the Court does not adopt every feature of its proposed presumption. The property's condition, existing interests, actual exposure to purchasers and realistic constraints may explain why a reliable distressed-sale value differs from an ordinary appraisal. Conversely, minimal procedural compliance cannot make a depressed exchange equivalent without considering competent contrary evidence. Durrett's percentage discussion supplies no nationwide threshold. BFP's allegation of value exceeding $725,000, compared with the $433,000 sale price, is not a finding and does not by itself discharge the avoidance burden. The comparison must account for realizable value and all legally relevant consideration without treating ordinary uncertainty as proof of undervalue. The bankruptcy court must decide that question and any remaining statutory or recovery conditions on a properly developed record.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gelfert v. National City Bank, 313 U.S. 221 (1941): uses its recognition of forced-sale conditions without converting price into a conclusive federal measure.
- In re Bundles, 856 F.2d 815 (7th Cir. 1988): supports attention to the transaction's circumstances; no wholesale adoption of a formal presumption or every part of its method.
- Durrett v. Washington National Insurance Co., 621 F.2d 201 (5th Cir. 1980): does not supply a controlling minimum percentage.
- In re Madrid, 21 B.R. 424 (BAP9 1982), and In re Winshall Settler's Trust, 758 F.2d 1136 (6th Cir. 1985): their conclusive regular-foreclosure approach cannot govern the federal value issue after this decision.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Butner's state-property principle remains subject to enacted federal consequences; Owen supports independent avoidance. Dewsnup and Nobelman retain their Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 limits. Gelfert informs real forced-sale conditions. The Court rejects the conclusive Madrid/Winshall approach, adopts no Durrett percentage, and uses Bundles's contextual insight without adopting a formal presumption.

Effective May 23, 1994, regularity of a noncollusive real-property mortgage foreclosure is relevant evidence, not conclusive satisfaction of §548's value element. The bankruptcy court compares transferred value with total legally relevant consideration under realistically comparable forced-sale conditions and the claimant's unchanged statutory burden. Transfer coverage, the applicable one-year period, the asserted insolvency branch, transferee protection and recovery remain separate. The Court does not decide tax sales, personal-property sales, actual fraud, unasserted capital or debt-payment branches, or whether this particular sale ultimately fails the standard.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, dissents. They read reasonably equivalent value in the legal market Congress actually included: a compelled mortgage foreclosure conducted under the State's governing requirements. Ordinary market value assumes a voluntary sale, and a judicially constructed reasonable foreclosure price would replace the legal conditions that define this transaction. The dissent's rule would be conclusive only for a noncollusive real-property mortgage foreclosure complying with governing law. An irregularity permitting state-law invalidation would remove that conclusive treatment and require the otherwise applicable federal inquiry; insolvency, timing, other avoidance grounds and recovery would remain separate. The dissent relies on the property-law setting recognized by Butner and the concrete harm that uncertain titles and avoidance risk can cause lenders, purchasers and bids. It would affirm both lower-court routes on the value element, not on the rejected theory that BFP lacked a property interest. It does not extend its rule to tax sales, personal property, collusion or defective procedure.

#### Source Notes

The official [United States Reports, 511 U.S. 531](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511531/usrep511531.pdf), the [records and briefs collection](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0383), and the [Ninth Circuit opinion, 974 F.2d 1144](https://openjurist.org/974/f2d/1144) support the dates, transaction and two-route posture. The claimed value above $725,000 remains an allegation; no full trial-record valuation or presently recoverable amount is supplied. The operative statutory value and insolvency clauses are verified; unasserted statutory branches do not supply an additional holding.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Ninth Circuit for further proceedings in both affected lower-court routes. The rejected no-property-interest alternative is not revived. No automatic avoidance, title transfer, refund, fixed recovery or nationwide percentage is ordered. Any remedy must rest on proved avoidance and recovery requirements and applicable transferee protections; final titles outside this timely proceeding are not reopened.

**End of entry: BFP v. Resolution Trust Corp., Merits decision, 1994-05-23.**

### O'Neal v. McAninch, No. 93-7407

Merits decision, 1995-02-21

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 31, 1994; decided February 21, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, reviewing O'Neal v. Morris, 3 F.3d 143, which reversed a conditional grant of federal habeas relief. The admitted question concerns grave doubt about the effect of constitutional trial error; deciding the standard governing this instructional claim is antecedent to that question.

The Sixth Circuit found the instructions alone constitutional, then assumed a constitutional violation from possible instructional confusion combined with the prosecutor's misstatement concerning each defendant's required intent. It assessed that assumed error under a substantial-and-injurious-effect standard and placed prejudice on the prisoner. It found no injurious influence and did not expressly find grave doubt. The Court decides the governing review rule, without finding the assumed violation or determining its actual effect.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Sixth Circuit's judgment is vacated and the case remanded, 7–2. The direction to apply Chapman commands five votes. O'Connor announces the Court's opinion except for Part III, which states a broader three-Justice position.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Set aside the challenged appellate disposition and return the combined-error claim for further proceedings | Vacate and remand, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia, Thomas | No immediate writ, release, acquittal or reinstatement of the earlier conditional order. |
| Governing standard and directions on remand | Chapman instruction, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Stevens, Kennedy, Scalia, Thomas | Apply the State's whole-record harmlessness burden beyond a reasonable doubt to the preserved personal-intent instructional/argument claim. Stevens and Kennedy would instead remand under Kotteakos; Scalia and Thomas would affirm. |

The Sixth Circuit shall reconsider the challenged combined claim, with further District Court proceedings as appropriate. If a properly available constitutional trial violation is established, the State must show beyond a reasonable doubt on the existing trial record that it did not contribute to the verdict. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Chapman governs the preserved personal-intent instructional claim

**Controlling proposition:** For a preserved constitutional trial violation, properly available on federal habeas, in which the combined jury instructions and prosecutor's statement permit conviction without proof of the defendant's own intent required for the offense, the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt, on the whole record, that the error did not contribute to the verdict. Kotteakos's substantial-and-injurious-effect and grave-doubt framework does not govern this claim; the standard for other reserved categories is not decided.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct joins at this level of generality. Part III's proposed extension to ordinary preserved constitutional trial errors generally has only O'Connor, Stone-Zsela and Souter and does not control.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the State must establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt for this preserved constitutional instructional claim. Chapman supplies the inquiry whether the violation contributed to the actual verdict, rather than whether sufficient untainted evidence could support another verdict. Brecht retained that rule for preserved Doyle error on habeas; it left other categories open. The alleged permission to convict without finding O'Neal's own required intent concerns the jury's constitutional task as directly as the protected-silence error addressed there. No feature of this claim justifies reducing the State's showing merely because review is collateral. Finality and respect for state judgments remain substantial interests, but independent habeas requirements continue to protect them, and Chapman permits harmless convictions to stand. Section 2254(a)'s custody requirement does not expressly reverse the harmlessness burden after a qualifying violation. The Court resolves this instructional category, without deciding every other constitutional collateral claim, finding the assumed violation, or treating it as structural error.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brecht v. Abrahamson, April 21, 1993: extended from preserved Doyle error to the preserved personal-intent instructional/argument category; its original rule survives, while other reserved categories remain unresolved by the Court.
- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967): applied with the State's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt burden and actual-verdict contribution inquiry intact.
- Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946): not extended to replace Chapman for this constitutional claim; its existing operation for genuinely nonconstitutional trial error remains unchanged.
- In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970): applied as the constitutional requirement that the prosecution prove the elements of the charged offense; no new personal-intent element is imposed on offenses that do not require it.
- Sullivan v. Louisiana, June 1, 1993: preserved as the distinct rule for a defect vitiating the jury's governing reasonable-doubt standard; the assumed error here is not declared structural.

##### The proper standard requires renewed review, not an automatic writ

**Controlling proposition:** The Sixth Circuit's substantial-influence assessment with a prisoner prejudice burden does not supply the required Chapman determination; its challenged judgment must be vacated for review of the combined claim under the correct rule. If an available constitutional violation is established and the State fails to prove beyond a reasonable doubt on the existing record that it did not contribute to the verdict, the writ shall issue for the affected conviction or convictions unless the State retries O'Neal within a reasonable time; no immediate writ or present retrial deadline is imposed.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct joins in the remedy and its conditions. Stevens and Kennedy add votes for vacatur and further proceedings on a different harmlessness ground, making the judgment 7–2 without enlarging the Chapman coalition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a new application of the proper standard is necessary, but does not award relief merely because the court of appeals used the wrong rule. The instructions-only ruling did not finally resolve the assumed combined effect of the charge and the prosecutor's misstatement. Nor did a finding of no substantial injurious influence under a prisoner burden establish the stronger assurance Chapman requires from the State. The evidence of principal participation remains part of the whole-record inquiry; the Court does not reject that evidence or decide its weight. Brecht supplies the distinction between correcting the review rule and ordering a writ after the State fails the required showing. The receiving courts may resolve the constitutional claim or, on an express assumption of error, determine harmlessness under Chapman. A writ requires an established violation that warrants relief. Ordinary habeas gates and the connection between the violation and each affected conviction remain; neither an unrecorded state of grave doubt nor the terms of the earlier conditional order are presumed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brecht v. Abrahamson, April 21, 1993: its whole-record remand and conditional-relief distinction are extended to this instructional claim, without automatic reinstatement of a prior writ.
- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967): applied to the existing trial record; a weaker substantial-influence finding is not treated as the required finding beyond a reasonable doubt.
- Estelle v. McGuire, December 4, 1991, and Victor v. Nebraska / Sandoval v. California, March 22, 1994: their whole-charge reasonable-likelihood inquiry remains the rule for identifying an ambiguous instruction's constitutional defect; the Court does not decide that antecedent application here.
- Schlup v. Delo, January 23, 1995: its gateway for otherwise barred claims remains independent; neither passage through that gateway nor an innocence showing is invented for this claim.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Brecht's preserved-Doyle Chapman holding remains in force and now extends to the preserved personal-intent instructional/argument category presented here. Chapman retains its burden and proof standard. Kotteakos continues within its existing genuinely nonconstitutional trial-error domain; this decision creates no habeas remedy for ordinary state-law error. Winship's element-proof requirement and Estelle/Victor's identification of instructional error remain distinct from harmlessness. Sullivan's structural rule and Schlup's separate gateway remain unchanged. No precedent is overruled.

Effective February 21, 1995, Chapman governs this preserved personal-intent instructional/argument category on federal habeas as well as the preserved Doyle category already governed by Brecht. The State must establish noncontribution to the verdict beyond a reasonable doubt on the whole record. Kotteakos grave doubt is not an alternative dispositive test for this claim.

The Court has not adopted a general Chapman rule for every other category of preserved constitutional trial error. O'Connor, Stone-Zsela and Souter support that further extension in Part III; Ginsburg and Breyer reserve it. That three-Justice position is not current law. Nor does the Stevens–Kennedy concurrence establish a constitutional-habeas Kotteakos rule. The controlling propositions rest on five direct joins; no Marks construction or aggregation of different standards is necessary or adopted.

Proof of the constitutional violation, preservation, exhaustion, other independent habeas gates, structural-error rules and any prejudice element defining a different constitutional claim remain separate. The decision neither shifts every habeas burden to the State nor determines actual harmlessness or the ultimate entitlement to a writ.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, joined by Stone-Zsela and Souter, Part III:** These three Justices would extend Chapman beyond the category necessary to decide this case, to ordinary preserved constitutional trial errors generally when properly before a federal habeas court and susceptible to harmless-error review. They read Brecht's reason for retaining the State's burden as arising from the constitutional character of an established trial violation and the risk of its contribution to the verdict, not a feature peculiar to protected silence. In their view, distinguishing ordinary trial-error categories would add complexity without identifying a different basis for allocating that risk. Rose v. Clark and Yates v. Evatt supply continuity from prior collateral applications of Chapman, although neither is misdescribed as having already settled the later choice between standards. Independent habeas gates protect finality, and the State may preserve a conviction by proving harmlessness. Their proposed extension leaves structural error, procedural default, exhaustion and prejudice defining a distinct constitutional claim untouched. Ginsburg and Breyer withhold a join from this generalization while joining the specific instructional rule and mandate. This broader position has three votes and does not settle Brecht's remaining reservations.

**Stevens, joined by Kennedy, concurring in the judgment:** They would vacate because the lower court's prisoner-burden formulation does not preserve the grave-doubt consequence of the Kotteakos rule they would adopt for this non-Doyle category. Collateral review may use a substantial-influence threshold while still requiring the reviewing court to assess the whole record and the State to explain why an established trial violation did not substantially influence the verdict. Kotteakos supplies both that actual-verdict inquiry and the consequence of genuine grave doubt; mere evidence sufficient to convict is not enough. They would respect Brecht's binding preserved-Doyle holding without extending Chapman to this instructional claim. They do not find the appellate judges actually doubtful, declare a constitutional violation, or order a present writ. Their remand would require renewed Kotteakos review, with any ultimate relief confined to convictions affected by a proved violation that cannot be treated as harmless under their rule. They support vacatur and further proceedings but oppose the Court's Chapman direction. Their alternative standard has two adherents and does not govern the remand.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, dissenting:** They would affirm within the accepted legal question. In their view, section 2254(a)'s requirement of custody in violation of federal law supports requiring a prisoner in this non-Doyle trial-error category to establish substantial injurious influence before obtaining collateral relief. They accept that the statute does not expressly state a tie-breaking rule and that Kotteakos and Chapman allocate uncertainty differently in their own settings. Their proposed distinction concerns the collateral remedy after criminal review has ended. Brecht's preserved-Doyle holding remains binding, but they would not extend it to the category it reserved. A prisoner unable to establish the requisite substantial injury—including one whose showing leaves that matter evenly balanced—would not obtain relief under their rule. They rely on the court of appeals' existing rule and no-influence conclusion without making a new whole-trial factual finding. Their position does not demand innocence in every habeas case, recast constitutional error as merely state-law error, or classify a Sullivan defect as ordinary trial error. This petitioner-prejudice rule has two votes and is not controlling.

### California v. Roy, No. 95-2025

Summary merits disposition, November 4, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

On petition for a writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, reviewing its en banc habeas judgment in Roy v. Gomez, 81 F.3d 863. No oral argument is held in this Court. The questions concern whether omission of an aider and abettor's purpose to facilitate robbery is structural or trial error, the harmlessness burden and permissible method on federal habeas, and the antecedent objection that the named petitioners were not parties below. California seeks vacatur or reversal; Roy seeks rejection of review or retention of the habeas-relief judgment.

The trial charge required knowledge of the perpetrator's unlawful purpose and affirmative assistance, excluding mere presence, but omitted Roy's purpose to facilitate the robbery. The jury returned Mannix robbery and first-degree felony-murder convictions and a knife-possession finding without personal knife use. It also convicted Roy of Clark second-degree murder with personal knife use and acquitted him of Clark robbery. The state court reversed robbery-murder and multiple-murder special circumstances and their sentence while otherwise affirming. This federal challenge concerns only the two Mannix convictions. The state and district courts found harmlessness through their functional-equivalence assessments; the en banc Ninth Circuit expressly reached the contrary Chapman conclusion before its separate collateral-standard analysis.

#### Judgment

The petition for a writ of certiorari is granted.

**Vacated and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Stevens, Scalia and Ginsburg, who would affirm the habeas-relief judgment on their respective grounds.

The remand concerns only the Mannix robbery and first-degree felony-murder convictions. The Ninth Circuit must conduct the qualified whole-record inquiry, with District Court proceedings if otherwise proper. It may reach the same result. If an available violation is not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, conditional relief must permit a reasonable retrial period for those convictions; proper harmlessness permits denial. The Court makes no present harmlessness finding, directs no immediate release, fixes no retrial deadline, grants no new stay, restores no special-circumstance punishment and does not dispose of the separate Clark conviction.

#### Holdings

##### I. The existing official appellate party may seek review

**Rule:** Section 1254(1) requires a petitioner who was a party in the court of appeals; on this record, that court's repeated identification of the Attorney General as an official-capacity respondent and the timely petition expressly naming the incumbent in that capacity satisfy the requirement. The Court accepts the existing official party's identity despite the contrary earlier custodial pleadings; it neither adds a party nor holds that a caption, counsel's participation or a State's interest invariably establishes party status.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion controls with the eight-Justice coalition identified above. Stone-Zsela agrees only with rejecting the objection and reaching the case, without joining this rationale.

The Court holds that the official litigant identified by the court of appeals may seek review through the incumbent Attorney General. Scofield requires a party below; Perini keeps that statutory requirement distinct from injury. California's interest in its conviction alone would not suffice.

The amended habeas petition names Gomez and Borg, the answer identifies them as proper respondents, and the subsequent order substitutes Merkle for Borg. Those documents establish custodial participation and replacement. They do not supply an appellate ruling removing the Attorney General's office. The June 1995 panel opinion, September rehearing order and April 1996 en banc opinion repeatedly name Van de Kamp as a respondent. The Court accepts that appellate identification on these competing documents. Lungren's timely, expressly official petition identifies the incumbent of the same office.

FRAP 43(c)(2) and FRCP 25(d)(2) permit designation of an existing official-capacity party by title, subject to the court's authority to require a name. Their corresponding automatic-substitution provisions operate when an existing official-capacity party leaves office during the respective appellate proceeding or action. They disregard an immaterial misnomer, and omission of a formal substitution order does not defeat substitution. These rules continue an existing party; they do not create one. The Court does not assume that the change of Attorney General occurred during this appeal or this Court's proceeding. The appellate identification is the decisive predicate, not an imagined intervention or succession order.

Smith v. Barry supports reading a timely document for the litigant it actually identifies, while retaining mandatory party information. No custodian is added to the petition under Rule 12.4, and Rule 35.3 does not establish original party status below. Whether the People separately qualify is unnecessary to decide. The Attorney General's appearance as counsel does not independently establish party status.

##### II. This personal-intent omission permits harmless-error review under Chapman

**Rule:** The preserved omission of Roy's purpose to facilitate robbery is a constitutional trial error subject to harmlessness review, not an error requiring automatic reversal merely because an element was omitted. For this personal-intent violation properly available on federal habeas, the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt, on the whole record, that the error did not contribute to the verdict; neither collateral posture nor absence of accompanying prosecutorial argument substitutes a weaker burden.

**Authority:** Part II controls unanimously. Agreement on this category and burden does not establish agreement on the method or result of harmlessness review.

The Court holds that the jury's responsibility to determine the defendant's required intent remains protected, while the effect of this instructional omission may be examined. Gaudin requires jury determination of an offense element but does not classify every failure to submit an element as structural. Sullivan addresses a different defect: an unconstitutional reasonable-doubt standard governing all findings leaves no valid reasonable-doubt verdict to examine. The omission here does not destroy that standard throughout the charge.

O'Neal requires the State's Chapman showing for its preserved personal-intent category. That case involved combined instructions and prosecutorial argument permitting conviction without the defendant's own required intent. This charge permits the same constitutional failure by omitting the purpose to facilitate robbery. An additional prosecutorial statement is not the basis of the protection and supplies no reason to withhold it here. Knowledge of the perpetrator's unlawful purpose and affirmative assistance were required, so mere presence was insufficient; neither knowledge nor assistance is legally identical to the omitted purpose.

The State retains the burden. Roy need not prove innocence, and the reviewing court may not supply the missing intent itself. This decision does not adopt Chapman for every constitutional habeas claim, reopen a resolved exhaustion issue, or decide the temporal operation of AEDPA.

##### III. Necessary equivalence is sufficient, but not the exclusive permissible inquiry

**Rule:** Under Chapman, the State may establish that this omission did not contribute to the actual verdict through supported whole-record inferences as well as findings necessarily embracing the omitted purpose; it may not substitute evidence sufficient for a hypothetical properly instructed conviction, equate knowledge plus assistance with purpose, or infer purpose from general mental capacity. Under Yates, the court must examine whether the instructions led the jury to consider the evidence supporting the proposed inference; if the omission kept the relevant personal-purpose question from the jury so that the inference cannot reliably address its actual verdict, strong evidence alone does not carry the State's beyond-reasonable-doubt burden.

**Authority:** Part III controls through the five direct joins identified above. Stone-Zsela supports remand on his separate whole-record Chapman ground; his sixth judgment vote does not enlarge that coalition. Stevens, Scalia and Ginsburg dissent from remand. This holding rests on the Court's opinion, without combining separate rationales.

The Court holds that the inquiry concerns what the error did to the verdict actually returned. Yates requires examination of the evidence the instructions put before the jury and distinguishes actual contribution from proof sufficient to convict. Necessary equivalence between a finding and the omitted purpose is one sufficient route. It is not invariably the only way to determine the effect of this knowledge-and-assistance charge.

The Ninth Circuit expressly found the omission not harmless under Chapman before applying a separate collateral-review analysis. Remand is warranted because its necessary-findings restriction foreclosed the qualified actual-effect inquiry, not because Chapman was never considered. The charge submitted knowledge, affirmative aid and related mental-state questions. That permits examination of their connection to the actual verdict, without assuming the jury considered the omitted purpose. The receiving court must address the possibility that the omission itself prevented the relevant consideration; inability to establish a reliable connection defeats the State's showing.

The record requires more than an overwhelming-evidence label. Possession of property, wet clothing, assistance testimony and reported statements must be assessed with the verdicts and the conflicts in those accounts. The clothing allocated to McHargue in the lower opinions cannot be treated as undisputed property in Roy's backpack. Hudspeth's impeachment and rebuttal and Hall's reported statements and retraction matter together; no verdict establishes wholesale acceptance or rejection of every witness. The possible inference that Roy helped a companion without intending to facilitate robbery is an inference to assess, not a newly found event. The State's and district court's functional-equivalence conclusions are legal assessments, not express jury findings. These matters remain for the proper inquiry, and the Ninth Circuit may again conclude that the State fails its burden.

##### IV. The limited remand preserves the record, argument and remedial boundaries

**Rule:** The Ninth Circuit judgment is vacated for a qualified Chapman review of the preserved personal-intent claim affecting the Mannix robbery and first-degree felony-murder convictions, with District Court proceedings if otherwise proper; no present harmlessness finding follows. If an available violation is not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, conditional relief must allow the State a reasonable period to retry those convictions; proper harmlessness permits denial, and neither this remand nor the special-circumstance argument restores reversed punishment, affects the Clark conviction or orders immediate release.

**Authority:** Part III's remand instructions control through the same five direct joins. Stone-Zsela separately agrees with the Chapman and reasonable-retrial remedy, supplying the sixth judgment vote without joining Part III.

The Court holds that renewed review must apply the proper method to the supported record and respect genuine preservation limits. O'Neal distinguishes a corrected harmlessness inquiry from an automatic writ and requires a reasonable retrial opportunity if an available violation warrants relief. No fixed retrial deadline is set.

Yee distinguishes an additional argument about a preserved question from a new constitutional claim. The special-circumstance argument concerns this preserved harmlessness question, but Roy's objection to its first-instance use still requires consideration. The asserted oral concession is not established, and the objection alone creates no forfeiture.

The instructions must be read together. CALJIC 8.80's intentional-aid requirement applies conditionally to a defendant who was not the actual killer; it is not an unconditional finding of Roy's purpose to facilitate robbery. Absence of personal knife use does not itself eliminate every actual-killer theory. CALJIC 8.81.17's robbery-related purpose of the killing differs from Roy's own aiding purpose. The state reversal prevents restoration of the special-circumstance punishment; it does not automatically determine every evidentiary implication of the findings. General specific-intent or diminished-capacity instructions do not silently insert the omitted purpose clause.

The Court therefore adopts neither an automatic special-circumstance cure nor a categorical exclusion of the findings from consideration. It supplies no new trial finding, admissibility ruling on a juror declaration, exhaustion bar, or factual resolution from an unavailable transcript passage. Preservation, independent habeas requirements and any event-date statutory transition remain distinct. This remand addresses the identified method defect, without reviving a resolved gate or ordering new factfinding merely because a source is unavailable.

#### Law After the Decision

Effective November 4, 1996, the defined personal-intent habeas category includes this preserved omission of the defendant's purpose to facilitate robbery. It does not depend on accompanying prosecutorial argument. The State must satisfy Chapman through a permissible inquiry into the actual verdict. Necessary findings may suffice, but the controlling method also permits supported inferences meeting the express Yates condition; strong evidence alone fails when the omission prevents a reliable connection to the actual verdict. Scalia and Ginsburg's exclusive actual-findings approach does not control, and Stevens's vote to affirm does not join that approach.

The existing official-party requirement remains unchanged. This record satisfies it through the appellate identification of the Attorney General's office and the timely official petition, without a rule making every caption conclusive. No general Chapman rule for all habeas errors, new innocence gate, automatic special-circumstance cure, exhaustion ruling, statutory-transition ruling or universal structural-error rule is established. No holding on harmlessness of this actual record is made. The conditional-relief rule remains operative if the State fails its showing on an available violation.

**Justice Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He joins Parts I and II but would affirm the habeas-relief judgment on this record. **Justice Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justice Ginsburg.** They join Parts I and II but would affirm. For this omitted-purpose error, harmlessness requires that the jury's other determinations effectively embrace Roy's required purpose, or that the evidence and findings establish that the jury could not have reached what it did find without also finding that purpose. The State must establish that connection beyond a reasonable doubt. Carella's separate opinion and Scalia's Yates opinion explain why independent appellate certainty that a properly instructed jury would convict cannot replace the jury's actual findings; Sullivan and Gaudin preserve the jury's constitutional role. Knowledge and assistance do not necessarily establish purpose. Neither the Clark findings nor general capacity answers the missing Mannix purpose. The conditional nonkiller clause, the distinction between intentional assistance to first-degree murder and purpose to facilitate robbery, the robbery-related purpose of the killing, and the cross-reference to deficient aiding instructions prevent treating the special circumstances as an established equivalent finding. A valid equivalent finding could make an omission harmless, so their rule is not automatic structural reversal. They would affirm conditional relief only for the two Mannix convictions, allowing a reasonable retrial period. They adopt no innocence prerequisite or discretionary-habeas rule, and Ginsburg joins no such proposition.
