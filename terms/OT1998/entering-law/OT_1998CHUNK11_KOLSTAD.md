## Standards and Tests selection: The 1991 Civil Rights Act’s new liabilities and jury election

### The 1991 Civil Rights Act’s new liabilities and jury election

**Governing question:** Whether the 1991 Act’s new damages and contractual coverage reach preenactment conduct.

**Current rule:** Section 102’s new compensatory and punitive damages do not apply to conduct before November 21, 1991. Its jury election exists only when statutory damages under that section are available and requested; either party may then demand a jury, which must not be told the statutory caps. Section 102 damages supplement rather than duplicate §706(g) relief and exclude backpay, interest on backpay and other relief authorized there. Punitive damages require malice or reckless indifference to federally protected rights and are unavailable against governments, government agencies and political subdivisions. Section 101’s expansion of §1981 to contractual performance, modification, termination and enjoyment of benefits likewise does not govern preenactment conduct. Corrective purpose alone supplies no contrary temporal command; a defendant need not prove actual reliance on the former interpretation.

**Authority by component:**

- New damages, their limits and dependent jury election: Landgraf v. USI Film Products — Section 102 does not add its new damages to this preenactment conduct; The jury election depends on an available statutory damages claim, 1994-04-26. Stevens’s §102 application, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter and Ginsburg, controls by five votes. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas independently reach the same unavailability of new damages through their concurrence. Blackmun disagrees about compensation and reserves punitive damages. Stevens’s jury portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter and Ginsburg, supplies the controlling five-Justice explanation. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas reach the same conditional conclusion in their concurrence; Blackmun would permit a jury election because he would make compensation available.
- Expanded contractual coverage and temporal reach: Rivers v. Roadway Express, Inc — Section 101’s new contractual coverage does not govern the earlier conduct; Corrective legislative purpose does not itself give an amendment retrospective reach, 1994-04-26. Stevens’s opinion, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter and Ginsburg; these five adopt the complete explanation. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas reach the same temporal result through their separate relevant-activity and textual-command method. Stevens’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter and Ginsburg, controls by five votes at this explanatory level. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas accept the judicial/legislative distinction through their separate textual method, not through a presumed full join.

**Present operation effective:** Rivers v. Roadway Express, Inc, 1994-04-26.

**Exceptions and limits:** Claims already available under the earlier law remain available on their own terms. Neither section supplies a new remedy for every earlier Title VII or §1981 wrong.

**Holdings navigation:** Section 102 does not add its new damages to this preenactment conduct; The jury election depends on an available statutory damages claim; Section 101’s new contractual coverage does not govern the earlier conduct; Corrective legislative purpose does not itself give an amendment retrospective reach.

## Standards and Tests selection: Meaningful review of punitive-damage amounts

### Meaningful review of punitive-damage amounts

**Governing question:** Due process protection against arbitrary punitive amounts, including meaningful judicial review, substantive excessiveness and territorial limits.

**Current rule:** A State may not remove meaningful judicial review of the amount of a punitive-damage award without an adequate substitute protecting against arbitrary deprivation. Review limited to whether any evidence supports an award, or safeguards directed only to entitlement rather than amount, do not necessarily fill that gap. The inquiry concerns meaningful protection against arbitrary magnitude; it does not establish a fixed constitutional ratio, mandatory remittitur, a prescribed written-opinion format or a required number of reviewing courts. Independently of adequate procedure, due process forbids punitive awards grossly excessive in relation to legitimate punishment and deterrence; fair notice includes possible severity as well as punishable conduct. Review considers reprehensibility, the relationship to actual or supported potential harm to the plaintiff, and authorized or imposed civil or criminal sanctions for comparable misconduct. Reprehensibility is especially important; the guideposts are neither an exhaustive mechanical checklist nor a ratio or dollar ceiling. Small compensation, egregious wrongdoing, difficult detection or difficult valuation can support a larger ratio; speculative potential loss and wealth cannot replace the required relationship to the wrong. A State may not punish conduct lawful where it occurred and without impact on the State or its residents, or use punitive deterrence to impose its policy on other States. Relevant foreign transactions may nevertheless illuminate pattern, intent and reprehensibility.

**Authority by component:**

- Amount review and inadequate substitutes: Honda Motor Co. v. Oberg — Removing amount review without adequate replacement violates due process; Oregon's actual safeguards do not cure the amount-review gap, 1994-06-24. Justice Blackmun's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight Justices adopt this bounded procedural rule, without a substantive numerical rule. Justice Blackmun's opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight Justices adopt the application and limited review remedy.
- BMW — Parts I and III-A, substantive excessiveness, notice and qualified guideposts, 1996-05-20; Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer (five).
- BMW — Part II, territorial punishment and evidence distinction, 1996-05-20; the same five plus Stone-Zsela (six).

**Present operation effective:** BMW, May 20, 1996; Oberg’s procedural protection remains operative.

**Open question:** The decision does not itself determine the permissible amount of the award or prescribe the only constitutionally sufficient system of review.

**Holdings navigation:** Removing amount review without adequate replacement violates due process; Oregon's actual safeguards do not cure the amount-review gap; BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore, 517 U.S. 559.

**Open question concerning territorial punishment:** Whether a State may seek to change unlawful conduct elsewhere remains unresolved. The rule imposes no universal exclusion of foreign evidence or rule governing repeated punitive awards for the same conduct.

## Standards and Tests selection: Age motivation, pension tenure and willfulness

### Age motivation, pension tenure and willfulness

**Governing question:** What instructions govern age motivation, pension tenure and willfulness?

**Current rule:** ADEA disparate treatment requires age actually to motivate and have a determinative influence on the employment decision. Service-based pension interference alone does not establish it; deliberate use of pension status as an age proxy or mixed age and pension motivation may. Age-triggered vesting and disparate impact remain unresolved. A disparate-treatment violation is willful if the employer knew or recklessly disregarded whether conduct violated the Act, for individual decisions as well as formal policies. Once shown, liquidated damages require no additional outrageousness, direct evidence or predominant-age motive. A good-faith nonreckless mistake about a statutory permission is not willful. Within the assumed McDonnell Douglas framework, the initial showing must support an inference of age discrimination; replacement by someone at least forty does not defeat it. A substantially younger replacement may support that inference, whereas an insignificant difference standing alone does not. No numerical threshold or indispensable replacement element is imposed when other evidence supplies the inference. Employer production of a lawful reason remains distinct from the employee’s ultimate persuasion burden; rejection of the explanation does not compel liability.

- Thurston governs individual acts as well as formal policies: Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins — Thurston governs individual acts as well as formal policies, 1993-04-20. ## Standards and Tests selection: Employer attribution of supervisor-created harassment

### Employer attribution of supervisor-created harassment

**Governing question:** When Title VII attributes a supervisor-created hostile environment to the employer without higher-management notice.

**Current rule:** The plaintiff must prove by a preponderance that actual delegated supervisory power materially aided creation or maintenance of an actionable hostile environment. Hiring or firing authority, alter-ego rank, higher-management notice, an express threat, completed personnel action or tangible economic loss is not invariably required; rank, employment or proximity alone is insufficient. Personal purpose or breach of a harassment prohibition does not by itself negate actual aid. Ordinary scope of employment, employer negligence and apparent authority remain separate routes. Apparent authority requires a reasonable belief traceable to the employer's manifestations and reasonable reliance on that appearance, not merely the supervisor's asserted power; it is not an additional element of actual-authority attribution. Prohibitions and correction after notice do not themselves erase earlier authority-aided conduct.

## Public authority excerpt: Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins, 507 U.S. 604 (1993)

#### Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins, 507 U.S. 604 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-1600.

**Decided:** April 20, 1993.

##### Service-based vesting is distinct from age motivation

**Holding:** ADEA disparate treatment requires age actually to motivate the employment decision and have a determinative influence; pension interference based only on years of service does not by itself establish that requirement. Liability remains possible when pension status is deliberately used as an age proxy or age and pension status both motivate the decision; age-triggered vesting and disparate impact are not decided.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court, I; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Aikens, 460 U.S. 711, and Burdine, 450 U.S. 248 — applied: actual motivation may be proved circumstantially; no automatic inference from any unlawful motive.
- Ingersoll-Rand, 498 U.S. 133 — distinguished: ERISA pension interference and age discrimination remain distinct.
- McDonnell Douglas, 411 U.S. 792 — preserved; its legitimate-reason terminology does not make every improper motive an ADEA violation.

##### Thurston governs individual acts as well as formal policies

**Holding:** An ADEA disparate-treatment violation is willful when the employer knew or recklessly disregarded whether its conduct violated the Act, whether the decision was individual or a formal policy. Once that showing is made, liquidated damages require no additional outrageousness, direct evidence or predominant-age motive; a good-faith, nonreckless mistake about a statutory permission is not willfulness.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court, II; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Thurston, 469 U.S. 111 — reaffirmed and applied to individual disparate-treatment decisions.
- Richland Shoe, 486 U.S. 128 — applied: negligence and mere awareness of statutory relevance are not willfulness.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Pension status is no safe harbor when used as an age proxy. Age-based vesting and disparate impact are not decided. A nonreckless good-faith mistake about a statutory defense or exemption can defeat willfulness without defeating ordinary liability. No direct-evidence, outrageousness or predominant-age requirement is added.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Vacate and remand for reconsideration of ADEA sufficiency and, if needed, willfulness. The younger replacement, confidentiality demands and comments are not held sufficient or insufficient here. Independent ERISA and state-law judgments retain their own effect; no liquidated-damages award is made.

## Public authority excerpt: Faragher v. City of Boca Raton

Source: `state/holdings/labor-and-employment.md`.

#### Faragher v. City of Boca Raton

**Docket or dockets:** Eleventh Circuit No. 94-4878; no separate Supreme Court docket number is supplied.

**Decided:** September 8, 1997.

##### Whether actual delegated authority establishes attribution without higher-management notice

**Holding:** Actual delegated supervisory authority materially aiding creation or maintenance of an actionable hostile environment supplies an independent ground for employer responsibility under Title VII; the plaintiff must establish that connection. Supervisory rank or workplace proximity alone is insufficient; higher-management notice, an express threat, a completed personnel decision and tangible economic loss are not invariably required.

**Proposition-level authority:** Breyer's Part I, unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Ordinary employment scope, apparent authority and negligence remain distinct alternatives and are not decided. An actionable environment and discriminatory causation remain separate predicates. Personal purpose and violation of an employer prohibition do not alone negate actual aid.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Meritor requires agency analysis and rejects both automatic supervisory liability and notice-only immunity. Andrade supplies the plaintiff-proved actual-power connection; Oncale preserves distinct causation, environment and attribution inquiries. Harris and DeAngelis govern hostile-environment severity, not attribution or constructive notice.

##### Whether the supported operational findings establish material aid in this work unit

**Holding:** The established allocation and exercise of assignments, discipline, daily duties and supervision in this work unit materially aided maintenance of its accepted hostile conditions. These supported bench findings establish the actual-authority ground without a new threat, fear or employment-penalty finding, and do not require a further attribution proceeding.

**Proposition-level authority:** Breyer's Parts II–IV, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; eight votes. Stone-Zsela joins Part I only and opposes this conclusive application and restoration mandate.

**Material application:** Actual control over daily work, assignments and discipline in the geographically separate unit supported the connection to maintaining its hostile conditions. Historical findings receive clear-error review; legal significance receives independent review.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No new subjective fear, discharge threat, order to submit, lost wage or employment-penalty finding is added. Remoteness and failed policy communication are relevant circumstances, not universal prerequisites or substitutes for actual aid.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Meritor's unresolved findings and Andrade's Rule 50 questions warranted their own remands; Oncale's allegation-stage posture likewise does not require renewed factfinding on these adequate bench findings.

##### Whether the ineffective policy and eventual response defeat this proved attribution

**Holding:** In this case, the combination of a policy not communicated to the relevant workforce, failure to complain to distant higher management and correction after another employee's eventual report does not defeat the proved authority-aided liability. The Court leaves open whether genuinely effective protection and unreasonable employee nonuse could establish a complete defense, including its elements, burden, relationship to tangible employment action and any distinct limitation on particular relief.

**Proposition-level authority:** Breyer's Parts II–IV, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; eight votes. Stone-Zsela joins Part I only and opposes this conclusive application and restoration mandate.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No general complete defense, categorical rejection of every defense, burden-shifting formula, tangible-action exception, universal complaint duty or freestanding dissemination duty is adopted. Effective measures, negligence, apparent authority and avoidable-loss issues retain significance when their predicates exist; dissemination failure alone is not negligence per se.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Meritor does not make paper policy and noncomplaint conclusive. Andrade's reserved defense question remains open; later correction does not retroactively erase earlier attributable misconduct.

##### What relief the supported Title VII findings require

**Holding:** The appellate judgment is reversed only insofar as it reversed Faragher's Title VII judgment against the City, and the case is remanded for reinstatement of the existing one-dollar judgment without a new merits trial or further agency findings. No individual or state-law award, remaining appellate disposition, fee entitlement or additional remedy is adjudicated.

**Proposition-level authority:** Breyer's Parts II–IV, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; eight votes. Stone-Zsela joins Part I only and opposes this conclusive application and restoration mandate.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reinstate the existing one-dollar City judgment without another trial or attribution findings. Individual §1983 and battery awards, other state claims and remaining appellate dispositions are undisturbed.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Landgraf prevents new §102 compensatory and punitive liability for the 1985–1990 conduct and forecloses its dependent new jury right. Farrar distinguishes nominal relief from reasonable fees under §1988, supplying only an analogy; Title VII fees retain their own requirements.

## Public authority excerpt: Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth, No. 97-569

Source: `terms/OT1997/output/OT_1997CHUNK12.md`.

### Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth, No. 97-569

Merits decision, June 26, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 22, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit's en banc judgment in Jansen v. Packing Corp. of America, 123 F.3d 490 (1997), reversing employer summary judgment. The question is employer responsibility for an actionable hostile environment when a supervisor's sexual threats were not carried out and the employee neither submitted nor suffered the described tangible retaliation.

Ellerth alleged repeated misconduct by Slowik, a manager above her immediate supervisor who could make hiring and promotion decisions subject to superior approval. A threatened consequence at an interview accompanied an actual promotion; a later alleged threat concerned work authorization. The District Court treated the alleged conduct as sufficiently severe or pervasive but entered employer summary judgment for lack of notice, and separately dismissed constructive discharge. Ellerth knew a harassment policy existed but did not report to higher authority during employment. These are the supported summary-judgment facts and allegations, not findings of every claim or defense element.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Seventh Circuit's judgment is affirmed insofar as it reversed summary judgment on the appealed Title VII employer-attribution claim. Further proceedings must apply the actual-authority rule and the limits below. The Court endorses no other en banc rationale, finds neither liability nor a defense established, and orders no mandatory trial, second agency factfinding proceeding, damages award or discovery supplementation. Genuinely preserved independent grounds remain for ordinary resolution. The separately dismissed constructive-discharge claim is not revived; no preservation of an appeal from that dismissal is established.

#### Opinion Topology

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine join Parts I–III, including the complete-defense reservation and ordinary remand.

Justice Ginsburg files a concurrence, joined by Souter and Breyer. They join the Court's opinion and judgment and propose an additional causal limit on recovery for proved avoidable harm. That proposal has only their three votes and is not a holding of the Court.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Unfulfilled threats may support a hostile-environment claim under the existing actual-authority rule

**Holding and operative rule:** An employee who rejects sexual advances and suffers no completed tangible retaliation may pursue a Title VII hostile-environment claim, but must prove discrimination because of sex, an objectively and subjectively abusive environment sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter employment conditions, and employer attribution. For the actual-authority route, the employee must prove by a preponderance that delegated supervisory power materially aided creation or maintenance of that environment; rank, employment or workplace proximity alone is insufficient, while final hiring or firing power, a fulfilled threat, economic loss and higher-management notice are not invariably necessary.

**Authority:** Part I of Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by all eight Associate Justices; nine direct joins to the rule, application and limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Meritor requires an agency inquiry, rather than either automatic supervisor liability or exclusive reliance on management notice. Andrade, Faragher and Harrison give that inquiry a concrete actual-power connection and place its proof on the plaintiff. Harris supplies the separate objective and subjective environment standard; Oncale keeps discrimination because of sex distinct from offensive sexual content and employer responsibility.

Slowik's supported role in hiring and promotion, although subject to superior approval, and his alleged use of work-related authority permit inquiry into whether that power materially aided the asserted environment. The threats cannot be excluded merely because Ellerth refused, no retaliation followed, his purpose was personal, he was not the employer's alter ego or higher management lacked notice. Conversely, a title or opportunity to encounter Ellerth would not alone establish the connection. Ordinary plaintiff-favorable summary-judgment principles govern; the Court neither finds actual aid nor independently establishes all environment elements.

Ordinary employment scope, employer negligence and apparent authority remain distinct routes. Under Harrison, an apparent-authority theory requires an employer-attributable manifestation and reasonable reliance on that appearance; the supervisor's own assertion of power alone does not establish it. Those requirements are not additional elements of actual-power attribution. The Court decides no separate preserved quid-pro-quo claim by relabeling this one, no universal tangible-action definition and no rule that one unfulfilled threat always suffices.

##### II. Policy knowledge and nonreporting alone do not supply a complete defense

**Holding and operative rule:** Ellerth's knowledge that a harassment policy existed and her failure to report during employment do not alone eliminate earlier material aid from delegated authority or establish a complete defense. Whether genuinely effective protection and unreasonable employee nonuse can establish a general complete defense remains open, including its elements, burden, relationship to tangible employment action and any distinct limitations on particular relief.

**Authority:** Part II of Stone-Zsela's opinion, joined by all eight Associate Justices; nine direct joins. The three-Justice concurrence's additional recoverability proposal is not part of this holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Faragher rejects the proposition that a paper prohibition, noncomplaint and eventual correction automatically erase attributable harassment, while reserving a genuinely effective protection defense. Harrison preserves that reservation and distinguishes the plaintiff's attribution burden from any proposed defense. A known policy makes prevention and avoidability relevant; it does not establish that the means of protection were practically available or effective, or that nonuse was unreasonable.

The employer's strongest argument is that effective prevention and unreasonable nonuse should limit responsibility. That question does not require a general formula here. Existing law already limits attribution to plaintiff-proved actual power materially aiding the wrong, rather than treating supervisory status as sufficient. Neither that rule nor the present record supplies an automatic negligence safe harbor. The Court does not find this policy effective or ineffective, its channels safe or unsafe, or Ellerth's conduct reasonable or unreasonable. No exact policy wording, prevention practice or reporting route is assumed.

The decision adopts neither a general complete defense nor categorical rejection of every defense; it creates no new burden shift, universal reporting duty, publication duty, tangible-action exception or rule erasing already incurred harm. Prevention, negligence, apparent authority and particular avoidable loss retain their proper significance when supported, without importing a new recovery formula into this holding.

##### III. The appellate reversal stands, with ordinary further proceedings

**Holding and operative rule:** The Seventh Circuit's judgment reversing employer summary judgment on the appealed attribution claim is affirmed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings under Parts I–II. The legal error in categorical nonliability establishes no final liability, damages or entitlement to trial; genuinely preserved independent grounds may be resolved under ordinary procedures, and the separately dismissed constructive-discharge claim remains outside this disposition absent established preservation.

**Authority:** Part III of Stone-Zsela's opinion, joined by all eight Associate Justices; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The lack of higher-management notice or completed retaliation cannot foreclose the supported actual-power inquiry. That is enough to sustain the appellate rejection of summary judgment on the categorical ground presented. It is not a finding that Ellerth will prove an actionable environment, material aid or recoverable loss. Andrade illustrates correction of an attribution error without deciding all preserved alternatives; Faragher's restoration of an existing nominal judgment rested on supported bench findings not present here. Harrison likewise separates an agency error from independent claim and remedy questions.

The lower courts must resolve genuinely disputed facts and supported independent grounds in their proper posture. The Court mandates no trial, additional agency hearing, pleading amendment, discovery supplementation, liability finding or award. The conduct occurred in 1993–94, so Landgraf's temporal bar for pre-1991 conduct is not triggered merely by this case; the separate statutory conditions governing compensatory and punitive relief, fees and jury entitlement remain unadjudicated.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Meritor Savings Bank, FSB v. Vinson:** Applied to require agency analysis rather than automatic supervisory imputation or a notice-only rule; policy and noncomplaint alone remain inconclusive.
- **Andrade v. Mayfair Management, Inc.:** Applied to actual supervisory power materially aiding harassment and to correction without automatic liability or trial.
- **Faragher v. City of Boca Raton:** Applied to the actual-power rule and the express complete-defense reservation. Its supported bench findings and nominal-judgment restoration do not establish facts or mandate a second agency proceeding here.
- **Harrison v. Eddy Potash, Inc.:** Applied to the plaintiff's preponderance burden, distinction between actual and apparent authority, and continuing defense reservation. Apparent authority requires an employer-attributable manifestation and reasonable reliance; the requirement is not added to actual authority.
- **Harris v. Forklift Systems, Inc.:** Preserves the objectively and subjectively abusive severe-or-pervasive standard, without mandatory psychological injury, treatment, diagnosis, lost productivity or economic harm.
- **Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services, Inc.:** Preserves discrimination because of sex, actionable conditions and attribution as separate requirements; sexual content or grave misconduct alone supplies neither causation nor employer responsibility.
- **Landgraf v. USI Film Products:** Retains the temporal limits on the 1991 Act's new remedies and dependent jury right; no award or jury entitlement is adjudicated for this 1993–94 conduct.

No precedent is overruled. Gebser's separate recovery discussion supplies no controlling Title VII defense.

#### Law After Decision

The existing actual-power/material-aid rule governs supervisor harassment involving unfulfilled threats, subject to the plaintiff's preponderance burden and separate discrimination and environment requirements. Title, unfulfilled threats, absence of alter-ego status and lack of management notice are not categorical answers. Policy knowledge and nonreporting alone establish no complete defense. The general complete-defense question and its operative terms remain reserved. The concurrence's causal avoidable-recovery rule has three supporters and creates no governing burden or recovery limitation. Liability and relief remain for ordinary proceedings.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Ginsburg, joined by Souter and Breyer, concurring.** They join the opinion and judgment but would add a causal limit on otherwise recoverable harm. After the employee proves the underlying claim and otherwise compensable harm, the employer would bear the burden of proving by a preponderance that internal remedies were adequately publicized and likely to provide redress without undue risk, effort or expense; that the employee unreasonably failed to use them; and that the particular loss consequently could have been avoided. Recovery would be reduced or barred only to that proved extent.

They distinguish attribution from compensation, applying to employment the remedial position they expressed in Gebser without treating Title IX as controlling Title VII. A paper policy, nonreporting alone, unsafe or ineffective channels, eventual correction or the mere possibility of a complaint would not suffice. An employee need not use an unsafe route or complain to the harasser. Harm incurred before a reasonable opportunity for protection and harm the measures could not have prevented would remain recoverable if otherwise established. Their rule would neither shift the employee's attribution burden nor create a special immunity for cases without tangible action or a categorical exception for cases with one. If all compensable loss were proved avoidable, loss recovery could be eliminated, but discrimination would not be erased and other relief and fees would not thereby be decided. No fact here establishes application of their proposal. The Court reserves adoption of these terms.

No factual issue, defense, damages, fee award, jury entitlement or constructive-discharge claim is resolved by the mandate. Supreme Court review of this event is complete; further proceedings below remain.

### BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore, No. 94-896

Merits decision, May 20, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 11, 1995. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 to the Supreme Court of Alabama, 646 So. 2d 619. The questions are whether Alabama provided a meaningful remedy after identifying improper reliance on out-of-state transactions and whether the remitted $2 million punitive award is grossly excessive under the Fourteenth Amendment. The latter question includes, but is not confined to, the 500-to-1 ratio to compensation.

Gore paid $40,750.88 for a BMW sold as new. Acid-rain paint damage during transit had required $601 in refinishing, which was not specifically disclosed. BMW NA's policy withheld disclosure of repairs costing less than three percent of suggested retail price. A detailer discovered the refinishing after about nine months; no apparent finish problem during that use or physical injury was established. The signed damage/inspection acknowledgment did not defeat the adjudicated suppression claim. The jury awarded $4,000 for diminished value and $4 million in punitive damages. Alabama found sufficient clear-and-convincing evidence of the conscious or deliberate fraud required for punitive entitlement in this action.

The parties stipulated to 983 refinished vehicles; BMW estimated 11–14 Alabama transactions. Counsel proposed roughly 1,000 transactions multiplied by $4,000. Alabama found substantial jury reliance on foreign transactions but distinguished their permissible use to show pattern or intent from their use as a punitive multiplier. Because the record did not establish which foreign transactions were unlawful, the state court rejected that multiplier and expressly excluded foreign acts from its own amount analysis. The trial court had held an evidentiary postverdict hearing and found no bias, passion or prejudice. The Alabama Supreme Court applied its punitive-review criteria, compared awards and conditionally reduced punitive damages to $2 million.

The operative opinion is the August 19, 1994 substituted opinion, not the withdrawn October 1993 opinion. Gore accepted the remittitur on August 26. The September 9 certificate affirms a total $2,004,000 judgment, with interest and costs. The separate judgment against manufacturer BMW AG was reversed and rendered for that defendant. BMW NA's punitive challenge does not reopen that disposition or the $4,000 compensation.

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision. No Justice is recused or otherwise nonparticipating.

#### Judgment

**Reversed insofar as the Alabama judgment sustains $2 million in punitive damages, and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg, who would affirm the remitted judgment against the presented federal challenge.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Stevens delivers the Opinion of the Court. The substantive framework, invalidation of the remaining award and remand command five votes; Part II commands six.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I, III-A–B and IV | Justice Stevens | Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer | Supports reversal and remand | All of these Parts: gross excessiveness, legitimate punitive interests, fair notice, the three guideposts, the substantive invalidity of the remaining $2 million, and the limited remand. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Justice Stevens | Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer | Controls the territorial and evidentiary questions and accepts Alabama's stated correction; the Chief Justice does not join the judgment | Part II in full. The Chief Justice joins only this Part of the Court's opinion. |

Justice Breyer concurs, joined by Justices O'Connor and Souter, while all three join the Court's opinion in full. His additional constraints rationale has three votes. Justice Scalia dissents, joined by Justice Thomas. Justice Ginsburg separately dissents. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in Part II and otherwise dissents, including from the judgment.

The dissenters' agreement on affirmance does not merge their reasons. The Court's five direct joins require no *Marks* synthesis.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Grossly excessive punitive damages violate due process; fair notice includes severity

**Controlling proposition:** A punitive award grossly excessive in relation to the State's legitimate interests in punishment and deterrence violates due process. The inquiry begins with those interests. Fair notice encompasses both conduct subject to punishment and the possible severity of the penalty; wealth does not remove that constitutional constraint.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part I, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: five.

**Controlling explanation:** States may punish fraud and deter its repetition through damages exceeding compensation. The different functions of compensation and punishment preclude a rule requiring the two to move in exact proportion. They do not authorize every amount. Haslip sustained punitive adjudication bounded by reasonable constraints and a relationship to legitimate punitive purposes; it did not approve every later Alabama award.

The strongest objection is that traditional procedures and actual judicial review should leave the selected amount to state judgment. Those safeguards are indispensable, but the conclusion that review occurred does not dispose of a separately presented claim of gross excessiveness. TXO sustained its particular process without supplying a controlling substantive formula. Oberg requires meaningful examination and lawful correction of the amount, while reserving the substantive question answered here.

Notice of liability and notice of severity perform related but distinct work. Alabama's established disclosure duty remains valid, and the adjudicated suppression is not made lawful by disagreement among States or a later statute. Nonetheless, the punishment must remain within the constitutional protection against grossly excessive deprivation. Corporate wealth can bear on deterrence without replacing the required relationship to the wrong. The Court adopts neither all criminal procedural protections for civil actions nor a right to mathematical advance calculation of every permissible award.

**Precedent treatment:** Haslip's substantive and procedural protections are applied. TXO's record-specific procedural holding and Oberg's amount-review requirement remain intact; neither is recast as an earlier adoption of today's substantive framework.

##### II. Foreign evidence and punishment are distinct; Alabama's stated correction is accepted

**Controlling proposition:** Alabama may not punish conduct lawful where it occurred and having no impact on Alabama or its residents, or use punitive deterrence to impose its policy on other States. Whether a State may seek to change unlawful conduct elsewhere remains open. Relevant foreign transactions may illuminate pattern, intent and reprehensibility; their evidentiary use does not itself impose punishment for those transactions.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: six.

**Controlling explanation:** Alabama has a legitimate interest in protecting its purchasers from fraud. That interest does not give it authority to punish lawful transactions disconnected from the State simply because it would prefer a different disclosure policy elsewhere. Differences among state policies therefore matter to the permissible reach of deterrence. The holding is qualified by lawfulness and absence of Alabama impact; it supplies no universal immunity for conduct outside the State.

Evidence and punishment answer different questions. A recurring policy, including transactions elsewhere, can support knowledge, intent or the character of the Alabama wrong when governing law makes it relevant. The Court neither excludes all such evidence nor adjudicates the legality of every sale in the national sample. Nor does it decide whether another State could punish conduct unlawful within its own authority.

The original multiplier presented the identified problem. Alabama rejected it and expressly excluded foreign acts from its own amount determination. That statement is accepted; a separate state concurrence's description does not establish that the majority covertly retained the multiplier. The judgment under review is the accepted $2 million remittitur. Recognizing the correction neither reinstates the original verdict nor determines whether the remaining amount survives the independent substantive inquiry in Part III.

**Precedent treatment:** Shutts and Allstate v. Hague support attention to the State's connection with the transactions affected. Their choice-of-law principles are not transformed into an evidence exclusion or a numerical punitive ceiling.

##### III-A. Three qualified guideposts structure constitutional excessiveness review

**Controlling proposition:** Review considers the reprehensibility of the defendant's conduct; the relationship of punitive damages to actual or potential harm to the plaintiff; and civil or criminal sanctions authorized or imposed for comparable misconduct. These are operative guideposts, with reprehensibility especially important, but not an exhaustive mechanical checklist, fixed ratio or dollar ceiling.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part III-A, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: five.

**Controlling explanation:** Reprehensibility connects severity to the wrong being punished. The nature of the injury, deliberate or affirmative misconduct, repeated behavior and vulnerability can distinguish more blameworthy conduct from less aggravated wrongdoing. Economic injury is not categorically immune from substantial punishment; intentional misconduct or exploitation can justify a substantial award without physical injury.

The harm comparison likewise informs rather than mechanically decides proportionality. Actual compensation need not measure the harm deliberately threatened. Supported potential harm belongs in the inquiry, while speculative loss cannot be invented to justify a chosen number. Small compensation, egregious conduct, injury difficult to detect or harm difficult to value can support larger ratios. The Court announces no bright line beyond which every award fails.

Comparable authorized or imposed sanctions inform the seriousness with which the State treats the misconduct and the notice of possible severity. They do not automatically become exclusive civil-punishment caps. State discretion, deterrence and differences among remedies remain relevant, but cannot make every comparison immaterial.

These guideposts supply reasons for constitutional judgment rather than an unexplained impression that an award seems large. Haslip's refusal to establish a universal mathematical boundary is preserved. TXO's divided potential-harm reasoning cannot be treated as binding arithmetic, and disparity among jury outcomes alone establishes no bias. The inquiry requires judgment applied to lawful purposes and supported facts.

**Precedent treatment:** Haslip's reasonableness inquiry is developed into the stated guideposts. TXO establishes no controlling substantive formula, and this decision adopts no fixed ratio.

##### III-B. The remitted $2 million is grossly excessive on this record

**Controlling proposition:** After accepting Alabama's removal of foreign transactions from its punitive calculation, the remaining $2 million is grossly excessive in light of the established wrong, supported harm and comparable sanctions. Neither adequate power to review nor actual reduction conclusively establishes a constitutionally permissible amount.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part III-B, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: five.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court accepts the deliberate-suppression finding and entitlement to punitive damages. Recurrence and concealment support punishment beyond compensation. The injury nevertheless was economic, without an established health or safety danger, and the character of this nondisclosure does not equate it with the most aggravated fraud. A repair bill alone would understate Gore's loss; the jury's $4,000 diminution finding supplies the actual compensation.

No supported much larger injury to Gore supplies the missing scale. The estimated 11–14 Alabama transactions can illuminate pattern and culpability, but are not adjudicated individual claims or an automatic aggregate punishment base. Neither national loss nor a larger future injury is established in this record. The 500-to-1 disparity therefore raises a substantial concern on this record, though that number is not itself a universal rule.

Variable disclosure laws inform notice and blameworthiness without erasing Alabama's adjudicated duty. The identified $2,000 statutory sanction and the comparison awards provide no adequate notice or justification for this magnitude. The statutory figure is a comparator, not an exclusive cap, and BMW's proposed $56,000 calculation is not adopted.

The strongest argument for affirmance combines deliberate recurring conduct, deterrence and Alabama's actual hearing, review and halving. Those circumstances receive weight; they do not justify the extraordinary remaining amount under the guideposts. BMW's ability to pay cannot fill the gap. This is a substantive conclusion about the corrected award, not a finding that the reviewing judges were dishonest, that no review occurred or that the original multiplier remained secretly operative.

**Precedent treatment:** Haslip did not immunize every Alabama result. TXO's supported threatened harm is not presumed present here. Oberg's protection of amount review is preserved without treating its distinct procedural defect as established in this case.

##### IV. Alabama chooses the lawful means of redetermining punitive damages

**Controlling proposition:** Reverse only the judgment sustaining the $2 million punitive award and remand for a lawful punitive disposition. Alabama initially determines whether a new punitive trial or an independent appellate determination is appropriate. The Court selects no sum and does not require a new jury merely because the original verdict used an improper multiplier.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part IV, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: five.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court has decided that the corrected amount is grossly excessive, not merely that Alabama omitted a hearing or failed to provide enough explanatory words. Its remedy therefore requires a disposition consistent with that substantive holding. Repeating the same $2 million on this record with fuller prose would not answer it.

The accepted remittitur establishes the operative judgment; it does not prevent further lawful correction after federal review. State procedures retain the first choice between a new punitive trial and an appropriate appellate redetermination. No constitutional maximum, mandatory reduction or universal rule governing every judicial alteration is selected.

The compensation and suppression liability supply no basis for reopening, and entitlement to a lawful punitive award survives. BMW AG's separate favorable disposition remains outside this challenge. Neither revival of the discarded $4 million nor judgment on other purchasers' claims follows. The limited remand respects Alabama's punitive authority while enforcing the constitutional constraint applied here.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current Law

Haslip remains governing: States may employ punitive damages with reasonable constraints, actual review and a relationship to legitimate punishment and deterrence. Its rejection of a universal mathematical line survives. The present guideposts and application develop substantive excessiveness review without facially invalidating Alabama's punitive system.

TXO's five-Justice procedural sufficiency proposition remains intact. Its distinct substantive writings establish no common ratio or potential-harm formula. Oberg continues to require meaningful examination and lawful correction of punitive **amount**, or an adequate functional substitute; review of entitlement or legal error alone does not necessarily suffice. The Court does not overrule that safeguard or impose its Oregon-specific result on Alabama's different process.

Shutts and Allstate v. Hague support territorial limits within their stated roles; neither creates a general prohibition on relevant foreign evidence. No precedent is overruled. The three-Justice supplemental constraints explanation and the dissenters' competing federalism and source-of-right theories retain their separate authority.

Due process forbids grossly excessive punitive awards in relation to legitimate state punishment and deterrence. Fair notice includes possible severity. Reprehensibility, the relationship to actual or potential harm to the plaintiff, and comparable sanctions are controlling guideposts, with their stated qualifications. They are neither optional observations nor a fixed ratio, exclusive checklist or dollar cap.

The qualified territorial rule has six votes. It excludes punishment of conduct lawful elsewhere and having no Alabama impact, preserves relevant evidence, and reserves the question of efforts to change unlawful foreign conduct. Alabama's stated removal of foreign transactions is accepted. Five Justices nevertheless invalidate the surviving $2 million on substantive grounds and require a lawful punitive redetermination.

Meaningful amount review remains required. The existence of process does not immunize every result, and this result does not establish absence of process. Compensation, punitive entitlement, BMW AG's dismissal, the validity of every form of judicial alteration and questions of repeated punishment for the same conduct remain within the limits stated above. No new general corporate-rights catalogue is adopted.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Breyer, concurring, joined by O'Connor and Souter.** All three join the Court's opinion in full and supply an additional explanation. A procedurally proper award ordinarily deserves a strong presumption of validity. Here both the standards' failure significantly to constrain discretion as interpreted and applied and the gross disproportion to legitimate punitive objectives overcome that presumption. The existence of hearings, state criteria and actual appellate correction is acknowledged.

Clear-and-convincing proof of punitive entitlement does not distinguish every permissible amount. Harm, culpability and profit criteria must constrain the actual sum; wealth and litigation incentives do not supply a missing limit, and inapplicable mitigation factors do not do so. A supported economic theory can provide meaningful discipline, but a hypothetical model cannot replace the grounds and evidence before the state court. Historical comparisons illustrate the concern without creating a damages formula. The concurrence neither invalidates Alabama's system on its face nor requires written arithmetic, a compulsory economic model or an automatic new trial. Its conjunctive rationale is supported by three Justices, not an additional Court holding.

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** They would affirm. Their substantive/process distinction preserves traditional adjudication and meaningful judicial review enforcing state-law limits while rejecting an independent federal constitutional entitlement to a different punitive sum. They regard the guideposts as lacking a sufficient constitutional source and workable measure for federal supervision of amounts. Disagreement with a number does not prove the absence of a safeguard.

Alabama held a postverdict hearing, applied its review criteria, expressly removed the foreign multiplier and reduced the judgment. Assuming the identified error required correction, the correction occurred; they need not adopt the Court's territorial rule. Their position approves neither the original $4 million nor every possible remittitur. Both retain Oberg's amount-review protection; Thomas's agreement rests on his own TXO and Oberg positions, without adopting a separate opinion he did not join.

**Ginsburg, dissenting.** She would affirm from institutional restraint, federalism and respect for actual state correction. Alabama's courts addressed deliberate recurring fraud, considered their substantive criteria and comparison awards, and replaced the jury's national measure with a reduced judgment. The difficulty of supplying a workable federal scale counsels against displacing that state judgment here. Her concern is not answered simply by identifying a high ratio.

She does not join Scalia's categorical rejection of every substantive due-process protection or affirm by applying the Court's new guideposts. Her earlier reservation of a properly presented gross-excessiveness challenge remains meaningful. The present federal challenge does not justify additional intervention in light of the safeguards and correction actually supplied. State reform may address punitive practice, but proposed reforms are not treated as operative law, and a later disclosure amendment does not erase this adjudicated wrong. Her conclusion rests on the established review and correction.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in Part II and otherwise dissenting.** He would affirm the remitted $2 million punitive judgment. His answer to the first question is yes: Alabama supplied a constitutionally sufficient remedy for the identified use of foreign transactions through meaningful review and its established, accepted remittitur procedure. His answer to the second is no: constitutionally adequate adjudication and meaningful review do not leave an additional federal substantive power to invalidate an amount merely because its magnitude or ratio appears excessive.

BMW's corporate form does not remove its property and procedural protections, but those protections do not themselves establish a substantive entitlement to the sum a federal judge prefers. Each claimed constitutional consequence requires its own source. TXO established no controlling ratio or general substantive formula; Oberg requires meaningful examination and lawful correction of the amount without supplying a maximum, mandatory remittitur or federal recalculation formula. That amount-review function cannot be reduced to checking punitive entitlement alone.

He agrees that Alabama cannot punish beyond its lawful authority and that relevant foreign evidence differs from foreign conduct made an independent object of punishment. Knowledge, intent, deliberateness, notice or a common policy may make such evidence relevant. Alabama expressly corrected its identified error; another potentially lawful remedy does not automatically become constitutionally necessary.

Compensation measures loss, while punitive damages serve punishment and deterrence. A large ratio alone cannot determine whether the latter rests on a lawful basis; nor does a large compensatory award justify any punishment. Reprehensibility, ratios and comparable sanctions may inform state-law or otherwise established review without becoming the Court's new constitutional elements. Courts retain meaningful amount review and correction of unlawful punitive availability, instructions, proof, considerations, procedural unfairness and statutory violations. Those examples are not an exhaustive checklist.

His conclusion neither makes punitive damages unreviewable nor authorizes punishment outside Alabama's lawful authority. It preserves traditional remittitur, restores no part of the original $4 million, and reserves whether every form of judicial alteration is permissible. He joins Part II's narrow territorial and evidentiary propositions, but not the Court's independent substantive framework, application or remand.

## Holdings

Both holdings are adopted directly by Souter's opinion for Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; six Justices join the complete rules and explanations.

### The EEOC may administer the compensation Congress expressly authorized

When federal-sector Title VII intentional discrimination falls within 42 U.S.C. §1981a's damages authorization, §2000e-16(b) permits the EEOC to award that compensation as an appropriate remedy. The agency must preserve §1981a's substantive conditions and monetary limits; its power does not depend on an administrative jury, and a proper court action retains the statutory jury election.

Section 717(b) delegates enforcement through appropriate remedies and introduces the listed equitable forms as examples. Congress subsequently added compensation to the remedies for intentional discrimination under §717. Reading those enactments together gives effect to both the express monetary authorization and the administrative mechanism Congress retained. Section 1981a's reference to an action does not silently confine §717 as a whole to its judicial subsection. Its jury clause regulates trial in court; it neither requires a jury in an administrative proceeding nor creates a governmental appeal from an adverse EEOC award. The Government's inability to demand judicial review therefore does not negate the separately authorized administrative remedy. This conclusion satisfies Lane's requirement of unequivocal enacted consent and Blue Fox's demand for a genuine statutory entitlement; administrative convenience supplies no independent waiver.

The compensation category applies to unlawful intentional discrimination, not disparate impact, and requires that the complaining party cannot recover under §1981. It excludes backpay, interest on backpay and other relief authorized by §706(g). The statutory caps remain applicable, and punitive damages are unavailable against governments, government agencies and political subdivisions. When available §102 damages are sought in court, any party may demand a jury; the court must not tell that jury the statutory caps. No damages under another statute or free-standing appropriations authority follow.

### Statutory authority does not decide Gibson's preserved procedural alternatives

The Seventh Circuit's no-power premise cannot sustain its judgment, but identifying an available administrative remedy does not establish whether Gibson exhausted it or has a properly preserved alternative answer to the exhaustion defense. The reviewed judgment must be vacated and remanded for consideration of proper presentation and, if appropriate, resolution of those alternatives.

McCarthy requires attention to the governing statutory scheme and the actual administrative remedy; its result involving a compensation-incapable prison process cannot resolve a claim involving an EEOC that may compensate. Gibson's asserted cash-award request, completed complaint procedures and inadequate counseling do not become judicial findings merely because the Court rejects the appellate statutory premise. The Court decides neither preservation nor actual exhaustion, estoppel, compensable loss or amount. It creates no categorical exception to an applicable exhaustion requirement. No new damages award or trial is ordered by this decision itself.

## Independent legal relevance and limits

This is an express, setting-specific restriction on damages under §1981a for the identified disability-accommodation practices. It places the demonstration on the covered entity and requires good-faith efforts *in consultation with the informed requesting individual* toward an equally effective accommodation without undue business hardship. It concerns §1981a damages, not an erasure of the underlying statutory obligation or automatic displacement of §706(g) relief. Its words reach the damages category under the section rather than only punitive damages. Kolstad’s intentional sex-discrimination promotion claim is not the accommodation practice this paragraph describes, so the paragraph supplies no direct defense here.

The provision is nevertheless material statutory context for a proposed TitleVII punitive-compliance limitation. One supported argument is that Congress knew how to enact a particular good-faith damages exception, specified both its setting and predicates, and did not insert the same exception into the TitleVII paragraph or §1981a(b)(1). That express choice can weigh against importing an unstated, general defense solely because good compliance incentives seem desirable.

The competing argument distinguishes paragraph(a)(3)’s direct bar on all §1981a damages in a defined accommodation setting from the separate determination of punitive culpability and vicarious attribution under(b)(1), TitleVII’s agency terms and pre-existing common-law punitive principles. An agency rule limiting when another actor’s culpability is attributed could perform different legal work, so the express accommodation exception does not by itself resolve every punitive-attribution question. Its existence can still constrain the justification and scope of any proposed adaptation.

Neither argument is an adopted holding. A new TitleVII punitive limitation must identify its own statutory/common-law basis, actor and agency predicates, allocation of proof, operative good-faith content and remedial scope. Paragraph(a)(3) does not automatically furnish those terms, prove the Association’s compliance efforts, or convert a written equal-opportunity policy into a defense. Conversely it does not eliminate(b)(1)’s distinct mental-state requirement or prove that every liability finding permits punitive damages.
