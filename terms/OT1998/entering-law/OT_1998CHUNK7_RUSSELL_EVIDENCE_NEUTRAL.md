# Russell — neutral published trial-record supplement

Sources: district opinion, 978 F. Supp. 1211, 1220–1227 (published judicial recitation), and official Eighth Circuit opinion in Nos. 97-3922, 97-4033, 97-4038, Parts III–IV, [official PDF](https://ecf.ca8.uscourts.gov/opndir/98/06/973922P.pdf). The following distinguishes evidence and trial findings from appellate evaluation. Neither court's legal conclusion is a new finding by this Court. The full trial transcript has not been recovered or read for this extraction. The source record is affirmative evidence, not an inference from unavailable research material.

## Candidate limits — corruption evidence and contrary accounts

The district court heard seven trial days. It expressly stated that no actual undue influence was shown or claimed; the asserted interest in the evidence was appearance. A fundraiser for Representative Ode Maddox, House Insurance and Commerce chairman, raised over $22,000 from lobbyists, PACs and corporations out of more than $30,000 in total campaign funds. Donors did not reside in his district; most or all lobbied committee members; several gave $1,000. The court credited Maddox's uncontradicted testimony that he had never solicited a contribution in forty years and gave contributors and noncontributors equal hearing access. Its concern was how those contributions appeared, particularly their proportion of a small campaign budget.

Representative James Dietz received $2,700 in aggregate tobacco-related contributions in an election cycle and sponsored a bill preempting local tobacco regulation. Other supporting committee members also received tobacco-related donations. An opposing legislator, Ted Thomas, testified to a uniform public perception of corruption. The newspaper evidence also recorded Dietz's policy defense of the bill. The district court described media criticism and treated tobacco money, approximately twenty percent of Dietz's campaign expenses, as supporting an appearance of undue influence. The circuit emphasized that no single contribution exceeded $1,000 and that the record supplied neither a contribution-induced policy change nor concealment. It rejected treating alignment with a donor's interests, standing alone, as corruption. These are competing evaluations of the identified evidence.

The circuit similarly discussed Little Rock real-estate contributions: no contribution over $1,000, no demonstrated policy shift or concealment, and donors supporting officials whose initiatives they favored. Its concern was that the State's evidence emphasized contributor identity and interests rather than contribution size. Its conclusion that the appearance showing was insufficient is a reviewed legal/evidentiary judgment, not a neutral declaration that the State produced no evidence.

An ACORN report examined 1994 House/Senate contributions above $100: interest groups supplied seventy percent, and business interests alone supplied approximately sixty-two to sixty-five percent. Robert Stern's Center for Governmental Studies evidence covered three election cycles and similarly described business interests' large share. The district court regarded contribution size relative to the campaign's total finances as relevant. It reported averages raised in general elections of $22,500 for Senate and $7,550 for House campaigns during 1992, 1994 and 1996, including candidates' own money and loans. A $1,000 gift was over four percent and over thirteen percent respectively. In the 1994 gubernatorial general election, by contrast, each candidate spent above $900,000; $1,000 was about one-tenth of one percent. The district court used that difference to distinguish non-statewide and statewide offices.

## Candidate limits — burdens and effectiveness

The limits reset for primary, general and runoff elections, rather than covering an entire election cycle. Stern's evidence indicated that the $100 limit would affect as much as eighty percent of contributions in Arkansas House/Senate races. The circuit compared the limits with inflation-adjusted Buckley amounts, while acknowledging Buckley's $1,000 figure was not a constitutional minimum.

The district court reported historical per-election campaign costs of about $7,000–$16,000 for House and $23,000–$39,000 for Senate. Its illustrative calculation was that $50 donations would require 320 House donors or 780 Senate donors to match the high ends; this was a calculation, not a finding that candidates had actually recruited those numbers. The relevant district populations were approximately 24,000 and 68,000. The calculation omitted candidates' own money and party/small-donor-PAC contributions.

The court credited officeholders Mark Pryor and Phil Wyrick's testimony that adequate funding remained feasible. It reported that no officeholder testified that an effective campaign was impossible, although two professional fundraisers did. In two January 1997 special elections under Act I, candidates raised more than the historical average for open-seat primary campaigns. Wyrick testified that he raised about $24,000 in his successful Senate race and his opponent about $40,000. The district court relied on those experiences to uphold non-statewide caps; the circuit's burden analysis reached a different conclusion.

Judicial candidates faced additional constraints: no personal solicitation or acceptance, with committee solicitation limited to 180 days before the primary through forty-five days after the last contested election. Supreme Court seats were statewide; Court of Appeals districts were larger than Senate districts. The district court concluded those combined constraints made the $100 judicial cap impermissible. Its judgment did not invalidate the same cap for every non-statewide office.

## Ordinary PAC receipts and small-donor treatment

For the $200 annual ordinary-PAC receipt cap, the district court reported several years of operation since 1990 and robust PAC candidate giving. It stated that plaintiffs did not contend the existing cap had significantly impaired their speech/association, and found no appreciable impairment shown in operation. That is the trial court's characterization of the contentions/evidence; it is not a new party waiver. The State and intervenor defended the cap as preventing circumvention; Arkansas did not require an ordinary PAC to have fifty donors, to support five candidates, or impose the federal-style aggregate annual individual cap. The circuit stressed lower direct-corruption risk in PAC giving and compared the cap with Buckley/California Medical amounts. It invalidated the cap despite the district court's contrary operational assessment.

The district court described the small-donor category as restricted to individual donors and no more than $25 in annual contributions from an individual, unlike ordinary PACs' larger receipts from individuals and entities. It reasoned that diffuse individual intake reduced contributor control, and that ordinary PACs retained broader fundraising sources and no aggregate overall candidate-giving limit. The circuit's objection concerned the PAC's potential influence over a candidate receiving $2,500, even if the PAC's own donors each supplied little. These are distinct asserted corruption relationships. The State also contended ordinary-PAC members could form a separate qualifying small-donor PAC; that availability did not itself resolve the pleaded unequal-treatment claim.

The published circuit source abbreviates the small-donor intake restriction as no contribution larger than $25. The district opinion specifies an annual individual limit. When stating the statute's terms, retain the annual/individual qualifications; do not turn the abbreviated appellate description into permission for unlimited repeated $25 gifts.
