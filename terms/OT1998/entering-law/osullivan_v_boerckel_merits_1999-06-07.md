## Event

O'Sullivan v. Boerckel, No. 97-2048. Merits decision, June 7, 1999. Argued March 30, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, 135 F.3d 1194. The question is whether a prisoner must present federal claims in the ordinary petition for discretionary review to the State's highest court before seeking federal habeas relief. Boerckel presented claims concerning knowing and intelligent Miranda waiver, confession voluntariness and evidentiary sufficiency to the Illinois intermediate appellate court but omitted them from his petition for leave to appeal to the Illinois Supreme Court. Other claims were included in that petition. The Seventh Circuit held the three omitted claims were not defaulted for this reason and remanded; it did not decide their merits. His federal application was filed in September 1994 and amended in March 1995.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated in argument and decision.

## Public Action

The judgment of the Seventh Circuit is reversed, and the case is remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

Reversed and remanded, 6–3. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. Opposing: Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer. The three omitted claims are procedurally defaulted because their ordinary Illinois review opportunity has expired, subject to the governing exceptions and the bounded further review stated below. The Court awards no writ, new trial, release or new evidentiary hearing and decides no underlying federal claim.

## Holdings

#### I. An available ordinary discretionary petition is part of one complete round

**Holding and operative rule:** Under the pre-AEDPA law governing this application, a prisoner must fairly present each federal claim through one complete round of the State's established ordinary appellate process, including a petition for discretionary review to its highest court when that petition is an ordinary available step. Former section 2254(b) preserves exceptions for absence of a state corrective process or circumstances making that process ineffective to protect the applicant's rights; actual state-law unavailability must be respected, and the rule does not require every extraordinary remedy or repetitive collateral presentation after a complete ordinary round.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, commands six direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Lindh makes former chapter 153 law govern Boerckel's pre-enactment application. Former section 2254(c) turns on a state-law right to raise the question through an available procedure, not a right to obtain a merits decision. Illinois Rule 315 supplies a regular petition for leave to appeal. Its selective criteria—importance, conflict, supervisory need and finality—allow the Illinois Supreme Court to choose cases; they do not make the ordinary filing unavailable. The State's highest court must have its own opportunity to consider each federal claim. The contrary argument stresses the burden of repeating rejected claims in a selective system. But selection belongs to the state court, and the ordinary petition gives it the choice. Picard supplies fair presentation; it does not permit omission of an otherwise required ordinary step. Castille involved a claim first raised through discretionary review and therefore does not mechanically answer whether an already presented claim must pass through the next ordinary stage. Lonchar requires the governing procedural sources, not a generalized finality preference. Here Illinois supplied the ordinary leave procedure and no established directive excluded it from the process. Mere discretion and unlikely acceptance do not establish unavailability. Petitioning this Court is not a substitute for giving the State's own highest court the opportunity, and no equivalent federal-certiorari exhaustion requirement follows.

#### II. Expiration of the omitted opportunity produces default, with existing gateways preserved

**Holding and operative rule:** When a prisoner omits a claim from an otherwise required ordinary state-review step and the opportunity to use that step has expired, the claim is procedurally defaulted rather than awaiting exhaustion through a still-available remedy. Boerckel's three omitted claims fall within that rule, subject to recognized cause-and-prejudice and actual-innocence gateways at their existing scope; further proceedings must respect the actual prior gateway rulings and the governing pre-AEDPA law.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court; the same six-Justice coalition directly adopts the classification and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Coleman distinguishes failure to exhaust an available remedy from default when a required remedy has closed. The three claims were omitted from an ordinary petition and their opportunity has expired. The Seventh Circuit's contrary premise therefore cannot support further consideration without addressing default. Gray and Lambrix preserve claim-specific procedural treatment; a ruling on these claims does not decide all claims in the application. Nor does default eliminate the recognized gateways. The district court invited a cause showing, and Boerckel instead pursued actual innocence. It heard evidence in July 1996, incorrectly invoked AEDPA to exclude it, and alternatively concluded that evidence of other participants did not establish Boerckel's absence. Because the court of appeals found no default, it did not resolve any properly preserved challenge to that gateway treatment. Such review must apply pre-AEDPA law and consider the significance of the alternative ground. The Court makes no new finding of innocence, credibility, cause, prejudice or forfeiture, changes no gateway burden and orders no second hearing. The remand permits only further proceedings consistent with these existing procedural conditions; the constitutional merits remain undecided.

#### III. The effect of an express state opt-out retaining occasional review remains open

**Reservation and operative limit:** The Court does not decide whether a State may expressly identify an occasionally available discretionary procedure as outside its standard review process and state that prisoners need not invoke it before federal habeas. No such Illinois directive is established here. The decision neither requires a State to eliminate every possibility of occasional discretionary relief nor conclusively defines what an effective express opt-out must contain.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's opinion is joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. It is a five-Justice reservation, not an adopted rule resolving the hypothetical procedure's availability. Stone-Zsela does not join this reservation.

**Explanation:** Ordinary selective review and a State's express definition of its standard process present different questions. The former is established here; the latter is not. Treating actual state-law unavailability as controlling therefore does not require a decision about a procedure that a State retains for occasional use while expressly excluding it from the standard exhaustion route. The Court leaves that question open without displacing the complete-round rule for Illinois's existing ordinary procedure.

## Precedent Treatment

- Lindh supplies pre-AEDPA chapter 153 for this application; the decision does not import amended exhaustion or evidentiary provisions merely because it is delivered after AEDPA.
- Picard's fair-presentation requirement is applied to each claim through the ordinary state-review round.
- Castille is distinguished: first presentation through a discretionary mechanism differs from omission at the next ordinary stage after proper presentation below. Its proper-presentation rule remains intact.
- Coleman supplies the distinction between an available unexhausted remedy and procedural default after an otherwise required route closes; it does not independently identify Illinois's required sequence.
- Lonchar preserves source-governed habeas procedure and does not itself decide which Illinois steps constitute exhaustion.
- Gray preserves separate treatment of distinct claims and defaults; it does not convert this exhaustion ruling into a decision of the constitutional merits.
- Lambrix preserves default as an independent procedural issue with its recognized gateway requirements; it does not convert this exhaustion ruling into a decision of the constitutional merits.

## Law After Decision

Illinois's ordinary petition for leave to appeal must include each federal claim sought to be preserved through a complete ordinary review round. Selective acceptance alone does not excuse omission. Expiration of that required opportunity produces default, subject to the existing gateways and actual procedural posture. Actual state-law unavailability and the former statute's absent-process and ineffective-process exceptions remain distinct from mere discretion. The effect of an express opt-out retaining occasional review is unresolved. No precedent is overruled, no universal duty to pursue extraordinary remedies is adopted and no new innocence standard is imposed.

## Separate Writings

Stone-Zsela concurs in part and in the judgment. He agrees that Illinois supplied an ordinary available petition and had not told prisoners their federal claims could bypass it. He therefore joins the actual-case exhaustion and default holdings. He would also state affirmatively that a clear state rule making a step unnecessary controls: the inquiry concerns the process the State actually makes ordinarily available. His rule does not demand repetitive extraordinary remedies, applications the State expressly labels unnecessary, or futile petitions after final resolution through another ordinary route. He does not join the Court's reservation of the express-state-opt-out question. His remand preserves cause and prejudice, actual innocence, waiver and any clear applicable Illinois exclusion; it finds none of those predicates satisfied and directs no new hearing or favorable habeas result.

Stevens, joined by Ginsburg and Breyer, dissents. The three claims were fairly presented for merits decision at trial and through Illinois's appeal as of right. In the dissent's view, that process gave the State a full opportunity; omission from the selectively administered second tier should not create an additional federal default. Technical exhaustion because no remedy remains does not itself establish that the prisoner failed to provide a fair opportunity while remedies existed. Rule 315's institutional criteria matter to what the State's ordinary process requires, and mandatory repetition of every rejected error can burden a selective system without supplying a materially new merits opportunity. The dissent reads the statutory reference to available procedures against established limits on endless exhaustion, rather than treating every opportunity to request extraordinary or selective relief as mandatory. It accepts proper-presentation rules, genuine claim-specific state defaults and existing exceptions. It would affirm the no-default holding and allow ordinary habeas proceedings on these three claims, subject to other actual conditions, without awarding a writ or release.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for proceedings consistent with the three claims' procedural default. Any properly preserved review of the existing innocence-gateway ruling must use pre-AEDPA law and consider the district court's independent alternative ground. No new evidentiary hearing, cause showing, innocence finding or merits result is directed. Other claims and independent rulings remain in their existing posture.
