#### Day v. Holahan

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-672.
**Decided:** May 15, 1995.

##### Authority of the responsive-financing judgment

**Authority of the judgment:** The section 10A.25(13) responsive-financing invalidation is affirmed 5–4: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support the judgment. Kennedy's plurality, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, treats the adverse proportional legal trigger as an expressive burden; Stone-Zsela's judgment concurrence rests on insufficient programmatic connection on this record and rejects categorical invalidity. Neither framework controls, and no controlling rationale is established under Marks. The judgment binds the parties; the three independently majority-supported holdings below do not supply a unified financing rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The invalidated section 10A.25(13) mechanism increases candidate limits by independent expenditures opposing the candidate plus expenditures for the candidate's major-party opponents, excluding an association's communications solely to its dues-paying members. The provision itself has no $100 threshold; that figure describes the lower court's operational reporting scenario. The Board must notify every candidate in the race within 48 hours after receipt of the report or notice under section 10A.20(3), (6) or (6b), and within three days after notice must pay half the expenditure only to a subsidy-eligible candidate who has raised twice the minimum match, from an appropriation from the general fund. The lower court's electoral-result-eligibility concern describes implementation; it is neither a statutory payment exception nor a finding that every subsidy is immediately payable.

##### The special immediate-notice obligation falls with this trigger

**Holding:** Section 10A.20, subdivision 6b's additional immediate affidavit and service obligation cannot be enforced independently of the invalid responsive-financing mechanism on this scheme's demonstrated function and legislative relationship. Under Minnesota section 645.20, the special mechanism is essentially and inseparably connected with the invalid provision such that the legislature cannot be presumed to have enacted it alone; ordinary campaign reports remain enforceable within their separate terms.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct votes. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissent on this component.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The additional obligation is a twenty-four-hour affidavit and service on the candidates and campaign treasurers in the affected race. The holding rests on essential legislative connection under section 645.20, not physical impossibility of reporting. The independent vagueness and overbreadth theories are not reached.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Buckley, 424 U.S. 1: preserves distinct disclosure interests and associational protections; no categorical disclosure invalidation follows.
- Gibson, 372 U.S. 539: applies the need for a substantial relation between information demanded and the legitimate subject; does not eliminate ordinary reports.
- Brown v. Socialist Workers, 459 U.S. 87: recognizes genuine disclosure interests with protection for demonstrated associational harms; no new general exemption is created.

##### This $100 intermediary-contribution ceiling lacks sufficient fit

**Holding:** Minnesota's $100 annual ceiling on contributions received by the covered political committees and political funds is unconstitutional on the established burden and insufficiently fitted corruption/circumvention justification. Contributions remain distinct from independent expenditures: realistic routes around valid candidate limits may be regulated, but a broadly restrictive ceiling must preserve meaningful collective political association and fit an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism; no permanent dollar minimum follows.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes, at this stated functional application.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 10A.27(12) covers aggregate annual contributions from individuals, political committees and political funds, but excludes a candidate's principal campaign committee and a political party unit defined in section 10A.275 from its covered recipients. Higher limits, coordination controls, candidate transfers and demonstrated conduit restrictions remain open. The record's substantial pooling burden does not establish a permanent constitutional dollar floor or equate contributions with independent expenditures.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Buckley: applies contribution/expenditure distinction, corruption fit and effective collective advocacy; its historical dollar figure is no minimum.
- California Medical, 453 U.S. 182: preserves candidate-transfer anticircumvention regulation; its plurality is not a categorical answer for every committee.
- Citizens Against Rent Control, 454 U.S. 290: applies protection of pooling for collective political expression; does not immunize a candidate-contribution conduit.
- NCPAC, 470 U.S. 480: preserves independent advocacy at its scope; contributions are not silently equated with expenditures.

##### MCCL receives protection on its actual nonprofit characteristics

**Holding:** Minnesota may not prohibit MCCL's independent expenditures solely because its incidental business activity and absence of a formal policy against significant corporate contributions fall outside section 211B.15, subdivision 15's literal safe harbor. On the undisputed minimal-business-income and insignificant-corporate-funding record, its relevant political-association characteristics support as-applied MCFL protection; material commercial accumulation, economic claimants or corporate/union conduit funding requires a different assessment.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by all eight Associates: nine direct votes on this as-applied holding. No general overruling or endorsement beyond Austin's surviving scope is adopted.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The section 211B.15(15) safe harbor requires no business activity, no economic shareholders or claimants, no establishment by a business corporation or union, and a policy against significant contributions from them. Protection rests on MCCL's actual minimal business income and undisputed insignificant corporate funding. That checklist cannot foreclose this as-applied protection; materially changed operations or corporate or union financing may require reassessment. Direct corporate candidate contributions and ordinary business-corporation treasury restrictions are not decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- MCFL, 479 U.S. 238: applies protection to actual political-resource, structure and funding characteristics; descriptive facts do not foreclose this narrow as-applied claim.
- Austin, 494 U.S. 652: distinguishes materially different commercially accumulated corporate wealth; its surviving corporate-treasury rule is not overruled.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the Eighth Circuit on all four components. Maintain the permanent restraints on section 10A.25(13)'s responsive financing, section 10A.20(6b)'s linked special notice and section 10A.27(12)'s $100 annual ceiling, and MCCL's bounded as-applied protection under section 211B.15(15). Ordinary reports and otherwise lawful corporate and contribution regulation remain; no damages, general public-financing prohibition or facial excision of corporate restrictions follows.

#### Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. Federal Election Commission, 518 U.S. 604 (1996)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-489.
**Decided:** June 26, 1996.

##### Whether the Senate party-expenditure ceiling may reach this independent advertisement

**Holding:** A political party's own campaign advertisement that is genuinely independent of a candidate may not be subjected to the Senate-election expenditure ceiling in 2 U.S.C. §441a(d)(3) merely because its speaker is an official political party. Party identity and a continuing electoral relationship do not, without more, justify applying that ceiling to this independent expression; the adverse enforcement judgment must therefore be vacated.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s Part I, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; six direct votes.

**Material application:** The party’s $15,000 pre-nomination advertisement was genuinely independent. Its prior assignment of the whole $103,248 adjusted election allowance explains statutory excess, not constitutional validity. Legitimate corruption and circumvention concerns do not establish an actual candidate connection from party identity alone.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No majority adopts the complete narrower expenditure framework or its prudential reservation rationale (O’Connor, Souter, Breyer), or Kennedy/Scalia/Thomas’s broader coordinated-party-expression protection. No Marks combination is used. Coordinated expression, direct candidate cash, earmarks, republication, sham independence and actual conduits are neither generally immunized nor upheld by implication. No universal bribery-proof, express-advocacy formula, constitutional dollar floor, vagueness or administrative-deference rule.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Adverse enforcement/penalty mandate vacated and remanded, 6–3. The broader Senate-election counterclaim receives no majority constitutional determination; no fixed penalty, damages, refund or compelled new factual trial.

##### Whether party identity supplies the connection required by the alternative contribution theory

**Holding:** For the government's theory that this advertisement is a contribution under §441a(a)(7)(B)(i), party status alone cannot substitute for expenditure made in cooperation, consultation or concert with, or at the request or suggestion of, a candidate, the candidate's authorized political committees or their agents. On the accepted independence premise, that theory cannot preserve the adverse enforcement judgment under §441a(a)(2).

**Proposition-level authority:** The same six-Justice Part I coalition.

**Material application:** A categorical agency characterization is not a factual finding of consultation; competing assertions about novelty and noncontest establish neither forfeiture nor concession. Transfers among national, State, district, local and subordinate same-party committees are exempt from §441a(a)(1) and (2). Candidate-material dissemination, distribution or republication under (a)(7)(B)(ii), and original-source attribution for earmarked/intermediary contributions under (a)(8), have distinct predicates absent here.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The constitutionality of a transaction with an actual connection, all interactions with (d)(1)’s notwithstanding clause, presidential allowances and other House limits are not decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1:** Its distinction between independent expenditures and candidate contributions supplies the present independent-advertisement rule. Its treatment of the party provision's Fifth Amendment challenge did not decide this First Amendment question. The general distinction remains in force.
- **FEC v. National Conservative Political Action Committee, 470 U.S. 480:** Its protection of genuinely independent political advocacy supports the result; it supplies no categorical immunity for actual contribution channels.
- **Day v. Holahan, May 15, 1995:** Its controlling protection of meaningful intermediary association and preservation of realistic conduit regulation inform the required distinction. Its recipient category excluded political-party units, and its responsive-financing judgment had no unified controlling rationale. Neither is converted into an earlier decision of this party-spending question.
- **FEC v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life, Inc., 479 U.S. 238:** Its protection of political expression and its nonprofit setting remain distinct; the Court does not rest today's judgment on a universal express-advocacy formula or extend the nonprofit exception to every entity.

#### Kentucky Right to Life, Inc. v. Stengel

**Docket or dockets:** 97-60; Sixth Circuit No. 95-6581.

**Decided:** 1998-09-14.

##### Nonprofit status does not require a categorical exemption for corporate candidate gifts

**Holding:** Kentucky may apply §121.025's direct candidate-contribution restriction to KRL's general corporate treasury without creating a categorical exemption merely because KRL is an ideological nonprofit. The rejection of that facial exemption does not authorize restrictions on protected independent advocacy or determine whether KRL satisfies an independent-expenditure funding condition.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part III of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices.

**Material application:** The Court holds that a payment to a candidate remains different from an association's own expression. NRWC supports prophylactic treatment of the corporate contribution channel, and MCFL expressly distinguishes direct contributions from the independent spending it protects. The destination of the payment and the risk of candidate obligation supply the relevant ground; the State need not first prove that this corporation completed a corrupt transaction. KRLPAC supplies a separate voluntary path for candidate-directed support, while lawful independent advocacy remains distinct. Day protects an association according to its actual resources and structure; its nonprofit holding expressly left direct corporate gifts undecided. The contrary categorical exemption would erase that distinction. The Court does not find KRL to be a commercial treasury or conduit, decide its funding eligibility, or endorse every restriction that can be attached to incorporation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** NRWC applies to the contribution channel; MCFL's independent-expression protection remains intact. Day's nonprofit holding is distinguished according to the transaction it decided. Austin is neither overruled nor enlarged beyond its surviving corporate-treasury scope.

##### The paid candidate-advertisement identification rule survives this facial challenge

**Holding:** The surviving facial challenge to §121.190(1)'s payer-identification requirement fails in the paid candidate-finance and organizational setting presented. The rule requires the payer's name and address, introduced by the paid-for-by designation, on newspaper and magazine advertising, posters, circulars, billboards, handbills, sample ballots and paid television or radio announcements expressly advocating election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate, slate or group of candidates; ordinary issue advocacy is not covered merely because it mentions a candidate.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part IV of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight Justices. Thomas dissents.

**Material application:** The Court holds that identifying the financer of organized paid candidate advocacy serves source-tracing and enforcement interests different from general curiosity about an ordinary issue leafleteer's identity. The address helps identify the legal payer and connect an advertisement to financial transactions; it is a burden requiring justification, not dispensable statutory language. Buckley's disclosure interests and McIntyre's express reservation of materially different campaign-finance and organizational settings permit this bounded facial judgment. The on-message command is more intrusive than filing a report, but the actual financer-identification function supplies the necessary connection here without limiting the amount or content of the independent expenditure. The State cannot presume corruption or coordination merely from an advertisement's independence or political identity. McIntyre continues to protect ordinary anonymous issue leaflets, and materially different individual applications remain open. So does a claim supported by a reasonable probability of threats or reprisals. No actual retaliation, coordination, general membership-disclosure authority or requirement of permission before publication is found.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** McIntyre is distinguished on its reserved candidate-finance and organizational context without diminishing its individual-leaflet rule. Buckley supplies the distinct finance-disclosure interests. Colorado Republican preserves independence and forbids identity-based substitution for an actual candidate connection. Brown v. Socialist Workers preserves protection against demonstrated associational harms without creating a new blanket exemption.

##### The preserved challenges to the two direct $1,000 limits fail

**Holding:** The preserved facial challenge to §121.150(6)'s $1,000 giving and acceptance limit in any one election is rejected under the direct-contribution distinction. The separate challenge to §121A.050(1)'s $1,000 slate limit in any one election is likewise rejected, preserving that section's knowing-acceptance and knowing-giving terms and the giving prohibition's express exception for independent expenditures as statutorily defined.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part V of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; the coalition covers both distinct provisions.

**Material application:** The Court holds that the surviving direct-transfer challenges can be resolved under Buckley's contribution rule without treating contribution limits as constitutionally unlimited. A direct payment to the candidate presents a corruption and circumvention concern different from financing one's own advocacy. The lower court reported that counsel had proposed the $1,000 figure and essentially conceded its constitutionality. That reported position confines the preserved controversy; it is not a general unseen waiver or a reason to accept an incorrect legal rule. The direct-transfer character and bounded claim supply the judgment. The Court does not move the slate provision's knowledge requirement or independent-expenditure exception into a different section. Nor does it turn a per-election limit into an annual one, establish $1,000 as a constitutional minimum or safe harbor for every office, or authorize limits meant to equalize political influence. Future demonstrated cumulative or prohibitory burdens remain distinct.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Buckley's direct-contribution distinction and fitted anticorruption rationale apply; its historical figure is not a constitutional floor. Day preserves scrutiny of meaningful association and cumulative burdens without equating contributions with independent expenditures.

##### The annual all-intermediary aggregate requires the inquiry the lower court bypassed

**Holding:** A $1,500 ceiling on one person's annual contributions to all permanent committees and contributing organizations together must be assessed for meaningful collective political association and fit to an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism. Because the lower judgment sustained §121.150(10) without giving that inquiry its required force, the judgment on this component is vacated and remanded for application of Day to the existing record and ordinary remaining remedial questions.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part VI of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer: six Justices. Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Ginsburg dissent from this remand.

**Material application:** The Court holds that the ability to regulate a real candidate-funding conduit does not answer the fit of an aggregate that exhausts one allowance across unrelated recipients. Day requires meaningful opportunity to pool resources for political expression. California Medical permits prevention of circumvention through candidate-transfer channels, but its four-Justice low-protection reasoning is not a controlling categorical answer to every intermediary contribution. KRLPAC's candidate transfers identify a legitimate concern; they do not establish the fit of the law's complete combined-recipient reach. The lower court must consider actual recipient functions, the aggregate's breadth, the supported burden and the separate opportunity for direct candidate gifts under the proper standard. A higher figure than Day's $100 does not end the inquiry, and a general reference to smaller state campaigns does not supply it. Conversely, the Court finds neither every application invalid nor the State's evidentiary showing absent. This is a legal-standard remand, not a compulsory new trial, discovery order, new burden of proof, constitutional dollar minimum or direction to exempt all independent recipients automatically.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Day applies its meaningful-association and fit rule beyond the particular $100 ceiling while retaining its limits. California Medical preserves genuine conduit regulation without converting plurality reasoning into law. Citizens Against Rent Control protects collective expression; NCPAC preserves independent advocacy without immunizing candidate contributions.

##### Both gubernatorial receipt branches survive KRLPAC's facial challenge

**Holding:** KRLPAC's facial challenge to both §121A.030(4) receipt limits is rejected: the Fund participant's permanent-committee receipts are limited to 25 percent of qualifying contributions received, up to $150,000 per election, while the nonparticipant is subject to the separate $150,000 ceiling. Direct candidate receipts present a source-specific dependency concern, and this facial record does not demonstrate obstruction of effective advocacy or association; concrete later burdens under either branch remain open.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part VII of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: six Justices. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would vacate and remand both branches.

**Material application:** The Court holds that candidate receipts from one organized financing channel differ from the annual pool across independent recipients addressed in Part VI. Buckley's contribution distinction permits a fitted response to dependence on that channel without requiring proof of a completed corrupt bargain. The percentage branch limits the share measured against the participant's own qualifying contributions; it remains operative at receipts below the maximum. The nonparticipant branch uses its separate fixed ceiling. The district court reported no indication that the provision had actually limited PAC contributions or was likely to do so on this record. That reported conclusion supports this facial judgment; it is not a statutory exception or an irrebuttable fact about every race. The contrary concern that earlier donors may exhaust a later association's opportunity is substantial but does not establish facial invalidity here. Neither the maximum-fundraising example nor the number of committees needed to reach a maximum is a premise of this judgment. The Court does not rely on consent to unknown funding conditions, disfavor of PAC views, or equalization. Later proof of ordinary fundraising disabled by the percentage at low receipts, selective foreclosure or prohibitory cumulative effects requires separate consideration.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Buckley's direct-contribution rationale applies at this source-specific and facial level. Day preserves meaningful association and rejects a permanent dollar safe harbor; its different annual-intermediary and responsive-financing components do not resolve these candidate-receipt rules. Colorado Republican preserves independent advocacy without converting direct candidate receipts into independent spending.

#### Clifton v. Federal Election Commission

**Docket or dockets:** 97-786.

**Decided:** 1998-09-14.

##### Asking a candidate about public issues does not itself finance the candidate

**Holding:** Under 2 U.S.C. §441b, 11 C.F.R. §114.4(c)(4) cannot prohibit a publisher's mere inquiry to a candidate about positions or recorded votes as a contribution or candidate-directed expenditure. The required connection concerns campaign spending: actual cooperation, consultation, concert, request or suggestion concerning the expenditure remains governed by its own statutory predicates.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous, at the stated narrow statutory and review-specific scope.

**Material application:** The Court holds that gathering information is not itself spending on a candidate's behalf. Buckley v. Valeo distinguishes independent expression from candidate financing; Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. Federal Election Commission requires a real candidate connection rather than a label substituting for that connection; Akins v. FEC likewise requires actual covered financing. MRLC's effort to learn the meaning of public votes supplies no finding that a candidate directed the guide's content, collaborated in distribution, or received an agreed campaign service. The Commission may investigate such arrangements when supported. Its concern that a survey could conceal collaboration cannot make every inquiry collaboration. This holding protects neither staged questions supplying campaign work nor a paid political advertisement disguised as an independent guide.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Buckley's contribution/expenditure distinction, Colorado Republican's actual-connection requirement and Akins's transaction-specific coverage remain in force and apply to this information-gathering setting.

##### The existing written-only and equal-space declaration stands

**Holding:** In this private-petitioner review, the declaration invalidating §114.4(c)(5)'s compulsory written questions and replies and equal space and prominence remains in place. General rulemaking and enforcement authority does not authorize treating every independently edited guide involving candidate inquiry as a prohibited contribution or imposing these indiscriminate contact and editorial conditions on that premise.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous, at the stated narrow statutory and review-specific scope. Stevens and Ginsburg join only within this private-petitioner review and its limited statutory ground; they do not adopt a general constitutional ban on contact safeguards.

**Material application:** The Court's limited statutory ground respects the difference between a real contribution and dialogue used to compose independent speech. A written record can aid enforcement, but administrative convenience does not establish that an oral discussion transfers value to a candidate. Miami Herald Publishing Co. v. Tornillo protects editorial allocation; Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group of Boston protects the speaker's selection of an expressive composition. Those principles reinforce the need for actual statutory authority before requiring a private publisher to give every candidate equal space and prominence. The petition seeks broader protection for the private plaintiffs; no established cross-petition seeks enlargement of the Commission's rights by restoring restrictions already invalidated. That review limit independently preserves the existing relief. The Court does not decide that Congress could never authorize a specifically fitted contact safeguard, that accurate disclosure could never be required under lawful authority, or that proved coordinated services are exempt. A government-authored guide or a condition on a properly defined public resource presents different questions.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Tornillo and Hurley reinforce editorial protection without becoming a universal prohibition on regulation of campaign contributions. Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce is neither overruled nor treated as a delegation of every restriction on corporate speech.

##### The unfinished provisions and organizational status remain for the existing remand

**Holding:** The Court does not expand the declaration to invalidate the whole regulation or uphold the unresolved electioneering-message clauses. Their operative meaning, ripeness and severability remain for the District Court; MRLC's entitlement to nonprofit constitutional protection remains undecided on its actual funding characteristics.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous, at the stated narrow statutory and review-specific scope.

**Material application:** The Court cannot supply an operative definition the appellate record leaves unsettled or decide severability from the label alone. The existing remand permits those questions and any properly supported interim-relief request to be decided under their own standards. Day v. Holahan prevents automatic denial of MCFL protection solely because an ideological nonprofit accepts some corporate money; it does not establish that MRLC's funding is insignificant, noncommercial or free of conduit features. The Court therefore neither grants an organizational exemption nor assumes commercial-treasury status. Genuine-coordination restrictions, express-advocacy provisions not successfully challenged, and distinct statutory membership exclusions retain their lawful scope.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Day and MCFL require actual organizational and funding characteristics; Austin's surviving corporate-treasury rule remains available in its materially different setting. No prior decision is overruled.

### Independent political-party campaign expression and candidate connections

**Governing question:** Application of the Senate party-expenditure ceiling and the alternative candidate-contribution theory to a genuinely independent party advertisement.

**Current rule:** Section 441a(d)(3)'s Senate-election expenditure ceiling cannot be applied to a party's own genuinely independent campaign advertisement merely because the speaker is an official political party. Party identity or a continuing electoral relationship also cannot replace §441a(a)(7)(B)(i)'s required cooperation, consultation, concert, request or suggestion by a candidate, authorized political committee or agent for the asserted contribution theory. Actual independence defeats that theory; realistic corruption and circumvention concerns do not alone establish the statutory candidate connection.

**Authority by component:**

- Independent-expression protection: Independent party expression; distinct contribution predicate: The alternative contribution theory, 1996-06-26; O’Connor Part I, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer (six).

**Present operation effective:** Colorado Republican, 1996-06-26.

**Exceptions and limits:** The general contribution/expenditure distinction survives. Transfers between national, State, district, local and subordinate committees of the same party retain their exemption from §441a(a)(1)–(2); candidate-material republication under §441a(a)(7)(B)(ii) and earmarking or intermediary direction under §441a(a)(8) have different predicates. The rule creates no immunity for candidate cash, genuine conduits, sham independence or coordinated expression.

**Open question:** No majority either validates or invalidates all coordinated party-expression limits or resolves every interaction with §441a(d)(1)’s notwithstanding clause. Neither three-Justice further framework supplies controlling law.

**Holdings navigation:** Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. Federal Election Commission, 518 U.S. 604 (1996).

### Candidate financing, political-committee thresholds and membership exclusions

**Governing question:** Whether predominantly issue-oriented groups are exempt from FECA political-committee duties for otherwise covered candidate financing.

**Current rule:** Buckley and MCFL supply no categorical organization-wide major-purpose exemption for genuinely covered candidate financing. Section 431(4)(A) reaches a committee, club, association or other group receiving contributions aggregating over $1,000 in a calendar year or making expenditures aggregating over $1,000 in a calendar year, subject to actual definitions, exclusions and constitutional limits. Establish membership, covered transactions, actual candidate connection and thresholds before imposing duties. Where a contribution theory requires cooperation, consultation, concert, request or suggestion, ideological alignment, lobbying or partisan identity alone is insufficient. Independent expression retains its protection.

**Authority by component:**
- No categorical major-purpose exemption: Akins, Akins v. FEC — III. Major purpose is not a categorical exemption for otherwise covered candidate financing, Souter Part III with Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer (five for both candidate-cash/conduit and broader covered candidate-financing subparts; Kennedy joins only the former), 1997-06-25.
- Distinct exclusions and reporting conditions: Akins, Akins v. FEC — IV. Membership exclusions and their distinct reporting proviso govern the remand, seven-Justice Part IV, 1997-06-25.

**Present operation effective:** Akins, 1997-06-25.

**Controlling construction:** Section 431(9)(B)(iii) excludes membership-organization or corporate communications to members, stockholders, or executive or administrative personnel when the organization is not primarily organized to influence federal nomination or election. Its separate proviso requires reporting direct costs of communications expressly advocating a clearly identified candidate's election or defeat when costs exceed $2,000 per election, except communications primarily about other subjects, under §434(a)(4)(A)(i) and, for a general election, (ii). Section 441b(b)(2) separately excludes corporate communications on any subject to stockholders and executive or administrative personnel and their families, and labor communications to members and their families; its nonpartisan registration/get-out-the-vote exclusion covers those respective recipients. Its separate-fund exclusion covers establishment, administration and solicitation for a political separate segregated fund by a corporation, labor or membership organization, cooperative or corporation without capital stock. Its loan exception concerns ordinary-course national or State bank loans under banking law and regulations. Sections 431(8)(B)(vi) and (9)(B)(v) cross-reference activity allowed by §441b without importing the $2,000 proviso.

**Open question:** Particular organizational or donor-disclosure burdens retain supported constitutional challenges. The construction supplies no automatic membership, transaction, threshold or liability finding.

**Holdings navigation:** Akins v. FEC (1997-06-25) — III. Major purpose is not a categorical exemption for otherwise covered candidate financing; Akins v. FEC (1997-06-25) — IV. Membership exclusions and their distinct reporting proviso govern the remand.

#### Holdings

##### Judicial-campaign expenditures and the preliminary protection

**Holding and operative rule:** A mandatory ceiling on the total amount of judicial-campaign advocacy receives the demanding First Amendment review applicable to campaign expenditures. Ohio's interest in impartial adjudication and its appearance does not justify this source-independent $75,000 ceiling on the present record; the candidates' likelihood of success supports the existing preliminary expenditure injunction at its actual, consent-extended scope.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion for the unanimous Court supplies the complete rule, application and remedial limits. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer join every controlling proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the judicial office's distinctive function does not remove campaign advocacy from the expenditure rule. Buckley v. Valeo distinguishes the quantity of a candidate's expression from a donor's contribution. City of Cincinnati v. Kruse applies that distinction to a source-independent total ceiling while reserving materially different institutional restrictions. The Court now considers judicial impartiality directly: judges must decide cases impartially, and public confidence in that function is a weighty interest. The lower court's suggestion that corruption is the only interest worthy of consideration is not adopted.

The asserted interest nevertheless does not establish this ceiling's necessary fit. The record concerns contribution influence, adjudicative commitments and bias; the total limit also forbids further personally financed and qualifications-related advocacy without identifying the corresponding danger. That a candidate can communicate adequately below the ceiling does not explain why additional advocacy may be prohibited. A concern about interested donors does not make every expenditure a contribution restriction. Colorado Republican preserves that transaction-specific distinction rather than immunizing transfers or other financial arrangements.

Separate contribution, solicitation, disclosure and recusal measures address different dangers. Their relevance does not establish that every such measure is valid or invariably sufficient, and no proof of a completed bribe or compulsory empirical waiting period is required. The candidates' continuing exposure under the old canon gives the preliminary protection practical legal effect despite the completed election and amendment. The amended canon remains for consideration below on its own text and record. The Court affirms the confined preliminary protection; it neither enters a final statewide decree nor adjudicates the separate transfer ruling.

**Application and limits:** Protection continues for the plaintiffs, plaintiff-intervenors and their respective opponents under the October 22, 1996 consent extension, including Maloney's later preliminary protection. It does not extend to every Ohio judicial candidate. The removed $18,750 primary sublimit and its separate consent decree are outside this decision. Ordinary recusal and disqualification, distinct ethics restrictions and lawful donor-contribution controls are undisturbed. The state-law remand is preserved, and no new general preliminary-injunction test is adopted.

#### Precedent Treatment

- *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1, is applied to the direct restraint on the quantity of campaign expression. Its expenditure/contribution distinction remains controlling; the judicial-office setting does not supply an exemption from demanding expenditure review.
- *City of Cincinnati v. Kruse*, December 16, 1998, is applied to this source-independent ceiling after separately examining the judicial-impartiality interest it reserved. Its rule against justifying a total speech cutoff by sufficient remaining speech remains effective, and its reservation of materially different institutional restrictions survives.
- *Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. Federal Election Commission*, June 26, 1996, is applied for its distinction between political expression and actual candidate-resource transactions. Its actual-connection requirements and limitations remain intact; neither its party-specific transfer exemption nor its treatment of coordinated activity supplies a ruling on Ohio's unreviewed transfer canon.

#### Law After Decision

As of January 28, 1999, the demanding expenditure rule applies to mandatory judicial-campaign spending ceilings. Judicial impartiality and its appearance receive independent consideration, but do not justify the challenged source-independent $75,000 limit on this record. The existing preliminary injunction stands at its actual scope. The Court makes no final adjudication of the amended canon, no statewide decree, no decision on the nonjudicial-account transfer restriction and no general invalidation of judicial ethics or contribution rules.

### National Committee of the Reform Party of the United States of America v. Democratic National Committee; Ninth Circuit No. 98-15443; 168 F.3d 360. — 1999-03-17

#### Holdings

##### I. Demonstrated electoral support does not create a duty to equalize subsidies

**Operative rule.** The Constitution does not require equal presidential-campaign subsidies merely because parties compete or qualify for public financing. On the pleaded claim, a formula tied to prior electoral support and the alleged inability to match larger major-party resources do not establish the materially different practical suppression or discriminatory administration that remains open to challenge.

**Authority.** Parts I-III of Thomas's opinion are joined by all eight other Justices and control their respective holdings. Part IV has the distinct eight-Justice coalition in the opinion table.

**Controlling explanation.** Buckley v. Valeo, Part III, sustains support-sensitive public funding while preserving a properly supported different application. Libertarian Party of Florida v. Smith applies that distinction to the actual burden: access to electoral competition does not itself entitle a party to equal government assistance, but the funding label supplies no constitutional immunity.

The reported comparison is about $29 million for the Reform campaign and $62 million for each major-party campaign. The statutory categories use prior vote shares: at least 25 percent for a major party and at least five but less than 25 percent for a minor party. The scheme also contains a returning-candidate route and post-election eligibility for minor or new candidates reaching five percent. Those routes are not eliminated by the present decision. Section 9003(c) permits a minor- or new-party candidate's total qualified expenses to reach the major-party entitlement, with private contributions covering the excess of those expenses over public receipts; the smaller subsidy is not itself a total-spending ceiling. The Court does not find that fundraising succeeded or decide a new payment entitlement.

The Court accepts the reported disparity and inability to close it. Those allegations assert parity, not a different pleaded practical disability from the one Buckley considered. A party need not disappear or stop fielding candidates before challenging a substantially suppressive application. Viewpoint favoritism, concrete exclusionary administration and a materially different cumulative burden remain open; none is adjudicated on unreported facts here.

##### II. The actual contribution-limit argument requires its own narrow answer

**Operative rule.** The alleged inability to close this public-subsidy gap under the $1,000 individual-candidate contribution restriction does not, by itself, state a materially different constitutional impairment from the parity claim Buckley rejected. This holding neither establishes a permanent dollar safe harbor nor resolves a properly supported prohibitory or cumulative contribution burden.

**Controlling explanation.** A direct contribution to a candidate differs from the candidate's own expenditure, independent advocacy and an aggregate restriction on association through intermediaries. Buckley preserves those distinctions. Kentucky Right to Life's direct-limit holding applies contribution doctrine within its actual facial scope; its separate aggregate and sourcewide-receipt holdings do not make one amount valid for every time and circumstance. Day's intermediary holding protects a different recipient class and meaningful pooling. Colorado Republican protects actual independent expression while preserving scrutiny of genuine candidate channels.

The plaintiffs' contribution argument is considered separately, not dismissed for the affiliation claim's standing defect or because public funding was voluntary. Accepting their inability-to-close allegation, equality of total resources remains the asserted constitutional demand. No finding of actual corruption, successful solicitation or consent to every contribution restriction supplies the decision. Nonrecipients do not accept the program's spending ceiling, while generally applicable contribution restrictions remain a separate question. A genuinely different burden would require its own analysis and the proper statutory tribunal.

##### III. Invalidating this affiliation maximum would not redress the asserted representation injury

**Operative rule.** These plaintiffs lack standing to challenge the FEC's maximum of three members affiliated with one party because they identify no concrete exclusion caused by that maximum that its removal would likely redress. The jurisdictional dismissal decides neither the cap's constitutional merits nor a different discriminatory appointment or enforcement claim.

**Controlling explanation.** Lujan requires a concrete injury, a connection to the challenged provision and likely redress. Northeastern Florida protects the opportunity to compete free of an actual discriminatory barrier without demanding proof that the claimant would ultimately succeed. Commerce and Glavin likewise preserve concrete injury and redress without making certainty a universal requirement.

The provision requires six presidentially appointed, Senate-confirmed Commissioners and limits one party to three seats. It does not reserve seats to named major parties or prohibit Reform affiliates. The reported three-and-three practice is not the statutory command. Removing the maximum would permit the same appointees, and the supported account identifies no appointment opportunity or particular enforcement injury obstructed by this maximum. The defect is the missing cap-created barrier and remedial connection, not failure to guarantee an appointment. Financial injury from the separate allocation statute cannot supply standing against every provision governing its administrator. No Appointments Clause, separation-of-powers, equal-protection, due-process or associational merits ruling follows.

##### IV. The granted claims supply no basis for bypassing or requiring a different statutory tribunal

**Operative rule.** A claim lacking Article III standing or presenting a constitutional theory plainly foreclosed by controlling law may be rejected before invoking the extraordinary statutory tribunal. A live substantial constitutional question cannot be bypassed merely because a court expects it ultimately to fail; a substantial FECA question follows section 437h, while a substantial Fund Act implementation or construction claim follows section 9011(b).

**Authority.** Thomas, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join Part IV. Stone-Zsela does not join it.

**Controlling explanation.** California Medical Association v. FEC supplies the standing and wholly-insubstantial-question distinction. It does not equate a weak ultimate claim with a frivolous question. The Court's present conclusions concern the reported legal theories, accepting their allegations, rather than an adverse factual finding or failure to produce unavailable papers.

Section 437h directs qualifying FECA constitutional questions brought by an authorized plaintiff to the court of appeals sitting en banc. Statutory eligibility, including that of a national committee or presidential voter, does not establish standing on every claim. Section 9011(b) separately provides the three-judge district court and direct Supreme Court appeal for its Fund Act suits, without requiring exhaustion of administrative or other remedies. Section 9011(a)'s D.C. Circuit review of Commission action within thirty days is another route, not a substitute for either. The financing theories as presented are foreclosed and the affiliation claim lacks standing; no statutory-tribunal remand follows. This antecedent explanation enters no independent disposition of the separate certification judgments outside the grant.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1:** Applied to support-sensitive public financing and the actual parity theory; its different-application reservation and contribution/expenditure distinction remain effective.
- **Libertarian Party of Florida v. Smith:** Applied at its actual-burden scope. Its unrestricted-spending facts are not attributed to this statutory program.
- **Day v. Holahan:** Its intermediary-contribution rule remains controlling in its recipient class. Its fractured responsive-financing judgment supplies no unified rule deciding this allocation formula.
- **Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. FEC:** Its protection of genuine independent expression and preservation of actual candidate channels remain distinct; party identity alone does not decide the constitutional category.
- **Kentucky Right to Life v. Stengel:** Its direct-limit, aggregate-intermediary and sourcewide-receipt rulings remain separate. Neither its amount nor its litigant's waiver becomes an immutable rule for this as-applied claim.
- **Lujan; Northeastern Florida; Department of Commerce v. United States House of Representatives and Glavin:** Applied to concrete injury, actual opportunity barriers and likely redress, without requiring certain appointment or success.
- **FEC v. NRA Political Victory Fund; FEC v. Legi-Tech:** Their statutory-authority and actual-reconsideration holdings remain intact. They do not decide this affiliation cap's merits or validate a particular appointment practice.
- **California Medical Association v. FEC, 453 U.S. 182:** Applied in Part IV to the threshold distinction; it does not authorize ordinary adjudication of a substantial claim assigned to a special tribunal.

#### Law After Decision

The support-sensitive formula and the asserted contribution-gap theory survive this challenge. A constitutional right to equal funding does not arise from the reported resource comparison. Different suppressive burdens, viewpoint discrimination and exclusionary administration remain reviewable on their own supported claims. The affiliation cap receives no constitutional merits judgment. Statutory eligibility to sue remains distinct from Article III standing, and the Fund Act and FECA review channels remain separate. No new subsidy amount, contribution ceiling or appointment entitlement is created.

### Brower v. Washington; No. 98-1510; judgment below Brower v. State, 137 Wash. 2d 44, 969 P.2d 42. — 1999-03-25

#### Holdings

##### I. The reimbursement condition does not establish the claimed exclusion or transfer of electoral authority

**Holding and operative rule.** A legislature may refer this particular public-private proposal subject to the beneficiary's undertaking to reimburse public election costs when the arrangement preserves equal franchise, viewpoint neutrality and meaningful legal participation. On this record the condition neither charges citizens for voting or ordinary petition access nor transfers electoral administration or the public's approval power to the beneficiary.

**Authority.** Kennedy's unanimous Court opinion controls this and the following two holdings, including each stated limit.

**Controlling explanation.** Anderson and Burdick require examination of the actual injury, the State's precise interests and how those interests require the burden. Severe restrictions require narrow tailoring to a compelling interest; reasonable nondiscriminatory administration receives proportionate review. Here the legislature selected the proposal and its financial contingency. The affiliate has a real choice whether to assume the expense, but voters need not pay to vote and public officials administer the election. Requiring this proposal's beneficiary to bear its special-election costs serves the identified fiscal function without making wealth a citizen qualification. The strongest objection is that the State has conferred private gatekeeping over electoral machinery. That objection requires examining what the private consent actually controls: the undertaking to finance this particular arrangement, not the citizen franchise, tally or public approval. Ordinary petition routes also remain available. Their availability supports this access analysis but would not cure a proven discriminatory referral practice or actual exclusion. The Court establishes no general right to purchase an election, categorical immunity for private funding or presumption that fiscal savings justify any burden.

##### II. The supported timing and resource-disparity claim establishes no right to delay or equalize the campaign

**Holding and operative rule.** The short campaign and opposition's inability to match the beneficiary's resources do not, on this record, establish the asserted abridgment of political expression or a right to postpone the election or equalize advocacy. A supported practical inability to organize or communicate, or a deliberately disabling calendar, would require analysis of that actual burden and the State's precise scheduling justification.

**Controlling explanation.** ACLF protects political conversation against inadequately justified direct restraints, while Reform Party rejects the presented entitlement to equal campaigning resources without excusing actual exclusion or suppression. The claim here establishes no separate restriction on the opposition's speech, fundraising, spending or organization. The lower court found no support for the alleged suppressive purpose. That record assessment does not make hostile intent indispensable: a practically prohibitive calendar can require scrutiny without it. Neither public control of counting nor the absence of a voter fee would answer such a burden. But duration and resource disparity alone do not establish that different record. The Court does not supply an emergency rationale for the calendar, find that opponents raised enough money, establish a minimum campaign period or declare every period shorter than sixty days adequate. The State's administration and financing interests support this arrangement at its demonstrated scope; they are not a sufficient answer to every possible restriction.

##### III. Financial consent is not an additional governmental electoral vote

**Holding and operative rule.** The affiliate's agreement to reimburse costs is an antecedent financial condition of this legislative proposal, not an allocated vote in the public electorate's decision. Because it gives the affiliate no ballot and changes no citizen's voting weight or the public tally, it does not violate the asserted federal equal-vote rule.

**Controlling explanation.** Reynolds protects equality in governmental electoral choice. Brower argues that an entity able to prevent the election by withholding reimbursement has more consequential power than an ordinary voter. The condition indeed affects whether this arrangement proceeds, but legal consequence alone does not turn private consent to an expense obligation into an electoral ballot. The legislature controls the referral; citizens approve or reject it; the affiliate controls acceptance of its own financial undertaking. No citizen's vote is multiplied or diminished, and no private choice displaces the public result. White's district-dilution setting is different, but absence of districts is not itself an exemption from equal-vote review. The Court rejects this characterization after examining the actual allocation of authority. It leaves separate access and speech claims to their own analysis and does not authorize private control of counted votes or any different arrangement conferring unequal governmental electoral power.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Anderson and Burdick supply the actual-burden and justification inquiry; Burdick's write-in application creates no categorical presumption for private reimbursement.
- Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation, decided January 12, 1999, supplies protection against direct restraints on political conversation. Its invalid registration, badge and disclosure restrictions are not facts of this case.
- Citizens for a Constitutional Convention v. Yoshina, decided January 21, 1999, supplies attention to actual legal effect and preserves review of fundamentally unfair administration. State-law authorization alone remains insufficient to answer a federal injury.
- National Committee of the Reform Party v. Democratic National Committee, decided March 17, 1999, rejects the presented resource-parity entitlement while preserving actual suppression and discriminatory administration. That limit is retained here.
- Gable v. Patton remains a threshold disposition, not a controlling election-financing merits rule.
- Reynolds v. Sims supplies equality in governmental voting weight; it does not classify this financial undertaking as an electoral vote.
- White v. Regester is distinguished because its district-dilution setting differs; equal-vote review is not categorically confined to district elections.

#### Law After Decision

This reimbursement arrangement and compressed campaign survive the presented federal challenge. Courts must keep financing consent, electoral access, actual expressive burdens and citizen voting weight distinct, and assess their actual legal operation. Private payment, public administration and unequal resources are each relevant facts, not categorical answers. No precedent is overruled and no general private-financing or campaign-duration safe harbor is established.
