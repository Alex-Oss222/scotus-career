#### Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-535.
**Decided:** June 8, 1992.

##### What level of constitutional scrutiny applies to a restriction on voting or candidate access

**Holding:** A court must weigh the character and magnitude of the injury to First and Fourteenth Amendment rights against the State's precise asserted interests and the extent to which they require the burden. Severe restrictions must be narrowly drawn to advance a compelling interest; reasonable, nondiscriminatory restrictions ordinarily are justified by important regulatory interests, so strict scrutiny does not govern every election rule.

**Proposition-level authority:** White's opinion of the Court, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas; five Justices control the complete rule. Kennedy's dissent, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, and Stone's dissent also accept the calibrated framework without making their contrary application controlling.

**Material application:** A write-in prohibition must be examined with the available routes to ballot access and its role in the selection system. Neither the prohibition alone nor the general need for election administration decides the burden.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Ordinary administration remains subject to meaningful scrutiny. The holding neither imposes strict scrutiny on all ballot regulations nor extends *Burson*'s public-sidewalk campaign-content holding to the official ballot.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, supplies the burden, precise-interest and necessity inquiry. *Norman v. Reed*, January 14, 1992, preserves actual access review and permissible electorate-specific support requirements, including its protection against cross-component exclusion. *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, recognizes substantial regulation of candidate selection while preserving review of severe barriers. *Burson v. Freeman*, May 26, 1992, concerns ordinary public sidewalks; neither its strict-scrutiny proposition nor its fractured judgment changes this ballot analysis.

##### Whether Hawaii's complete write-in prohibition is valid within the ballot-access system reviewed

**Holding:** Where the complete ballot-access system imposes only reasonable burdens, a write-in prohibition is presumptively valid and may be sustained by proportionate state interests. Hawaii's party and nonpartisan routes provide adequate access, and its prohibition validly supports primary finality, prevention of sore-loser candidacies and party raiding, and its treatment of uncontested primaries.

**Proposition-level authority:** White's opinion of the Court, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas; five Justices support both the rationale and affirmance. Stone, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy disagree with this application.

**Current force:** Remains controlling for the complete access system reviewed and its reasonable burdens.

**Material application:** New parties need one percent of registered-voter signatures 150 days before the primary; qualifying established parties retain access. Candidates need 15 signatures for legislative or county offices, or 25 for statewide or federal offices, 60 days before the primary. Nonpartisan candidates advance with ten percent of the primary vote or the votes sufficient to nominate a partisan candidate, whichever is lower; voters choose one party or nonpartisan primary ballot for all offices. These combined routes and restrictions support the limited-burden finding. Lawful selection deadlines need not accommodate every later preference.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Both primary and general-election applications within this system are reached. The Court does not independently validate every surrounding access rule or decide a prohibition within materially more restrictive access rules. Candidate qualifications and reasonable write-in procedures elsewhere remain open; no absolute write-in right or guarantee of competitive races is created.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the Ninth Circuit; its reversal of the district court's injunction remains effective. No prospective injunction or replacement election-administration scheme issues.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Jenness v. Fortson*, 403 U.S. 431, permits reasonable demonstrations of support. *Munro v. Socialist Workers Party*, 479 U.S. 189, permits primary winnowing and preventive regulation without proof of a particular past disruption. *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, supports primary finality and restrictions against circumvention. *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, governs this limited-burden application; the decision creates no exception to its inquiry. *Norman v. Reed*, January 14, 1992, retains protection against unjustified party-name and cross-component disqualification.

##### Whether the Constitution requires the State to count and publish fictional candidates or arbitrary protest messages

**Holding:** The First and Fourteenth Amendments do not require a State to count and publicize write-in votes for fictional figures or arbitrary protest messages as an independent form of expression. Elections select public officials, and a desire to use the ballot for such messages does not create an entitlement to a write-in option.

**Proposition-level authority:** White's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas, controls. Kennedy's dissent, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, and Stone's dissent expressly agree on rejection of this broader claim; all nine support the component without issuing a joint opinion.

**Limits and questions not reached:** A genuine interest in supporting an actual alternative candidate requires the contextual access analysis separately stated above. Political expression outside the ballot and every restriction on eligible candidates in a different system are not decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reject the arbitrary-message tally claim; it supplies no independent ground for relief from the prohibition.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, supplies the office-selecting function of elections; the ballot is not a general expressive forum. *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, protects electoral participation without requiring an arbitrary-message tally.

#### Franklin v. Massachusetts, 505 U.S. 788 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-1502.
**Decided:** June 26, 1992.

For standing and APA review in Franklin v. Massachusetts, June 26, 1992, No. 91-1502, see Federal Courts — Standing and representation injury and Administrative Law — Census determinations and judicial review.

##### Temporarily absent residents may be included in the constitutional count

**Holding:** The constitutional command to count persons in each State permits inclusion of federal personnel temporarily overseas whose enduring ties support residence in a particular State. It does not require a literal census-day physical presence rule, and it does not authorize attribution to a State without a supportable residence connection.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone's opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by the other eight Justices; all nine adopt the constitutional inclusion rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The rule requires a supportable residence connection; it does not mandate inclusion of every person abroad or relax intrastate district equality. Different connections and methodological records remain open.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The District Court's judgment is reversed and its corrective relief dissolved. No replacement allocation, further factual proceedings or injunction against the President is ordered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- United States Department of Commerce v. Montana, March 31, 1992, this Court: applied for the constitutional structure of interstate apportionment and the distinction from intrastate districting; no new tolerance for unequal districts is adopted.
- Wesberry v. Sanders, 376 U.S. 1 (1964): distinguished as an intrastate congressional district-equality rule; its equal-population command remains effective in its own setting.
- Karcher v. Daggett, 462 U.S. 725 (1983): distinguished on the same interstate/intrastate ground; the present residence decision does not excuse avoidable inequality among a State's districts.

#### MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co., 512 U.S. 218

**Docket or dockets:** Nos. 93-356 and 93-521.

**Decided:** 1994-06-17.

**Companion or consolidated matters:** United States et al. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co. et al., No. 93-521, is consolidated with No. 93-356; the same rule invalidation applies to both.

##### The modification power does not permit this replacement of mandatory filing

**Holding:** Under 47 U.S.C. §203(b)(2), the Commission may, in its discretion and for good cause shown, modify tariff requirements in particular instances or by general order applicable to special circumstances or conditions, but may not replace §203(a)'s mandatory filing obligation throughout the nondominant long-distance sector with an optional system or lengthen the statutory notice period beyond 120 days. The challenged permissive-filing rule exceeds that delegation and is invalid; Chevron does not require deference to a construction outside this statutory boundary.

**Proposition-level authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg. Five of eight participating Justices directly adopt the structural modification ground, its application and its stated limits; no aggregation of separate rationales is necessary.

**Material application:** The November 1992 rule makes filing optional, not forbidden, for nondominant long-distance carriers; AT&T alone remains classified as dominant in this record. A modification can have consequential effects. Courts must examine the obligation changed, the scope of the modification clause and whether the agency adjusts the obligation's operation or removes its essential operation for the regulated class. This inquiry does not turn on a numerical market-share threshold or automatically invalidate a class-wide adjustment. Changes to filing form, contents, timing or place remain possible under the statutory conditions; timing changes cannot extend the 120-day notice ceiling. The Court does not categorically foreclose deferral or waiver in limited circumstances, and decides no particular proposed waiver. Good cause alone cannot authorize replacement of mandatory filing throughout this sector.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide whether the challenged order independently fails the special-circumstances-or-conditions requirement; no separate alternative ground commands a majority. It prescribes no tariff rates or replacement program, decides no damages or retrospective nonfiling consequences, resolves no general rule for individual nonenforcement decisions, and adopts no independently sufficient ratification or prohibition theory from §§226 or 332.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984): applies the inquiry into statutory meaning before deference; no new exception based on economic importance and no replacement of Chevron is adopted.
- National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp., March 25, 1992: preserves contextual interpretation within an actual delegation and the requirement of a discernible agency ground; the agency's competitive-policy rationale is examined, not replaced by a judicial rationale.
- Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala, June 7, 1993: preserves permissible interpretive change within a statutory system; the defect here is the rule's statutory reach, not the fact that the Commission changed its policy.
- Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB, January 27, 1992: preserves the principle that agency expertise operates within binding statutory limits; its distinct nonemployee-access rule is not imported into tariff regulation.
- NLRB v. Health Care & Retirement Corp. of America, May 23, 1994: distinguishes a permissible functional construction preserving the statute's operative distinctions from removal of the filing obligation here; its supervisory-status holding remains intact.
- PUD No. 1 of Jefferson County v. Washington Department of Ecology, May 31, 1994: distinguishes broad conditioning authority grounded in that Act's operative compliance language; no general rule against consequential agency action follows.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The D.C. Circuit's judgment setting aside the permissive-detariffing rule is affirmed in Nos. 93-356 and 93-521. No tariff rate, replacement program, damages or retrospective monetary consequence of earlier nonfiling is decided, and no new direction is given in the distinct complaint proceeding.

#### Smiley v. Citibank (South Dakota), N.A., 517 U.S. 735

**Docket or dockets:** 95-860
**Decided:** June 3, 1996

##### Does interpretive change or a rule's development during litigation categorically defeat deference?

**Holding:** A lawful, adequately explained interpretation within delegated statutory ambiguity does not lose eligibility for deference merely because it changes or addresses an old statute during litigation. Unexplained reversal or disregard of legitimate reliance may be arbitrary and capricious; the holding independently rejects the categorical inconsistency objection without approving every change.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), Part III-A, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight-Justice controlling majority. Stone-Zsela joins the judgment and Parts I, II, IV and VI but does not join Parts III-A, III-B or V. Parts III-A and III-B are independently sufficient answers to the asserted prior-policy barrier; they are not combined into a narrower rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** Good Samaritan permits reasoned change within lawful authority while requiring statutory fit, adequate explanation and attention to legitimate reliance. Neither statutory age nor litigation impetus supplies a categorical exclusion. The OCC formally proposed its interpretation on March 3, 1995, received 112 comment letters, and explained its response and grounds in the final rule published February 9, 1996. That process differs from a position invented by litigation counsel. The Court relies on the agency's stated clarification of uncertainty, not a judicially invented economic or reliance finding.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Those procedural facts do not certify compliance with every rulemaking statute. Permission to change an interpretation does not itself authorize retrospective alteration of completed conduct's legal consequences.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Good Samaritan permits explained interpretive change while retaining authority, statutory-fit and reliance limits. Bowen continues to reject automatic weight for unsupported litigation rationales.

##### Did the earlier letters establish clear official guidance contrary to this rule?

**Holding:** Independently, the identified 1964 and 1988 letters did not establish clear, settled official guidance contrary to the current interpretation; their existence does not itself make the formal rule an unexplained reversal of a binding prior position. This is a second independently sufficient answer to the claimed prior-policy obstacle.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), Part III-B, joined by the same seven other Associate Justices; eight-Justice majority. Stone-Zsela does not join this part.

**Controlling explanation:** The 1964 informal automobile-financing statement listed late charges as noninterest items, while the 1988 deputy-counsel response used a home-state-definition approach. Their authority and context differ, and they conflict on whether federal or state law defines the category. They therefore do not establish the clear prior interpretation asserted. The formal rule's explanation that it resolves uncertainty supplies its own reason for a federal definition. Good Samaritan requires attention to agency reasons without treating every informal formulation as binding; Bowen's rejection of unsupported litigation positions does not erase the separately published rule's own explanation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** This conclusion concerns these documents' legal force, not a factual finding about any borrower's actual reliance or a general rule that informal agency materials never matter.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Good Samaritan's reason-giving requirement remains; Bowen's bar against unsupported litigation rationales is distinguished from the independently explained formal interpretation.

##### May a court use the current authoritative interpretation for these antecedent transactions?

**Holding:** In determining statutory meaning for antecedent transactions that lacked clear prior agency guidance, a court may use the current authoritative interpretation without backdating the rule's effective date. The materially different case of a later interpretation displacing clear earlier guidance remains reserved.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), Part V, joined by the same seven other Associate Justices; eight-Justice majority. Stone-Zsela does not join this part.

**Controlling explanation:** The issue is what section 85 means for these transactions. Part III-B establishes that the cited letters supplied no clear contrary guidance; Part III-A's general allowance for reasoned change would not alone establish retrospective permissibility. Bowen bars unauthorized retroactive legislative rulemaking but does not require a court to disregard current authoritative statutory interpretation in these circumstances. An objection based on displacement of a clear prior interpretation could have substance and is not decided.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The April 1, 1996 effective date remains unchanged. The rule has no special transition or retroactivity clause; no settled judgment is reopened, no actual-reliance finding is made, and no waiver or preservation ruling resolves the objection. The Court gives no universal permission to alter completed conduct's legal consequences.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Bowen's prohibition of unauthorized retroactive legislative rulemaking remains; qualified judicial use of current statutory interpretation without clear prior guidance is distinguished.

##### Does merits deference resolve independent section 43 procedure objections?

**Holding:** Deference to the substantive statutory interpretation does not excuse an established procedural violation or adjudicate every independent rule-validity challenge. Section 43's coverage, advance procedure, final-publication duty and differently scoped exceptions remain operative, without a finding of applicability, compliance, violation, preservation or an exception here.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), Part VI, joined by all eight other Justices; unanimous controlling qualification.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 43 covers agency opinion letters or interpretive rules, issued on request or on the agency's initiative, concluding that federal law preempts application to a national bank of state community-reinvestment, consumer-protection, fair-lending or intrastate-branch law, and the specified section 36(f)(1)(A)(ii) determinations. Subsection (a) requires advance Federal Register notice describing the issues and each state law, at least thirty days for written comment, and consideration of comments received. Subsection (b) separately requires publication of the specified final action.

Subsection (c)(1) exempts the entire section where essentially identical preemption issues were previously resolved by courts or addressed in an agency opinion or interpretive rule, or where a response concerns a request with no significant legal basis for a preemption determination. Subsection (c)(2) exempts the entire section for materials prepared for judicial proceedings, submission to Congress or a Member, or intragovernmental use. Subsection (c)(3) exempts only advance subsection (a) procedure: it requires a written necessity determination to avoid a serious and imminent threat to a national bank's safety and soundness, or concerns an opinion or rule connected to acquisition of banks in statutory default or danger of default, or an acquisition receiving FDIC assistance under section 1823(c). Final publication remains required under that exception.

Institutional default requires official custodial appointment. Danger of default requires the specified agency advice or determination of inability to meet demands or obligations, or depletion of substantially all capital, with no reasonable prospect of recovery without federal assistance. Borrower delinquency supplies none of these institutional conditions. Published proposals and received comments do not prove every statutory requirement; interpretive form, later use in litigation, section 93a authority and lack of clear prior guidance prove neither exemption nor compliance. Distinguishing substantive classification from its preemptive consequence does not categorically remove section 43.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No independently dispositive procedural defect is established; missing documentary proof creates no presumption for either party or alternative disposition. The Court does not decide every paragraph of the rule or every potential procedural challenge.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The California judgment is affirmed, 9-0, without agency or evidentiary remand, a special transition rule or newly ordered agency action. The confined section 85 classification and state-law displacement appear under Business and Commercial Law, National-bank interest classification and interstate permission.

#### Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB

**Docket or dockets:** No. 96-795.

**Decided:** 1998-01-26.

##### The announced reasonable-doubt polling threshold is permissible

**Holding:** The Board may require an employer to possess a good-faith reasonable doubt, supported by objective considerations, of an incumbent union's majority support before conducting a safeguarded poll. Using the same prerequisite for polling, withdrawing recognition and seeking a Board election is not, by itself, irrational or inconsistent with the Act; objective doubt is uncertainty about majority support, not proof that the union has lost it.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's Part II, joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies five direct votes.

**Limits and questions not reached:** This threshold is permissible, not obligatory for every future Board; §§7 and 8(c) supply no unconditional right to poll. Existing safeguards require a majority-claim inquiry purpose, its communication, assurances against reprisal, secret ballot, no unfair practices or coercive atmosphere, and advance time-and-place notice. Certification, contract and representation protections retain their distinct predicates.

##### The presented challenge reaches the standard actually applied

**Holding:** A challenge expressly directed to the amount of evidence demanded before polling fairly includes whether the Board applied its announced reasonable-doubt standard to the order under review. That inquiry reaches the associated whole-record application here; it creates no general permission to add an independent factual question merely because merits briefs discuss it.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, supplies five direct votes.

##### An announced doubt standard cannot silently become minority proof

**Holding:** Under NLRA §§10(e)–(f) and APA §706, the Board's finding must be supported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole under the substantive standard actually governing the case. When the announced predicate is objective reasonable doubt, the agency cannot sustain its order by effectively demanding proof approaching actual loss of majority support, or silently convert recurring evidentiary outcomes into a different substantive or proof rule.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's Parts III–IV have Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, providing five direct votes for this framework and its application.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The agency’s §10(c) preponderance burden differs from whole-record substantial-evidence review. Lawful expressly adopted presumptions remain possible; expertise cannot silently alter the governing standard, and no systematic-manipulation finding or automatic credit for unverified reports follows.

##### This record requires denial of enforcement

**Holding:** The finding that Allentown lacked objective reasonable doubt when it announced the poll is unsupported by substantial evidence on the whole record. The enforcement judgment must therefore be reversed with instructions to deny enforcement of the challenged Board order; the record does not call for another agency weighing under the same or a newly demanding threshold.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's Part IV has Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, supplying five direct votes for the application and complete mandate.

**Material application:** The individually accepted reports together with Marsh, Bloch and steward Mohr establish objective uncertainty without a headcount of actual minority support. Their cumulative relation to the continuing workforce controls; the later 19–13 poll provides no retroactive justification.

##### The separate remedy objection remains outside review

**Holding:** Section 10(e) bars an objection not urged before the Board unless extraordinary circumstances excuse the omission. The separate objection to the bargaining order's explanation and decertification bar was not preserved, and no such excuse is established; that objection is not reviewed.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's opinion: Part V has all eight Associate Justices. Stone-Zsela agrees only in leaving the objection unreviewed.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The facial challenge is rejected by Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The different majority of Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas reverses enforcement and directs its denial. No second weighing of the unchanged record, retroactively higher threshold, new poll or permanent decertification follows.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Auciello's accepted-offer cutoff remains intact. Its statutory limits, distinction between circumstantial doubt and actual minority, and separate §10(e) requirement are applied; it had not resolved incumbent polling or the weight of these reports.
- Fall River supplies separate review of rational Board policy and supported factual application. Its continuity and successor principles are not reopened, and its rebuttable presumption does not become proof of employee preference.
- Universal Camera supplies whole-record substantial-evidence review, including contrary evidence and the examiner's actual findings, while retaining deference between fairly conflicting views. The Court applies that standard to this unsupported finding rather than replacing it with de novo factfinding.
- Gissel retains its protection of noncoercive expression and its attention to the employment setting. Its reservation concerning mandatory permission for reasonable inquiry is preserved; the Court derives no unconditional polling entitlement from it.
- Hubbard's distinction between supporting arguments and new questions is applied to the wording and challenged order here. No exception based merely on merits briefing is adopted.
- Struksnes's procedural safeguards and the additional advance-notice requirement remain operative. Their satisfaction does not eliminate the separately permissible incumbent-polling prerequisite.

#### John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank, 510 U.S. 86

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-1074.

**Decided:** 1993-12-13.

For the other questions in John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank, 1993-12-13, No. 92-1074, see Labor and Employment — The guaranteed-benefit exclusion operates by component; Labor and Employment — Insurance regulation does not displace ERISA's express direction.

##### An agency interpretation cannot erase the qualified exclusion

**Holding:** The Labor Department's reading excluding general-account assets categorically cannot control because it exceeds the permissible statutory range by eliminating §401(b)(2)'s component limitation. The Court does not hold that inconsistency or presentation in litigation invariably defeats deference, and leaves the general degree of deference due agency litigation positions unresolved.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, and Souter. These six adopt the statutory-limit ground; the dissenters separately reject the categorical agency position without adopting the Court's entire investment-risk analysis.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC: applied at the limit imposed by the statute; no categorical exception for inconsistent interpretations or litigation positions is adopted.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The contract contains guarantees and a residual floor; those do not guarantee the disputed free-funds benefits. A guaranteed reasonable return and fixed conversion protection are indicators, not an exhaustive mandatory formula. No breach, loss or remedy follows from status alone, and no categorical anti-deference rule for inconsistency or litigation positions is adopted.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Second Circuit's relevant judgment is affirmed, 6–3. Asset status and applicable fiduciary functions remain distinct from breach, causation, loss and relief, which must be addressed in further proceedings; no monetary or equitable award is made.

#### Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-1935.
**Decided:** March 6, 1995.

##### The company-amendment clause satisfies §402(b)(3)

**Holding:** In this employer-sponsored welfare plan, a written reservation of amendment to the Company provides an amendment procedure and identifies an authorized person under ERISA §402(b)(3), because §3(9) includes corporations and applicable corporate law identifies actors empowered to bind the Company. The statute does not invariably require a list of individual officers; actual additional procedures adopted by the plan remain binding.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden, 503 U.S. 318 — ordinary legal meaning informs ERISA where the enacted text supplies no contrary rule; no employee-classification test is imported into amendment authority.
- John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank (December 13, 1993) — status and function remain separate from breach and relief; procedural sufficiency establishes no final amendment or immunity.

##### A sufficient procedure still requires authorized corporate action

**Holding:** A legally sufficient reservation of amendment power does not establish adoption of the challenged amendment. The lower courts must determine who had express or implied authority, whether an authorized actor approved the 1983 cutoff, and, if necessary, whether later ratification was legally effective, including its timing and consequences, before entering a conforming benefits judgment.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, 508 U.S. 248 — the reviewed §502(a)(3) compensation limit remains; it does not bar benefits due under §502(a)(1)(B).
- John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank — the distinction between legal status and ultimate liability is preserved; no new breach or remedy follows merely from identifying amendment power.

**Limits and questions not reached:** ERISA §402(b)(3), 29 U.S.C. §1102(b)(3), requires every plan to provide a procedure for amending it and for identifying the persons with amendment authority. Section 3(9), §1002(9), includes a corporation among persons, as well as an individual, partnership, joint venture, mutual company, joint-stock company, trust, estate, unincorporated organization, association, and employee organization. The plan Constitution reserves to the Company the right at any time and from time to time to modify or amend any or all provisions, in whole or in part, and separately reserves termination. Delaware §141(a) places corporate business and affairs under the board’s management or direction except as otherwise provided by that chapter or the certificate of incorporation. No charter, bylaw, delegation, or approval is established as a fact here. Section 402(a)(1)’s written-instrument and named-fiduciary requirements remain distinct from sponsor amendment authority. Sections 1022 and 1024 retain actual summary, disclosure, and instrument-access duties; an SPD is not proof of authorized amendment. Section 502(a)(1)(B) benefits enforcement is distinct from §502(a)(3)’s appropriate-equitable-relief category.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Third Circuit is reversed and the case remanded to identify authorized corporate actors, determine actual approval and legally effective ratification, including timing and consequences, and enter a conforming benefits judgment. Actual termination and amounts are not decided; rejected lifetime vesting is not automatically reopened.

#### Lockheed Corp. v. Spink, 517 U.S. 882

**Docket or dockets:** 95-809
**Decided:** June 10, 1996

##### Does sponsor adoption of pension amendments create fiduciary prohibited-transaction liability?

**Holding:** Designing and adopting these pension-plan amendments is sponsor conduct, not ERISA fiduciary conduct. Subject to §408's statutory exceptions, §406(a)(1) requires a fiduciary to cause a transaction the fiduciary knows or should know is prohibited; employer party-in-interest status alone is insufficient, and actual implementation must be assessed separately under §3(21)(A)'s functional terms, including asset-disposition control without an additional discretion requirement.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Ginsburg), Part I, unanimous; Part IV-A's independent-claims directions likewise unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Amendment power establishes neither compliance with amendment procedures nor lawful administration. Surplus remains plan property. The sponsor-capacity rule forecloses §404 liability resting solely on design or adoption, while actually preserved fiduciary-implementation and §403(c)(1) anti-inurement grounds retain their own predicates and remedies.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The reviewed adverse judgment is reversed and remanded, 9–0 overall. The theory resting solely on amendment is dismissed; preserved independent Count III alternatives receive ordinary consideration without a new allegation, automatic liability or unconditional dismissal of Count III. Count V, refusal of offensive collateral estoppel and withdrawn Count IV remain outside the relief. Appellate fees are vacated for reconsideration because their stated grounds included the rejected substantive rulings; the fee rationale has eight Associate joins, with Stone-Zsela agreeing in that disposition only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Curtiss-Wright's sponsor-amendment role is applied to a pension plan while its procedural requirements remain distinct. Varity separates sponsorship from administration and preserves its qualified equitable-remedy rule. John Hancock keeps asset status, function, breach and remedy separate. Mertens and Peacock retain the distinct predicate and remedy requirements for any preserved claim.

##### Does employer benefit from a required release alone make enhanced retirement payments prohibited transfers?

**Holding:** Genuine enhanced retirement benefits under an otherwise lawful plan are not prohibited transactions under §406(a)(1)(D) merely because employment-related releases benefit the employer. The rule assumes fiduciary implementation for this inquiry and does not immunize sham transfers, kickbacks, an independently unlawful plan or an unenforceable release.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; seven. Souter and Breyer would reserve the question, not decide the payments unlawful.

**Material application:** The accepted complaint allegations describe participant benefits rather than diversion solely because releases have employer value. No sham or kickback is alleged in the decided issue.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No §408 exemption or general net-benefit defense is created. Independent participation, funding, vesting, loyalty and anti-inurement duties survive. Section 406(b)'s distinct prohibitions and particular waiver enforceability under the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act or other law are not decided; no loss, deception or hidden diversion is found.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Keystone's transfer of property to discharge an enforceable funding obligation is distinguished; valuable consideration alone does not equate that transaction with these participant benefits.

##### Does OBRA require credit for Spink's earlier lawful exclusion from pension participation?

**Holding:** OBRA §§9201–9202 do not require credit for service during Spink's earlier lawful nonparticipation. Section 9204(a)(1) applies the new accrual protections to plan years beginning on or after January 1, 1988 and employees with an hour of service in a covered year; a smaller total benefit caused by earlier uncredited service is not itself a reduced accrual rate during a covered year.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's Part III, all eight Associate Justices; Stone-Zsela agrees in the judgment only. Dismissal of Counts I–II is restored, 9–0.

**Material application:** The relevant plan year began December 25, 1988. The later calculation date does not turn prior lawful nonparticipation into credited service or establish a separate violation earlier in that calendar year.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 9204(b) separately applies §9203's participation amendments to covered plan years and service performed on or after January 1, 1988; that added service-date condition does not enter subsection (a). Under §9204(a)(2), benefits pursuant to and persons covered by employer–employee-representative collective agreements ratified before March 1, 1986 use the first plan year beginning on or after the earlier of January 1, 1990 or the later of January 1, 1988 and termination of the last qualifying agreement, disregarding extensions after February 28, 1986; no allegation invokes that transition. Section 9204(c) permits formal amendment to await the first plan year beginning on or after January 1, 1989 only if interim operations comply and amendment covers that interval retroactively. #### Varity Corp. v. Howe, 516 U.S. 489

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-1471.
**Decided:** March 19, 1996.

##### The integrated benefit-security presentation was fiduciary administration

**Holding:** ERISA §3(21)(A) attaches fiduciary responsibility to the particular exercise of discretionary plan administration, including plan information communicated in that capacity. Varity exercised that function when it used administrative authority, benefit comparisons and linked assurances of benefit security to induce the class's transfer; employer status, corporate forecasting or participant perception alone would not suffice.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: six Justices.

**Material application:** Current-benefit descriptions and deliberately linked security assurances formed one administrative presentation. Warnings that future conditions depended on MCC's success and that changes might occur did not displace the unchallenged trial findings that the combined presentation was knowingly misleading.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Every employment statement or corporate-solvency prediction is not thereby fiduciary. Corporate restructuring and plan-amendment powers remain distinct from administration.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** John Hancock's particular-function inquiry is applied while keeping status, breach and remedy separate. Curtiss-Wright preserves sponsor amendment authority but distinguishes retained power from authorized exercise; it creates no immunity for this administrative act.

##### Deliberate disloyal deception violates §404

**Holding:** An administrator acting as a fiduciary violates §404(a)(1)'s loyalty duty by knowingly and significantly misleading participants about benefit security to induce surrender of existing plan interests for the employer's benefit. Varity's found deception and the class's reliance meet that rule despite reserved lawful amendment or termination powers.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part II, joined by the same six Justices as Part I.

**Limits and questions not reached:** This intentional-deception holding decides no general duty to volunteer information, answer every inquiry, disclose every forecast or avoid negligent misstatement. It supplies no substantive rule for the ten retirees' different assignment claim and no permanent-benefits promise.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Firestone's trust-law guidance explains loyalty within ERISA's statutory function, without replacing that threshold. Curtiss-Wright's amendment rule supplies no permission to deceive participants into surrendering present membership.

##### Section 502(a)(3) permits appropriate individual equitable redress for fiduciary breach

**Holding:** Sections 409 and 502(a)(2) do not impose a categorical plan-only bar on §502(a)(3)'s express authorization of appropriate individual equitable relief for a §404 violation. Relief must be traditionally equitable; where another ERISA provision adequately remedies the injury, further equity normally is unnecessary and inappropriate, without an absolute rule governing every alternative pleading.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part III, joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: five Justices. Stone-Zsela's stricter adequate-remedy condition supports the class's judgment but is not the Court's rule.

**Material application:** The class sought restoration after fiduciary deception procured surrender of membership. Rejection of its lifetime-benefits contract theory established neither an adequate §502(a)(1)(B) remedy for that injury nor an automatic bar to restoration.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not classify each monetary item as equitable, including replacement-insurance premiums or all unpaid benefits. It neither revives the rejected $7.6 million and $712,332 compensatory awards nor permits general compensation through trust law. Section 510 and estoppel remain unadopted alternatives.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The clarified restoration decree is affirmed. Reinstatement confers only the rights of the M-F plan as it now exists. Past entitlement may be adjusted for intervening events, with relevant additional evidence and a conforming decree. Future lawful changes remain possible subject to applicable law and preserved retaliation or discrimination objections. No new payment amount or immutable lifetime entitlement is entered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Russell's plan-centered §409 construction is preserved while its distinct §502(a)(3) question is answered. Mertens's equitable-category limit applies; its reserved antecedent action issue is not treated as an earlier action bar. Peacock's statutory-or-plan-violation requirement is met by the proved §404 breach; its later new-debtor jurisdictional holding does not govern this original action. Firestone supplies guidance within enacted limits.

##### The ten retirees' independent assignment-breach ground was not adequately challenged

**Holding:** The categorical plan-only objection does not defeat the ten retirees' individual equitable recovery, and their distinct lower-court assignment-breach determination remains undisturbed because the petition did not sufficiently present a challenge to it. Affirmance decides no new substantive assignment rule, precise monetary classification or categorical unavailability of §502(a)(1)(B) to those retirees.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part IV, joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: five Justices. Stone-Zsela provides a sixth judgment vote on the distinct assignment component without a ground.

**Material application:** The ten had retired before the purported assignment and did not consent. Their unauthorized-administration ground did not depend on the class's presentation or voluntary departure; arguments concerning misrepresentation and lifetime contractual benefits did not identify error in that separate ground.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the clarified decree for this group on the same existing-plan and adjustment terms stated above, without entering a new amount or reviving terminated-employee, punitive, lifetime-contract, settled-disability or pension claims.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Curtiss-Wright does not transform an amendment reservation into proof of an authorized assignment or permanent vesting; Mertens's monetary-category limits remain without a new classification decision.

## Standards and Tests selection: Concrete personal interests and procedural citizen-suit standing

### Concrete personal interests and procedural citizen-suit standing

**Governing question:** Article III standing for a citizen-suit plaintiff alleging a deprivation of procedure, including ESA §11(g) enforcement.

**Current rule:** A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction must establish a concrete and particularized, actual or imminent injury; a causal connection fairly traceable to the defendant's challenged conduct rather than to independent third parties' own decisions; and a likelihood, not mere speculation, that the requested relief will redress the injury. Authorization for any person to sue does not supply standing for only the public's undifferentiated interest in lawful administration. A procedural right must protect the plaintiff's own threatened concrete interest; when that connection exists, the plaintiff need not prove that the required procedure will certainly alter the ultimate agency decision. Aesthetic, recreational or professional wildlife interests may qualify, but past visits and indefinite return intentions without concrete plans or another supported basis for imminent renewed exposure do not establish threatened interference with personal use. At summary judgment the plaintiff must support challenged standing elements with specific facts by affidavit or other evidence, with favorable reasonable inferences available; allegations cannot replace missing evidence.

**Authority by component:**
- Stage-specific evidentiary requirement and existing injury, traceability and redressability elements: Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, “1. Article III elements must be supported at the litigation stage”, June 12, 1992; Scalia Part II, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas (five).
- Prospective personal-use boundary: Lujan, “2. Indefinite return intentions and general species interests do not establish threatened personal injury here,” June 12, 1992; Scalia Part III-A, same five-Justice opinion; Stone separately supports the record-specific conclusion.
- Citizen-suit and procedural-right boundary: Lujan, “3. Citizen-suit authorization does not dispense with a threatened concrete interest,” June 12, 1992; Scalia Part IV, same five Justices.

**Present operation effective:** Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, June 12, 1992, for the identified citizen-suit and procedural-right clarification; ordinary Article III elements and evidentiary burdens are retained.

**Exceptions and limits:** No purchased-ticket or particular-document requirement is adopted. The redressability application to absent agencies, binding force of the challenged regulation upon those agencies, effect of particular funding changes and ESA §7(a)(2)'s foreign reach are not controlling holdings.

**Holdings navigation:** Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992) — Standing and personal injury.

## Standards and Tests selection: Constitutional review of census adjustment methodology

### Constitutional review of census adjustment methodology

**Governing question:** Whether to supplement a nationwide enumeration with a proposed post-enumeration survey for population-based representation.

**Current rule:** The Secretary's choice must bear a reasonable relationship to enumerating the population and securing population-based representation. Courts enforce that boundary while respecting the delegated methodological judgment. A genuine enumeration effort and explained comparison addressing geographic accuracy and uncertainty do not become unconstitutional merely because another method improves some accuracy measures. Secretary title, adviser numbers or technical disagreement alone do not supply a reason; the constitutional inquiry does not import the intrastate districting requirement of showing that population deviations are essential to a legitimate objective.

**Authority by component:**

- Constitutional methodology standard and reservations: II. This nonadjustment decision satisfies the constitutional standard and III. Sampling authority remains open; relief follows the constitutional holding, 1996-03-20; Stevens Parts II–III, all nine Justices.

**Present operation effective:** Wisconsin v. City of New York, 1996-03-20.

**Open question:** Rejection of a duty to use the proposed adjustment decides neither whether the Constitution prohibits it nor whether §195’s apportionment exception prohibits sampling or instead removes sampling from that provision’s otherwise mandatory feasible-method direction. Section 141(a) does not alone settle that interaction.

**Exceptions and limits:** No perfect-count presumption, numerical tolerance or automatic superiority of enumeration is created. Proven discrimination, unauthorized methods and unsupported reasons retain their own review. The agency guideline favoring enumeration is not constitutionally prescribed.

**Related current rules:** “Reviewability of the Secretary’s completed census determination” retains distinct APA finality and actual-reasons requirements; constitutional reasonableness is not a substitute for every APA requirement.

**Holdings navigation:** Wisconsin v. City of New York; Oklahoma v. City of New York; Department of Commerce v. City of New York, 517 U.S. 1.

## Public prior-term writing selection: John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co.

Source: terms/OT1993/output/OT_1993CHUNK1.md; complete copied entry: ../sources/chunk4_a/public_john_hancock_mutual_life_insurance_co.md

### John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank, No. 92-1074

Merits decision, 1993-12-13

Argued October 12, 1993. On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit, 970 F.2d 1138 (1992), which reversed in relevant part summary judgment treating the contract as exempt. The questions concern plan-asset and fiduciary status for the free funds under Group Annuity Contract No. 50, the insurance exclusion and state insurance regulation, and the claimed controlling agency interpretation.

Reported at 510 U.S. 86.

The Court affirms the Second Circuit's relevant fiduciary-status judgment, including rejection of the asserted insurance and agency obstacles, 6–3. Justice Ginsburg delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, and Souter. Those six adopt the entire component-specific status, insurance, and agency analysis and support affirmance. Justice Thomas dissents, joined by Justices O'Connor and Kennedy; they would remand for the contract inquiry under their different construction. Their rejection of a blanket exemption and the overbroad agency view does not join the Court's investment-risk analysis.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and all eight Associate Justices participated at argument and decision.

Under ERISA §401(b)(2), a guaranteed-benefit policy itself is a plan asset, while the insurer's underlying assets are not deemed plan assets solely because it issued the policy to the extent the policy genuinely guarantees the amount of benefits. The disputed nonguaranteed free-funds component is not excluded: its plan-asset character subjects Hancock to fiduciary obligations to the extent it exercises authority or control over management or disposition of those assets under §3(21)(A), without deciding breach, causation, loss, or remedy.

Ginsburg's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, and Souter. Six Justices adopt the component-specific construction and its application to the disputed free funds.

The Court holds that an insurer cannot remove nonguaranteed plan investments from ERISA merely by placing them in a general account. Section 401(b)(2) confines its exclusion to the extent that the policy supplies benefits whose amount the insurer guarantees. That language requires attention to the particular component, not a choice between exempting or regulating every asset associated with a mixed contract. United Benefit supplies the useful distinction between investment risk borne by the purchaser and a genuine insurance guarantee; ERISA's own text fixes this exclusion. This contract includes guaranteed benefits and a residual floor, but those features do not guarantee the amount of benefits payable from the disputed free funds. A guaranteed reasonable return and fixed conversion protection are significant indicators, not an exhaustive mandatory formula. Commingling, administrative difficulty, and the possibility of later annuity purchases cannot erase the statutory limitation. The Court therefore affirms the status ruling while leaving the separate fiduciary-function, breach, loss, and relief questions for their proper application below.

SEC v. United Benefit Life Insurance Co.: used for the distinction between a genuine guarantee and retained investment risk; the ERISA text supplies the governing exclusion. Mertens v. Hewitt Associates: kept separate; its treatment of available equitable categories does not decide whether these funds are plan assets or whether Hancock committed a breach.

McCarran-Ferguson does not bar application of ERISA's express insurance-related fiduciary and guaranteed-benefit provisions to this general-account contract. ERISA's insurance-saving clause preserves compatible state insurance regulation, but does not make a conflicting state obligation controlling over ERISA itself; a concrete conflict must be assessed as such rather than treated as a categorical exemption for all insurer assets.

Ginsburg's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, and Souter. This six-Justice ground rejects the asserted state-insurance obstacle to affirmance.

The Court holds that state insurance supervision and federal fiduciary duties can operate together. McCarran-Ferguson protects state insurance laws against displacement by federal laws that do not specifically relate to insurance. ERISA expressly addresses insurance, including this very exclusion, so that limitation does not surrender the subject exclusively to the States. The preemption and insurance-saving clauses must likewise be read together. Metropolitan Life and FMC recognize the coexistence of ERISA and state regulation where a plan purchases insurance; the saving clause does not supersede an operative provision of ERISA itself. Silkwood supplies the ordinary distinction between compatible regulation and an actual obstacle to federal law. Hancock's concern for pooled-account regulation therefore requires attention to a concrete conflicting obligation, not elimination of the federal statute at the outset. Fabe addressed a different federal-priority conflict and supplies no blanket immunity here. The Court preserves compatible state regulation and rejects the asserted categorical bar without deciding every possible future conflict.

Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. Massachusetts: applied to the coexistence of ERISA and state insurance regulation; no blanket withdrawal of federal obligations follows. FMC Corp. v. Holliday: applied to the coexistence of ERISA and state insurance regulation; no blanket withdrawal of federal obligations follows. Silkwood v. Kerr-McGee Corp.: applied for the distinction between compatible state regulation and a conflicting obstacle to federal law. United States Department of Treasury v. Fabe: distinguished because its federal-priority and policyholder-protection issue is different; its holding remains intact.

The Labor Department's reading excluding general-account assets categorically cannot control because it exceeds the permissible statutory range by eliminating §401(b)(2)'s component limitation. The Court does not hold that inconsistency or presentation in litigation invariably defeats deference, and leaves the general degree of deference due agency litigation positions unresolved.

Ginsburg's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, and Souter. These six adopt the statutory-limit ground; the dissenters separately reject the categorical agency position without adopting the Court's entire investment-risk analysis.

The Court holds that deference does not permit an agency to replace a limited statutory exclusion with an unlimited one. Chevron makes the statute's permissible meaning the boundary of administrative interpretation. The Department's 1975 bulletin addressed prohibited transactions; it did not establish how the qualified guaranteed-benefit definition could exempt every general-account asset from fiduciary obligations. The later interpretation cannot solve that problem merely by expanding the bulletin's asserted reach. Its reading effectively treats a policy containing some guaranteed benefits as sufficient to exclude all associated funds, leaving no work for the words limiting the exclusion to the extent of the guarantees. Reliance on general-account practice does not cure that textual defect. The Court therefore rejects this interpretation at the statutory boundary. Its discussion of the Department's changing positions reinforces the need to identify what the guidance actually addressed, but does not create a rule that every inconsistent interpretation fails. The broader treatment of agency views first advanced in litigation remains open.

Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC: applied at the limit imposed by the statute; no categorical exception for inconsistent interpretations or litigation positions is adopted.

No precedent is overruled. 

Effective December 13, 1993, the guaranteed-benefit exclusion operates only to the extent of genuine guarantees; this contract's nonguaranteed free funds remain plan assets despite general-account placement. The policy itself and the insurer's underlying assets remain distinct. Fiduciary responsibility follows the statutory asset-and-function inquiry, while breach and remedies remain separate. Compatible state insurance regulation survives, actual conflict yields to ERISA, and the categorical agency exclusion exceeds the statutory boundary. Mertens's remedial categories and Fabe's different insurance-priority holding remain unchanged. No universal guarantee formula, rule making illiquidity alone dispositive, general denial of deference to changed agency views, or remedy entitlement is adopted.

Thomas dissents, joined by O'Connor and Kennedy. They would remand for consideration of the 1977 amendment under their reading of a policy that provides for guaranteed benefits. That phrase can cover arrangements making provision for future guaranteed payments before every benefit amount has vested; it need not require the insurer to guarantee an aggregate investment return at every stage. But the statutory limitation still excludes only qualifying components. They therefore reject a blanket exemption for all general-account assets. The amendment ended automatic new guarantees and allowed nonguaranteed payments, so its actual operation requires assessment under their construction. Their position is a contract-specific remand, not a finding that every free fund is exempt. They also reject the agency's categorical exclusion and claimed entitlement to Chevron deference. That common negative conclusion does not make them join the Court's different guarantee-and-risk analysis. No member of either side decides fiduciary breach or the amount or form of relief.

**Source Notes:** The contract, record, and chronology are supported by [the official report, 510 U.S. 86](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510086/usrep510086.pdf) and the Second Circuit opinion, 970 F.2d 1138. The contract contains some guarantees and a residual floor; the disputed component is not accurately described as a contract with no guarantees of any kind. The 1977 amendment and its alleged operation are kept distinct from new judicial factfinding. 

The Second Circuit's relevant judgment is affirmed. Breach, causation, loss, and any monetary or equitable relief remain for the district court. Fiduciary consequences extend only to the assets and functions ERISA and the actual contract reach.

The case returns for further district-court proceedings on the fiduciary claims under the affirmed judgment. Fiduciary status does not establish breach, causation, loss, or a monetary or equitable award. No further hearing date is fixed.

### Spencer v. Kemna, No. 96-7171

Decision after plenary review, March 3, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 12, 1997. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Eighth Circuit, which affirmed dismissal of the §2254 petition. Spencer challenges the September 1992 parole revocation, not the original burglary and stealing convictions. He filed on April 1, 1993 while imprisoned under the revocation; the challenged sentence expired October 16, 1993. Review concerns statutory custody, continuing Article III injury, mootness exceptions and the asserted effect of possible civil-remedy limits.

All nine Justices participate.

#### Judgment

**Affirm dismissal as moot, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens.

The dismissal rests on absence of a continuing Article III controversy, not failure of custody when the petition was filed or a decision on the revocation’s merits. Stevens would reverse and remand the live petition for its remaining lawful adjudication.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg | Affirms mootness dismissal | O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer join all parts; Stone-Zsela joins Part II’s actual-continuing-injury ground and mootness result, but agrees only with Part II’s capable-of-repetition and litigation-delay dispositions and Parts I and III’s ancillary dispositions, without those additional rationales |
| Dissent | Stevens | — | Would reverse and remand | Recognizes the particular official adverse adjudication’s continuing reputational consequence; does not decide the revocation merits or an affirmative section 1983 exception |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Custody at filing and a continuing controversy are separate requirements

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 2254’s custody requirement is satisfied when the petitioner is held under the challenged revocation at filing; later expiration does not retroactively defeat that threshold. Article III nonetheless requires a concrete continuing injury after the challenged sentence expires.

**Authority:** Part I is joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, seven direct votes. Stone-Zsela agrees with the custody disposition without an additional ground; Stevens also accepts custody at filing.

**Controlling explanation:** Spencer was imprisoned under this revocation on April 1, 1993. The District Court’s alternative suggestion that expiration removed statutory custody is incorrect. The later seven-year imprisonment arises from an unrelated conviction, rather than the unexpired consecutive aggregate addressed in Garlotte. A timely filing therefore satisfies the statutory entry condition without itself supplying an enduring constitutional controversy. Lujan and Arizonans require an actual personal interest throughout adjudication, not simply an interest that existed when suit began.

##### II. The asserted collateral consequences and exceptions do not keep this petition live

**Holding and operative rule:** After this parole-revocation sentence expires, collateral injury is not presumed merely from the revocation; the petitioner must show an actual continuing consequence redressable in this action. Speculative future parole, sentencing or evidentiary uses, the possibility of recurrence and litigation delay do not supply that showing on this record.

**Authority:** Part II’s continuing-injury rule and mootness result have eight direct joins: Stone-Zsela and the seven Associates joining the Court opinion. The seven Associates also directly adopt the specific exception and delay applications.

**Controlling explanation:** An attack on a conviction is distinct from this challenge to a revocation of an otherwise valid sentence. Lane supports examining the actual surviving interest, although Spencer, unlike Lane’s respondents, challenges the adverse revocation finding itself. Future parole use depends on discretionary treatment, sentencing use on later legal developments, impeachment on future testimony and permissible use, and other-crimes evidence on additional contingencies. No present mandatory disability or demonstrated imminent adverse use is established. MacArthur’s actual statutory information interest and existing damages action do not make every retained adverse record a live habeas controversy; the Court adopts no categorical exclusion of reputational injury. The capable-of-repetition exception requires both a duration too short for full review and a reasonable expectation that this claimant will again face the action. Neither the asserted delay nor possible injury to others establishes those conditions. State extensions and delayed judicial action cannot create Article III power through fault or estoppel. Expiration occurred by the sentence’s terms, not a newly established strategic cessation. The Court decides no revocation claim, criminal guilt or exoneration.

##### III. An unresolved future civil remedy does not create this habeas controversy

**Holding and operative rule:** Heck and Edwards do not establish an unavoidable favorable-invalidation bar for every former prisoner: their controlling rules expressly reserve that category and persons without a realistic lawful custody-review opportunity. The unresolved scope of a possible later civil action does not create a damages claim in this habeas case or dispense with continuing Article III injury.

**Authority:** Part III is joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, seven direct votes. Stone-Zsela agrees with the disposition of this ancillary contention without adding a ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Heck’s decided prerequisite concerns the specified in-custody, conviction-negating damages claim; Edwards preserves that limit while requiring genuinely independent injuries to be classified by their consequences. Neither decision supplies the universal former-prisoner bar assumed by Spencer’s argument. A claim whose success does not necessarily invalidate the revocation would also require its own analysis. The Court need not choose an affirmative former-prisoner exception to reject the asserted inevitable bar. No damages claim is pleaded here, and possible difficulty obtaining another remedy does not empower the Court to decide an otherwise moot habeas petition. The existing reservations remain open rather than becoming either a categorical bar or a newly adjudicated exception.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Lane is applied for the continuing-interest inquiry while its unchallenged-revocation posture is distinguished; no automatic consequence is inferred for this revocation.
- Lujan and Arizonans preserve an actual personal controversy throughout review. Arizonans’ vacatur remedy does not require clearing a nonexistent intervening merits judgment here.
- Garlotte’s continuous consecutive-sentence aggregate is distinguished from Spencer’s unrelated later custody; custody at filing remains sufficient for the statutory threshold.
- MacArthur preserves supported actual personal information interests without creating a universal reputation-based habeas rule.
- Heck and Edwards retain their bounded invalidation prerequisite and express former-prisoner/realistic-opportunity reservations; no affirmative section 1983 exception or universal bar is added.

#### Law After Decision

A timely custody-based habeas filing does not survive expiration of the challenged sentence without continuing Article III injury or a valid exception. This record establishes neither. The revocation’s merits remain unadjudicated, and the separate civil-remedy questions expressly reserved by Heck and Edwards remain open.

#### Separate Writings

**Stevens, dissenting.** Stevens distinguishes Lane because Spencer challenges the official adverse revocation finding itself, not merely completed custody while leaving that finding untouched. He considers the particular government adjudication and the State’s continuing reliance on the incorporated serious-misconduct report a present personal reputational consequence that invalidation could meaningfully remove. He does not presume every revocation carries statutory disabilities or depend on a future crime, audience reaction or invented damages claim. The written order found condition violations and incorporated a report; it was not an express rape conviction, and the State’s characterization remains distinct. MacArthur’s requirement of a supported personal interest does not, in his view, erase this already imposed official determination. He would remand for unresolved lawful adjudication, without granting a writ, finding procedural wrongdoing or deciding the civil-remedy question.

#### Source Notes

The revocation order, incorporated report, custody dates, claimed procedural defects and later separate sentence are reported in [523 U.S. 1](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-523/pdf/USREPORTS-523-1.pdf). The woman’s allegations, Spencer’s admitted cocaine use and denial of force, the written condition-violation order and the State’s description remain distinct. The underlying reports and complete record are described through published recitations; they establish no criminal conviction for the alleged conduct. No reputation-only submission or damages demand by Spencer is attributed to him. [Lane, 455 U.S. 624](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-455/pdf/USREPORTS-455-624.pdf), supplies the earlier posture and continuing-interest analysis.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The habeas action remains dismissed as moot, without constitutional-merits preclusion. No writ, new revocation hearing, expungement, damages or exoneration is ordered. This Supreme Court review is complete, and no continuing return condition is created.

## Holdings

### Actual Article III standing before contested constitutional merits

**Holding and operative rule:** A federal court may not assume disputed Article III standing to decide these contested constitutional claims about judicial-election financing and optional candidate statements. It must determine, with support appropriate to the litigation stage, whether the plaintiffs have a concrete and particularized, actual or imminent injury fairly traceable to the challenged conduct and likely to be redressed by the prospective relief they seek; the Ninth Circuit's merits judgment is vacated so that determination can be made on the actual pleadings and appropriate record.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the disputed jurisdictional question must be answered before these constitutional merits. *Citizens for a Better Environment v. Steel Co.* forbids deciding contested merits on assumed Article III power. *Lujan v. ## Event

Wisconsin Right to Life, Inc. v. Paradise, No. 98-118. Merits decision, November 24, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, 138 F.3d 1183, No. 97-3668. The appellate opinion issued March 13, 1998; rehearing was denied April 16. The matter was submitted for merits review.

The question is whether WRTL may pursue its pre-enforcement challenge to Wisconsin political-committee registration and enforcement provisions under Article III. The District Court found standing but abstained under Railroad Commission v. Pullman Co.; the Seventh Circuit vacated and directed jurisdictional dismissal. WRTL sought facial invalidity and nonenforcement, while the officials disputed credible threatened injury and the ability of relief against them to address independent private suits.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Seventh Circuit's judgment is vacated, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with the Court's opinion.

## Judgment & Remedy

Vacated and remanded, 7–2. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Opposing: Scalia and Thomas, who would affirm the direction to dismiss without prejudice for want of jurisdiction.

The remand requires consideration of the actual claims, threatened personal injury, proper defendants and proposed relief. It neither affirms the District Court's standing finding nor decides whether Pullman abstention was warranted. No Wisconsin provision is invalidated and no injunction issues.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

The complete Court-opinion coalition is Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Scalia and Thomas do not join that opinion.

## Holdings

### Credible personal injury and effective relief remain necessary

**Operative rule:** A speaker seeking pre-enforcement relief must establish concrete actual or imminent personal injury, fairly traceable to a defendant and likely to be redressed by the requested relief, with support appropriate to the litigation stage; a completed prosecution is unnecessary when an objectively credible threat burdens the speaker's intended expression. Self-censorship, a facial label or the existence of private enforcement provisions alone does not establish those requirements, and a threat from independent private parties must be connected to relief lawfully available against the defendants before the court.

**Authority:** The seven-Justice Opinion of the Court identified in Opinion Topology directly adopts this rule, its qualifications and the application requiring reconsideration; no aggregation of separate writings is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** Lujan supplies the personal-injury, causal and remedial requirements and the proof appropriate to each stage. Babbitt and Virginia v. American Booksellers permit supported pre-enforcement review without waiting for punishment. Maine Right to Life likewise recognizes recurring speech actually stopped by an effective rule's credible application, while retaining dismissal of an official who posed no supported threat. The speaker's decision to remain silent therefore does not answer whether these defendants threaten this speech.

The parties agree that WRTL's ordinary comparative guides and flyers do not require registration. No official has threatened WRTL or required it to register. Those facts weigh against the asserted official threat. The Board's Travis proceedings and private suits concern other speakers and cannot be treated as enforcement against WRTL. They nevertheless require consideration in assessing the actual claimed exposure; they are not erased by broad agreement about the governing speech principles. WRTL must identify its intended communications and the threatened burden.

Bennett requires a supported connection to the defendant's conduct; Pic-A-State permits meaningful partial relief without requiring removal of every independent barrier. Lee v. Harcleroad requires inquiry into an actually applicable duty and present burden. Together they require a defendant-specific assessment rather than either automatic standing or automatic defeat from the existence of an additional private impediment. The Court determines neither whether WRTL has standing nor whether persuasive effects on absent litigants could suffice in a materially different case.

### The actual facial claim must receive the correct jurisdictional inquiry

**Operative rule:** A court evaluating this facial invalidity and nonenforcement request may not dispose of the entire action solely by treating the requested relief as a command prescribing future state-court construction, or by treating predicted failure of an otherwise substantial constitutional claim as lack of jurisdiction. The Seventh Circuit must reconsider the actual injuries, defendants and available relief; contested merits and the District Court's abstention ruling remain for proper consideration only after the necessary Article III inquiry.

**Authority:** The same seven-Justice Opinion of the Court directly adopts the rule, its application and the confined remand.

**Controlling explanation:** A declaration that an enforceable provision is invalid differs from a continuing federal instruction telling state tribunals how to decide hypothetical future cases. The former may remove an identified official constraint when a plaintiff establishes the required personal threat and remedial connection. The latter cannot be supplied merely because a speaker wants assurance about every future state interpretation. The appellate analysis acknowledged WRTL's facial attack but chiefly evaluated the latter kind of decree. It did not adequately resolve the actual request on its own terms.

Steel Co. distinguishes jurisdiction from success on a substantial federal claim, and NAACP requires an actual standing determination before contested election-law merits. A prediction that a narrowing construction would defeat the claim is not that determination. Conversely, the facial form of the claim supplies no missing threatened injury. Baker preserves the limits on a judgment's reach: a declaration against officials does not automatically bind absent candidates or state judges. The Court does not adopt the suggestion that invalidation would itself end all private suits.

The official assurances and independent private enforcement remain substantial objections on remand. They must be assessed against the actual communications, defendants and relief, rather than an assumed demand for universal judicial supervision. Ordinary further proceedings may resolve that inquiry; no new claim, waiver, discovery, amendment or hearing entitlement is created. The Court expresses no view on Wisconsin's constitutional validity, detailed statutory exceptions or the ultimate propriety of Pullman abstention.

## Precedent Treatment

- Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife: applies the controlling personal-interest, traceability, redressability and stage-specific support rules; its noncontrolling absent-agency application is not adopted.
- Babbitt v. United Farm Workers: preserves pre-enforcement review of a credible personal threat without a completed prosecution.
- Virginia v. American Booksellers Ass'n: preserves supported pre-enforcement speech injury; the speech setting does not eliminate Article III requirements.
- Federal Election Commission v. Maine Right to Life Committee, Inc. (September 14, 1998): applies the distinction between a credible effective speech constraint and an unthreatening defendant. Its contextual express-advocacy holding remains governing and is not replaced by an exclusive verbal formula; its unresolved constitutional questions remain open.
- Bennett v. Spear: applies the need for a supported defendant-linked causal and remedial connection without demanding certainty about every later choice.
- Pic-A-State Pennsylvania, Inc. v. Reno: preserves meaningful partial relief from an actual constraint; not every independent barrier must disappear.
- Lee v. Harcleroad: applies the actual-duty, present-burden and proper-defendant inquiry without finding jurisdiction or ordering an automatic hearing.
- Citizens for a Better Environment v. Steel Co.: applies the distinction between Article III power and disputed merits; no exhaustive order for all nonmerits issues is prescribed.
- NAACP, Los Angeles Branch v. Jones (November 5, 1998): applies its requirement of actual standing adjudication before contested election-law merits.
- Baker v. General Motors Corp.: preserves lawful judgment reach and the requirements for binding absent persons; facial relief is not universal preclusion.
- Railroad Commission v. Pullman Co.: leaves its distinct abstention inquiry available for proper consideration after jurisdiction is addressed; its application here is not decided.

## Law After Decision

Pre-enforcement speech challenges remain subject to the existing concrete-injury, causation and redressability requirements. This decision requires the facial invalidity and nonenforcement claims actually presented to be assessed separately from a demand to control all future state adjudication. Wisconsin's provisions remain uninvalidated, and the Court has neither found standing nor rejected every possible basis for it. Official enforcement and independent private enforcement retain their distinct jurisdictional and remedial questions.

## Separate Writings

Scalia dissents, joined by Thomas. They accept that a credible threatened injury may support review before prosecution and that a facial invalidity claim differs from an advisory instruction about future litigation. They would nevertheless affirm dismissal because the existing official and private-enforcement facts already defeat this action. The officials agree that WRTL's comparative guides do not require registration, and no threat directed at WRTL is shown. Proceedings involving different speakers do not supply the missing official connection. Independent private plaintiffs and state judges are absent, so an injunction against the named officials cannot simply stop their actions.

The dissent distinguishes Maine's effective rule credibly reaching ceased speech and Bennett's announced governmental action affecting the plaintiffs. NAACP corrected a court's bypass of disputed standing to reach merits; it does not require another inquiry when the defendant-specific defect has already been addressed. A facial prayer cannot create that missing connection, and hoped-for persuasive effect against nonparties does not itself supply likely redress here. The dissent adopts no universal prosecution prerequisite, no rule that every facial challenge is advisory and no conclusion about Wisconsin's constitutional merits. It would affirm jurisdictional dismissal without prejudice.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Seventh Circuit for consideration of the actual claims and requested relief under the stated Article III requirements, and appropriate treatment of the District Court's abstention disposition thereafter. No injunction, statutory invalidation, state-registration command or direction governing an unpresented state case issues. Further proceedings below remain open; no additional Supreme Court proceeding or deadline is set.

# Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation, Inc. — 1999-01-12

## Event

Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation, Inc., No. 97-930. Argued October 14, 1998; decided January 12, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Tenth Circuit, reviewing 120 F.3d 1092. The Court reviews the registered-voter qualification, the personal-name component of the circulation badge, and final and monthly reports identifying individual paid circulators and their compensation. Other petition-administration and reporting fields retain the limits stated below.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the reviewed invalidations, with the component votes and limits below.

## Judgment & Remedy

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Registered-voter condition | Affirm invalidation, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg | O'Connor, Breyer | Reviewed invalidation remains in force; scope and reserved fields stated in the holdings. |
| Personal-name badge | Affirm invalidation, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | — | Reviewed invalidation remains in force; scope and reserved fields stated in the holdings. |
| Final individual-circulator disclosures | Affirm reviewed invalidation, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg | O'Connor, Breyer | Reviewed invalidation remains in force; scope and reserved fields stated in the holdings. |
| Monthly individual-circulator disclosures | Affirm reviewed invalidation, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg | O'Connor, Breyer | Reviewed invalidation remains in force; scope and reserved fields stated in the holdings. |

The Court neither independently validates nor invalidates unreviewed badge and monthly-report fields. Ordinary implementation respects the actual lower judgment and the precise review scope.

## Opinion Topology

|---|---|---|---|---|

## Holdings

**Common authority.** Stevens's Court opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg throughout. Those six directly support Parts I, III-A and III-B. O'Connor and Breyer additionally join Part II, giving that component eight full rationale votes. Thomas concurs in every invalidity judgment on the distinct grounds described below; his judgment votes are not full joins in the Court's methodology. The rule's limits accompany each component.

### I. Registered-voter condition

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado may not require otherwise qualified resident-adult initiative circulators to be registered voters on this record. Excluding otherwise qualified speakers because they are unregistered substantially burdens protected petition circulation, while the retained identification and enforcement mechanisms serve traceability more closely.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a person need not register to vote to engage in the protected political conversation involved in circulating a petition. Meyer v. Grant protects that interaction and the available pool of speakers. Colorado's condition excludes at least 400,000 otherwise qualified but unregistered voters. Easy enrollment does not eliminate that reduction or show that registration adequately measures residence, availability for process or honesty.

The State's strongest argument treats registration as an administrable qualification in a legally operative election process. Anderson and the governing Burdick rule permit appropriately fitted administration, but require attention to the actual burden and interest. Here the sworn identifying and residential-address affidavit, signature review, subpoenas and targeted fraud sanctions address traceability without the registration exclusion. Timmons's different candidate-nomination setting does not make this speaker restriction minor. The Court invalidates the registration condition, not the age floor, circulation period or residence rule whose validity is not presented. An affidavit cannot preserve the invalid condition merely by requiring a registration recital.

### II. Personal name displayed during solicitation

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado may not compel every circulator to display a personal name on a badge during protected face-to-face petition advocacy when the retained post-circulation identification and enforcement measures serve the asserted interests with materially less speech burden. The personal-name component is invalid; standalone paid-or-volunteer and employer-information requirements are not adjudicated.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that compelled personal attribution at the expressive encounter requires a justification fitted to that burden. McIntyre protects individual political advocacy against inadequately justified identification demands; Meyer protects petition circulation, and Riley prevents payment from stripping a solicitor of speech protection. The trial record shows deterrence from the personal badge, particularly when the subject is unpopular. That is a burden on the actual political conversation.

Even assuming fraud prevention and detection are compelling interests, Colorado has not shown the necessary fit. The identifying affidavit, signature assurances, review, protests and fraud penalties allow officials to identify and investigate circulators without exposing their names to everyone approached. The later public affidavit differs in timing and accessibility from a worn badge; the Court creates no anonymity from lawful investigation. A witness's interest in reporting misconduct does not establish the needed additional benefit of this blanket personal-name display. This holding does not decide standalone paid-status, sponsor or employer attribution, nor invalidate lawful post-circulation verification.

### III-A. Final individual-circulator reports

**Holding and operative rule.** The challenged final-report requirements publishing each paid circulator's identity, address and individual compensation fail the required substantial relationship to Colorado's asserted informational and enforcement interests on this record. Disclosure of initiative proponents and aggregate spending remains distinct; the decision creates no categorical exemption from initiative-finance disclosure.

**Controlling explanation.** Buckley v. Valeo permits campaign-finance disclosure substantially related to important governmental interests. That inquiry requires attention to what the information reveals. Proponent identity and aggregate expenditures reveal who finances an initiative and the extent of paid efforts. The individual messenger is not, for that reason, a financing source or a candidate contributor. Colorado has not demonstrated the added benefit necessary to justify publishing every paid circulator's identity and compensation, without a threshold separating consequential financing from any paid messenger.

The State argues that payee information permits tracing and checking spending beyond aggregate totals. That is a legitimate subject of inquiry, but the retained sponsor, aggregate, amount-per-signature and verification mechanisms make the asserted incremental need insufficient here. Final reporting is less immediate than a badge; it still requires its own disclosure justification. Kentucky Right to Life's payer-identification rule concerns a different financing function and does not answer this demand for every messenger's information. A narrower individual-reporting rule supported under the governing scrutiny remains open.

### III-B. Monthly individual-circulator reports

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado's challenged monthly requirements publishing each paid circulator's identity, residential and business addresses and individual amounts paid or owed are likewise invalid on this record. The Court decides that individual-information package, not the independent validity of every other monthly-report field.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court separately considers publication during the circulation campaign. Timely information can expose abuse and inform voters before qualification; that argument gives monthly reporting work that a final report cannot always perform. But timing does not cure the insufficient relationship between identifying each messenger and the State's demonstrated financing or enforcement need. It also exposes the individual while the political effort is underway. The retained sponsor and aggregate disclosures and enforcement mechanisms remain material alternatives. The decision does not hold that referendum disclosure has no informational value or that every individual challenge requires proof of reprisals.

The Tenth Circuit invalidated the monthly subsection as a whole. The Court affirms the reviewed invalidity of individual circulator disclosures and leaves the independent validity of its other prescriptions, including proponents and measure identity, undecided. It neither orders those fields conclusively reinstated nor independently declares them unconstitutional. Any ordinary remedial or severability implementation must respect the existing judgment and the scope actually decided.

**Common limits and remedy.** The reviewed registration, personal-name and individual-disclosure invalidations stand. Age, circulation timing, verification affidavits, fraud and signature-review mechanisms are outside the reviewed invalidations and remain governed by their own terms. This decision does not invalidate standalone paid-status or sponsor attribution, create a replacement initiative code, grant damages, destroy public records, authorize fraud or decide every future disclosure rule. Unreviewed requirements receive no automatic new constitutional endorsement.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414:** Applied to the protected political interaction and reduction of available speakers; ordinary properly fitted administration remains possible.
- **McIntyre, April 19, 1995:** Extended to the personal-name demand during live petition advocacy at the stated narrow-fit scope; candidate-finance, organizational and independent post-circulation settings retain their distinctions.
- **Riley v. National Federation of the Blind, 487 U.S. 781:** Applied to protection of paid solicitation and the burden of compelled speech during the encounter; its discussion supplies no adjudication of the reserved badge fields.
- **Anderson v. Celebrezze and Burdick, June 8, 1992:** Their burden-sensitive administrative inquiry remains; the Court applies the actual petition-speech burdens rather than treating every election regulation as equally severe.
- **Timmons, April 28, 1997:** Distinguished as a candidate-nomination setting; it supplies no automatic lesser review for these speaker restrictions.
- **Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1:** Its disclosure scrutiny and legitimate financial-information interests are applied to each report; no categorical initiative-disclosure immunity is adopted.
- **Kentucky Right to Life, September 14, 1998:** Its payer and source-identification distinctions remain; identification of the financer does not automatically justify identifying each messenger.

## Law After Decision

Registration cannot be used to exclude otherwise qualified resident-adult circulators; personal names cannot be compelled on the challenged solicitation badge; and the reviewed final and monthly individual-disclosure packages cannot be enforced. Governing law preserves distinct authority for lawful administration, verification, fraud controls, sponsor and aggregate disclosures. The independent validity and remedial treatment of unreviewed badge and monthly fields remain outside this judgment. No fractured-opinion or narrowest-ground synthesis is needed: each Court holding has at least six direct joins, and the badge holding has eight.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** Thomas agrees that every reviewed restriction is invalid but applies traditional strict scrutiny to the direct speech and severe associational burdens. Registration reduces the Meyer-protected speaker pool without a narrowly fitted traceability justification. The personal-name badge directly intrudes on core political speech and fails narrow tailoring even assuming a compelling anti-fraud interest. The reports' effect on circulation is more attenuated; compelled disclosure independently burdens association and belief. Singling out paid circulators poorly fits general identification, and the need to expose each individual's identity and compensation has not been demonstrated. Monthly exposure is considered separately from final reporting. As an alternative, the challenged packages also fail Buckley's more forgiving substantial-relationship test. He does not displace Buckley's surviving disclosure holdings, adopt the Court's entire method, invalidate retained reports or decide reserved fields.

**O'Connor, joined by Breyer, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They join Part II's complete personal-name-badge explanation: direct intrusion into political conversation requires narrow tailoring, and the retained affidavit and enforcement tools defeat this demand on this record. They would sustain registration and the challenged final and monthly reports. Under Anderson–Burdick, readily available enrollment provides an administrable connection to the formal petition process without the severe burden found by the Court; Meyer does not make every qualification equivalent to a ban on paid circulators. Reports are indirect financial-disclosure burdens rather than badges worn during persuasion. Identifying payees and individual disbursements can permit checking campaign activity beyond aggregate sums, and contemporaneous monthly information can expose abuse before the vote. They find the relationship sufficient even under more demanding disclosure review. They acknowledge the excluded unregistered speakers and possible exposure costs, but reach different applications. They propose no automatic admission rule, universal minimum scrutiny or independent disposition of reserved fields.

## Procedure After Action

The reviewed invalidations and corresponding injunctive protection remain in force. The Court orders no replacement system or further Supreme Court proceeding. The lower judgment and ordinary remedial law govern any remaining implementation of unreviewed fields; their independent constitutional validity is not decided.

# Lopez v. Monterey County — 1999-01-20

## Event

Lopez v. Monterey County, No. 97-1396. Argued November 2, 1998; decided January 20, 1999. On direct review of the three-judge Northern District of California's December 19, 1997 dismissal after the Court's November 6, 1996 mandate. The questions concern the County's administration of state-mandated judicial-election changes, the reach of the 1983 submission, prospective nonuse and the dependent judicial-tenure order. California defends dismissal; the County supports the appellants.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The dismissal is reversed and the case remanded; the dependent tenure-order disposition is set aside to the extent stated below.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0**, with the dependent tenure-order disposition set aside insofar as based on the rejected exemption, also **9–0**.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The judgment of dismissal is reversed and the case remanded to implement the November 6, 1996 mandate. The dependent disposition of the judicial-term-extension order is set aside insofar as based on the rejected exemption. The District Court must identify the actual reach of the 1983 submission, prevent prospective use of the remaining unprecleared operative changes and apply the confined judicial-remedy rule. No officeholder outcome, completed-election consequence, damages award, new deadline or automatic Supreme Court return is directed.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I, II and IV | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports reversal and remand | Administration, actual submission and confined nonuse/judicial-plan rule. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | Thomas | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer; Stone-Zsela only as stated | Rejects the additional defenses | Eight full joins. Stone-Zsela joins rejection of delay as authorization for prospective use and of the internal-administration characterization; he agrees in rejection of the collateral-designation defense without adding grounds. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part V | Thomas | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Sets aside the dependent tenure disposition to the stated extent | Eight rationale joins; Stone-Zsela agrees in that disposition without adding grounds. |

No separate writing is filed. The Chief's disposition-only agreement on the collateral-designation defense and dependent tenure component does not supply an additional rationale.

## Holdings

**Authority and scope.** Thomas delivers the opinion of the Court. The eight Associate Justices join Parts I–V. Stone-Zsela joins Parts I, II and IV, and Part III only insofar as it rejects delay as authorization for prospective use and rejects the internal-administration characterization of a direct electorate change. He agrees in rejection of the collateral-designation defense and in the dependent tenure-order disposition without adding grounds to those parts. Each controlling part thus has at least eight direct joins. No separate constitutional justification for Section 5 is adopted.

### I. State compulsion does not exempt covered administration

**Holding and operative rule.** A covered political subdivision must obtain Section 5 preclearance before administering a postbaseline voting change within that subdivision, even when a noncovered State mandates the change and leaves no local discretion. The duty attaches to the County's administration; it does not require California to submit its laws in the abstract.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court applies its November 6, 1996 Lopez mandate. That decision expressly assumed complete state displacement of local discretion and nevertheless held that giving the change operative effect is administration. The lower court's renewed reliance on state compulsion cannot reopen the settled question. Neither the 1979 law nor the January 1995 elimination of justice courts supplies an exemption from the County's duty.

Young preserves the difference between the source of a policy and its actual administration, while reserving a different question about wholly federally compelled conduct. Foreman likewise rejects the proposition that an earlier authorization automatically clears subsequent local implementation. California's argument that the County cannot choose a different arrangement confuses authority to enact with authority to administer an unprecleared change. Practical difficulty affects lawful implementation, not whether the completed mandate governs. The Court decides no discriminatory purpose, retrogressive effect or entitlement to substantive preclearance.

### II. The actual submission controls the reach of approval

**Holding and operative rule.** Replacing district electorates with a countywide electorate for judges is a covered change in voting, and a submission does not preclear earlier operative changes it failed to identify. The District Court must determine the actual reach of the 1983 submission as to the final ordinance; its attachment and the Attorney General's nonobjection do not establish blanket approval of the preceding consolidations.

**Controlling explanation.** The prior Lopez decision fixes the direct electoral character of the change and the treatment of the earlier unidentified ordinances. Presley distinguishes internal allocation of governmental authority from changes in who votes for an official; this alteration concerns the electorate itself. Allen and Perkins preserve coverage of such practical electoral changes.

McCain and Clark require identification of what was submitted. Young and Foreman preserve that submission-specific boundary. The 1983 additional-judgeships submission, attached final ordinance and nonobjection are relevant evidence, but they do not establish the operative terms needed to decide conclusively whether the final ordinance itself was approved. That question remains for determination below. The Court does not invalidate an actual approval, review the merits of the Attorney General's decision, or treat an unavailable source as a party's failure to comply.

### III. Delay and a collateral designation challenge do not authorize prospective use

**Holding and operative rule.** Delay does not authorize prospective administration of an operative covered change that lacks preclearance, and the effective Section 4(b) coverage determination cannot be collaterally retried in this Section 5 enforcement action. Statutory bailout remains a distinct route; reliance, completed acts and practical timing may bear on legally available retrospective relief or a lawful transition, without supplying clearance.

**Controlling explanation.** California's remaining defenses must be separated rather than treated as another version of compulsion. Lopez already resolved prospective nonuse and the improper collateral challenge to designation. Clark and Hampton County preserve attention to remedial circumstances without making delay an authorization to use an uncleared practice. The direct change in judicial electorates likewise cannot be recast as merely internal administration under Presley. These applications enforce the existing mandate; they announce no new facial constitutional holding, decide no bailout request and create no general retrospective equitable rule.

### IV. Nonuse and the confined judicial-plan alternative

**Holding and operative rule.** The District Court must prevent prospective use of established operative changes lacking preclearance until approval is obtained through the Attorney General or the District Court for the District of Columbia. A necessary temporary plan wholly formulated by the federal court is distinct only if supported by independent lawful remedial authority, consistent with the Constitution and Voting Rights Act, no more displacing of state law than necessity requires, and limited to the period before a lawful precleared arrangement becomes available.

**Controlling explanation.** Connor distinguishes a genuinely judicial plan from governmental policy, and McDaniel prevents a governmental plan from becoming exempt merely through judicial adoption. Lopez and Young apply that distinction while preserving nonuse. The lawful alternatives include the lawful precleared baseline, completed preclearance, or an independently justified judicial plan meeting the stated limits. The Court selects none here and does not authorize a court to adopt the County's policy as its own.

Clark left open whether extreme circumstances could justify allowing an election to proceed while preclearance remained pending. No such circumstance is established here, and ordinary administrative difficulty supplies no exception. The earlier ordinary six-year at-large elections did not qualify under the prior mandate. Shaw preserves substantive purpose-and-effect determinations for the designated preclearance forums. The enforcement court's task is coverage, actual submission and lawful implementation, not its own substantive approval.

### V. The dependent tenure order requires lawful reconsideration

**Holding and operative rule.** The disposition of the judicial-term-extension order must be set aside insofar as it depends on the rejected exemption and reconsidered under the corrected legal premise and actual circumstances. Reversal does not automatically revive an expired arrangement, extend any judge's tenure, install or remove an officeholder, cancel a completed election or invalidate judicial acts.

**Controlling explanation.** The District Court vacated its September 25, 1996 extension order together with dismissal of the enforcement action. Once the exemption premise fails, that dependent disposition cannot stand on the same ground. Its correction nevertheless requires lawful authority and the actual tenure circumstances; the mandate did not select officeholders or create indefinite holdovers.

Prior preclearance of a four-division option resolves only its preclearance status. It does not settle independent state-law, electorate-and-jurisdiction linkage, population, divided-municipality, residence or remedial-authority questions. Johnson's rejection of a design-only racial injury likewise does not approve all features of a proposed arrangement. The District Court must apply the existing mandate to the actual remaining questions without treating preclearance as complete legal approval. No new submission deadline, compulsory hearing or automatic return to this Court is ordered.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Lopez v. Monterey County, November 6, 1996:** Enforced unchanged as the governing administration, direct-electorate, submission, nonuse and confined-remedy mandate; its state-compulsion question is not reopened.
