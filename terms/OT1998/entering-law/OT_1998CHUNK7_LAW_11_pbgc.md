#### United States v. Noland, 517 U.S. 535

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-323.
**Decided:** May 13, 1996.

##### Administrative penalties after conversion and susceptibility to equitable subordination

**Holding:** Allowed §503(b)(1)(C) postpetition tax penalties retain case-era §507(a)(1) administrative priority after conversion because §348(d) expressly excepts §503(b) claims from its ordinary treatment of post-order, preconversion claims. Priority does not immunize them from lawful §510(c) equitable subordination: §726 makes distribution subject to §510.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Thomas), Part I, unanimous.

**Material application:** Section 726(a)(4)'s fourth rank applies to covered fines, penalties, forfeitures, and multiple, exemplary, or punitive damages arising before the earlier of the order for relief or appointment of a trustee, and only insofar as they do not compensate actual pecuniary loss. It does not demote every noncompensatory penalty. Section 510(c), after notice and a hearing, permits all-or-part distributional subordination of an allowed claim to another allowed claim, or an allowed interest to another allowed interest, and transfer to the estate of a lien securing the subordinated claim. Both priority and the express exception retain effect; their coexistence establishes no conflicting congressional choices.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No §510(a) agreement, §510(b) securities ground, or lien-transfer basis is established here. Susceptibility to equitable subordination does not prove a sufficient ground for its exercise or freeze equitable principles into a closed catalogue.

##### Insufficiency of the lower courts' class-based reasons

**Holding:** Section 510(c) does not authorize subordinating this statutory penalty class merely because the penalties compensate no pecuniary loss and payment reduces recovery for creditors who supplied value. The lower courts' stated reasons are legally inadequate even though they expressly disclaimed automatic subordination of every penalty. Lawful claim-specific equity remains available, but the Court establishes no other sufficient ground on this record.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II of the same unanimous Court opinion.

**Material application:** Noncompensation, insufficient assets, losses to value-giving creditors, and retained priority for tax and interest recur whenever this class competes with compensatory claims in an insolvent estate. Those reasons express the category-wide preference Congress's administrative classification rejects; calling them individualized balancing does not supply a different equitable circumstance. Statutory susceptibility and legislative statements about continued equitable development do not create a replacement ranking. Innocent-creditor and financing concerns cannot alone displace priority or bypass separate financing conditions.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court neither requires misconduct universally nor establishes an affirmative entitlement to subordination without it. Permissible development is not frozen at 1978; particular inequitable conduct or another recognized claim-specific ground remains addressable when lawfully presented and proved.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse and remand, 9–0. The same class reasons cannot preserve subordination under a new label. Any other consideration depends on the record and preservation; no factual reopening, misconduct hearing, lien transfer, distribution schedule, or payment amount is ordered. Penalty allowance and tax-and-interest priority remain unchanged.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Ahlers* requires equity within the Code without itself construing §510(c) or prohibiting all equity. *Taylor v. Standard Gas*, *Pepper*, and *Comstock* preserve their misconduct-based applications without establishing a universal prerequisite. *Simonson*'s former statutory penalty prohibition supplies no present ranking. *Boteler* and *Nicholas* preserve postpetition penalties' distinct treatment and compliance function. *Things Remembered* supports giving compatible statutes separate effect; its remand-review ruling and separate equitable-remand theory do not authorize a new distribution rank.

#### United States v. Reorganized CF&I Fabricators of Utah, Inc., 518 U.S. 213 (1996)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-325.
**Decided:** June 20, 1996.

##### Whether the allowed §4971(a) funding-default assessment receives excise-tax priority

**Holding:** The allowed 1989 section 4971(a) assessment is a noncompensatory penalty, not an excise tax entitled to priority under the governing pre-1994 section 507(a)(7)(E). When the Bankruptcy Code does not direct adoption of an external classification, the exaction's actual statutory operation controls its bankruptcy tax characterization; neither a tax label nor a deterrent purpose alone resolves that inquiry.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter’s Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight Justices.

**Material application:** Payment of the deficiency-measured sanction neither satisfies the independent funding obligation nor replaces pension or PBGC liability. The ten-percent assessment is distinct from §4971(b)’s additional hundred-percent assessment. Minimum-funding waivers under §412(d) and 29 U.S.C. §1083(a) can remove the deficiency and prevent either assessment. Section 507(a)(7)(G) separately requires a penalty related to a qualifying claim and compensation for actual pecuniary loss, absent here.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The governing text is pre-1994 §507(a)(7)(E), not later (a)(8). Its excise-tax timing requirements are not separately disputed. The claim remains allowed. No general Lorber checklist, exclusion of regulatory taxes, constitutional punishment rule, dischargeability or proportionality holding follows.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Denial of priority preserved, 8–1; Thomas would accord tax treatment.

##### Whether recurring creditor-loss reasons suffice for equitable subordination

**Holding:** Section 510(c) does not authorize distributional subordination of this noncompensatory penalty merely because its payment reduces innocent creditors' recoveries. Its equitable authority remains available on a legally sufficient, supported claim-specific ground, but the stated class-based reasons are inadequate even if described as individual balancing; the challenged subordinate ranking must be set aside. Rejection of that equitable ground does not itself confer tax priority; the Part I majority separately resolves priority.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter’s Part II, unanimous; Thomas joins this part only.

**Material application:** The assessment’s noncompensatory character, insolvency and reduction of innocent creditors’ recoveries recur across the claim class; calling those reasons individualized balancing does not make them sufficient. Section 510(c), after notice and a hearing, permits distributional subordination of allowed claims to allowed claims and allowed interests to allowed interests. Priority does not immunize a claim from that power and lack of priority does not justify its use.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No universal misconduct prerequisite, frozen catalogue of equitable grounds or entitlement to subordination without misconduct is adopted. Properly presented §§1122(a) and 1129(a)(7) arguments, including the asserted relevance of §726(a)(4), and supported lawful claim-specific equity remain undecided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Judgment vacated and remanded. The subordinate ranking cannot stand on the recurring class grounds; Class 12 ordinary-unsecured treatment remains the plan’s fallback absent an independently lawful basis for different treatment. No fixed distribution, new misconduct hearing, lien transfer or restoration of another disallowed claim.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **City of New York v. Feiring, 313 U.S. 283 (1941):** Applies the federal bankruptcy inquiry into the exaction's legal operation rather than the creating statute's label.
- **United States v. New York, 315 U.S. 510 (1942):** Applies that inquiry to a federal exaction and preserves the distinction between a revenue tax that creates incentives and a sanction for violating an independent duty.
- **United States v. Sotelo, 436 U.S. 268 (1978):** Applies the rule that a penalty label does not change the character of liability for already-withheld taxes; distinguishes this independent funding-default sanction.
- **United States v. Noland, May 13, 1996:** Applies the limit on class-based equitable reprioritization, including recurring reasons described as balancing. Preserves statutory equitable susceptibility even for priority claims. Its distinct administrative classification of postpetition tax penalties is not extended to this prepetition assessment.
- **Pepper v. Litton, 308 U.S. 295 (1939):** Preserves its misconduct-based equitable application without converting misconduct into a necessary condition for every section 510(c) case. No precedent is overruled.

#### Associates Commercial Corp. v. Rash

**Docket or dockets:** No. 96-454.

**Decided:** 1997-06-16.

##### Retained Chapter 13 collateral receives adjusted replacement valuation

**Holding:** When a Chapter 13 debtor retains and uses collateral under §1325(a)(5)(B), §506(a) values the secured claim by the price a willing buyer in the debtor's trade, business or situation would pay a willing seller for comparable property of like age and condition serving that proposed use. The court must use replacement value with appropriate evidentiary adjustments, rather than net foreclosure value for a sale the plan prevents.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's opinion, joined by all Justices except Stevens; eight direct joins.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Replacement value is neither new-property cost, debtor-specific earnings, a compulsory retail quote nor a midpoint. Any retail measure excludes separately priced warranties, storage, reconditioning and benefits not received through retention, and the creditor’s lien cannot capture accessories or modifications beyond it. Dealer profit and overhead are not mechanically deducted from a supported comparable-market price. Surrender, abandonment, materially different uses, lease earnings and the unargued income-capitalization theory remain outside the decision.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse and remand for relevant-market evidence, age-and-condition adjustments, secured-claim valuation and consequential plan review. No dollar amount, interest rate or disposition expense is fixed.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- United Savings Association of Texas v. Timbers of Inwood Forest Associates: preserves its no-delay-compensation rule; distinguishes that question from the §506(a) market used at confirmation.
- Dewsnup v. Timm: preserves its bounded Chapter 7 §506(d) lien holding; it supplies no universal valuation rule.
- Nobelman v. American Savings Bank: preserves protection of the specified principal-residence mortgage rights without extending it to this truck.
- BFP v. Resolution Trust Corp.: preserves independent §548 valuation under realistically comparable forced-sale conditions; the different proposed transaction here requires the §506(a) retention measure. No foreclosure-price safe harbor is adopted.

#### Varity Corp. v. Howe, 516 U.S. 489

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-1471.
**Decided:** March 19, 1996.

##### The integrated benefit-security presentation was fiduciary administration

**Holding:** ERISA §3(21)(A) attaches fiduciary responsibility to the particular exercise of discretionary plan administration, including plan information communicated in that capacity. Varity exercised that function when it used administrative authority, benefit comparisons and linked assurances of benefit security to induce the class's transfer; employer status, corporate forecasting or participant perception alone would not suffice.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: six Justices.

**Material application:** Current-benefit descriptions and deliberately linked security assurances formed one administrative presentation. Warnings that future conditions depended on MCC's success and that changes might occur did not displace the unchallenged trial findings that the combined presentation was knowingly misleading.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Every employment statement or corporate-solvency prediction is not thereby fiduciary. Corporate restructuring and plan-amendment powers remain distinct from administration.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** John Hancock's particular-function inquiry is applied while keeping status, breach and remedy separate. Curtiss-Wright preserves sponsor amendment authority but distinguishes retained power from authorized exercise; it creates no immunity for this administrative act.

##### Deliberate disloyal deception violates §404

**Holding:** An administrator acting as a fiduciary violates §404(a)(1)'s loyalty duty by knowingly and significantly misleading participants about benefit security to induce surrender of existing plan interests for the employer's benefit. Varity's found deception and the class's reliance meet that rule despite reserved lawful amendment or termination powers.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part II, joined by the same six Justices as Part I.

**Limits and questions not reached:** This intentional-deception holding decides no general duty to volunteer information, answer every inquiry, disclose every forecast or avoid negligent misstatement. It supplies no substantive rule for the ten retirees' different assignment claim and no permanent-benefits promise.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Firestone's trust-law guidance explains loyalty within ERISA's statutory function, without replacing that threshold. Curtiss-Wright's amendment rule supplies no permission to deceive participants into surrendering present membership.

##### Section 502(a)(3) permits appropriate individual equitable redress for fiduciary breach

**Holding:** Sections 409 and 502(a)(2) do not impose a categorical plan-only bar on §502(a)(3)'s express authorization of appropriate individual equitable relief for a §404 violation. Relief must be traditionally equitable; where another ERISA provision adequately remedies the injury, further equity normally is unnecessary and inappropriate, without an absolute rule governing every alternative pleading.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part III, joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: five Justices. Stone-Zsela's stricter adequate-remedy condition supports the class's judgment but is not the Court's rule.

**Material application:** The class sought restoration after fiduciary deception procured surrender of membership. Rejection of its lifetime-benefits contract theory established neither an adequate §502(a)(1)(B) remedy for that injury nor an automatic bar to restoration.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not classify each monetary item as equitable, including replacement-insurance premiums or all unpaid benefits. It neither revives the rejected $7.6 million and $712,332 compensatory awards nor permits general compensation through trust law. Section 510 and estoppel remain unadopted alternatives.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The clarified restoration decree is affirmed. Reinstatement confers only the rights of the M-F plan as it now exists. Past entitlement may be adjusted for intervening events, with relevant additional evidence and a conforming decree. Future lawful changes remain possible subject to applicable law and preserved retaliation or discrimination objections. No new payment amount or immutable lifetime entitlement is entered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Russell's plan-centered §409 construction is preserved while its distinct §502(a)(3) question is answered. Mertens's equitable-category limit applies; its reserved antecedent action issue is not treated as an earlier action bar. Peacock's statutory-or-plan-violation requirement is met by the proved §404 breach; its later new-debtor jurisdictional holding does not govern this original action. Firestone supplies guidance within enacted limits.

##### The ten retirees' independent assignment-breach ground was not adequately challenged

**Holding:** The categorical plan-only objection does not defeat the ten retirees' individual equitable recovery, and their distinct lower-court assignment-breach determination remains undisturbed because the petition did not sufficiently present a challenge to it. Affirmance decides no new substantive assignment rule, precise monetary classification or categorical unavailability of §502(a)(1)(B) to those retirees.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part IV, joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: five Justices. Stone-Zsela provides a sixth judgment vote on the distinct assignment component without a ground.

**Material application:** The ten had retired before the purported assignment and did not consent. Their unauthorized-administration ground did not depend on the class's presentation or voluntary departure; arguments concerning misrepresentation and lifetime contractual benefits did not identify error in that separate ground.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the clarified decree for this group on the same existing-plan and adjustment terms stated above, without entering a new amount or reviving terminated-employee, punitive, lifetime-contract, settled-disability or pension claims.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Curtiss-Wright does not transform an amendment reservation into proof of an authorized assignment or permanent vesting; Mertens's monetary-category limits remain without a new classification decision.

### Statutory priorities and claim-specific equitable subordination

**Governing question:** Whether §510(c) permits a court to reduce rank because an allowed penalty competes with compensatory creditors.

**Current rule:** Statutory priority does not immunize a claim from §510(c), and lack of priority does not itself justify subordination. After notice and a hearing, §510(c) permits distributional subordination of all or part of an allowed claim to another allowed claim, or an allowed interest to another allowed interest, under principles of equitable subordination, and transfer of a lien securing a subordinated claim. A legally sufficient, supported claim-specific ground is required; noncompensatory character, estate insufficiency and reduced recovery for innocent or value-giving creditors cannot alone replace Congress’s distribution choices, even when described as individual balancing. This limit applies to both postpetition administrative penalties and prepetition nonpriority penalties.

**Authority by component:**

- Noland — Parts I–II, real statutory equity and forbidden class replacement, 1996-05-13; Thomas for all nine.
- CFI — Part II, the same boundary within the general-unsecured class, 1996-06-20; Souter for all nine.

**Present operation effective:** CFI, June 20, 1996, for application to nonpriority penalties; Noland’s administrative-priority rule effective May 13, 1996.

**Controlling construction:** Allowed §503(b)(1)(C) postpetition tax penalties retain case-era §507(a)(1) priority after conversion through §348(d)’s §503(b) exception. Section 726(a)(4)’s fourth-rank category instead requires a fine, penalty, forfeiture or multiple/exemplary/punitive damages arising before the earlier of the relief order or trustee appointment, and only to the extent noncompensatory. Section 726 distribution remains expressly subject to §510.

**Open question:** Misconduct’s universal necessity remains unresolved; no entitlement without misconduct or frozen catalogue of equitable grounds follows. Rejection of a class-based reason neither orders a new misconduct hearing nor establishes a different ground, final payment or tax priority.

**Holdings navigation:** United States v. Noland, 517 U.S. 535; United States v. Reorganized CF&I Fabricators of Utah, Inc., 518 U.S. 213 (1996).

### Actual statutory operation in bankruptcy tax characterization

**Governing question:** Whether an assessment is a tax entitled to Bankruptcy Code priority.

**Current rule:** Absent a Bankruptcy Code direction adopting an external classification, the exaction’s actual statutory operation controls federal bankruptcy characterization. A tax label and a deterrent purpose are each insufficient alone. The §4971(a) funding-default assessment at issue operates as a noncompensatory penalty because it sanctions failure of an independent pension-funding obligation without satisfying that obligation or replacing pension/PBGC liability; it receives no pre-1994 §507(a)(7)(E) excise-tax priority. The related-penalty route under §507(a)(7)(G) independently requires a qualifying related claim and compensation for actual pecuniary loss.

**Authority by component:**

- CFI — Part I, text-and-operation tax characterization, 1996-06-20; Souter and all participating Justices except Thomas (eight).

**Present operation effective:** CFI, June 20, 1996.

**Exceptions and limits:** Minimum-funding waivers can remove the deficiency underlying either the ordinary or additional assessment; the distinction is not a finding that only the additional assessment is avoidable. Congress may expressly assign priority. This supplies no general Lorber checklist, constitutional punishment classification, disallowance, dischargeability determination or independent ground of equitable subordination.

**Holdings navigation:** United States v. Reorganized CF&I Fabricators of Utah, Inc., 518 U.S. 213 (1996).

### Replacement valuation for retained Chapter 13 collateral

**Governing question:** Valuation under §§506(a) and 1325(a)(5)(B) when the debtor retains collateral for use.

**Current rule:** Use the price a willing buyer in the debtor's trade, business or situation would pay a willing seller for comparable property of the same age and condition serving the proposed retained use. This is adjusted replacement value, not net foreclosure value for a sale the plan prevents. The court selects appropriate market evidence; it must exclude separately included warranties, storage, reconditioning or other benefits not received through retention, and accessories or modifications outside the creditor's lien cannot enlarge secured value.

**Authority by component:**

- Associates Commercial Corp. v. Rash — Holdings, 1997-06-16; Ginsburg with Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; eight, holding and operative rule for retained-use replacement valuation

**Present operation effective:** Rash, 1997-06-16.

**Exceptions and limits:** Replacement value is not new-property cost, a mandatory retail quote, debtor-specific earnings or a midpoint between incompatible standards. Appropriate adjustments do not mechanically remove every dealer profit or overhead component from a demonstrated comparable-market price. Surrender, abandonment and materially different uses remain unresolved. No dollar valuation, interest rate, disposition expense or universal rule for other bankruptcy provisions is fixed.

**Holdings navigation:** Associates Commercial Corp. v. Rash (1997-06-16) — Bankruptcy / Valuation of retained-use collateral.

### ERISA sponsor design, fiduciary implementation and genuine benefit payments

**Governing question:** Whether plan amendment or release-conditioned retirement benefits establish a prohibited transaction.

**Current rule:** An employer designing and adopting pension-plan amendments acts as sponsor, not fiduciary. Actual implementation is a separate functional inquiry; party-in-interest status alone does not satisfy §406(a)(1)’s requirement that a fiduciary cause a transaction knowing or having reason to know that it is prohibited, subject to §408’s statutory exceptions. The asset-disposition branch does not acquire an extra discretion requirement. Payment of genuine enhanced retirement benefits under an otherwise lawful plan is not prohibited by §406(a)(1)(D) merely because required employment-related releases benefit the employer. This assumes fiduciary implementation for that classification and creates neither a §408 exemption nor a general net-benefit defense.

**Authority by component:**

- Spink — Part I, sponsor capacity and separate implementation, 1996-06-10; Ginsburg for all nine.
- Spink — Part II, genuine release-conditioned benefit payments, 1996-06-10; Ginsburg, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas (seven); Souter and Breyer reserve the question.

**Present operation effective:** Spink, June 10, 1996.

**Exceptions and limits:** Amendment procedures, participation, funding, vesting, loyalty, anti-inurement and independently applicable §406(b) duties survive. No sham transfer, kickback, unlawful plan or unenforceable release is immunized. Particular release validity under the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act or other law remains open. Surplus does not become employer property.

**Related current rules:** “Corporate procedures for ERISA plan amendments” governs actual authorization and amendment procedure; “Discharge of pension funding obligations as an exchange” governs the distinct property-for-release transaction.

**Holdings navigation:** Lockheed Corp. v. Spink, 517 U.S. 882.

### Hughes Aircraft Co. v. Jacobson; No. 97-1287; Ninth Circuit judgment, 105 F.3d 1288. — 1999-01-25

#### Holdings

##### I. Genuine payments within the continuing plan do not inure to the employer

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 403(c)(1) does not prohibit payment of genuine benefits to participants under the amended single plan merely because those benefits reduce future employer contributions or payroll costs. Surplus remains plan property, and the anti-inurement rule independently governs an actual diversion, unlawful reversion or payment outside the lawful plan.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion's Part I is joined by all eight Associate Justices and controls; Stone-Zsela agrees in dismissal of the anti-inurement claim without adding a ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that adding a benefit structure and eligible participants does not, on the acts alleged, create a second legal plan to which assets were transferred for Hughes's separate obligations. Both benefit structures draw from the continuing defined-benefit fund. A contribution holiday does not itself distribute cash to the employer. John Hancock separates asset status from a particular unlawful use, while Spink preserves anti-inurement as an independent claim even when adoption of the design was not fiduciary conduct. The retirees correctly insist that employee-derived surplus is protected plan property; that premise does not establish that genuine payments to the plan's participants are an employer diversion. The Court rejects the separate-plan and incidental-employer-benefit theories rather than creating an exception to §403 or declaring the surplus Hughes's property. A different actual recipient or unlawful transfer would require its own statutory analysis.

##### II. Amendment design is distinct from fiduciary implementation

**Holding and operative rule:** Design and adoption of these pension amendments are sponsor functions, even though employees contributed to the plan; those acts alone create no fiduciary breach under §§404–405. Actual asset management or disposition and administration remain subject to §3(21)(A)'s functional requirements, including authority or control over disposition without an additional discretion requirement.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join the sponsor-capacity rule and its preservation of independent duties. The eight Associates additionally join the claim-specific conclusion that these original implementation and derivative cofiduciary theories identify no independent breach after their surplus-ownership and separate-plan predicates fail; Stone-Zsela agrees in that disposition without supplying an additional implementation ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that employee contributions do not make participants co-sponsors entitled to control plan design. Spink directly separates amendment adoption from implementation and requires independent claims to receive their own analysis. Varity preserves responsibility for actual administration and misleading benefit communications; it does not turn this design decision into administration. John Hancock keeps plan assets, fiduciary function and breach distinct. Assuming fiduciary implementation of the genuine payments, the original claims identify no independent imprudent management or disloyal diversion beyond the failed surplus-ownership and separate-plan theories. The cofiduciary label supplies no underlying breach. The holding neither adds a discretion condition to asset-control responsibility nor immunizes misrepresentation, investment misconduct, discriminatory administration or an actual diversion. Dismissal follows from the particular pleaded acts and failed legal predicates, not from treating everything Hughes did as sponsor conduct.

##### III. Accrued benefits are protected without individual ownership of all surplus

**Holding and operative rule:** Participants in this defined-benefit plan have enforceable rights to promised and statutorily protected accrued benefits, including the employee-contribution floor, but no present individual ownership of all actuarial surplus. Because the original theory identifies no reduction of those protected benefits, use of pooled surplus for lawful plan benefits establishes neither forfeiture nor an anti-cutback violation.

**Authority:** The nine Justices join the no-surplus-ownership and no-reduction holding. The statutory distinction between required and voluntary contributions explains the eight Associates' rejection of the original employee-contribution theory; no benefit amount is calculated.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that §§203(a)(1) and 204(c) protect accrued employee-derived benefits without converting all actual investment appreciation into a separate participant account. For mandatory contributions, the statutory measure uses accumulated contributions with the enacted interest treatment and expresses the result as an annual benefit beginning at normal retirement age; another benefit form requires actuarial equivalence. The contribution conditions are employment, plan participation or obtaining employer-derived benefits. Section 204(c)(4)'s separate treatment of voluntary contributions remains intact; the original complaint describes mandatory payroll contributions. The Court decides no disputed computation, applicable rate or amount under those provisions. John Hancock identifies protected plan assets and fiduciary functions; it does not give each retiree title to a share of an ongoing defined-benefit plan's overfunding. No promised benefit or statutory floor was reduced by the theories alleged here. Actual underpayment, a reduction below the statutory floor or violation of a greater benefit promised by the plan remains actionable under its own elements.

##### IV. Continuing benefit obligations do not trigger termination distribution

**Holding and operative rule:** Closing the contributory structure to new entrants while continuing the plan and its benefit obligations is not a statutory termination triggering §4044 distribution. A common-law characterization as a wasting trust cannot replace §4041's standard-or-distress termination process or PBGC proceedings under §4042.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join the nontermination holding on these continuing-plan facts. The eight Associates join the separate statutory distribution analysis; no general rule for every partial termination is decided.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the 1991 amendment changed benefit structures within an ongoing plan; it did not terminate the plan merely because new employees could no longer enter its contributory structure. The plan continues to owe and pay benefits. Sections 4041–4044 govern termination and distribution. When their distribution trigger occurs, plan liabilities and the statutory allocation order must be respected, including protection of remaining assets attributable to employee contributions. Employer reversion also requires satisfaction of liabilities, consistency with law and authorization under the plan, and remains subject to the enacted timing and employee-residual protections. The Court applies none of those residual-distribution rules to authorize a present reversion or calculate a distribution here. Protecting employee contributions does not require a court to invent an earlier termination event. Genuine termination, mass exclusion and another event satisfying an enacted termination rule remain outside this holding. The deleted lower-court common-law discussion supplies no alternative trigger.

##### V. Incidental employer advantage does not make these benefit payments prohibited transactions

**Holding and operative rule:** Under §406(a)(1)(D), genuine pension payments under an otherwise lawful plan do not become prohibited transfers merely because they save the employer money. Nor does §406(b)(2), which bars a fiduciary from acting in a transaction for an adverse party, establish a violation merely from the sponsor's adoption of a benefit design advantageous to itself.

**Authority:** All eight Associates join Part V, which controls both statutory grounds; Stone-Zsela agrees only in dismissal of the prohibited-transaction component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the alleged recipients are participants of the continuing plan, not Hughes or strangers renamed as beneficiaries. Spink's genuine-benefit-payment rule applies after the separate-plan, unlawful-benefit and premature-distribution predicates fail. Its rule assumes fiduciary implementation for that inquiry and preserves sham transfers, kickbacks, independently unlawful plans and other prohibited transactions. Souter's and Breyer's earlier reservation about a technical release-payment question does not establish that these genuine pension payments are prohibited; they apply the controlling rule to the narrower original allegations here. The distinct adverse-party prohibition of §406(b)(2) does not turn sponsor adoption itself into a fiduciary transaction for an adverse party. There is no blanket benefit-payment exemption, no net-benefit defense, and no reliance on §408 to excuse an otherwise established violation. A later merger transaction or separately pleaded implementation wrong is not decided.

##### VI. Fair administration does not promise a later incentive to ineligible retirees

**Holding and operative rule:** The plan's fair-and-equitable administration, interpretation and application clause does not give every retiree a contractual right to an incentive later offered to specified active employees. The original sixth claim fails independently under §502(a)(1)(B) because no benefit due under the operative eligibility terms is withheld, and under §404(a)(1)(D) because the asserted difference is lawful eligibility design rather than unequal administration among eligible participants.

**Authority:** All eight Associates join Part VI and its distinct statutory and plan grounds. Stone-Zsela agrees only in dismissal of this separate plan-enforcement component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Article V, §5.2 has work in governing administration, interpretation and application of plan terms, but does not require identical benefit design for existing retirees and active employees whom the sponsor seeks to induce to retire. Spink defeats fiduciary liability resting solely on creation of the eligibility class. That classification alone would not answer a benefits-enforcement claim: the separate answer is that the administrative clause promises no incentive to persons outside the class and no due benefit is alleged to have been withheld. Curtiss-Wright preserves both actual amendment procedures and authorized adoption; these six original theories identify no separate unauthorized-adoption defect. Mertens does not bar an otherwise proper §502(a)(1)(B) claim and is not the basis for dismissal. Specific statutory benefit, allocation, anti-discrimination or amendment restrictions remain effective. A fairness clause gives no free-standing judicial power to redesign an eligibility scheme.

#### Precedent Treatment

- *Lockheed Corp. v. Spink* is applied to sponsor design and genuine benefit payments while its independent-claims directions are enforced. The present dismissal rests on separate statutory and plan answers, not categorical sponsor immunity.
- *John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank* is applied to separate plan property, function and breach; it establishes no participant ownership of every dollar of actuarial surplus.
- *Varity Corp. v. Howe* is preserved for function-specific administration, disloyal communications and appropriate relief. No conduct of that kind is immunized by amendment authority.
- *Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen* retains its distinction between a sufficient amendment procedure and actual authorized adoption; no separate adoption defect is decided here.
- *Mertens v. Hewitt Associates* retains its §502(a)(3) equitable-category limitation. It is distinguished from the §502(a)(1)(B) claim, which fails for lack of a promised due benefit rather than a categorical bar on benefits enforcement.

#### Law After Decision

As of January 25, 1999, employee contributions do not convert amendment design into a fiduciary act or establish individual ownership of an ongoing defined-benefit plan's surplus. Plan-property restrictions, employee-derived accrued benefits, functional fiduciary obligations, genuine termination requirements and contractual benefit promises remain independently enforceable. These six original theories fail after those distinct predicates are examined. No blanket immunity for implementation, new distribution entitlement or general rule disposing of a later merger claim is established.
