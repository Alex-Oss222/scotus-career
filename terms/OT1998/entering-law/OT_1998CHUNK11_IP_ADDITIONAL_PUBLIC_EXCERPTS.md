## Hess v. Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp., including Walsh’s employee action, No. 93-1197 (513 U.S. 30)

Source: terms/OT1994/output/OT_1994CHUNK1.md

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reverse and remand both actions | Entire entity-specific status holding, application, limits and remedy. |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Scalia, Thomas | Would affirm both dismissals | State-control ground for arm status, unmet conditional consent and absence of FELA abrogation; no universal treasury guarantee. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### PATH’s actual compact and financial structure does not confer the States’ immunity

**Controlling proposition:** A bistate compact entity does not acquire the States’ Eleventh Amendment immunity merely by exercising governmental power: its actual legal, financial and institutional relationship must supply good reason to conclude that the States structured it as their arm and Congress concurred, with state-treasury exposure highly probative but not categorically indispensable. PATH is a distinct, financially self-supporting enterprise responsible for its obligations; these employee suits impose no judgment obligation on either State and do not treat either State itself as a defendant, so PATH lacks that immunity.

**Authority:** Justice Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Six of nine participants directly support this entity-specific rule and its application to both employee actions. O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas dissent; no fragmented-rationale analysis is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that PATH’s actual relationship to New York and New Jersey does not make these suits actions against the States. Lake Country Estates requires examining the compact structure, and Mt. Healthy distinguishes separate public entities from the State itself. Gubernatorial appointment and veto, legislative restrictions and public transportation responsibilities favor PATH’s position, but do not overcome this enterprise’s separate identity, revenues, assets and obligations. Article XV requires equal state appropriations only while operating revenues are inadequate, only for salaries, office and other administrative expenses, in amounts recommended by the Authority and approved by both governors, with each State obligated only up to $100,000 annually. It is no promise to pay judgments. Financial independence therefore carries great weight in this mixed setting, without making treasury liability immunity’s sole condition. Feeney assumed status when deciding consent; it did not resolve the antecedent question now presented. Because PATH is not an immune arm, its conditional consent is unnecessary to overcome an Eleventh Amendment bar. The Court leaves ordinary defenses and liability for the alleged injuries to proceedings on remand.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Lake Country Estates, Inc. v. Tahoe Regional Planning Agency*, 440 U.S. 391 (1979), applies its entity-specific compact inquiry: exercising state power does not itself make a bistate corporation the States, and the compact’s actual structure matters.
- *Mt. Healthy City School District Board of Education v. Doyle*, 429 U.S. 274 (1977), preserves the distinction between a separate public entity and an arm of a State; a governmental function alone supplies no immunity.
- *Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp. v. Feeney*, 495 U.S. 299 (1990), is distinguished as deciding federal-forum consent while assuming, rather than deciding, PATH’s arm status. Its consent ruling is not an earlier holding that PATH is immune.
- *Hilton v. South Carolina Public Railways Commission*, 502 U.S. 197 (1991), preserves state-court FELA coverage without constructive waiver of federal-forum immunity; no abrogation or waiver rule is enlarged here.
- *Puerto Rico Aqueduct & Sewer Authority v. Metcalf & Eddy, Inc.*, 506 U.S. 139 (1993), preserves immediate review of a qualifying immunity denial without establishing that a particular entity is an immune arm. Its appealability holding does not decide PATH’s status.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not declare every compact entity nonimmune, make a debt disclaimer alone dispositive, decide a materially different entity’s practical dependence, abolish state sovereign immunity or hold that FELA abrogates federal-forum immunity. Negligence, causation, damages, the Boiler Inspection Act merits, every independently applicable nonimmunity limitations defense and hypothetical consent exceptions remain undecided.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective November 14, 1994, PATH is not an arm entitled to the States’ Eleventh Amendment immunity from these employee actions. Lake Country’s compact inquiry is clarified through the actual separate legal and financial structure, with treasury exposure carrying great weight but no universal sole-treasury test. The Third Circuit’s contrary PATH-status rule cannot sustain these dismissals. FELA coverage, consent of an entity that actually is immune, immunity abrogation and collateral-order appealability remain separate and unchanged.

## Lane v. Peña, No. 95-365

Source: terms/OT1995/output/OT_1995CHUNK9.md

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Souter delivered the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg. Justice Stevens dissented, joined in full by Justice Breyer.

#### Holdings

**Federal program coverage does not supply the missing consent to compensatory damages.** Sections 504(a) and 505 of the Rehabilitation Act, read with §1003 of the Rehabilitation Act Amendments of 1986, do not unequivocally waive the United States' immunity from Lane's claim for compensatory damages arising from this federally conducted academy program. The damages judgment is affirmed; the existing discrimination determination and reinstatement remain undisturbed.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg; seven Justices adopt the rule, explanation and limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress imposed §504(a)'s nondiscrimination duty on programs conducted by Executive agencies and the Postal Service. Section 505(a)(2), however, supplies Title VI remedies to persons aggrieved by recipients or federal providers of financial assistance. That assistance-related formulation does not unequivocally expose every federal program operator to compensatory damages. Nordic Village requires enacted consent to the particular money judgment; Ohio requires an incorporated remedial provision's limits to receive independent effect. A substantive prohibition and an affirmative obligation to pay from the Treasury are distinct.

The combined-enactment argument does not cure that difference. Section 505(a)(1) separately supplies Title VII remedies for §501 federal employment complaints, while §505(b) permits a reasonable attorney's fee as costs, in the court's discretion, to a prevailing party other than the United States. Neither channel converts this academy enrollment claim into a federal employment case or a general damages waiver. Section 1003 removes specified State immunity and compares remedies against States with remedies against other public or private entities. Its public-entity reference can encompass nonfederal recipients; its comparison does not independently grant every remedy against every sovereign. The Court need not choose among all possible readings of that State-directed comparison to find the claimed federal consent unclear.

Franklin permits compensation for proved intentional discrimination in the established Title IX action, subject to recipient responsibility, causation and compensable loss; it did not supply consent to this federal Treasury award. Their satisfaction is not decided because waiver fails. Meyer likewise keeps waiver, cause of action and entitlement to recovery distinct. Legislative purpose and the desirability of compensation cannot supply missing enacted clarity. This conclusion neither licenses discrimination nor removes independently authorized prospective relief. It decides no differently situated federal assistance-provider claim, fee application, new officer action or implied damages cause.

#### Separate Writings

Justice Stevens dissents, joined by Justice Breyer. In their view, Congress's express extension of §504 to federal programs carries the remedial setting in which that prohibition already operated. Section 505's differentiated enforcement channels codify particular means of enforcement without withdrawing ordinary damages for the newly express federal obligation; the 1986 recognition of legal and equitable remedies reinforces that reading. They accept that consent must come from enacted law and that Franklin alone cannot waive immunity, but find the combined provisions sufficient and distinguish Nordic Village's ambiguous bankruptcy formulation. Treating §505's assistance language as a restriction on the separate federal-program obligation gives the remedial scheme an unwarranted gap. They would reverse the categorical damages bar and remand for litigation of entitlement, amount and any remaining lawful defense. They would neither award damages automatically nor reopen the injunction.

## Wisconsin Department of Corrections v. Schacht, No. 97-461

Source: terms/OT1997/output/OT_1997CHUNK11.md

#### Opinions and Joins

Justice Thomas delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine join the federal-question removal rule, claim-specific immunity analysis, construction of §1447(c), reservations and vacatur with remand.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Immunity affecting one claim does not eliminate removal jurisdiction over the remaining federal claims

**Controlling proposition:** An action presenting substantial federal claims within §1331 is removable under §1441(a) despite an Eleventh Amendment bar applicable to particular State or official-capacity claims. The bar prevents adjudication of the affected claim when properly asserted, but does not erase otherwise valid original and removal jurisdiction over the remaining federal claims or require remand of the entire action under §1447(c).

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court directly adopts the rule, explanation, reservations and appellate remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The complaint itself supplies a federal-question basis independent of the asserted immunity. Hafer distinguishes a claim against a State from a personal-capacity claim for an officer’s own violation. Immunity affecting the former does not make the latter cease to present federal law. City of Chicago likewise separates the federal anchor for removal from questions concerning particular claims and their later treatment.

State immunity can be waived and does not automatically destroy every federal claim in a case. It therefore differs from failure of complete diversity where that is the sole original-jurisdiction basis. That distinction does not classify immunity as ordinary personal jurisdiction in every respect or establish waiver here.

Even assuming immunity counts as subject-matter jurisdiction for this analysis, §1447(c)’s direction to remand a case lacking such jurisdiction does not require erasing the independent federal claims. The statute’s separate thirty-day rule for a nonjurisdictional removal-defect motion creates no new defect or timeliness finding in this litigation. The Court need not decide the general jurisdictional classification of Eleventh Amendment immunity.

Removal authority must be established and immunity and any genuinely remaining remand questions treated at their proper claim-specific scope. The actual complaint contains federal claims; City of Chicago’s supplemental-claim discussion is an analogy, not an additional claim here. Wisconsin’s waiver by removing, the merits of the personal-capacity claims, any new relief against the State and compulsory retention of every hypothetical supplemental claim remain undecided. The unappealed dismissals stay outside the returned appeal.

#### Law After Decision

An immune claim does not make State immunity spread to every otherwise removable federal claim in the action. Federal courts must distinguish the statutory jurisdictional anchor from immunity and remaining claim-specific questions. The decision creates no removal-waiver rule, new State liability or automatic merits disposition.

## Public authority cedar_rapids_v_garret_f_merits_1999-03-03.md

Source: terms/OT1998/entering-law/cedar_rapids_v_garret_f_merits_1999-03-03.md

## Opinion Topology
| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Part I: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Part II: O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. | Affirmance | I: physician boundary, educational necessity, rejection of an added burden exclusion and qualified remedy. II: funding notice. |
| Dissent | Thomas | Kennedy | Would vacate federal affirmance and remand | Medical character and clear-notice grounds; independent Iowa law and the higher-state-standard argument remain undecided. |

Stone-Zsela agrees with rejecting the Spending Clause funding-notice objection without adding a ground, and joins Part I in full. No separate opinion is filed by him.

#### The existing statutory service obligation supplies clear funding notice

**Operative rule.** The express IDEA related-services duty, the qualified school-health regulation and Tatro's established construction provide clear notice of the covered nonphysician service obligation at issue here. Continuous or substantial need does not by itself make that specified condition an unannounced funding obligation.

**Authority.** Stevens's Part II is joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Six direct joins control this independently stated answer to the Spending Clause objection. Stone-Zsela agrees in rejecting the objection without joining its rationale.

**Controlling explanation.** Pennhurst and New York preserve the requirement that funding conditions be stated clearly. Tatro and the school-health regulation preceded the school-year expenses at issue. They identify the service category and retain educational necessity, rather than promising all medically useful care under a general educational purpose.

Suter, Franklin, Blessing and Gebser address distinct rights, enforcement or remedial questions; they neither abolish clear notice nor create categorical immunity from specific service conditions. This case uses IDEA's existing individual service and review framework. It does not imply a new section 1983 right, import Title IX attribution rules or create a general program-adequacy injunction. The district's contrary reading depends on rejecting Tatro's construction; it does not establish that continuity itself negates an otherwise stated condition.

## Public authority west_v_gibson_merits_1999-06-14.md

Source: terms/OT1998/entering-law/west_v_gibson_merits_1999-06-14.md

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joining Justices | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg, Breyer | Complete opinion and both holdings |
| Dissent | Kennedy | Scalia, Thomas | Would affirm for lack of unequivocal authority for administrative compensatory awards |

### The EEOC may administer the compensation Congress expressly authorized

When federal-sector Title VII intentional discrimination falls within 42 U.S.C. §1981a's damages authorization, §2000e-16(b) permits the EEOC to award that compensation as an appropriate remedy. The agency must preserve §1981a's substantive conditions and monetary limits; its power does not depend on an administrative jury, and a proper court action retains the statutory jury election.

Section 717(b) delegates enforcement through appropriate remedies and introduces the listed equitable forms as examples. Congress subsequently added compensation to the remedies for intentional discrimination under §717. Reading those enactments together gives effect to both the express monetary authorization and the administrative mechanism Congress retained. Section 1981a's reference to an action does not silently confine §717 as a whole to its judicial subsection. Its jury clause regulates trial in court; it neither requires a jury in an administrative proceeding nor creates a governmental appeal from an adverse EEOC award. The Government's inability to demand judicial review therefore does not negate the separately authorized administrative remedy. This conclusion satisfies Lane's requirement of unequivocal enacted consent and Blue Fox's demand for a genuine statutory entitlement; administrative convenience supplies no independent waiver.

The compensation category applies to unlawful intentional discrimination, not disparate impact, and requires that the complaining party cannot recover under §1981. It excludes backpay, interest on backpay and other relief authorized by §706(g). The statutory caps remain applicable, and punitive damages are unavailable against governments, government agencies and political subdivisions. When available §102 damages are sought in court, any party may demand a jury; the court must not tell that jury the statutory caps. No damages under another statute or free-standing appropriations authority follow.
