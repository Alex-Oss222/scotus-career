## Aguirre-Aguirre — applicable law and agency analysis

The relevant provision is former 8 U.S.C. §1253(h) (1994 ed.), not the later codification standing alone. Subsection (h)(1) bars deportation or return to a country where the Attorney General determines that life or freedom would be threatened for race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. Under (h)(2)(C), this withholding rule does not apply if the Attorney General determines there are serious reasons for considering that the alien committed a serious nonpolitical crime outside the United States before arrival. The inquiry is not a criminal conviction requirement.

The parties agreed IIRIRA did not govern. AEDPA §413(f)'s new discretion was also inapplicable because the BIA's March 5, 1996 decision was final agency action before April 24, 1996. Under the law governing this case, asylum remained a distinct discretionary request even where withholding was unavailable. The administrative authority provisions are 8 U.S.C. §1103(a)(1) and 8 C.F.R. §3.1(d)(1) (1998).

The immigration judge granted asylum and withholding. The BIA sustained the INS appeal, vacated that order and ordered deportation. It did not decide whether Aguirre showed the requisite persecution risk; it applied the serious-nonpolitical-crime exception assuming the possibility of that showing. The BIA's McMullen framework compared the offense's political aspect with its common-law character and treated gross disproportionality to the political objective or atrocious acts as circumstances in which the political aspect would not predominate. It found the criminal character predominant here because destruction of property and assaults on civilians disproportionately manifested the group's dissatisfaction. It separately declined discretionary asylum because of acts against innocent Guatemalans.

The published hearing recitation reports testimony to participation in approximately ten bus burnings after gasoline was applied; passengers were first ordered off, with those refusing subjected to stones, sticks or ropes. Store activity included breaking windows, removing people and throwing goods on the floor. The violence is testimony and agency characterization, not a new judicial finding by this Court. The Ninth Circuit, 121 F.3d 521 (1997), granted review and remanded, requiring additional consideration of feared persecution, disproportionality/atrociousness and political necessity/success. This describes the reviewed decision only.

## El Paso / Neztsosie — injunction and appeal scope

In 1995 the Neztsosies sued El Paso and Rare Metals in the Navajo Nation Tuba City District Court under Navajo tort law, alleging injuries from uranium mining exposures. Richards filed her related wrongful-death and consortium suit in 1996 in the Navajo Nation Kayenta District Court. These are allegations; the recitation does not establish liability or Price-Anderson coverage.

The companies separately sought federal injunctions in the District of Arizona. The district court denied preliminary injunctions **except insofar as the tribal plaintiffs sought relief under the Price-Anderson Act**. Thus the court did enter partial injunctions against pursuit of Price-Anderson claims. It nevertheless left the initial determination whether the pleaded claims actually fell under the Act to the tribal courts. Both companies appealed; neither tribal plaintiff appealed the partial injunction against her.

On the consolidated appeals, the Ninth Circuit affirmed the refusal to enjoin non-Price-Anderson claims and the decision to allow tribal courts to decide initial statutory coverage. It also, on its own initiative and invoking comity, reversed the partial injunctions despite the absence of plaintiffs' appeals. This produces two distinct review questions: the appellate alteration in favor of nonappealing parties and whether the federal district court must itself decide coverage before deferring. No final finding that all pleaded tort claims are covered is supplied by this posture.

The Act's relevant architecture: 42 U.S.C. §2014(w) defines public liability, generally liability arising from a nuclear incident or precautionary evacuation; §2014(q) defines nuclear incident by injury or loss arising from specified hazardous properties of source, special nuclear or byproduct material; §2014(hh) defines a public liability action and supplies its federal-law characterization; §2210(n)(2) supplies original federal jurisdiction, removal of pending state-court actions and consolidation mechanics. The express removal language refers to state court; whether/how the federal scheme operates for tribal-court actions is a question presented, not an assumed statutory command to tribal courts.

Source: published recitation, 526 U.S. 473, Part I and the description of the parties' requests at the beginning of Part III; 136 F.3d 610, 613–620.

## Saenz — material posture correction

The judgment under review is **Roe v. Anderson, 134 F.3d 1400 (9th Cir. 1998)**, reviewing a **preliminary injunction** entered by District Judge David F. Levi in the Eastern District of California. The Supreme Court route is certiorari to the Ninth Circuit. The neutral packet's description of a three-judge district court permanent injunction is inaccurate. The packet's supplemental citation 13 F. Supp. 2d 1036 should not substitute for the circuit judgment under review without separate identification.

On April 1, 1997, the two respondents filed this action challenging California's durational residency rule and federal statutory authorization. The district court issued a temporary restraining order, certified a class and subsequently issued the preliminary injunction. The Ninth Circuit affirmed the preliminary injunction without finally deciding the merits, finding probability of success and possible irreparable harm. The reviewed litigation is distinct from the earlier Green litigation concerning a federal waiver.

The program statute is California Welfare and Institutions Code §11450.03. The pertinent federal provision is 42 U.S.C. §604(c) (1994 ed., Supp. II), permitting a TANF State to apply another State's TANF rules, including benefit amounts, to a family that moved from that State and has resided in the new State for less than 12 months. Permission is not a federal command to adopt the differential. California's implementing All County Letter applied the lower prior-State benefit regardless of whether the family previously received welfare and regardless of motive for migration; it did not apply the durational requirement to families newly arriving from another country. The examples concerning a resident who leaves and returns describe the implementing instructions, not an independent constitutional finding.
