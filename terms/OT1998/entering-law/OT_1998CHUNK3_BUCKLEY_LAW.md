## Reading selection: Holdings: McIntyre

#### McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-986.
**Decided:** April 19, 1995.

##### Compelled identification on these political leaflets requires exacting scrutiny

**Holding:** A content-defined requirement that an individual identify herself on ordinary political issue leaflets burdens core political expression, including the choice to speak anonymously. The State must satisfy exacting scrutiny by showing narrow tailoring to an overriding state interest; viewpoint neutrality or the election setting alone does not lower that standard.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens), joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven Justices; Thomas concurs in the judgment only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies protection of anonymous handbills to this individual political leaflet; the Court does not establish immunity for fraudulent communications.
- Burson v. Freeman, May 26, 1992: applies the independently majority-supported scrutiny distinction; its fractured polling-place validity judgment does not settle this leaflet requirement.
- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780: distinguishes the election-administration setting; it does not reduce scrutiny for every election-related speech restriction.

##### The asserted voter-information interest does not justify this identification demand

**Holding:** A general interest in helping voters evaluate a source does not justify requiring this ordinary individual to place her name and residence or business address on her independently produced, truthful political leaflet. The Court leaves candidate finance, campaign committees, organizational spending, and other materially distinct disclosure settings for separate analysis.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens), joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven Justices; Thomas concurs in the judgment only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies protection for the ordinary author's choice of anonymous publication.
- Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1: distinguishes campaign-finance reporting and its specific interests; those disclosure holdings remain undisturbed.
- First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765: distinguishes corporate-advertising source information; its observation does not resolve this private individual's leaflet.

##### Fraud prevention does not justify this application to truthful independent advocacy

**Holding:** Although preventing electoral fraud is a weighty interest, Ohio may not impose this categorical identification requirement on truthful, independently produced individual issue leaflets without the necessary narrow fit to that interest. The enforcement order and $100 fine must be set aside; properly tailored fraud, impersonation, and materially different disclosure rules remain available.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens), joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven Justices; Thomas concurs in the judgment only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies anonymous-publication protection while distinguishing measures directed at fraud.
- Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1: leaves candidate-finance disclosure and corruption-related interests intact rather than treating them as dispositive of this ordinary leaflet.
- First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765: leaves materially different corporate source-disclosure questions outside the individual sanction decided here.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Ohio's judgment is reversed, 8–1, and the enforcement order and $100 fine must be set aside. No damages or invalidation of every disclosure provision is ordered; candidate, committee, organizational and other materially different disclosure settings remain outside this application.

## Reading selection: Standard: Write-in voting within the complete ballot-access system

### Write-in voting within the complete ballot-access system

**Governing question:** Whether a ban on write-in voting violates electoral-choice or independent expressive rights.

**Current rule:** Under the existing Anderson inquiry, the court assesses the character and magnitude of the actual injury against the State's precise interests and the extent to which those interests require the burden. Severe burdens require narrow tailoring to a compelling interest; reasonable, nondiscriminatory burdens ordinarily may be justified by important regulatory interests. When the complete access system imposes only reasonable burdens, a write-in prohibition is presumptively valid and may be sustained by proportionate interests, including primary finality and prevention of evasive candidacies or party raiding. The ballot need not provide an official count and publication of votes for fictional figures or arbitrary protest messages as an independent expressive entitlement.

**Authority by component:**
- Governing inherited framework: Burdick v. Takushi, “1. Review of election regulations follows the actual burden”, June 8, 1992; White, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas (five), applying Anderson and the earlier simulated Norman rule.
- Limited-burden write-in presumption: Burdick, “2. Hawaii's complete write-in prohibition is valid in its reasonably accessible system,” June 8, 1992; same five Justices.
- Arbitrary-message claim: Burdick, “3. The ballot need not serve as an official tally of arbitrary political messages,” June 8, 1992; same controlling five-Justice opinion, with all nine agreeing in rejection of that component.

**Present operation effective:** Burdick v. Takushi, June 8, 1992, for the write-in presumption and expressive boundary; the underlying Anderson framework is applied unchanged.

**Exceptions and limits:** No absolute validation of all write-in bans follows. Materially more restrictive access systems may impose severe burdens. The rule does not replace strict scrutiny of content-based campaign restrictions on public sidewalks.

**Holdings navigation:** Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428 (1992) — Ballot access and political parties.

## Reading selection: Holdings: Timmons

#### Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-1608.
**Decided:** 1997-04-28.

For the separate questions in Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party, 1997-04-28, see Federal Courts — Mootness and conditional reenactment.

##### This limitation is justified within the complete nomination system

**Holding:** Minnesota may prevent this otherwise qualified minor party from placing its consenting nominee on the ballot under its name when he also seeks or holds the other major party's nomination, where the complete system leaves the candidate available and the party able to nominate other candidates, endorse and campaign. In this application the burden is less than severe and proportionate interests in ballot integrity, defined nomination choices and a stable electoral system justify the restriction under Anderson and Burdick.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas's Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Breyer; five direct joins.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The challenged 1994 §204B.06 subdivision 1(b) barred an affidavit when another affidavit was on file for any office at the same primary or next general election. Section 204B.04 subdivision 2 barred petition nomination for the same office by a primary candidate, subject to two distinct exceptions. Under §204D.10 subdivision 2, if any major-party primary candidate received ten percent of the average vote for the party's state officers at the last state general election in the relevant district, its highest-vote candidates became nominees; if none met that threshold, none were nominated and all could seek petition nomination under §§204B.07–.09. The relevant state officers were governor, lieutenant governor, secretary of state, state auditor, state treasurer and attorney general. The §204B.13 subdivision 4 nonpartisan-vacancy exception allowed a petition within one week of the vacancy and no later than four calendar days before election; an eligible voter could sign despite voting in the primary or signing another petition. Neither exception applied to Dawkins. The distinct ban on nomination by two major parties is not decided.

The conditional replacement permitted a major-party and one-or-more-minor-party nomination for the same partisan office with written consent of the parties' state chairs; petition nomination also required the candidate's written consent. Losing the major-party primary forfeited the simultaneous minor-party nominations, and a party candidate could not also file as an independent. It retained the nonpartisan-vacancy exception, barred a defeated major-party primary candidate except to fill a §204B.13 vacancy, and repealed the old primary-turnout provision. Its affidavit rule referred to any other office, required petition acceptance and the consent forms, and contained a distinct dual-office exception for a soil-and-water district supervisor outside Anoka, Hennepin, Ramsey and Washington counties and a mayor/council or town-supervisor office in a city or town of no more than 2,500 people wholly or partly within the district. That different-office exception is not fusion. A multiply nominated candidate's name appeared once with party names; major/minor nomination used the major-party position and multiple-minor nomination the first filing party's position. Votes for multiply nominated candidates were excluded from the specified party-status qualification or retention counts. These features neither establish compliance in this case nor receive independent constitutional approval.

The Court recognizes no right to duplicate votes, separate party vote totals or a particular ballot design and adjudicates no independent challenge to consent, counting, format, support or deadline rules.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Anderson and Burdick retain contextual burden, precise interest and fit review. Norman's authorized expansion and independent-candidate protections and Colorado Republican's independent campaign speech remain distinct. Tashjian and Eu protect association without compelling every ballot mechanism; Storer, Jenness and Munro permit reasonable preventive selection rules without proof of past disruption. No voter-incapacity premise, incumbent-protection rationale, pure rational-basis rule or universal anti-fusion approval is adopted.

## Reading selection: Holdings: Kentucky Right to Life paid identification

##### The paid candidate-advertisement identification rule survives this facial challenge

**Holding:** The surviving facial challenge to §121.190(1)'s payer-identification requirement fails in the paid candidate-finance and organizational setting presented. The rule requires the payer's name and address, introduced by the paid-for-by designation, on newspaper and magazine advertising, posters, circulars, billboards, handbills, sample ballots and paid television or radio announcements expressly advocating election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate, slate or group of candidates; ordinary issue advocacy is not covered merely because it mentions a candidate.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor). Part IV of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight Justices. Thomas dissents.

**Material application:** The Court holds that identifying the financer of organized paid candidate advocacy serves source-tracing and enforcement interests different from general curiosity about an ordinary issue leafleteer's identity. The address helps identify the legal payer and connect an advertisement to financial transactions; it is a burden requiring justification, not dispensable statutory language. Buckley's disclosure interests and McIntyre's express reservation of materially different campaign-finance and organizational settings permit this bounded facial judgment. The on-message command is more intrusive than filing a report, but the actual financer-identification function supplies the necessary connection here without limiting the amount or content of the independent expenditure. The State cannot presume corruption or coordination merely from an advertisement's independence or political identity. McIntyre continues to protect ordinary anonymous issue leaflets, and materially different individual applications remain open. So does a claim supported by a reasonable probability of threats or reprisals. No actual retaliation, coordination, general membership-disclosure authority or requirement of permission before publication is found.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** McIntyre is distinguished on its reserved candidate-finance and organizational context without diminishing its individual-leaflet rule. Buckley supplies the distinct finance-disclosure interests. Colorado Republican preserves independence and forbids identity-based substitution for an actual candidate connection. Brown v. Socialist Workers preserves protection against demonstrated associational harms without creating a new blanket exemption.

## Reading selection: Published McIntyre separate positions

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Thomas concurs in the judgment. Talley's protection and the historical expressive tradition it recognizes support an individual's anonymous political pamphlet. The asserted justification cannot sustain a penalty imposed solely for missing identification on this ordinary leaflet. He does not adopt the Court's modern value-balancing and content-based-scrutiny methodology as his own ground. His agreement in this judgment therefore adds no vote to the Court's complete scrutiny explanation, and his writing does not invalidate every financing or organizational disclosure rule.

Justice Scalia dissents. Election-specific attribution is a limited accountability requirement that helps voters identify a communication's source and assists enforcement against electoral wrongdoing. Buckley's disclosure principles and Bellotti's source-information discussion support that safeguard in his view. Talley's general prohibition covering all anonymous handbills is distinguishable from Ohio's election category. He recognizes the expressive burden but regards the State's justification as sufficient here; he would sustain the fine. He preserves appropriate protection where a speaker establishes threats or reprisals, without finding that McIntyre experienced either. He does not endorse a general power to forbid anonymous discussion outside the election setting.
