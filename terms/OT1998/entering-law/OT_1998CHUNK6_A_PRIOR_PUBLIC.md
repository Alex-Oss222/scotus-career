### Heller v. Doe, No. 92-351

Merits disposition, 1993-06-24

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-03-22; decided 1993-06-24. On writ of certiorari to Sixth Circuit, Doe v. Cowherd, 965 F.2d 109 (1992).

**Question:** Whether Kentucky’s different burdens of proof and family-participation rules for involuntary commitment of persons with intellectual disability and persons with mental illness violate equal protection, and whether family party status violates procedural due process.

**Material record:** Kentucky required proof of dangerousness, likely benefit from treatment, and a least restrictive placement predicate in the relevant commitment proceedings. For the category described in the then-operative statute as “mental retardation,” proof was clear and convincing and guardians or immediate family could participate as parties. For mental illness, proof was beyond a reasonable doubt and that party status was not supplied. The class challenged these differences. The medical and developmental distinctions offered as rationales concern typical onset, diagnostic history, prediction, and the information held by families; they are not individual findings that any class member is dangerous or incompetent.

**Relief sought:** Kentucky seeks reversal of the challenged federal rulings. The committed class seeks affirmance. Other district-court grounds outside the questions under review remain separate.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Affirmed in part, reversed in part and remanded. Unequal proof burdens invalidated, 5–4; categorical invalidation of family-party participation reversed, 6–3.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The lower overall proof protection lacks a rational relation to the disadvantage imposed

**Controlling proposition:** Kentucky’s clear-and-convincing burden for involuntary commitment of adults with intellectual disability, compared with proof beyond a reasonable doubt for mental illness, violates equal protection on the preserved rational-basis challenge: the offered diagnostic, prediction and treatment differences do not rationally justify assigning this class the greater risk of mistaken confinement. Addington’s federal minimum remains unchanged; the remedy must address lawful equalization and severability rather than impose a national reasonable-doubt standard or order every class member released.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statutory distinction must relate to the particular procedural disadvantage. Cleburne permits relevant distinctions between conditions but does not allow a label to substitute for that relationship. A longer history may make diagnosis or some predictions easier. Commitment, however, also requires dangerousness, likely treatment benefit and a least restrictive placement; the interests at stake remain physical liberty and protection from erroneous confinement. Addington explains the allocation of that error risk and supplies a constitutional minimum, not immunity from an equality challenge when a State provides greater protection to another class. The asserted difference in the intrusiveness of treatment likewise does not adequately explain the lower overall safeguard against confinement. Beach does not demand formal findings or perfect empirical fit, and the Court imposes neither requirement. It rejects the proffered relationship, not legislative generalization as such. The appropriate limited remedy must respect severability and the State’s lawful choices, while every continuing confinement still needs an independent lawful basis.

**Precedent treatment:**

- City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S.432: rational relationship to the actual disadvantage required; no new scrutiny tier adopted.
- Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S.418: the clear-and-convincing minimum survives; unequal safeguards remain separately reviewable.
- FCC v. Beach Communications, June 1, 1993: no formal-findings or empirical-certainty requirement imposed; its specific economic justification does not decide this classification.
- Foucha, May 18, 1992: current lawful confinement predicates preserved; noncontrolling equal-protection reasoning is not promoted to law.

##### Family-party participation has a distinct rational basis

**Controlling proposition:** Kentucky may distinguish the two commitment proceedings by allowing guardians and immediate family members to participate as parties for adults with a lifelong developmental disability. Continuing family knowledge rationally supports that participation; the availability of testimony or a less extensive form of participation does not by itself defeat ordinary rational-basis review.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that gathering useful information is different from assigning a person less protection against erroneous confinement. A developmental condition and sustained family involvement plausibly give relatives and guardians knowledge of abilities, daily functioning and available care that assists the proceeding. Parham recognizes the value of family information without giving a relative authority to decide whether an adult should be confined. Cleburne requires a rational relationship, and that informational relationship supports the participation distinction here. Kentucky need not show that every family has useful knowledge or that no less extensive procedure could work. Nor does the existence of mental illness within some families eliminate the permissible general distinction. The Court does not presume that family members always share the adult’s interests. Their preferences or convenience establish none of the statutory grounds for confinement, and the independent adjudicator must evaluate their evidence under the lawful burden. This holding sustains party participation as a category, not every application of it.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cleburne, 473 U.S.432: relevant differences may support a particular procedural distinction.
- Parham v. J.R., 442 U.S.584: family information may be useful; adult confinement is not delegated to relatives.
- Schweiker v. Wilson, 450 U.S.221: ordinary review does not require the least restrictive legislative alternative.

##### Party participation does not itself deprive the adult of fair adjudication

**Controlling proposition:** Allowing relatives or guardians to participate as parties does not categorically violate procedural due process where the adult has counsel and an effective opportunity to contest adverse evidence before an independent decisionmaker. Relatives may neither supply a substitute for the statutory commitment grounds nor control the decision or suppress the adult’s position.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the challenged party status must be examined within the actual procedural safeguards. Mathews requires consideration of the private interest, the risk of error and value of safeguards, and the government’s interests. The liberty interest is substantial. Kentucky nevertheless provides counsel, professional examination, an opportunity to testify and present evidence, cross-examination and independent adjudication. Relevant family information can improve accuracy when tested through those procedures. The possibility of adverse family interests requires such testing; it does not establish that adding a party inherently makes an accurate decision impossible. Parham does not permit the State to call an adult’s involuntary placement voluntary merely because a relative requested it. The adjudicator must find the statutory grounds under the lawful burden, regardless of the family’s preference. Proceedings that defeat effective contest or independent decisionmaking remain challengeable. The Court reverses the categorical due-process invalidation without determining that every class member’s hearing was fair.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S.319: private interest, error risk and safeguards, and government interests applied separately from equality review.
- Parham, 442 U.S.584: informational participation distinguished from final decisional control.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 24, the proof distinction is invalid under rational-basis review, with severability and lawful equalization left for remand. The family distinction and party status survive the categorical challenges subject to independent adjudication and effective contest. Addington, the substantive confinement predicates and the unresolved heightened-scrutiny question remain distinct. Kentucky’s repealed voluntary-admission rule and other unreviewed grounds are not decided.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy, joined by White, Scalia and Thomas, would sustain the proof distinction. They regard easier diagnosis, a longer behavioral history supporting prediction, and generally less intrusive treatment as independently sufficient rational grounds. Addington’s minimum is met, and ordinary review permits imperfect generalizations. Souter, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, would also invalidate family-party status: useful information can be obtained through witnesses, while party status adds an adversary able to call and cross-examine witnesses and appeal a refusal to confine. They find no rational connection between that additional burden and the different condition and therefore do not reach due process. Blackmun separately preserves his view that disability classifications and substantial restraints on liberty warrant heightened review, while accepting that the proof distinction fails the ordinary standard used here.

### Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, No. 92-102

Merits disposition, 1993-06-28

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-03-30; decided 1993-06-28. On writ of certiorari to Ninth Circuit, 951 F.2d 1128 (1991).

**Question:** Whether the Federal Rules of Evidence displaced Frye’s general-acceptance prerequisite for scientific expert evidence and what admissibility inquiry governs the causation evidence offered in this federal diversity action.

**Material record:** Two children with limb-reduction birth defects and their parents allege that maternal Bendectin use caused the defects. The manufacturer relies on published epidemiological research showing no demonstrated association. Plaintiffs offer expert opinions drawing on animal studies, chemical-structure analysis, and reanalysis of epidemiological data. The courts below treated the lack of general acceptance and unpublished, unreviewed reanalyses as grounds for exclusion. No jury has resolved causation. The current record does not establish that every proposed method is reliable merely because the experts are qualified.

**Relief sought:** The children and their parents seek vacatur and reconsideration under the Federal Rules. Merrell Dow seeks affirmance because the proposed evidence lacks an adequate scientific foundation and cannot support causation.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Vacated and remanded, 9–0. Frye’s mandatory acceptance prerequisite is displaced unanimously; eight Justices adopt the flexible scientific-foundation and fit rule.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The enacted Rules displace mandatory general acceptance

**Controlling proposition:** Frye’s mandatory general-acceptance prerequisite does not govern admission of scientific expert evidence in federal trials under the Federal Rules of Evidence. Acceptance remains potentially relevant under Rule 702, but its absence does not alone require exclusion and its presence does not dispense with the Rules’ other requirements.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted Rules supply the federal admissibility standard. Rule 402 generally admits relevant evidence subject to applicable exceptions, and Rule 702 addresses qualified expert knowledge that assists the factfinder without imposing a consensus condition. Beech Aircraft requires interpretation of these Rules as enacted law, while Abel permits compatible common-law guidance rather than a competing prerequisite. Bourjaily illustrates that an incompatible earlier doctrine does not survive merely because it was familiar before the Rules. Frye’s rigid condition therefore cannot exclude a proffer simply because it is new, unpublished or disputed within the relevant community. The contrary assertion that the Rules silently preserved Frye finds no support in their text or the identified drafting history. This displacement does not require admission of unsupported expertise, resolve the children’s causation claims, or establish that every scientific disagreement belongs before a jury. The Court vacates the judgment based on the wrong screening rule and leaves the actual proffers for proper evaluation below.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Frye v. United States, 293 F.1013: displaced as a mandatory prerequisite in federal trials; not a Supreme Court Rule 702 holding being overruled.
- Beech Aircraft v. Rainey, 488 U.S.153: statutory interpretation of enacted Rules applied.
- United States v. Abel, 469 U.S.45: compatible common-law guidance retained, incompatible prerequisite rejected.
- Bourjaily v. United States, 483 U.S.171: Rules supersede incompatible common law.

##### Reliable scientific foundation and fit require flexible preliminary screening

**Controlling proposition:** Under original Rules 104(a) and 702, the proponent must establish by a preponderance the preliminary foundation for scientifically reliable knowledge that fits the disputed factual question and will assist the factfinder. The judge examines principles, methodology and valid application using relevant nonexclusive considerations—testability and testing, peer review/publication, meaningful error information and operational standards, and acceptance—without making any one consideration an invariant prerequisite; admissibility does not determine ultimate causation or sufficiency.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (8 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that scientific knowledge requires more than credentials or unsupported assertion. Rule 702’s knowledge and assistance requirements demand a defensible scientific foundation and a valid connection to the offered inference. Bourjaily supplies the preliminary preponderance burden under Rule 104(a), distinct from the substantive trial burden. Testing, peer scrutiny, error information, standards and acceptance can illuminate reliability, but publication is neither indispensable nor conclusive. A method valid for one purpose may not support another: the lower courts must examine the link between the offered studies or reanalysis and the human-causation proposition. The contrary concern that judges must become final arbiters of scientific truth mistakes preliminary admissibility for ultimate factfinding. Cross-examination and contrary evidence remain appropriate for shaky but admissible proof; they do not replace the judge’s screening duty. Rules 403, 703 and 706 and the ordinary judgment rules remain available. The Court neither decides which experts qualify here nor extends this scientific discussion to every technical specialty, legislative fact or appellate review question.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bourjaily v. United States, 483 U.S.171, 175–176: preponderance for preliminary admissibility applied, not a substantive causation burden.
- Rock v. Arkansas, 483 U.S.44: adversarial testing remains appropriate for admissible evidence; no excuse for unsupported foundation.
- United States v. Downing, 753 F.2d 1224: helpful fit and flexible reliability discussion informs interpretation, not a binding Supreme Court multifactor checklist.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 28, Frye’s federal consensus prerequisite is displaced 9–0; reliability/fit and flexible preliminary screening govern by eight direct joins. Rules 104(a)/702 burden is preponderance, not scientific certainty. No later Rule 702 text, universal technical expertise rule, appellate review standard or substantive civil rights evidence test enters.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens concurs in displacement and the vacatur but declines the further scientific-method discussion. He accepts some judicial screening responsibility yet considers abstract guidance insufficiently tied to a decision admitting or excluding a particular proffer; lower courts should develop the issue on concrete records. Stone concurs in the Court’s operative standard and emphasizes that an unpublished reanalysis must explain its data, choices and inference, while animal studies require a defensible bridge to the human proposition. Data quality is relevant rather than a universal extra element; litigation origin and scientific minority status do not establish unreliability. He distinguishes expert admissibility from legislative facts and substantive civil rights standards: no consensus prerequisite for constitutional enforcement, universal statistical significance level, mandatory regression model or retrospective change to earlier voting cases follows.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review of this merits event is complete. Reevaluate material expert proffers under original Rules and reconsider summary judgment; further fair foundational submissions may be received. No finding of causation or direction to admit all experts. Ordinary mandate procedure applies; no special issuance date, later filing or retained Supreme Court proceeding is established.

### FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc., No. 92-603

Merits decision, 1993-06-01

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-03-29; decided 1993-06-01. Review of the District of Columbia Circuit, 965 F.2d 1103 (1992).

Question: Whether the Cable Act’s exemption for facilities serving multiple-unit dwellings under common ownership, control or management, without using a public right-of-way, survives the reviewed equal-protection challenge.

Satellite master antenna television operators challenged the distinction between systems serving commonly controlled buildings and systems serving separately owned buildings. After an earlier remand for an explanation, the court of appeals found the distinction unsupported and required extension of the exemption. The 1992 Cable Act renumbered the relevant definition from §522(6) to §522(7); that citation change did not enact the court of appeals’ constitutional conclusion. The reviewed exemption applies only to facilities serving subscribers in one or more multiple-unit dwellings under common ownership, control or management, unless the facilities use a public right-of-way. Separate First Amendment scrutiny was not adjudicated below on the new statutory footing.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated in argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9–0 in the judgment.

| Equal protection of the ownership-based exemption | Reverse: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9); no dissent | The ownership distinction survives ordinary rational-basis review; further proceedings may address the separate questions not decided here. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Reenactment does not moot the live constitutional controversy

**Controlling proposition:** Congress’s reenactment of the cable definition without changing the challenged distinction does not adopt a lower court’s constitutional invalidation or eliminate the live controversy defended by the FCC. The Court therefore reaches the reviewed equal-protection question.

**Authority:** Thomas’s threshold opinion; White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the parties continue to dispute the validity of an operative statutory distinction. Congress’s failure to alter that distinction while revising other portions of the cable legislation does not turn the court of appeals’ judgment into an act of Congress. A legislative adoption inference sometimes used to understand statutory language does not establish that Congress enacted a judicial conclusion about constitutional validity. The FCC continues to defend the distinction, and reversal would alter the parties’ rights under the exemption. The amendment therefore leaves a concrete controversy rather than an advisory question. This conclusion decides only the proposed ground for mootness. It does not treat congressional silence as approval of every agency interpretation, decide the new legislation’s constitutionality, or resolve the separate speech objections. Those objections must be addressed through the questions and record actually presented for their consideration.

**Precedent treatment:**

- The lower court’s constitutional judgment is reviewed, not treated as legislation adopted by congressional silence; no earlier controlling decision is overruled.

##### Common-owner bargaining and regulatory costs sustain the economic line

**Controlling proposition:** For an economic classification involving neither a suspect class nor a fundamental right under the question reviewed, a reasonably conceivable legitimate basis suffices; the challenger must negate such a basis, and formal legislative findings are unnecessary. Congress could rationally distinguish these commonly owned, controlled or managed dwellings from separately owned properties because bargaining arrangements and the relationship between regulatory cost and consumer protection differ; Moreno and Cleburne continue to forbid irrational or impermissibly motivated classifications.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Parts I and II-A; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (8 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress could make this ownership distinction without proving that it was the only satisfactory line. Fritz permits economic classifications supported by a conceivable legitimate relationship, and the Nordlinger decision confirms that imperfect fit does not itself defeat rationality. A common owner can bargain for service on behalf of the property and can choose among suppliers before committing the premises. Congress could also conclude that regulating small systems imposes costs disproportionate to the consumer protection obtained. Separate ownership changes those bargaining conditions and permits expansion without the same common control. The absence of a fixed subscriber cap and the existence of atypically large exempt facilities do not negate the general relationship. Neither legislative silence about an actual motive nor the absence of empirical findings shifts the challenger’s burden. Moreno and Cleburne still exclude a forbidden purpose or irrational prejudice; no such defect or settled contrary fact defeats this relationship here. Separate speech scrutiny remains undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States Railroad Retirement Board v. Fritz, 449 U.S.166: conceivable legitimate grounds and imperfect economic fit applied.
- Sullivan v. Stroop, 496 U.S.478: ordinary review of social and economic classifications applied.
- Nordlinger v. Hahn, June 18, 1992: the rational-basis holding applied without converting its particular tax grounds into universal immunity from review.
- Department of Agriculture v. Moreno, 413 U.S.528: illegitimate-purpose limit preserved.
- City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S.432: irrational-prejudice limit preserved.

##### Competitive entry independently supports the ownership distinction

**Controlling proposition:** Congress could independently distinguish separately owned buildings because an incumbent provider can add nearby buildings at a lower marginal cost than a new entrant can incur to install its own reception facilities, creating a plausible first-mover advantage. This conceivable competition concern is sufficient under ordinary rational-basis review without proof that every regulated system possesses monopoly power.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Part II-B; White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court also holds that the ownership line has a sufficient competition rationale independent of the regulatory-cost explanation. A provider already operating a reception facility may connect additional separately owned buildings more cheaply than a new competitor can construct the facilities necessary to serve them. Congress could regard that advantage as a reason to retain franchising oversight. In a commonly owned development, by contrast, a single owner can negotiate for the whole property before selecting a provider. Fritz permits Congress to address such differences through an administrable category even when particular systems do not display the predicted advantage. The argument that all systems avoid public rights-of-way identifies a common characteristic but does not eliminate this different competitive characteristic. The holding does not find actual monopoly power, decide an antitrust claim or require a regulator to prove market dominance in every application. Nor does it decide whether a different standard applies to a separately presented burden on protected speech.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Fritz, 449 U.S.166: a second conceivable legitimate economic basis independently sustains the line.
- Nordlinger, June 18, 1992: multiple sufficient rational grounds and tolerance for imperfect fit applied within ordinary review.

#### Current-Law Effect

Before this decision: ordinary rational-basis review and Nordlinger governed economic classifications, while the cable ownership line was contested. After this decision: the line is sustained by eight Justices on bargaining/regulatory cost and seven on the independently sufficient competition rationale. Seven supply the threshold mootness rationale. No First Amendment holding or heightened-scrutiny resolution enters; no precedent is overruled.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens, concurring in the judgment, accepts a concrete distinction between an owner’s private use of property and reaching beyond that property to participate in a regulated market. He doubts that hypothesized system size or competitive advantage, detached from the actual statutory distinction, provides a satisfactory account. Fritz’s presumption of legitimate, impartial legislative action does not in his view make any imagined relationship sufficient regardless of context. His narrower explanation supports reversal but is noncontrolling.

[Official report and underlying record description, 508 U.S.307](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep508/usrep508307/usrep508307.pdf); [Judgment below, 965 F.2d 1103](https://static.case.law/f2d/965/html/1103-01.html) supplies the actual invalidation and prior-remand posture. The official report at 309–315 identifies the Cable Act renumbering, continuing controversy and reserved First Amendment question.

The 1992 renumbering did not change the challenged ownership distinction. Unrelated provisions of that legislation and separate speech claims are outside this holding. No special mandate date or subsequent lower-court outcome is reported.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment invalidating the ownership distinction is reversed and the case remanded. No exemption is judicially extended to all private-property systems. The Court leaves separate speech claims, any properly presented heightened-scrutiny issue and challenges to particular applications for appropriate proceedings; it does not decide those claims or authorize public-right-of-way use under the exemption.

Supreme Court review is complete. The District of Columbia Circuit receives the case for proceedings consistent with the sustained economic distinction and the reserved questions. No special mandate date is supplied.

### South Dakota v. Bourland, No. 91-2051

Merits disposition, 1993-06-14

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-03-02; decided 1993-06-14. On writ of certiorari to Eighth Circuit, 949 F.2d 984: treaty authority sustained on 104,420 former trust acres; separate inquiry on approximately 18,000 formerly non-Indian fee acres remanded.

The question presented is whether the acquisition and public-recreation statutes abrogated treaty-derived regulation of non-Indian hunting and fishing, and what residual authority remains.

The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty reserved lands for Sioux use and occupation. The Flood Control Act of 1944 authorized the water project and public recreation. The Cheyenne River Act of 1954 transferred specified tribal interests for the project, provided compensation, and reserved stated access, hunting, and fishing rights for the Tribe or its members. The Government also acquired former non-Indian fee lands. The Tribe enforced hunting and fishing rules on the acquired area; South Dakota challenged its authority over non-Indians. The dispute distinguishes ownership, access, treaty-based regulation, and the different histories of the two land categories.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 6–3.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Treaty-derived authority and residual inquiry | Reversed and remanded; 6–3 | White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Souter | Reject treaty-derived exclusion as the sufficient regulatory source; consider unresolved Montana exceptions and any properly established federal authorization. Preserve distinct land histories; no universal exclusive-state-jurisdiction declaration. |

#### Opinion Topology

|---|---|---|---|

| Blackmun | Souter | Dissent on treaty and inherent authority | Would affirm former-trust-land ruling |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Acquisition displaces exclusion-derived regulation

**Controlling proposition:** The Flood Control Act and Cheyenne River Act displaced the treaty right of exclusive occupation on the taken former trust lands and the non-Indian hunting-and-fishing regulation derived from that exclusion right. Retained tribal access and personal hunting-and-fishing privileges do not themselves preserve authority to license or regulate non-Indian public users.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, supported on this holding by White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, controls by a majority of 6 Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress’s treatment of these lands supplies the clear indication required by Dion. The Fort Laramie Treaty originally protected exclusive use and occupation, including control of non-Indian access. Congress acquired the project lands, authorized public recreation under federal management, settled the rights taken, and retained shoreline access and hunting and fishing subject to the regulations governing corresponding public uses without retaining exclusion of the public. Montana explains the relationship between exclusive occupation and the regulatory authority claimed from it; Brendale confirms the importance of the land’s legal status. Tribal use rights remain meaningful without carrying power to condition the public access Congress provided. Menominee does not compel the opposite result, because this conclusion rests on the particular acquisition and access enactments together, not an assumption that every federal project extinguishes treaties. The Court requires no particular verbal formula for abrogation. It does not decide reservation boundaries, tribal criminal jurisdiction or every possible regulation on acquired acreage.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dion, 476 U.S.734 — clear congressional choice required, without an invariable express-words requirement.
- Montana, 450 U.S.544 — exclusion-derived authority and effects of alienation applied.
- Brendale, 492 U.S.408 — land-specific setting considered without inventing a universal rule from its fracture.
- Menominee, 391 U.S.404 — protection against casual implied abrogation preserved; particular enactments distinguished.

##### Residual authority remains a distinct inquiry

**Controlling proposition:** Loss of treaty-based exclusion does not decide whether a Montana exception or properly established federal authorization permits particular regulation. Remand preserves the exceptions for consensual commercial dealings, contracts, leases or other arrangements with the Tribe or its members, and for conduct threatening or directly affecting tribal political integrity, economic security, health or welfare, within Montana’s limits. General inherent sovereignty alone does not restore the abrogated regulatory power.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, supported on this holding by White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, controls by a majority of 6 Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the source rejected today must not be confused with every possible source of tribal civil authority. Montana generally limits regulation of nonmembers on non-Indian land but identifies two exceptions requiring attention to actual relationships and conduct. The court of appeals relied on treaty-based control for the former trust acreage while preserving a separate inquiry for formerly non-Indian fee land. The district court found neither exception satisfied on either land category, but the court of appeals did not review those findings for the former trust lands; that review remains. The Corps-regulation theory was not raised below, was omitted from the Government’s brief and conflicted with record evidence of the Corps’ view. The Court declines deference to that undeveloped litigating position. The State therefore receives no declaration that every non-Indian activity throughout the reservation is exclusively subject to state law. Any claimed authorization and concrete conflict with superior federal rules must be assessed on their own terms. Remand permits that inquiry without reviving the displaced exclusion right or collapsing the two land categories into a single historical ownership premise. No result on those undeveloped questions is directed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Montana, 450 U.S.544 — both consensual-relationship and serious tribal-interest exceptions expressly retained.
- United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S.313 — retained internal tribal powers do not themselves establish nonmember regulation after the abrogation found here.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Treaty-derived control is removed as a sufficient source on the taken former trust lands. Residual authority remains unresolved; no general state exclusivity or boundary ruling.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun, joined by Souter, dissents. Dion requires evidence that Congress considered the disputed treaty right and chose abrogation. Building a dam and assuring recreation can coexist with conservation regulation; loss of absolute exclusion does not prove loss of every incident of territorial sovereignty. Wheeler and Indian-law construction principles support retention absent congressional action or necessary incompatibility. The compensation settlement does not identify every surrendered governmental power. Superior federal project law remains controlling.

Stone-Zsela separately dissents on the treaty ground. He would sustain compatible hunting-and-fishing regulation on former trust land because public acquisition removes exclusive possession without clearly removing this distinct power. He does not require express words in every statute, treat retained personal privileges as a new delegation, or decree exclusive tribal control of access. He preserves the fee-land Montana inquiry and adjudication of concrete conflicts with federal access requirements.

[Official U.S. Reports source and record account](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep508/usrep508679/usrep508679.pdf); [judgment below](https://static.case.law/f2d/949/html/0984-01.html). These sources support identity, argument and decision dates, lower proceedings, factual posture and cited historical texts. The reporter citation identifies the source location. No verbatim decisional quotation is supplied.

Reject treaty-derived exclusion as the sufficient regulatory source; consider unresolved Montana exceptions and any properly established federal authorization. Preserve distinct land histories; no universal exclusive-state-jurisdiction declaration. Ordinary mandate procedure applies; no special issuance date, stay, retained Supreme Court proceeding or subsequent filing is established.

### General Electric Co. v. Joiner, No. 96-188

Merits decision, December 15, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

General Electric Co. v. Joiner, No. 96-188. Argued October 14, 1997; decided December 15, 1997. Certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, 78 F.3d 524, concerning review of scientific expert-admissibility rulings.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision; none was absent.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens, who would vacate for appellate reconsideration under the proper standard.

Kennedy, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer hold that the PCB-only exclusion was within the District Court's discretion. Stone-Zsela joins reversal without joining that particular application. The unchallenged furan/dioxin exposure ruling and remaining causation-admissibility questions survive; the complete defense summary judgment is not reinstated.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II: review and legal inferential-fit rule | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | States the unanimous review and legal-fit rules | Parts I–II |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III: PCB-only application and bounded mandate | Kennedy | O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Sustains the PCB-only exclusion and bounded remand | Part III |
| Concurrence in Parts I–II and the reversing judgment | Stone-Zsela | Author alone | Supports reversal without the PCB-only application | Parts I–II and reversal |
| Concurrence, joining the Court's opinion in full | Breyer | Author alone | Joins the Court’s opinion and judgment in full | Full Court opinion; separate discussion of scientific assistance |
| Concurrence in Parts I–II and dissent from Part III and the judgment | Stevens | Author alone | Would vacate for appellate reconsideration | Parts I–II joined; Part III and judgment opposed |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Admission and exclusion receive the same abuse-of-discretion review.

Appellate courts review a trial court's admission or exclusion of scientific expert testimony for abuse of discretion. Legal error remains independently correctable, historical findings receive their proper protection, and permissible case-specific evidentiary judgments receive deference. The importance of an exclusion to summary judgment supplies no different or asymmetric standard. Part I is unanimous.

Daubert left appellate review open. Koon and the task-sensitive reasoning of Pierce and Cooter & Gell support distinguishing correct law from permissible application; their other subject-specific rules do not transfer wholesale to expert testimony. Ornelas's independent review of ultimate constitutional suspicion and probable cause addresses another task. An appellate court may scrutinize a consequential exclusion carefully, but the Federal Rules' allowance of expert evidence does not justify a separate, particularly stringent standard for exclusions. Rule 56 protects the treatment of disputed merits facts; it does not remove the antecedent admissibility decision from the judge.

##### 2. Reliability includes the inferential connection to the offered proposition.

Original Rules 104(a) and 702 permit the judge to examine whether the expert's reasoning reliably connects the scientific material to the conclusion actually offered. Credentials, accepted terminology, or an assertion of a weight-of-evidence method cannot substitute for an adequate connection; screening that connection does not itself decide ultimate causation. Part II is unanimous at this legal level.

Daubert requires a preliminary foundation, established by a preponderance, of scientifically reliable knowledge fitting the factual question and assisting the factfinder. Principles, methodology and valid application cannot be separated by forbidding any inquiry into the inference drawn. But the rule requires neither a study identical to the plaintiff's circumstances nor independent proof of the conclusion from each study. Animal work, nonsignificant associations, competing causes and individually inconclusive materials may contribute to a supported combined inference. The offered theory here is promotion or acceleration, not necessarily sole causation. No later Rule 702 amendment or categorical statistical-significance requirement is adopted.

##### 3. The PCB-only exclusion was permissible on this proffer.

The described differences between the experimental materials and Joiner's exposure and disease, considered with the identified limitations of the human studies, permitted the District Court to find an insufficiently supported PCB-only inference. Part III controls through Kennedy, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The holding is that exclusion fell within the permissible range, not that admission was necessarily forbidden or that PCBs cannot promote lung cancer.

The animal work involved infant mice, concentrated injections and alveologenic adenomas; Joiner was an adult with small-cell carcinoma and lower alleged occupational exposure. Two reported human studies showed nonsignificant excesses whose authors did not connect them to PCB exposure. The Norwegian study addressed particular mineral oil and did not mention PCBs; the Yusho group had several potential carcinogenic exposures without an isolated PCB effect in the described findings. These limitations bear on the connection offered, rather than establishing automatic scientific disqualifications. The experts invoked a combined method, exposure information, interviews, medical records and additional research; the incomplete presentation of the scientific materials does not establish that the experts supplied no inferential connection. Considered at the proper discretionary level, the described fit deficiencies support the ruling.

The Eleventh Circuit's separate conclusion that evidence supported furan/dioxin exposure was not challenged in the petition. The District Court had not expressly assessed causation admissibility on that assumed exposure. Those questions require further proceedings and prevent restoration of the full defense judgment.

#### Precedent Treatment

Daubert's flexible foundation, valid-application and preponderance rules are applied and supplemented by today's appellate-review rule; its distinction between admissibility and ultimate causation remains. Koon, Pierce and Cooter & Gell inform the allocation of legal correction and discretionary application. Ornelas is distinguished by its particular ultimate constitutional inquiry, not narrowed into a universal rule about mixed questions. Frye's mandatory general-acceptance prerequisite remains displaced. Rules 403, 703 and 706 and Rule 56 keep their separate functions. No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

Scientific expert admissions and exclusions receive uniform abuse-of-discretion review, including independent correction of legal mistakes. The reliability inquiry may examine the actual inferential connection while remaining distinct from ultimate causal proof and preserving legitimate cumulative methods. The present PCB-only exclusion is sustained within that discretionary range. No universal study-matching condition, categorical animal-study rule, compulsory expert-assistance procedure, technical-specialty rule, collateral reopening entitlement or determination of the alternative-exposure theory follows.

#### Separate Writings

Justice Stevens concurs in Parts I–II but dissents from the application and judgment. The grant concerns review allocation, and the Eleventh Circuit should apply the correct standard to the actual cumulative method. Individually inconclusive studies do not necessarily defeat a combined inference; the described reliance on work by at least thirteen researchers and World Health Organization materials warrants attention to the whole method. He would vacate for appellate reconsideration, without commanding admission or finding causation.

Justice Breyer joins the Court's opinion in full and concurs separately. Difficult scientific questions do not excuse the judge's screening duty. Existing assistance, including a court-appointed expert under Rule 706 and lawful management of the foundation inquiry, may help assess tentative scientific knowledge and its application to an individual case. He requires no expert appointment, new hearing or mandatory panel; those suggestions are not controlling procedures.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in Parts I–II and reversal on the uniform-review and inferential-fit grounds. An expert's assertion cannot replace an adequately supported connection. He does not join the particular finding that this PCB-only exclusion was within discretion, and he preserves the unresolved exposure questions and the remainder of the tort action.

#### Source Notes

The District Court opinion, 864 F.Supp. 1310, and Eleventh Circuit opinion, 78 F.3d 524, together with published record descriptions, establish the rulings, proffered promotion theory and study limitations. Full studies, complete depositions and the full record are not reproduced. The Court makes no independent medical findings and adds no numerical data beyond the published account. The limits of these materials do not establish a litigation default.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for further proceedings consistent with the review rule and sustained PCB-only exclusion. The unchallenged furan/dioxin exposure ruling remains, and causation admissibility on that theory remains open. No finding of causation, damages award, compulsory admission, automatic new trial or expert appointment is entered.

**End of entry: General Electric Co. v. Joiner, Merits decision, December 15, 1997.**

### Almendarez-Torres v. United States, No. 96-6839

Merits decision, March 24, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 14, 1997; decided March 24, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, reviewing its affirmance of an 85-month sentence. The question is whether the prior aggravated-felony conviction in 8 U.S.C. §1326(b)(2) is an element of aggravated unlawful reentry or a sentencing factor that need not appear in the indictment.

The September 1995 indictment charged unlawful reentry under §1326 without alleging the prior aggravated-felony convictions. At his December 1995 plea, Almendarez-Torres admitted the prior convictions and deportation; he preserved his objection that the indictment authorized no more than §1326(a)'s two-year maximum. The District Court imposed the enhanced sentence in March 1996.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.

The enhanced sentence is vacated. On the existing indictment and plea, the sentence may not exceed §1326(a)'s two-year maximum. The guilty plea is not withdrawn and no acquittal is entered. Whether another source of law permits a further proceeding on a properly charged aggravated offense is not decided.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg | Supports reversal and resentencing | Statutory element classification, charging consequence and resentencing remedy |
| Opinion announcing the judgment, Part II | Scalia | Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg | Supports reversal and resentencing | Constitutional-avoidance explanation; no definitive constitutional classification rule |
| Concurrence in Part I and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Concurs in Part I and in the judgment | Definitive constitutional ground for the element classification; does not join Part II's reservation of that question |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer | Would affirm | Penalty construction and bounded constitutional permission for this recidivist enhancement; would affirm |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Section 1326(b)(2) requires an aggravated-offense allegation before its higher maximum may apply

**Holding and operative rule:** The prior aggravated-felony conviction specified in §1326(b)(2) is an element of the aggravated unlawful-reentry offense. It must be alleged in the federal indictment and, unless validly admitted as an element of the charged offense, proved to the jury beyond a reasonable doubt; an admission to the fact of a prior conviction does not cure its omission from this indictment, so the existing conviction permits no sentence above §1326(a)'s two-year maximum.

**Authority:** Part I of Scalia's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg. These five expressly adopt the statutory rule, its ordinary element consequences and the remedy. Four explain the construction through constitutional avoidance; Stone-Zsela gives a definitive constitutional ground separately. No majority adopts a general constitutional rule governing all facts that increase a statutory maximum.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that subsection (a)'s complete unlawful-reentry offense and two-year maximum must be distinguished from subsection (b)(2)'s aggravated offense carrying a twenty-year maximum. The prior conviction authorizes a different punishment ceiling; it does more than inform selection of punishment within the ordinary range. The provisions' cross-references permit that element construction. Hamling requires the indictment to state the offense's elements, and Gaudin requires proof of an actual element to the jury beyond a reasonable doubt when it has not validly been admitted. Almendarez-Torres admitted the prior convictions, but the indictment did not charge that aggravating element. His factual admission therefore does not answer the preserved objection to the punishment legally authorized by this charge.

The traditional use of recidivism at sentencing does not decide this statute's classification. McMillan addressed a minimum within an unchanged maximum, and Watts permits ordinary sentencing findings within conviction-based authority while reserving element evasion and other constitutional limits. Custis addresses collateral attacks on existing convictions, not the indictment's content. Old Chief confirms that prior-conviction status can be proved without unnecessary prejudicial detail; that evidentiary concern does not require treating status as a sentencing factor. The decision does not determine a subsidiary standard-of-proof claim, invalidate the admitted prior convictions, select a replacement sentence, or decide facts that do not increase the statutory maximum.

**Precedent treatment:** Hamling and Gaudin govern the consequences of the statutory classification; McMillan, Watts, Custis and Old Chief retain the distinct boundaries stated below.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Hamling v. United States:** Applied to require an indictment alleging each element of the aggravated offense.
- **United States v. Gaudin:** Applied after statutory classification; its jury-and-proof rule for actual elements remains controlling without deciding all sentencing classifications.
- **McMillan v. Pennsylvania:** Distinguished because its mandatory minimum left the statutory maximum unchanged; its limited holding remains intact.
- **United States v. Watts / United States v. Putra:** Preserved for ordinary sentencing within punishment authorized by the conviction; it does not resolve this higher-maximum classification.
- **Custis v. United States:** Distinguished as a collateral-review decision; the existing limits on attacking prior convictions remain intact.
- **Old Chief v. United States:** Applied to the distinction between element status and the form of adequate prior-conviction proof; no broader compulsory-stipulation rule is created.

#### Law After Decision

Section 1326(b)(2)'s higher range requires the aggravated-offense element and the corresponding charging and proof protections. This statutory rule binds lower courts. The Court establishes no general constitutional prior-conviction exception and no general constitutional rule that every maximum-increasing fact is an element. Ordinary consideration of prior convictions within an independently authorized range remains available, subject to governing law.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Scalia, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, Part II.** These Justices regard the tenfold increase in maximum punishment as presenting serious doubt about Congress's ability to assign the aggravating fact solely to a sentencing judge. The element reading is fairly available, so they apply constitutional avoidance and decline to decide that ultimate question. McMillan's unchanged ceiling and Watts's ordinary sentencing setting do not eliminate the doubt. The risk of prejudice from prior-conviction evidence can be addressed through adequate admissions or other proper proof rather than removal of an element from the charge.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in Part I and in the judgment.** The constitutional allocation turns on the fact's legal effect. A fact necessary to increase the authorized maximum from two years to twenty years performs the work of an element and must be charged and either validly admitted as an element of that offense or proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Legislative placement cannot transfer that work entirely to the sentencing judge. Gaudin protects element determination; Watts permits ordinary judicial sentencing findings only within conviction-based authority. Custis concerns collateral attack, and Old Chief demonstrates how adequate status proof can avoid unnecessary prejudice. The admitted facts here do not cure an indictment that never charged the aggravated offense. This position permits consideration of prior convictions within a separately authorized range, preserves Custis's collateral-review limits, requires no prejudicial details when adequate admission or stipulation establishes the element, and decides no sentencing fact that leaves the statutory maximum unchanged.

**O'Connor, joined by Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer, dissenting.** Subsection (b)(2)'s recidivist subject, reciprocal cross-references and punishment structure make it a penalty provision for the alien already described in subsection (a). Existing conviction status differs from facts about commission of the present crime, and Congress may treat that prior adjudication as a sentencing factor in this setting. The admission supports the application but is not a substitute for their statutory classification. These Justices acknowledge that McMillan and Watts do not alone resolve an increased ceiling; the distinct recidivism ground does that work. They would affirm without adopting unlimited legislative power to remove offense elements or resolving an unpresented subsidiary proof-burden question.

#### Source Notes

The applicable provision is the [1994 text of 8 U.S.C. §1326](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title8/pdf/USCODE-1994-title8.pdf), which increased the earlier fifteen-year aggravated-felony maximum to twenty years. The published account of the indictment, plea, preserved objection and sentence appears in [523 U.S. 227–229](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-523/pdf/USREPORTS-523-224.pdf). That account does not establish the exact charged found-in date or an intentional waiver of the indictment objection. No temporal challenge or broader waiver is decided.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Fifth Circuit for resentencing. The enhanced sentence cannot remain in effect under the existing indictment and plea; the sentencing court must act within §1326(a)'s maximum. No new aggravated-offense proceeding or particular replacement term is ordered.

**End of entry: Almendarez-Torres v. United States, Merits decision, March 24, 1998.**

### Burdick v. Takushi, No. 91-535

Merits decision, June 8, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 24, 1992; decided June 8, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1), reviewing its replacement opinion filed June 28, 1991, [937 F.2d 415](https://static.case.law/f2d/937/cases/0415-01.json). The March 1, 1991 opinion at 927 F.2d 469 was withdrawn. Hawaii's Supreme Court construed state law on July 21, 1989 to prohibit write-ins. The District of Hawaii entered summary judgment and an injunction for Burdick on May 10, 1990, 737 F.Supp. 582; that injunction was stayed and the Ninth Circuit reversed. The lower court rejected standing and certification/full-faith-and-credit objections. The argument and decision dates appear in the [official report, 504 U.S. 428](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep504/usrep504428/usrep504428.pdf).

The questions are whether Hawaii's complete prohibition violates First and Fourteenth Amendment electoral-choice rights when considered within its access system, and whether the Constitution independently requires an official tally of arbitrary protest votes. Burdick seeks prospective relief from the prohibition; Hawaii seeks affirmance. The Court reaches primary and general-election applications within the challenged system, but does not independently adjudicate every surrounding access rule.

In 1986 only one candidate filed for Burdick's legislative district, and he wished to vote for someone who had not filed. Hawaii requires new parties to obtain signatures from one percent of registered voters 150 days before the primary; qualifying established parties retain access. Party and nonpartisan candidates file 15 signatures for legislative or county office, or 25 for statewide or federal office, 60 days before the primary. A nonpartisan candidate advances with ten percent of the primary vote or the vote sufficient to nominate a partisan candidate, whichever is lower. A voter chooses one party or nonpartisan primary ballot for all offices. These combined routes and restrictions frame the constitutional burden.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas all participate at argument and decision. Thomas took his seat before argument.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the Ninth Circuit and sustains Hawaii's write-in prohibition within the access system reviewed.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electoral-choice challenge to the complete write-in prohibition | Affirm, 5–4 | White, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy: reverse and remand | No prospective injunction issues; the Ninth Circuit's reversal of the district court's injunction remains effective. |
| Claimed right to official count and publication of arbitrary protest messages | Rejected, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | — | No relief on this component. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |

| Opinion of the Court | White | O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas | Affirmance; five votes for judgment and complete controlling rationale | Complete opinion. |

The Court's rationale commands a majority. The dissenters' agreement on calibrated scrutiny and rejection of a general protest tally does not constitute a joint opinion of all nine Justices.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Review of election regulations follows the actual burden

**Controlling proposition:** A court reviewing a restriction on voting or candidate access must weigh the character and magnitude of the injury to First and Fourteenth Amendment rights against the State’s precise asserted interests and the extent to which those interests require the burden. Severe restrictions must be narrowly drawn to advance a compelling interest; reasonable, nondiscriminatory restrictions ordinarily are justified by important regulatory interests, so strict scrutiny does not apply to every election rule.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Thomas, supplies five votes for the complete rule and controls. Kennedy’s dissent, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, and Stone-Zsela’s separate dissent also accept this calibrated framework; their agreement does not make their contrary application controlling.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that constitutional review must account for the regulation and the election system in which it operates. Anderson v. Celebrezze supplies the inquiry into the actual injury, the precise state interests, and the need for the particular burden. Norman v. Reed preserves that inquiry while recognizing that States may require support in the electorate a candidate or party seeks to represent. Storer v. Brown explains why orderly elections require substantial regulation of candidate selection. Treating every restriction as severe would disable the primary procedures and qualification rules those decisions permit. The claimed injury here therefore cannot be measured simply by observing that Hawaii forbids write-ins. The Court must examine the other means of obtaining access and the function the prohibition serves within that system. This approach preserves demanding review when access is severely restricted and meaningful scrutiny when a lesser burden is asserted; it does not make ordinary administration immune from constitutional review.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983): applied as the governing burden, interest, and necessity inquiry; no categorical strict-scrutiny rule replaces it.
- Norman v. Reed (January 14, 1992): applied for actual access review and permissible electorate-specific support requirements; its protection against cross-component exclusion remains effective.
- Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 724 (1974): applied for the State’s authority to organize candidate selection; severe barriers remain reviewable.

##### 2. Hawaii’s complete write-in prohibition is valid in its reasonably accessible system

**Controlling proposition:** Where the complete ballot-access system imposes only reasonable burdens, a prohibition on write-in voting is presumptively valid and may be sustained by interests proportionate to that burden. Hawaii’s party and nonpartisan routes provide adequate access, and its write-in prohibition validly supports primary finality, prevention of sore-loser candidacies and party raiding, and its treatment of uncontested primaries; the electoral-choice challenge therefore fails.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Thomas, controls with five votes. Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, and Kennedy disagree with this application and its resulting judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Hawaii’s prohibition imposes a limited burden when assessed with its nomination rules. Small signature requirements and party and nonpartisan routes permit access before the election, although the nonpartisan route requires a primary showing of support and a choice of primary ballot. Jenness v. Fortson permits reasonable demonstrations of support, and Munro v. Socialist Workers Party permits primary winnowing without proof of a particular past disruption. The Constitution does not require the State to accommodate every preference arising after its lawful selection deadlines. Storer v. Brown supports preserving the primary’s role in settling candidacies. The prohibition also prevents candidacies that would evade that selection process, discourages primary raiding, and complements the designation of certain unopposed primary victors. The contrary position underestimates these available access routes and treats the inability to vote for an unlisted choice as decisive. The Court sustains this system without deciding whether the same prohibition would survive materially more restrictive access rules.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Jenness v. Fortson, 403 U.S. 431 (1971): applied to permit reasonable candidate-support requirements; it does not validate every access barrier.
- Munro v. Socialist Workers Party, 479 U.S. 189 (1986): applied to permit primary winnowing and reasonable preventative regulation without an empirical-disruption prerequisite.
- Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 724 (1974): applied to primary finality and restrictions preventing circumvention of candidate selection.
- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983): applied to the complete Hawaii system; this decision supplies a limited-burden application, not an exception to the inquiry.

##### 3. The ballot need not serve as an official tally of arbitrary political messages

**Controlling proposition:** The First and Fourteenth Amendments do not require the State to count and publicize write-in votes for fictional figures or arbitrary protest messages as an independent form of political expression. Elections select public officials, and a desire to use the ballot for such messages does not establish a constitutional entitlement to a write-in option.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Thomas, controls. Kennedy’s dissent, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, and Stone-Zsela’s separate dissent expressly agree in rejecting this broader claim. All nine reject this component, although four would grant relief for genuine electoral choice.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the ballot’s purpose limits the claimed expressive entitlement. Voting carries political meaning, but an official election need not become a general forum in which the State records and disseminates any message a voter wishes to send. Storer v. Brown recognizes elections as a means of selecting officeholders through an orderly process, and the access inquiry under Anderson v. Celebrezze protects the ability to participate in that process. Neither principle requires an official tally for fictional figures or arbitrary statements. Burdick’s interest in expressing dissatisfaction therefore does not itself invalidate the prohibition. His distinct interest in supporting an actual alternative candidate requires the contextual analysis stated in the preceding holdings, and the Court has conducted it. Rejecting the broader theory does not diminish political expression outside the ballot or decide every restriction on votes for otherwise eligible candidates under a different access system.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 724 (1974): applied to the office-selecting function of elections; the ballot is not a general expressive forum.
- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983): distinguished from the demand for an arbitrary-message tally; meaningful electoral participation remains protected.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 8, 1992, Anderson's calibrated review includes the Court's holding that a write-in ban is presumptively valid when the complete access system imposes reasonable burdens, with Hawaii's arrangement sustained on this record. No absolute right to a write-in option or arbitrary-message tally exists. This does not establish that every prohibition is valid: a materially different access system may impose a severe burden requiring narrow tailoring to a compelling interest.

Norman v. Reed (January 14, 1992), which protects against unjustified name and cross-component disqualification, retains that protection; Hawaii's accessible selection routes present a different application. Burson v. Freeman (May 26, 1992) supplies a strict-scrutiny proposition concerning campaign-content restrictions on ordinary public sidewalks; that proposition is neither extended to all ballot rules nor displaced. Burson's fractured judgment does not supply a controlling validity exception here. No precedent is overruled, limited in force beyond these distinctions, or restored after displacement. Candidate qualifications, reasonable write-in procedures elsewhere, and independent challenges to other Hawaii provisions remain open.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, would reverse and remand. They accept contextual scrutiny and reject the ballot-as-general-expression theory, but consider the total prohibition a substantial burden in the actual nomination system. Low signature requirements do not answer the need to select one party or nonpartisan primary ballot for all offices, the advancement rules, or the inability to support a real alternative in an unopposed contest. They reject a presumption derived from the surrounding rules’ validity. Sore-loser prevention does not explain a primary ban and reaches only some general-election candidates; party-raiding and uncontested-primary interests do not justify the prohibition’s entire reach. Their position does not establish a general constitutional write-in entitlement.

Stone-Zsela separately would reverse and remand for workable prospective relief from the complete prohibition within this system. Fifteen signatures make initial filing accessible, but the primary-ticket and advancement conditions must enter the burden inquiry. An actual single-candidate contest presents electoral choice even though the Constitution guarantees neither competition in every race nor all late candidacies. Sore-loser prevention cannot explain the primary ban; raiding does not justify excluding genuine support for a candidate on the selected primary ticket; and an election omitted under the uncontested-winner rule has no general-election ballot needing protection. He rejects arbitrary fictional-message tallies and confines relief to legally eligible candidates. He reserves reasonable write-in procedures, valid qualifications, narrow anti-raiding and sore-loser restrictions, and materially different systems. He would not prescribe statewide filing, certification, or counting machinery or independently invalidate other access rules.

Both writings are noncontrolling on the electoral-choice application and remedy. Their agreement on contextual scrutiny and rejection of general protest expression does not make their contrary application or proposed remedy controlling.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The mandate follows ordinary procedures and returns the matter below consistently with affirmance; the Ninth Circuit's reversal of the district court's injunction remains effective. No prospective injunction issues from this Court. The Court orders no special timing, election administration scheme, special remand, retained jurisdiction, or nonroutine follow-on condition.

**End of entry: Burdick v. Takushi, Merits decision, June 8, 1992.**

### Nordlinger v. Hahn, No. 90-1912

Merits decision, June 18, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 25, 1992; decided June 18, 1992; [505 U.S. 1](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-505/pdf/USREPORTS-505-1.pdf). On writ of certiorari to the California Court of Appeal, Second Appellate District, which affirmed dismissal without leave to amend on December 3, 1990, [225 Cal. App. 3d 1259, 275 Cal. Rptr. 684](https://static.case.law/cal-app-3d/225/html/1259-01.html). The California Supreme Court denied review on February 28, 1991. All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The questions are whether California’s acquisition-value property-tax assessments and its over-55 replacement-residence and parent-child reassessment exceptions deny Nordlinger equal protection, and whether she may invoke absent travelers’ rights to obtain heightened review. Nordlinger bought a Los Angeles home after renting locally. She alleges that she pays approximately five times the tax paid by longer-term owners of comparable nearby homes. California caps the tax rate and annual assessment growth and generally reassesses property upon transfer or new construction, subject to the challenged exceptions. The disparities are accepted at the pleading stage and result from the enacted acquisition-value policy. Nordlinger alleges no personal interference with interstate movement; she seeks a declaration and a tax refund.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the rejection of Nordlinger’s own equal-protection challenge.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Nordlinger cannot assert absent travelers’ rights

**Controlling proposition:** A taxpayer who alleges no interference with her own interstate movement may not obtain heightened review by asserting absent travelers’ rights when she shows neither a qualifying relationship nor an obstacle to their own litigation. Nordlinger therefore cannot use those unasserted injuries to challenge this scheme; the merits of a proper traveler’s claim remain open.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Part II-A, joined by White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls with seven of nine Justices. Stone-Zsela states the same refusal separately; Stevens does not reach the issue.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Nordlinger may litigate her own tax injury but cannot supply the missing claims of people who wish to enter California. Allen v. Wright states the ordinary prohibition on asserting another person’s rights, and Caplin & Drysdale identifies the relationship and practical-obstacle considerations that may justify an exception. Nordlinger already lived in Los Angeles before buying her house. She identifies neither a personal impediment to interstate movement nor a reason that an affected traveler could not sue. Her own financial disadvantage gives her a concrete equal-protection controversy; it does not automatically authorize every constitutional theory that another owner might present. Warth v. Seldin keeps that distinction between a litigant’s injury and an absent person’s rights. The Court therefore applies ordinary review to this taxpayer’s claim without approving or rejecting the scheme’s application to a properly situated traveler.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737 — applied to preserve the ordinary limit on asserting others’ rights.
- Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490 — applied to distinguish personal injury from authority to assert absent persons’ rights.
- Caplin & Drysdale, Chartered v. United States, 491 U.S. 617 — applied; no qualifying relationship or obstacle is established.
- Zobel v. Williams, 457 U.S. 55 — its travel-related protection is not adjudicated on this absent-party claim.
- Memorial Hospital v. Maricopa County, 415 U.S. 250 — its travel-related protection is not adjudicated on this absent-party claim.

##### Neighborhood continuity independently supports acquisition-value assessment

**Controlling proposition:** Absent an implicated fundamental right or suspect classification, an acquisition-value property-tax system satisfies equal protection when its classification rationally advances a legitimate policy; substantial disparities alone do not establish irrationality. California may use acquisition values and capped increases to discourage displacement and rapid turnover, and that neighborhood-continuity ground independently sustains the challenged general assessment scheme.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that California may use its tax system to promote neighborhood continuity. Village of Euclid recognizes a legitimate interest in preserving communities, while Allied Stores and Lehnhausen permit substantial latitude in allocating tax burdens. Fritz requires a plausible policy connection, and Cleburne preserves the limit against an arbitrary or attenuated distinction. Capped acquisition assessments can reduce pressure on established households and businesses to leave as surrounding prices rise. New purchasers and existing owners receive the same rate ceiling and annual-growth limit, although their initial bases differ. The magnitude of the resulting inequality is serious, but it does not sever that connection. Allegheny Pittsburgh concerned an assessment practice for which the claimed acquisition-value purposes could not plausibly explain the departure from the governing current-value system. California deliberately adopted those purposes. The Court preserves equal protection against legislative and administrative discrimination alike and does not decide whether other fiscal or ability-to-pay explanations would suffice.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co., 272 U.S. 365 — applied for the legitimacy of community preservation, without importing a zoning test.
- Allied Stores of Ohio v. Bowers, 358 U.S. 522 — applied for broad but rational tax-classification latitude.
- Lehnhausen v. Lake Shore Auto Parts Co., 410 U.S. 356 — applied for broad legislative latitude in tax classifications.
- United States Railroad Retirement Board v. Fritz, 449 U.S. 166 — applied for a plausible policy connection supporting a classification.
- Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc., 473 U.S. 432 — applied to preserve the limit against arbitrary or attenuated distinctions.
- Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. County Commission, 488 U.S. 336 — distinguished because the acquisition-value purposes could not plausibly explain that record; its equal-protection holding remains in force.
- Sunday Lake Iron Co. v. Wakefield, 247 U.S. 350 — preserved: equal protection reaches intentional, arbitrary discrimination in tax administration.
- Nashville, C. & St. L. R. Co. v. Browning, 310 U.S. 362 — preserved: state-law error alone does not establish a federal equal-protection violation.
- Snowden v. Hughes, 321 U.S. 1 — preserved: unequal administration requires more than a bare violation of state law.

##### Existing ownership reliance independently supports the general scheme

**Controlling proposition:** California may rationally distinguish an owner already committed to property from a purchaser who can evaluate acquisition-based taxes before buying, protecting the former against unanticipated appreciation-driven tax burdens. That reliance interest independently sustains the general acquisition-value classification without requiring an immutable contractual right or proof that the policy is optimal.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground. Stone-Zsela gives a compatible separate explanation without joining the Court’s opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an existing owner’s commitment to a home may receive protection that a prospective purchaser does not yet need. Heckler v. Mathews recognizes protection of reasonable reliance as a legitimate governmental objective. Fritz permits a legislature to distinguish established entitlements from later expectations, and Dukes sustains a reasonable grandfather provision for businesses with greater reliance on continued operation. A new buyer can examine the acquisition-based liability before deciding to purchase. An existing owner facing sharp appreciation cannot undo that earlier commitment and may have to sell or divert money from necessities. California may consider those positions different even though both properties receive comparable public services. This is a permissible legislative assessment of reliance, not a finding of a contractual guarantee that taxes never change. The Court does not require the policy to be the fairest available system, and it leaves the separately asserted ability-to-pay and revenue-stability justifications undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Heckler v. Mathews, 465 U.S. 728 — applied for legitimate protection of reasonable reliance.
- United States Railroad Retirement Board v. Fritz, 449 U.S. 166 — applied for permissible distinctions based on established expectations.
- New Orleans v. Dukes, 427 U.S. 297 — applied by analogy to protecting longer-established reliance through a grandfather provision.
- General Motors Corp. v. Romein (March 9, 1992) — remains compatible; protecting reliance is permissible without making reliance an immutable bar to economic legislation.

##### The older-owner replacement exception is rational

**Controlling proposition:** California may permit qualifying owners over 55 to transfer their protected base-year assessment to a replacement principal residence of equal or lesser value. Facilitating a move suited to changing household needs or income rationally supports that reassessment exception and does not invalidate the general scheme.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a limited exception can preserve a tax policy while accommodating changing needs. Royster Guano recognizes broad discretion in granting tax exemptions for reasons of policy. Regan v. Taxation with Representation establishes that an exception need not make the larger tax classification constitutionally irrational. Older owners may need a smaller residence or one suited to changed income or household size. California could reasonably allow a qualifying replacement move without surrendering the assessment protection that otherwise discourages leaving the existing home. That accommodation does not eliminate the State’s general interests in stability and ownership reliance. Nordlinger’s objection that the exception benefits some new owners therefore identifies a distinction but does not show the absence of a rational basis for it. The Court decides this ordinary equal-protection claim only; it does not adjudicate a properly presented traveler’s challenge to any territorial limitation or require California to extend the exception to every purchaser.

**Precedent treatment:**

- F. S. Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia, 253 U.S. 412 — applied for broad but rational tax-exemption discretion.
- Regan v. Taxation with Representation of Washington, 461 U.S. 540 — applied; a limited policy exception does not itself invalidate a general tax scheme.

##### The parent-child transfer exception is rational

**Controlling proposition:** California may preserve the protected assessment on qualifying transfers between parents and children of a principal residence and up to $1 million of other real property under Article XIIIA §2(h). The familial and neighborhood continuity promoted by that exception supplies a rational basis; the exception does not render the challenged acquisition-value system unconstitutional on this claim.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that California may protect continuity when property passes between parents and children. Royster Guano permits tax exemptions grounded in a reasonable policy difference, and Regan rejects the premise that every narrow exemption makes a broader tax system invidious. A transfer within a family can preserve the family’s connection to a home and the neighborhood even though legal ownership changes. California may treat that continuity as sufficient to continue the existing assessment within the exception’s stated limits. The contrary argument properly identifies advantages unavailable to children whose parents own no property. But that unequal benefit does not itself establish an irrational distinction under the governing review, nor does the Court transform ownership or family transfer into a suspect classification. The holding does not endorse every inheritance preference or prescribe a new tax exemption. It resolves only the challenged reassessment exception and leaves independent travel, administration and statutory tax claims for a proper case.

**Precedent treatment:**

- F. S. Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia, 253 U.S. 412 — applied to the rationality of the specific exemption.
- Regan v. Taxation with Representation of Washington, 461 U.S. 540 — applied to reject automatic invalidity from a limited exemption.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

The Court resolves the federal ground on which the state appellate court ruled and does not decide timeliness under California Revenue and Taxation Code §4808. It does not adjudicate a proper traveler’s claim, an independent statutory tax objection, discriminatory administration contrary to the adopted system, or the alternative ability-to-pay and revenue-stability justifications.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 18, the general acquisition-value system is sustained on each of two independently sufficient rational grounds, and each specified exemption is sustained. The limit on asserting absent travelers’ rights controls. Ordinary rational-basis review applies without a new heightened proof or record-building duty. Allegheny Pittsburgh remains law at the boundary explained above. The decision creates neither a mandatory property-tax equality formula nor an immutable reliance right.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Thomas accepts the scheme’s rationality but finds the Court's distinction of Allegheny Pittsburgh unpersuasive and risking confusion of state-law error with equal protection. His criticism does not overrule that decision. Stevens concludes that the permanent acquisition and family preferences lack adequate fit with an impartial public purpose and would reverse even under rational-basis review. Stone-Zsela separately concurs on predictability, continuity and the exception grounds, preserving proper travel and discriminatory-administration claims.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The ordinary mandate leaves dismissal without leave to amend in place. The Court awards no refund, orders no reassessment or replacement tax schedule, and retains no implementation proceeding.

**End of entry: Nordlinger v. Hahn, merits decision, June 18, 1992.**
