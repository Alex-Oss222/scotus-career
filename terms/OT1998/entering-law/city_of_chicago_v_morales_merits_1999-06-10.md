## Event
City of Chicago v. Morales, No. 97-1121. Argued December 9, 1998; decided June 10, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Illinois, 177 Ill. 2d 440, 687 N.E.2d 53 (1997), which invalidated the gang-loitering ordinance and reversed the convictions. The question is whether the conditions for mandatory dispersal and punishment for disobedience supply constitutionally sufficient enforcement standards and notice.

The ordinance applies when an officer reasonably believes a criminal street gang member is remaining in a public place with one or more persons with no apparent purpose. The officer must order everyone to disperse and remove themselves from the area; failure promptly to obey is punishable. Absence of an actual gang member is an affirmative defense. Public place includes publicly accessible private locations. Penalties are a $100–$500 fine, up to six months' imprisonment, or both; up to 120 hours of community service may be imposed additionally or instead. General Order 92-4 restricts enforcement to designated officers and areas and provides gang-identification criteria, but the designated areas are not publicly disclosed.

## Participation
Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action
The judgment of the Supreme Court of Illinois is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy
**Affirmed, 7–2.** **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas. The dismissals and reversals resulting from invalidation remain in force. The dissenters would reverse the facial-vagueness disposition and remand unresolved preserved grounds, without independently reinstating every conviction through new factual findings.

## Holdings
**Meaningful standards for dispersal.** A criminal ordinance is facially vague when every prosecution depends on an officer's conclusion that people remain in public with no apparent purpose, and the law supplies no meaningful objective criterion governing that conclusion. A later refusal to disperse cannot cure the standardless condition authorizing the order.

**Authority:** The Court's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those seven directly adopt the enforcement-guidance ground and the remedy tied to its common trigger; no aggregation of distinct notice or liberty theories supplies their majority.

**Controlling explanation:** Kolender requires minimum legal guidance for enforcement independently of whether an individual knows that an officer demands compliance. Peoples Rights Organization distinguishes difficult factual proof from the absence of a central legal criterion; fault and apparent clear examples cannot automatically supply a missing criterion. Chicago's ordinance provides gang-identification, personnel and geographic limits, but none answers why a group's otherwise lawful presence may be ended. An innocent purpose need not be apparent. The ordinance requires no obstruction, threat, trespass, intimidation, criminal conduct or minimum duration before the officer must order everyone away. General Order 92-4 addresses officers, areas and gang identification, not this missing substantive condition. Illinois rejected the proposed narrowing constructions; the Court cannot insert an intimidation element. A warning makes departure avoid arrest but leaves the original choice of whom to displace uncontrolled.

The defect governs the common legal trigger of every dispersal order. Facial relief therefore rests on that central defect, not uncertain margins of an otherwise intelligible rule, nor a claim that every gang gathering is immune from regulation. Hoffman Estates preserves contextual limits on facial review; it does not transform an undefined predicate into a definite offense. The Court does not rely on an independently sufficient fair-notice ground or recognize an unlimited substantive right to remain anywhere. Objectively bounded public-order laws remain available. No severable independent offense survives within this single mechanism.

## Precedent Treatment
- Kolender supplies an independent enforcement-guidance requirement; an individual warning does not replace a legal standard for initiating the command.
- Peoples Rights Organization applies the distinction between difficult proof and a missing central legal criterion; its defect-specific approach supports relief here without a universal criminal facial-review rule.
- Hoffman Estates retains contextual facial-review and clear-core principles; it is distinguished because the Court finds a defect in the common legal trigger rather than merely uncertain margins.
- Grayned preserves intelligible notice and enforcement standards.
- Papachristou illustrates the danger of discretionary criminalization of ordinary public activity.
- Coates rejects criminal boundaries depending on variable personal reactions.

The decision applies those principles without establishing a new substantive liberty immunity.

## Law After Decision
Chicago cannot predicate punishment on this standardless dispersal mechanism. Defined rules addressing obstruction, trespass, intimidation, threats, criminal conduct or emergency dispersal remain available subject to their own constitutional requirements. The decision establishes neither an unlimited right to remain in every place nor a rule invalidating every law involving discretionary factual judgments.

## Separate Writings
Stevens, joined by Souter and Ginsburg, also finds inadequate fair notice. A command informs the listener that departure is demanded, but does not make the antecedent grounds intelligible or adequately settle how far and how long the listener must leave. Ordinary innocent public presence also implicates liberty against arbitrary displacement. That explanation is bounded: it does not confer immunity on threatening gatherings or invalidate reasonable, definite public-order laws. Those additional propositions have three votes and are noncontrolling.

Thomas, joined by Scalia, regards the enacted sequence as sufficiently ascertainable: a reasonable gang-membership belief, observed remaining without apparent purpose, an express order and deliberate disobedience. In their view, the ordinance thus differs from the missing modification relationship in Peoples Rights Organization. Gang-identification rules and practical administration add genuine constraints. Any excessive reach or unconstitutional particular application does not establish that the law lacks every intelligible core. They would reject facial vagueness while leaving unresolved preserved objections for further proceedings; they do not insert an intimidation element or hold that a warning cures every undefined law.

## Procedure After Action
The Illinois judgment remains effective. The Court does not decide status-offense, Fourth Amendment probable-cause, First Amendment overbreadth or the omitted detailed gang-definition objections. No replacement ordinance, damages or new criminal findings are ordered.
