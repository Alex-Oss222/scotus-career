## Holdings

### Card use communicates an intent to repay, not a warranty of solvency

**Operative rule:** When a cardholder obtains an advance by using the card, she communicates a present intention to repay the resulting debt, not a warranty of current solvency or an objectively reasonable prospect of repayment; on the actual-fraud theory presented, the creditor must prove by a preponderance that the professed contemporaneous intention was false and deceptive. Financial condition, losses, borrowing patterns and other circumstances may establish that state of mind, but inability to pay or unreasonable optimism alone cannot substitute for it, and the debtor's denial is not conclusive.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court identified in Opinion Topology directly adopts the rule, its evidentiary qualifications and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts debt obtained through actual fraud; it does not equate financially reckless borrowing with deceit. Field v. Mans supplies the established common-law content of actual fraud, while Grogan v. Garner supplies the ordinary preponderance burden. A promise concerns the debtor's present intention to perform. Use of revolving credit does not itself assert that the borrower has the money now or that a projected source of repayment is objectively sound.

AT&T correctly observes that a dishonest intention can be inferred from conduct. Repeated advances, severe financial distress and mounting gambling losses can be persuasive circumstantial evidence. The distinction is between using those facts to find actual deceit and making financial improbability a substitute for that finding. A debtor may honestly intend repayment through an irrational plan, and an assertion of hope may also be disbelieved on a supported record. Neither conclusion follows categorically from gambling or insolvency.

No confession, mandatory factor count, heightened burden or additional plan to file bankruptcy is required. Nor does repayment create a safe harbor for a deliberately false promise. The Court decides the presented actual-fraud theory, not every false-pretense or representation-only theory. Field's independent actual-and-justifiable-reliance requirements and the statutory causal connection remain necessary when otherwise material; resolving intent does not eliminate them.

### The record supports affirmance without a new fraud inquiry

**Operative rule:** A finding of fraudulent repayment intent cannot stand on an objective inability-to-pay substitute for the subjective inquiry; factual findings remain subject to clear-error review and the governing legal standard to independent review. Here the reported bankruptcy explanation rests on that erroneous substitute, while substantial actual repayments corroborate the intent to repay and no independent contrary contemporaneous-intent finding is identified, so the Sixth Circuit's dischargeability judgment concerning AT&T is affirmed without further factfinding.

**Authority:** The same unanimous Opinion of the Court directly adopts this record-specific application and disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** Rembert used casino advances while her income was reduced and testified that she expected winnings to fund repayment. Her evidence was not testimony alone: she obtained second-mortgage funds, paid identified balances and made substantial later payments. The published appellate account identifies no independent evidence that her repayment intention was false when the advances were taken. The bankruptcy explanation instead inferred lack of intent from the objective unreality of her gambling expectations.

That reasoning does not become a valid subjective finding merely because it uses the word intent. Nor does recognizing its defect authorize this Court to reassess demeanor or discard a supported independent credibility finding. The actual stated premise and reported record support the intermediate courts' rejection of nondischargeability under the ordinary standards of review.

Field requires application of the proper standard where a material issue remains unresolved. It does not require a new hearing whenever a trial court has applied an incorrect rule. Here the intermediate courts have already corrected the objective substitute against the existing repayment evidence, and no unresolved independent contrary intent finding requires another trial-level determination. A remand to seek a new ground for the creditor's failed proof is unwarranted. The Court does not decide whether AT&T actually or justifiably relied, whether a debt was obtained by another proved fraud, or what amount would be excluded from discharge if fraud had been established.

## Precedent Treatment

- Field v. Mans (November 28, 1995): applies its common-law actual-fraud framework and preserves independent actual and recipient-specific justifiable reliance. Its correct-standard remand rule applies when a material application remains unresolved; it does not compel a redundant remand on this record. The prior reliance holding is not treated as an earlier universal decision about cardholder intent.
- Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279: applies the creditor's preponderance burden without raising it or requiring an admission of deceit.
- In re Cohen (June 30, 1997): preserves the requirement of a qualifying fraudulent acquisition before its connected-liability rule can operate. That debt-extent rule supplies no missing fraud predicate here.

## Law After Decision

The presented card-use actual-fraud theory requires proof of contemporaneous deceptive repayment intent, which may be circumstantial but is not established as a matter of law by insolvency, gambling losses or unrealistic expectations. The decision creates no gambling exemption and no rule requiring acceptance of a debtor's claimed intent.

Field's separate reliance requirements remain intact: justifiability depends on the recipient's knowledge, capacities and circumstances, without a universal credit-check or periodic-investigation duty. Reliance on falsity the recipient knows or that is obvious to that recipient is not justifiable; a warning actually encountered may require inquiry under that recipient-specific standard. Card issuance, continued authorization or nonrevocation does not automatically establish actual or justifiable reliance. None of those matters is factually decided here. Causation and debt extent remain separate as well.

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excludes statements respecting the financial condition of the debtor or an insider from its coverage. Subsection (B) separately requires use of a materially false written statement respecting that financial condition, reasonable reliance by the creditor, and the debtor's causing the statement to be made or published with intent to deceive. Those distinct conditions remain intact and are not adjudicated on this card-use actual-fraud theory. Different false-pretense or representation-only theories, the recent-debt presumption under §523(a)(2)(C), and a fee claim under §523(d) likewise remain undecided.
