## Holdings

### Commercial offer and readiness for patenting

**Operative rule.** The on-sale bar in then-effective 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) applies when, before the date one year before the patent application in the United States, the actual claimed invention is both the subject of a commercial offer for sale in the United States and ready for patenting. Readiness may be shown by reduction to practice or by drawings or descriptions sufficiently specific to enable a person skilled in the art to practice the invention; a working prototype is therefore sufficient but not indispensable.

**Authority.** The opinion of the Court, joined in full by all nine Justices, controls both conditions, their application and the following limits.

**Controlling explanation.** The statute addresses an invention offered for sale, not the manufacture of a first physical specimen. Pennock v. Dialogue supplies the principle that an inventor cannot prolong commercial exploitation and then obtain a later period of exclusivity. The Telephone Cases recognize that an enabling description can establish an operative invention without an indispensable successful prototype. Neither decision supplies a different statutory grace period or itself resolves the present on-sale question.

Both conditions must be proved. A substantial commercial commitment cannot make an incomplete conception ready for patenting, and later successful development cannot retroactively establish readiness before the critical date. Requiring enabling drawings or descriptions of the claimed invention gives that requirement an objective content. An open-ended judgment that development was merely substantially complete is not a substitute.

Here, the Texas Instruments purchase order establishes the commercial commitment, and the engineering drawings were complete enough to permit production before the critical date. The later manufacture of a physical embodiment does not postpone the bar. Pfaff's proposed prototype prerequisite would let the manufacturing schedule extend the period for exploiting an already enabling design.

Mere preliminary negotiations, proposals for future inventive research and incomplete conceptions do not meet the two conditions. Elizabeth v. Pavement Co. preserves the distinction between genuine controlled experimentation and ordinary commercial exploitation. The Court decides no separate experimental-use dispute and invalidates no unidentified claims beyond those under review.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Pennock v. Dialogue, 27 U.S. 1:** Applied for the restriction on prolonged commercial exploitation followed by later exclusivity. Its older statutory setting does not alter the one-year period governing this case.
- **The Telephone Cases, 126 U.S. 1:** Applied for the significance of an enabling description without an indispensable successful prototype; not treated as independently deciding the on-sale trigger.
- **Elizabeth v. Pavement Co., 97 U.S. 126:** Preserved. Genuine controlled experimentation remains distinct from ordinary commercial exploitation; no experimental-use question is separately adjudicated here.
- **Markman v. Westview Instruments:** Its allocation of claim-construction and infringement functions remains intact. It does not itself decide when an invention is on sale.
- **Warner-Jenkinson Co. v. Hilton Davis Chemical Co.:** Its claim-element and prosecution-history rules remain intact. The present inquiry concerns readiness of the actual claimed invention, without changing those rules.

## Law After Decision

Courts applying the then-effective on-sale bar must separately determine whether a commercial offer in the United States and enabling readiness existed more than one year before the patent application in the United States. A prototype is one way to establish readiness; enabling drawings or descriptions are another. Neither commercial purpose alone nor later successful development supplies a missing precritical-date condition.
