**Case and dockets:** Hughes Aircraft Co. v. Jacobson; No. 97-1287; Ninth Circuit judgment, 105 F.3d 1288.
**Event and date:** Merits decision, 1999-01-25; OT1998, chunk 4.
**Result:** Reversed and remanded, 9–0, for dismissal of the six original theories on their independent legal grounds.
**Version / lineage:** Original decision.

## Holdings

### I. Genuine payments within the continuing plan do not inure to the employer

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 403(c)(1) does not prohibit payment of genuine benefits to participants under the amended single plan merely because those benefits reduce future employer contributions or payroll costs. Surplus remains plan property, and the anti-inurement rule independently governs an actual diversion, unlawful reversion or payment outside the lawful plan.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion's Part I is joined by all eight Associate Justices and controls; Stone-Zsela agrees in dismissal of the anti-inurement claim without adding a ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that adding a benefit structure and eligible participants does not, on the acts alleged, create a second legal plan to which assets were transferred for Hughes's separate obligations. Both benefit structures draw from the continuing defined-benefit fund. A contribution holiday does not itself distribute cash to the employer. John Hancock separates asset status from a particular unlawful use, while Spink preserves anti-inurement as an independent claim even when adoption of the design was not fiduciary conduct. The retirees correctly insist that employee-derived surplus is protected plan property; that premise does not establish that genuine payments to the plan's participants are an employer diversion. The Court rejects the separate-plan and incidental-employer-benefit theories rather than creating an exception to §403 or declaring the surplus Hughes's property. A different actual recipient or unlawful transfer would require its own statutory analysis.

### II. Amendment design is distinct from fiduciary implementation

**Holding and operative rule:** Design and adoption of these pension amendments are sponsor functions, even though employees contributed to the plan; those acts alone create no fiduciary breach under §§404–405. Actual asset management or disposition and administration remain subject to §3(21)(A)'s functional requirements, including authority or control over disposition without an additional discretion requirement.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join the sponsor-capacity rule and its preservation of independent duties. The eight Associates additionally join the claim-specific conclusion that these original implementation and derivative cofiduciary theories identify no independent breach after their surplus-ownership and separate-plan predicates fail; Stone-Zsela agrees in that disposition without supplying an additional implementation ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that employee contributions do not make participants co-sponsors entitled to control plan design. Spink directly separates amendment adoption from implementation and requires independent claims to receive their own analysis. Varity preserves responsibility for actual administration and misleading benefit communications; it does not turn this design decision into administration. John Hancock keeps plan assets, fiduciary function and breach distinct. Assuming fiduciary implementation of the genuine payments, the original claims identify no independent imprudent management or disloyal diversion beyond the failed surplus-ownership and separate-plan theories. The cofiduciary label supplies no underlying breach. The holding neither adds a discretion condition to asset-control responsibility nor immunizes misrepresentation, investment misconduct, discriminatory administration or an actual diversion. Dismissal follows from the particular pleaded acts and failed legal predicates, not from treating everything Hughes did as sponsor conduct.

### III. Accrued benefits are protected without individual ownership of all surplus

**Holding and operative rule:** Participants in this defined-benefit plan have enforceable rights to promised and statutorily protected accrued benefits, including the employee-contribution floor, but no present individual ownership of all actuarial surplus. Because the original theory identifies no reduction of those protected benefits, use of pooled surplus for lawful plan benefits establishes neither forfeiture nor an anti-cutback violation.

**Authority:** The nine Justices join the no-surplus-ownership and no-reduction holding. The statutory distinction between required and voluntary contributions explains the eight Associates' rejection of the original employee-contribution theory; no benefit amount is calculated.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that §§203(a)(1) and 204(c) protect accrued employee-derived benefits without converting all actual investment appreciation into a separate participant account. For mandatory contributions, the statutory measure uses accumulated contributions with the enacted interest treatment and expresses the result as an annual benefit beginning at normal retirement age; another benefit form requires actuarial equivalence. The contribution conditions are employment, plan participation or obtaining employer-derived benefits. Section 204(c)(4)'s separate treatment of voluntary contributions remains intact; the original complaint describes mandatory payroll contributions. The Court decides no disputed computation, applicable rate or amount under those provisions. John Hancock identifies protected plan assets and fiduciary functions; it does not give each retiree title to a share of an ongoing defined-benefit plan's overfunding. No promised benefit or statutory floor was reduced by the theories alleged here. Actual underpayment, a reduction below the statutory floor or violation of a greater benefit promised by the plan remains actionable under its own elements.

### IV. Continuing benefit obligations do not trigger termination distribution

**Holding and operative rule:** Closing the contributory structure to new entrants while continuing the plan and its benefit obligations is not a statutory termination triggering §4044 distribution. A common-law characterization as a wasting trust cannot replace §4041's standard-or-distress termination process or PBGC proceedings under §4042.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join the nontermination holding on these continuing-plan facts. The eight Associates join the separate statutory distribution analysis; no general rule for every partial termination is decided.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the 1991 amendment changed benefit structures within an ongoing plan; it did not terminate the plan merely because new employees could no longer enter its contributory structure. The plan continues to owe and pay benefits. Sections 4041–4044 govern termination and distribution. When their distribution trigger occurs, plan liabilities and the statutory allocation order must be respected, including protection of remaining assets attributable to employee contributions. Employer reversion also requires satisfaction of liabilities, consistency with law and authorization under the plan, and remains subject to the enacted timing and employee-residual protections. The Court applies none of those residual-distribution rules to authorize a present reversion or calculate a distribution here. Protecting employee contributions does not require a court to invent an earlier termination event. Genuine termination, mass exclusion and another event satisfying an enacted termination rule remain outside this holding. The deleted lower-court common-law discussion supplies no alternative trigger.

### V. Incidental employer advantage does not make these benefit payments prohibited transactions

**Holding and operative rule:** Under §406(a)(1)(D), genuine pension payments under an otherwise lawful plan do not become prohibited transfers merely because they save the employer money. Nor does §406(b)(2), which bars a fiduciary from acting in a transaction for an adverse party, establish a violation merely from the sponsor's adoption of a benefit design advantageous to itself.

**Authority:** All eight Associates join Part V, which controls both statutory grounds; Stone-Zsela agrees only in dismissal of the prohibited-transaction component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the alleged recipients are participants of the continuing plan, not Hughes or strangers renamed as beneficiaries. Spink's genuine-benefit-payment rule applies after the separate-plan, unlawful-benefit and premature-distribution predicates fail. Its rule assumes fiduciary implementation for that inquiry and preserves sham transfers, kickbacks, independently unlawful plans and other prohibited transactions. Souter's and Breyer's earlier reservation about a technical release-payment question does not establish that these genuine pension payments are prohibited; they apply the controlling rule to the narrower original allegations here. The distinct adverse-party prohibition of §406(b)(2) does not turn sponsor adoption itself into a fiduciary transaction for an adverse party. There is no blanket benefit-payment exemption, no net-benefit defense, and no reliance on §408 to excuse an otherwise established violation. A later merger transaction or separately pleaded implementation wrong is not decided.

### VI. Fair administration does not promise a later incentive to ineligible retirees

**Holding and operative rule:** The plan's fair-and-equitable administration, interpretation and application clause does not give every retiree a contractual right to an incentive later offered to specified active employees. The original sixth claim fails independently under §502(a)(1)(B) because no benefit due under the operative eligibility terms is withheld, and under §404(a)(1)(D) because the asserted difference is lawful eligibility design rather than unequal administration among eligible participants.

**Authority:** All eight Associates join Part VI and its distinct statutory and plan grounds. Stone-Zsela agrees only in dismissal of this separate plan-enforcement component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Article V, §5.2 has work in governing administration, interpretation and application of plan terms, but does not require identical benefit design for existing retirees and active employees whom the sponsor seeks to induce to retire. Spink defeats fiduciary liability resting solely on creation of the eligibility class. That classification alone would not answer a benefits-enforcement claim: the separate answer is that the administrative clause promises no incentive to persons outside the class and no due benefit is alleged to have been withheld. Curtiss-Wright preserves both actual amendment procedures and authorized adoption; these six original theories identify no separate unauthorized-adoption defect. Mertens does not bar an otherwise proper §502(a)(1)(B) claim and is not the basis for dismissal. Specific statutory benefit, allocation, anti-discrimination or amendment restrictions remain effective. A fairness clause gives no free-standing judicial power to redesign an eligibility scheme.

## Precedent Treatment

- *Lockheed Corp. v. Spink* is applied to sponsor design and genuine benefit payments while its independent-claims directions are enforced. The present dismissal rests on separate statutory and plan answers, not categorical sponsor immunity.
- *John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank* is applied to separate plan property, function and breach; it establishes no participant ownership of every dollar of actuarial surplus.
- *Varity Corp. v. Howe* is preserved for function-specific administration, disloyal communications and appropriate relief. No conduct of that kind is immunized by amendment authority.
- *Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen* retains its distinction between a sufficient amendment procedure and actual authorized adoption; no separate adoption defect is decided here.
- *Mertens v. Hewitt Associates* retains its §502(a)(3) equitable-category limitation. It is distinguished from the §502(a)(1)(B) claim, which fails for lack of a promised due benefit rather than a categorical bar on benefits enforcement.

## Law After Decision

Plan-property restrictions, employee-derived accrued benefits, functional fiduciary obligations, genuine termination requirements and contractual benefit promises remain independently enforceable. These six original theories fail after those distinct predicates are examined. No blanket immunity for implementation, new distribution entitlement or general rule disposing of a later merger claim is established.

## Holdings

### Judicial-campaign expenditures and the preliminary protection

**Holding and operative rule:** A mandatory ceiling on the total amount of judicial-campaign advocacy receives the demanding First Amendment review applicable to campaign expenditures. Ohio's interest in impartial adjudication and its appearance does not justify this source-independent $75,000 ceiling on the present record; the candidates' likelihood of success supports the existing preliminary expenditure injunction at its actual, consent-extended scope.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion for the unanimous Court supplies the complete rule, application and remedial limits. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer join every controlling proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the judicial office's distinctive function does not remove campaign advocacy from the expenditure rule. Buckley v. Valeo distinguishes the quantity of a candidate's expression from a donor's contribution. City of Cincinnati v. Kruse applies that distinction to a source-independent total ceiling while reserving materially different institutional restrictions. The Court now considers judicial impartiality directly: judges must decide cases impartially, and public confidence in that function is a weighty interest. The lower court's suggestion that corruption is the only interest worthy of consideration is not adopted.

The asserted interest nevertheless does not establish this ceiling's necessary fit. The record concerns contribution influence, adjudicative commitments and bias; the total limit also forbids further personally financed and qualifications-related advocacy without identifying the corresponding danger. That a candidate can communicate adequately below the ceiling does not explain why additional advocacy may be prohibited. A concern about interested donors does not make every expenditure a contribution restriction. Colorado Republican preserves that transaction-specific distinction rather than immunizing transfers or other financial arrangements.

Separate contribution, solicitation, disclosure and recusal measures address different dangers. Their relevance does not establish that every such measure is valid or invariably sufficient, and no proof of a completed bribe or compulsory empirical waiting period is required. The candidates' continuing exposure under the old canon gives the preliminary protection practical legal effect despite the completed election and amendment. The amended canon remains for consideration below on its own text and record. The Court affirms the confined preliminary protection; it neither enters a final statewide decree nor adjudicates the separate transfer ruling.

**Application and limits:** Protection continues for the plaintiffs, plaintiff-intervenors and their respective opponents under the October 22, 1996 consent extension, including Maloney's later preliminary protection. It does not extend to every Ohio judicial candidate. The removed $18,750 primary sublimit and its separate consent decree are outside this decision. Ordinary recusal and disqualification, distinct ethics restrictions and lawful donor-contribution controls are undisturbed. The state-law remand is preserved, and no new general preliminary-injunction test is adopted.

## Holdings

All three holdings are adopted in the complete Opinion of the Court by the nine Justices identified in Opinion Topology. Each has direct majority authority; no combination of separate rationales is required.

### I. Private approval does not itself make the auditor the public speaker

**Holding and operative rule:** An auditor's private review and oral approval do not, on these allegations, make an issuer's expressly unaudited release the auditor's own public certification when no auditor representation was communicated or attributed to investors. A general market expectation about an outside auditor's involvement cannot supply that missing representation; the Court does not adopt a universal public-name requirement for every deceptive act, agency relationship, scheme, or indirect communication.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that responsibility for one's own statement and responsibility for helping another's fraud are distinct. Central Bank preserves that distinction in recognizing a confined assistance action. First Jersey imposes primary responsibility for proved hands-on deception, not for office, ownership, or assistance alone. BT's January 30 release did not identify Ernst & Young or convey its assurances and expressly described the figures as unaudited. The pleaded January 22 oral approval therefore does not turn BT's statement into the auditor's public certification. The contention that investors assumed an auditor's involvement does not identify an assurance the auditor actually communicated through this release. This conclusion addresses the primary-speaker theory presented; it neither immunizes a culpable assistant nor decides responsibility for an auditor's own report, independently attributed statement, or other direct deception.

### II. The preserved assistance allegations require application of Central Bank

**Holding and operative rule:** A preserved allegation of knowing or severely reckless affirmative substantial assistance to an underlying securities fraud cannot be dismissed merely because the assistant's conduct was undisclosed or the public statement was not attributed to it. The defendant must know or severely recklessly disregard both the fraud and its assisting role; severe recklessness requires an extreme departure from ordinary care presenting a specific danger of deception known or so obvious that the defendant must have appreciated it, and the plaintiff must establish the underlying fraud, qualifying assistance, legally sufficient reliance on that fraud, causation, and recoverable loss.

**Controlling explanation:** Central Bank requires no separate disclosure duty for affirmative assistance, but excludes negligence, professional status, valuation concerns alone, ordinary services without the necessary culpability and assisting effect, and mere duty-free silence. Investors need not rely on concealed assistance itself; that rule creates no automatic reliance presumption. Wright's amended complaint alleges affirmative approval after discovery of a specific accounts-payable under-accrual, and substantial participation was argued below. These are existing allegations to test, not a new claim supplied by the Court. The release's unaudited label bears on primary attribution but does not license knowing assistance to deception. Conversely, knowledge that figures will reach investors is not knowledge that they are fraudulent. Later discovery of accounting irregularities, alleged failures to follow auditing standards, correction of the reported profit, and a subsequent price fall do not themselves establish the required January mental state or the other elements. The strongest independent objection—that the alleged conduct shows only ordinary professional error—must be considered under the correct rule. The categorical rejection of assistance cannot decide it. The Court vacates that ground and remands for ordinary application of the elements and applicable pleading requirements, without finding that the complaint satisfies them or prescribing discovery, amendment, or recovery.

### III. The separate prospectus branch remains dismissed

**Holding and operative rule:** Correction of the categorical assistance rule for the pleaded January 1996 conduct does not revive the separately deficient 1995 prospectus theory or insert a later duty-to-correct claim into the amended complaint. That dismissal remains intact, and the remand creates no entitlement to amend.

**Controlling explanation:** The prospectus branch has its own pleading and preservation defects, including the absence of the duty-to-correct claim later pressed in opposition papers and deficient allegations concerning the earlier scienter, this class's reliance, and causation. Wright declined leave to replead before final judgment. Those circumstances differ from the affirmative January approval already alleged and argued. Central Bank corrects the governing law for the preserved assistance claim; it neither supplies missing allegations nor erases the distinct prospectus defects. The Court therefore leaves that dismissal in effect while returning only the January assistance branch for consideration under the proper rule.

Actual auditor reports, independently attributed statements, direct market deception, SEC enforcement, and nonfederal professional liability remain separate. The Court decides no settlement credit or ultimate recoverable amount.

- United States v. O'Hagan, June 25, 1997: preserved in its separate source-deception setting; its duty, deception, and transaction requirements supply no shortcut from professional status to liability here.

## Holdings

### I. Qualification ends this federal controversy

**Holding and operative rule:** A challenge seeking relief from an initiative's failure to qualify becomes moot when the initiative qualifies and the claimant shows neither a surviving redressable injury nor a reasonable expectation of renewed exposure to the challenged restriction. The exception for a dispute capable of repetition yet evading review requires both duration too short for complete review and reasonably expected renewed exposure of the same claimant; the continuing existence of an election rule alone does not satisfy it.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by all eight other Justices. These nine directly support the claimant-specific mootness holding and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the December 23 qualification removed the ballot-access injury presented for review. The Secretary accepted 47,406 signatures against the new threshold of 42,101 and found the petition valid for legislative consideration. The determination also states that previously accepted signatures already exceeded the new threshold. Petitioners' asserted future harm concerned further collection to secure this same November 1999 effort; acceptance removes that need. The record supplies no further initiative plan, surviving individual-capacity damages claim or other collateral injury redressable by the requested federal ruling.

*Spencer v. Kemna* requires a concrete continuing injury and both elements of the recurrence exception. *Norman v. Reed*, *Chandler v. Miller* and *Libertarian Party of Illinois v. Rednour* preserved review because their records established continuing electoral consequences or actual future exposure. They establish no general exception for election disputes. The persistence of Maine's rules and petitioners' repeated work on this measure do not supply the missing post-qualification injury. Nor does possible legislative rejection revive a circulation obstacle the initiative has already overcome. *Arizonans for Official English* prevents treating a general declaratory request against an official-capacity defendant as an unpleaded surviving damages claim.

*DeFunis v. Odegaard* requires the Court to resolve federal mootness even when reviewing a state judgment. *Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation* reserved residency and supplies neither a residency holding nor jurisdiction here. The Court reaches no residence, registration or signature-validity merits. Qualification is not enactment, a completed election, repeal of the restrictions or a finding that petitioners will never circulate another initiative.

### II. Equitable vacatur leaves state proceedings to Maine

**Holding and operative rule:** When a changed qualification threshold removes this controversy while petitioners continue seeking qualification and review, section 2106 permits equitable vacatur of the reviewed constitutional judgment and remand for proceedings allowed by state law. Vacatur is justified here by those circumstances, rather than by an automatic entitlement whenever review becomes moot; Article III does not require an order directing dismissal of the state action.

**Authority:** Part II of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; those eight Associates directly support its equitable rationale. Stone-Zsela agrees in the vacatur and bounded state remand without joining Part II's additional equitable explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that mootness and the proper treatment of the judgment require separate decisions. *U.S. Bancorp Mortgage Co. v. Bonner Mall Partnership* preserves a just disposition under section 2106 while rejecting automatic vacatur; its settlement rule is not a bar to relief whenever a litigant continues trying to obtain the practical benefit sought. The gubernatorial election changed the qualifying threshold. Petitioners' resubmission pursued qualification while seeking review; it was neither a settlement, consent to the adverse constitutional judgment nor abandonment of review. *Kentucky Right to Life v. Stengel* supports clearing a constitutional merits determination that intervening circumstances prevent the reviewing loser from obtaining review of, subject to the actual equities.

Leaving the judgment intact is a substantial alternative because this is a state proceeding and petitioners voluntarily resubmitted. But the external threshold change and continued pursuit of review justify vacating the reviewed judgment here. *DeFunis* supplies the bounded state-remand form. *Arizonans* concerned lower federal judgments and does not require importing its federal-action dismissal direction into Maine's courts. Maine determines the status of the remaining pleadings under its own law.

The judgment is vacated and the cause remanded for such proceedings as state law provides. This neither declares the judgment void when entered nor reopens abandoned factual-residency challenges. The administrative qualification remains undisturbed. The Court orders no new signature count, validates no otherwise defective signature and determines no future legislative or electoral outcome.

**Result:** Affirmed, 5–4, on the granted First Amendment claim.
**Version / lineage:** Initial Decision Record; supersedes no completed adjudication. No repository commit or Git verification is claimed.

## Holdings

### Assigned instructional production and the asserted personal speech right

**Holding and operative rule:** A public-school teacher's selection, production, and direction of material as part of the assigned school instructional program constitute institutional curricular expression subject to the school system's lawful control; the artistic discretion entrusted to the teacher does not, on this claim, create a personal First Amendment right against curricular and related employment control. This rule concerns the assigned school production, and does not authorize punishment of separate citizen speech or an independently unconstitutional classification or exclusion.

**Authority:** The complete Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas, supplies five direct rationale votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the personal right asserted must be identified before deciding whether an employer's justification defeats it. Boring selected and directed *Independence* through her assigned advanced acting class and an interscholastic competition for which the school bore institutional responsibility. Artistic judgment and public controversy do not turn that instructional choice into a personal right to control the institution's expression. Hazelwood distinguishes personal student expression from school-sponsored activity and recognizes legitimate educational control; it does not give the employee administering that activity a parallel personal editorial entitlement. Settle protects a student's otherwise permissible perspective within an assignment against categorical religious exclusion disguised as pedagogy. Its constitutional limit remains, but the student's claim does not establish a teacher's personal right over the school's selected program. Jeffries protected an unaffiliated address expressly outside assigned work; Brewster addressed a particular attendance-error report, not selection of instructional content.

The strongest contrary argument is that Boring challenges retaliation after approval, not the school's final power to choose a play. The Court accepts the pleaded approval, compliance, and retaliatory motivation at their proper status. Those allegations do not create the missing personal curricular interest or make approval an irrevocable constitutional delegation. This conclusion does not establish that officials actually acted for legitimate reasons. Settle and the independently applicable constitutional prohibitions prevent treating every act labeled curricular as lawful by definition. But the granted claim identifies no separate citizen expression or independent forbidden classification supplying the personal right asserted here. Waters and Mt. Healthy regulate justification and cause once protected conduct is established; they do not supply that antecedent right. Dismissal therefore stands on this confined ground.

The conditional second question is not reached because the asserted personal interest in this assigned curricular act is absent. The Court decides no standard for a distinct protected interest outside assigned curricular responsibilities, no rule for every duty-related statement of every public employee, and no materially different university academic-freedom question. Statutory, contractual, tenure, and state-law limits remain separate. The Court finds neither insubordination nor lawful actual motive nor a same-decision defense.

## Precedent Treatment

- Hazelwood School District v. Kuhlmeier: applies the distinction between personal student expression and school-sponsored educational activity; its student-speech rule is not converted into an instructor's personal control over institutional content.
- Settle v. Dickson County School Board, June 25, 1996: preserves genuine curricular authority and the prohibition on categorical religious exclusion masquerading as pedagogy; distinguishes the student's limited interest within an assignment from the teacher's asserted interest in administering the program.
- Jeffries v. Harleston, November 17, 1997: distinguishes the unaffiliated citizen address expressly outside assigned official work; its protection, actual-cause rule, and limited mandate remain intact.
- Brewster v. Board of Education, August 17, 1998: distinguishes the particular internal attendance-error report and conduct-date immunity question; no general rule withdrawing protection from workplace reports follows.
- Waters v. Churchill, May 31, 1994: preserves its limited majority propositions on genuine lawful grounds and mistaken accounts of protected employee speech; supplies no personal curricular entitlement or universal investigation procedure.
- Mt. Healthy City School District Board of Education v. Doyle: preserves the claimant's protected-conduct and substantial-or-motivating-factor showing and the employer's separate preponderance same-decision defense; no defense is found proved here.
- Pickering v. Board of Education, Connick v. Myers, and Givhan v. Western Line Consolidated School District: retain contextual public-employee speech protection, including private speech; the Court adopts no categorical workplace or private-concern exclusion.
- Epperson v. Arkansas and Keyishian v. Board of Regents: preserve independently applicable constitutional limits on educational authority; the curricular label does not establish immunity from them.

No precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

The teacher's delegated selection and direction of this school instructional production do not supply the personal speech entitlement claimed against curricular and related employment control. That limited institutional-content rule leaves student expression, separate citizen speech, independent constitutional prohibitions, and other sources of employment protection in place. The decision adopts no universal rule for government employees' assigned speech and no constitutional safe harbor based on any conceivable pedagogical reason.

## Holdings

### I. Common issues may be tried together only with a meaningful defense

**Holding and operative rule:** A State may decide genuinely common issues together when each defendant has a meaningful opportunity to contest the evidence and every material defense to those issues, while the remaining claimant-specific elements must still be proved. Neither the number of parties nor the absence of brand names on a verdict form alone establishes a due-process violation; efficiency cannot replace a legally required individual showing.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, controls this holding and Holdings II and III at their stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the constitutional question concerns what the procedure actually decides and whether the affected party can defend against it. *Richards v. Jefferson County* requires a lawful, meaningful opportunity to litigate before a judgment binds a person; shared interests alone cannot supply one. Crane was an actual party and could contest the common evidence. That distinction does not excuse eliminating a material product defense merely because an individual damages hearing will follow.

*Amchem Products v. Windsor* requires the prerequisites of the procedural device actually used and protection of material differences. Its requirements for a federal settlement class do not govern consolidation under Maryland Rule 2-503. The State's authority to consolidate common questions does not alter substantive exposure, product connection, causation, injury or damages requirements. Five trial plaintiffs cannot statistically supply those different facts for every other claimant. Nor does a category label establish that materially different products share the characteristic at issue. A lost defense requires relief fitted to the affected adjudication, however useful aggregation might otherwise be.

The Court adopts no numerical ceiling, brand-name-verdict requirement, efficiency exception to due process, or rule binding strangers without lawful representation. The controlling inquiry remains the actual common issue, evidence, opportunity to defend, and elements left for individual proof.

### II. The reviewed Crane judgments satisfy that requirement

**Holding and operative rule:** The consolidated procedure and product-category findings do not deny Crane due process on this record because the actual common evidence, differentiated verdicts and retained individual elements preserved a meaningful defense. This conclusion does not extend a common finding to a materially different product or defense that was not fairly adjudicated.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the concrete record defeats Crane's asserted structural inference. Lawyers identified their clients; explanations and instructions distinguished earlier liability from contested liability; verdicts identified defendants; and the five trial plaintiffs illustrated issues without supplying extrapolated recoveries for the untried claimants. Receiving overlapping evidence across phases did not itself prevent Crane from disputing its significance.

The product-category finding rests on testimony connecting visible asbestos dust with excessive exposure, the absence of directed contrary proof for the products implicated in this appeal, and the continued requirement to prove actual exposure and resulting harm. The lower court acknowledged that products differ. It did not determine that a nonemitting or materially different product could never exist.

The jury distinguished Crane packing from Crane gaskets. Crane argues that Theis and Glensky described gaskets and thus received inconsistent judgments. The admitted catalog identified sheet packing from which gaskets could be cut; testimony described cutting sheets and the resulting dust. That evidence supports the packing finding despite the witnesses' description of the cut article as a gasket. The verdict did not simply impose liability on the separately exonerated gasket product.

The corrected starting dates for Stebbing and Hampshire, set-aside Harbison-Walker findings, and reversals of unsupported fiber-drift exposure findings are real errors. They do not establish that Crane lost a material defense or that the differentiated remaining findings resulted from an incapable jury. Conversely, appellate correction is no universal cure for unconstitutional aggregation. The present affirmance rests on the particular opportunity and evidence supporting Crane's judgments, applying the fair-adjudication requirement of *Richards* and the protection of substantive differences preserved in *Amchem*.

### III. The affirmance preserves individual proof and distinct remedies

**Holding and operative rule:** Affirmance of the reviewed due-process ruling leaves the state appellate court's independent reversals, reductions, vacaturs and remands in force. Later proceedings may apply only the common findings lawfully resolved; each claimant must still prove individual exposure, disease, sufficient causation and damages, and any extension to a materially different product or defense remains open to challenge on its actual record.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that rejection of the categorical attack does not erase specific errors or create final recoveries for claimants whose individual cases remain untried. The lower mandate separately reverses specified judgments against U.S. Mineral, Stebbing and Hampshire for insufficient evidence, requires reduction of Ciotta's judgment under the damages cap, directs proceedings on specified Hampshire contractor instructions, and adjusts judgment shares accordingly. Those dispositions retain their own grounds.

The common negligence and defect findings will not ordinarily be retried in later individual proceedings. That makes the scope of the original opportunity to defend essential; later mini-trials could not cure a material defense already foreclosed. The findings upheld here carry only the scope supported by the actual common evidence. The Court neither rewrites Maryland causation law nor authorizes averaging different exposures, products or injuries. No separate federal jury-trial claim is decided. The due-process judgment concerns Crane's preserved challenge to the actual final judgments before the Court, not every possible future application of the common findings.

## Precedent Treatment

- *Richards v. Jefferson County* is applied to preserve a meaningful opportunity to litigate and lawful grounds for binding a party. It does not prohibit an actual defendant's fairly conducted common-issue trial or permit binding strangers solely because their interests resemble those of participants.
- *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor* is distinguished as a federal settlement-class decision and applied for its protection of substantive differences and the requirements of the actual procedural device. Its Rule 23 prerequisites do not become rules for Maryland consolidation; its rejection of procedural shortcuts remains intact.

## Law After Decision

Common-issue consolidation remains available subject to a meaningful defense on every material common issue and separate proof of the individual elements. Trial size and category labels alone do not invalidate it, and efficiency alone cannot sustain it. These Crane judgments survive the asserted due-process challenge. The separate state appellate corrections remain effective. No universal asbestos finding, extrapolated injury award, or permission to expand a common verdict to unadjudicated material differences follows.

**Case and dockets:** National Collegiate Athletic Association v. Smith; No. 98-84.
**Event and date:** Merits decision, 1999-02-23; OT1998, chunk 4.

## Holdings

### I. Ordinary membership dues do not alone establish receipt of federal assistance

**Holding and operative rule:** An association does not become a recipient of federal financial assistance under Title IX merely because it receives ordinary membership dues from institutions receiving such assistance. Assistance may reach a recipient through an intermediary, but an ordinary downstream payment by a recipient is not, without more, assistance extended to the payee.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, controls this holding and Holding II at their stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that receipt and economic benefit are different. *Grove City College v. Bell* establishes that federal student aid intended for payment to a college can make the college an indirect recipient. *Department of Transportation v. Paralyzed Veterans of America* distinguishes a recipient accepting assistance from another entity benefiting economically from assisted operations. Ordinary membership dues fall on the latter side of that distinction unless additional facts establish a transfer of assistance.

Section 1687 enlarges the operations covered once assistance reaches part of the identified entity. Its provision for an entity established by two or more listed institutions still requires that some part receive assistance. The Civil Rights Restoration Act did not eliminate that condition. The complete definition in 34 C.F.R. §106.2(h) likewise requires assistance extended directly or through another recipient. Its following reference to an educational activity receiving or benefiting from assistance does not erase the first requirement.

The NCAA's role in collegiate athletics and its relationship to member institutions do not make every membership payment federal assistance. Neither the amount nor the percentage of dues supplies an exemption or threshold. The private action retains the recipient requirement; its implied character does not extend coverage to every beneficiary. *Kelley* governs equal athletic opportunity at covered institutions, and *Gebser* addresses attribution of discrimination to an established recipient; neither supplies receipt here. This holding does not relieve funded members of their own duties or decide independently sufficient coverage based on actual assistance or delegated control.

### II. The unadjudicated alternatives and pleading questions remain for the lower courts

**Holding and operative rule:** The dues-based Title IX disposition must be vacated and the case remanded; properly preserved theories of actual assistance through the National Youth Sports Program or its Fund and independently sufficient delegated control remain open. Dismissal alone does not make an amendment motion moot, but the remand grants neither leave to amend nor a finding that an alternative is preserved or sufficient.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the lower judgment's stated basis fails without deciding issues the lower courts did not independently resolve. The Third Circuit's description of the NCAA as an athletics surrogate supported its dues rationale. It was not a completed adjudication of either NYSP assistance or a control-only theory. Those alternatives require their own proper presentation and factual and legal support; the existence of a contention proves neither.

*Foman v. Davis* supplies the ordinary amendment principles: futility, undue delay, bad faith, dilatory motive and prejudice remain available grounds under the applicable procedure. An allegation relying only on ordinary dues cannot cure the recipient defect. The lower courts must consider the actual pleading history, preservation and the Marshall affidavit's procedural significance before determining any permissible amendment. The Court does not independently grant or reject the proposed addition of the universities.

*Steel Co.* distinguishes constitutional jurisdiction from disputed statutory liability. This decision concerns statutory coverage at the pleading stage; it does not declare an Article III defect. Expiration of the athletic seasons does not by itself eliminate Smith's compensation demand, but recipient status, responsibility, discrimination, causation, compensable loss and entitlement to prospective relief remain unproved. Contract-jurisdiction consequences follow the federal claim's resulting posture, and the antitrust judgment is not reopened.

## Precedent Treatment

- *Grove City College v. Bell* is applied to preserve indirect receipt where assistance is intended for the recipient; §1687 broadened covered operations without erasing receipt.
- *Department of Transportation v. Paralyzed Veterans of America* supplies the analogous distinction between receipt of assistance and economic benefit, which ordinary dues do not overcome.
- *Kelley v. Board of Trustees* remains the rule for equal athletic opportunity within covered institutions; it establishes no association coverage from dues.
- *Gebser v. Lago Vista Independent School District* remains concerned with attribution and liability at an established recipient; its grounds and coalition limits do not decide these alternative coverage theories.
- *Foman v. Davis* governs ordinary amendment discretion; dismissal alone does not moot the request, and no amendment is guaranteed.
- *Steel Co.* preserves the distinction between Article III jurisdiction and the statutory recipient element.

## Law After Decision

Title IX reaches actual recipients through direct or indirect delivery of assistance; ordinary payments from assisted institutions do not alone extend coverage to their associations. Institution-wide coverage under §1687 begins with receipt somewhere in the identified entity. The NCAA is neither automatically covered nor categorically exempt. Its possible NYSP assistance, independently sufficient delegated control, properly preserved pleadings and any ultimate liability remain for their own adjudication.

## Holdings

### I. Refusal to reopen is not a new appealable reimbursement determination

**Holding and operative rule:** A fiscal intermediary's discretionary refusal to reopen a final reimbursement determination is not a new final determination as to the amount of total program reimbursement under 42 U.S.C. §1395oo(a)(1)(A)(i). Judicial review of the Board's final jurisdictional ruling under §1395oo(f)(1) permits deciding that question; it does not give the Board jurisdiction over the refusal or authorize direct judicial calculation of reimbursement.

**Authority:** Part I-A of Kennedy's opinion is joined by all eight other Justices. Part I-B's additional regulatory construction and deference ground is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; Stone-Zsela does not join that ground. Each stated rationale commands a majority at that scope.

**Controlling explanation — I-A, statutory ground:** The Court holds that the refusal leaves the earlier amount determination in place. Congress supplied a 180-day appeal period; the regulation supplies a distinct, ordinarily three-year opportunity to request reopening. Treating every refusal as a new amount determination would allow a provider to renew the statutory appeal simply by requesting reconsideration. The provider's contrary argument—that refusal finally leaves payment unchanged—confuses the effect of preserving an earlier decision with a new decision about its amount. The Board's authority under §1395oo(d) over matters in a cost report operates once the hearing conditions are met; it does not remove them. The credibility of the asserted cost error supplies no missing jurisdiction.

**Controlling explanation — I-B, regulatory support:** Section 405.1885(c) gives reopening jurisdiction exclusively to the body that last decided the matter; §405.1889 separately recognizes review when actual reopening produces a revision in the reimbursement amount. The Secretary's considered reading respects that distinction. *Auer* requires a fair and considered regulatory interpretation consistent with the text, and *Thomas Jefferson University* applies that bounded deference to Medicare regulations. The manual explains this arrangement rather than creating jurisdiction or independently amending law, consistently with *Guernsey*. The interpretation leaves the regulation's mandatory reopening commands intact. An agency's jurisdictional label is not conclusive, and deference does not replace the statutory analysis.

### II. The claim cannot proceed directly under federal-question jurisdiction

**Holding and operative rule:** Incorporated §405(h) bars the asserted 28 U.S.C. §1331 route because this request seeks reopening for additional Medicare reimbursement and derives its standing and substantive basis from the Medicare scheme. Describing the requested order as procedural does not remove that claim from the statute.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part II, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, commands eight votes. Stone-Zsela agrees only in the judgment on this component. This same eight-Justice coalition controls Holdings III and IV.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the requested reopening is a means of obtaining payment under Medicare. *Heckler v. Ringer* supplies the arising-under inquiry and does not allow a claimant to escape statutory channeling by requesting a preliminary procedural step. *Michigan Academy* recognizes a presumption of review in its different Part B setting; it does not promise a new district-court route whenever the claimant has not used an original statutory appeal. This scheme offered an original Board appeal followed by judicial review. The missed deadline matters to that arrangement, but it is not treated as nonexhaustion of the separate, later refusal claim. No distinct colorable constitutional claim is presented, and no independently authorized constitutional channel is foreclosed.

### III. The APA supplies no independent jurisdiction

**Holding and operative rule:** The Administrative Procedure Act does not independently confer subject-matter jurisdiction over this refusal to reopen. Its review standards do not supply jurisdiction missing from the asserted statutory routes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that *Califano v. Sanders*, which rejects the APA as an independent jurisdictional grant, governs. The provider expressly asks the Court to reconsider that rule based on Medicare's corrective-adjustment command. That command regulates lawful reimbursement; it does not amend the APA's jurisdictional character or create an exception when §1331 is barred. Section 1395oo(f)'s use of APA standards in an authorized action likewise does not establish that every proposed action is authorized. The Court neither decides the reimbursement claim nor adopts a separate factual conclusion about the salary evidence. No distinct colorable constitutional claim is presented; the treatment of such a claim is not decided away.

### IV. No clear duty to reopen these determinations supports mandamus

**Holding and operative rule:** Assuming without deciding that incorporated §405(h) does not universally preclude mandamus under 28 U.S.C. §1361, mandamus fails because no clear nondiscretionary duty to reopen these final determinations is established. The denial rests on the absence of that duty, not on failure to exhaust the available avenues concerning the refusal.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that lawful reimbursement duties do not automatically require reopening closed reports. *Ringer* requires a clear nondiscretionary duty and exhaustion of other avenues. The provider exhausted the refusal claim. The reasonable-cost and comparable-compensation requirements govern reimbursement; *Good Samaritan* places corrective adjustments within authorized cost methods, and *Regions Hospital* distinguishes correction of historical inputs for future payments from reopening final annual payments. Neither supplies the requested duty.

Section 405.1885(a) permits reopening on a timely motion and ordinarily requires a request within three years of notice; reopening afterward is barred except under paragraphs (d) and (e). New evidence or alleged error alone does not command reopening. Paragraph (b) requires reopening and revision when HCFA, within those three years, notifies the intermediary that its determination or hearing decision is inconsistent with applicable law, regulations, or general instructions issued by HCFA in accordance with the Secretary's agreement with the intermediary. Paragraph (d) requires reopening and revision at any time when a party procured the determination or decision by fraud or similar fault. Neither trigger is established. A provider's allegation is not HCFA notification or proof of fraud.

Paragraph (e) applies paragraphs (a) and (b) to periods ending on or after December 31, 1971, and the three-year limit to earlier periods reopened after May 27, 1972; it changes nothing for 1989. Section 405.1887 requires written notice of reopening, an explanation when revision follows, and a reasonable opportunity for additional evidence or argument in the reopening. Section 405.1889 separately permits review of an actual post-reopening revision in reimbursement. Those provisions do not require granting every request. The manual's new-material-evidence, clear-error and inconsistency considerations cannot override the distinct regulatory commands. The compensation claim's possible accuracy therefore does not establish a clear present reopening duty.

### V. The affirmance decides the presented routes and preserves their limits

**Holding and operative rule:** The Sixth Circuit's judgment is affirmed without a reimbursement award, reopening order, Board merits hearing or further remand. Timely initial appeals, actual reopening followed by a new determination, genuinely presented regulatory procedural violations and independently authorized constitutional channels retain their own requirements and are not decided here.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part V's stated disposition and reservations. The conclusions about the independent routes remain controlled by the distinct coalitions stated above.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds only that the routes presented do not authorize relief on this record. An established mandatory reopening trigger, actual amount revision or independently supported constitutional claim would present its own question under the lawful channel governing it; none receives automatic review or relief. The possible absence of the intermediary as a party supplies no independent dismissal ground. Nor do four constituent reports establish an unraised amount-in-controversy or grouping defect. The decision does not determine the proper compensation amount, compel payment of a claimed cost, or declare all reopening-related actions unreviewable.

- *Califano v. Sanders* is followed: the APA supplies no independent subject-matter jurisdiction. Its treatment of genuine constitutional claims is not displaced.
- *Heckler v. Ringer* supplies the Medicare arising-under analysis and mandamus's clear-duty requirement. Its reservation about universal §405(h) preclusion of mandamus remains open.

These asserted federal-question, independent-APA and mandamus routes fail separately. Actual revisions after reopening, mandatory HCFA-notification and fraud-or-similar-fault triggers, properly presented procedural violations and independently authorized constitutional claims retain their distinct terms and channels; the decision promises no automatic remedy for them. The original reimbursement remains undisturbed without a decision approving its substantive amount.

## Holdings

### I-A. The withdrawal reaches the named acts in these pending proceedings

**Holding and operative rule:** From April 1, 1997, section 1252(g) reaches earlier, pending and future proceedings, and its named-action withdrawal concerns claims arising from the Attorney General's identified decisions or actions to commence proceedings, adjudicate cases or execute removal orders, rather than every legal challenge to completed adjudication. This selection challenge attacks commencement and the requested restraint of adjudication; neither its constitutional label nor its possible attachment to a later final-order petition moves it outside the named actions, and continuing proceedings is not a fourth statutory act.

**Authority:** Souter's Part I-A, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls by eight direct joins. The same eight-Justice coalition controls Parts I-B, II and III-A below. Stone-Zsela joins no part of the opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies *Yang*, which distinguishes commencement of the new rule from the proceedings it reaches, and *Reyes-Hernandez*, which separates the named executive actions from specified legal challenges to completed adjudication. This suit seeks to restrain the former. Those decisions preserved the additional constitutional-access question; they did not create a general exemption for old proceedings or constitutional claims.

Corrected section 309(c)(1) generally saves the former procedure, subject to its exceptions. Paragraph (2) permits election of the new procedure only before the evidentiary hearing has begun and on at least thirty days' notice; paragraph (3) permits termination and reinitiation only before a final administrative decision. Paragraph (4)'s transitional review rules require a final exclusion or deportation order entered more than thirty days after September 30, 1996. No election or final order is established here. The permanent scheme, including section 1252(b)(9), cannot be imported wholesale.

As *Salazar-Haro* and *Kolster* explain, section 306(c)(2) protects an actual judgment or order entered under former section 106, as amended, against paragraph (1)'s invalidation or compelled reconsideration. It grants no jurisdiction, reopens no merits review and does not bar ordinary review of a judgment's correctness. These collateral injunctions are not automatically qualifying section 106 judgments. The express transitions therefore defeat a blanket pending-case exemption without resolving the distinct question in Part III-B.

### I-B. The individual-relief exception does not grant jurisdiction

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1252(f)(1)'s exception for application of the designated provisions to an individual alien against whom proceedings have been initiated limits that subsection's prohibition on lower-court injunctive relief; it does not independently confer subject-matter jurisdiction. Section 1252(f)(2)'s clear-and-convincing-evidence condition concerns enjoining removal pursuant to a final order and does not govern this case, which presents no such order.

**Authority:** Souter's Part I-B; the eight-Justice coalition stated in Part I-A.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court rejects the Ninth Circuit's reliance on subsection (f) as a jurisdictional answer. An exception to a restriction on remedies does not establish the underlying power to hear a claim. The availability of individual relief may inform the surrounding statutory construction, but the original jurisdiction retained in Part III-B rests on section 1331 and the constitutional action against federal officers. Subsection (f)(2) addresses a different remedy at a different stage; its condition cannot be substituted for subsection (f)(1) or applied as though a final deportation order already existed. No categorical conclusion about every possible injunction application is necessary.

### II. The proposed factual transfer does not make this final-order channel adequate

**Holding and operative rule:** Former section 1105a(a)'s incorporation of chapter 158 for final-deportation-order review does not authorize the proposed district-court development of this selection claim's necessary extra-record evidence: paragraph (4)'s administrative-record restriction, subject to the nationality-transfer exception in (5)(B), and section 2347's hearing conditions must both be satisfied. A required deportation hearing cannot become an action taken without a required hearing merely by treating the earlier charging decision as the action reviewed while using the final order as the jurisdictional trigger.

**Authority:** Souter's Part II; the eight-Justice coalition stated in Part I-A.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the proposed route lacks the statutory capability attributed to it. Section 2347(a) uses the agency record when a hearing was held. Subsection (b) first requires that no hearing preceded the action reviewed. It then requires agency remand if a hearing was legally required; decision on pleadings and affidavits if none was required and no genuine material factual dispute exists; or district-court transfer if none was required and such a dispute exists. These conditions are not a general permission to develop any missing record.

The final deportation order follows a required hearing. The immigration judges and Board cannot adjudicate selection, but their inability does not erase the separate record-only command. The express nationality mechanism confirms that a general extra-record transfer cannot simply be assumed. Nor can the developed district-court record be imported merely because it exists.

Subsection (c) separately permits additional agency evidence upon materiality and reasonable grounds for the earlier omission. Any permanent or transitional prohibition of that subsection requires its own trigger; excluding (c) does not make (b)(3) available. The parties' accounts of a deleted Senate proposal are not adopted as legislative facts. The Government's proposed screening, explanation and material-dispute procedure, Rule 48, or a comparable future procedure cannot create missing authority. Its advocacy is neither a jurisdiction-conferring stipulation nor a concession that every selection claim constitutionally requires a forum.

This ruling concerns the necessary selection evidence, not every constitutional legal argument cognizable on a proper administrative record or a future constitutional remedy. *Kolster* preserves conditional section 2241 authority against AEDPA section 440(a); it does not decide subsection (g), establish custody, respondent, forum, exhaustion, filing, factual-review scope, stay or relief here. No new discovery or trial is required merely because the present statutory mechanism fails.

### III-A. Access, statutory defenses, constitutional merits and interim protection are distinct

**Holding and operative rule:** A substantial constitutional selection claim cannot be rejected merely because the actual immigration charges may be lawful or a statutory defense may avert deportation; those questions do not necessarily adjudicate selection, permit its necessary proof or repair an independently supported present injury. The developed preliminary findings make this access contention substantial, but establish neither ultimate discrimination, a general right to immediate review nor an automatic entitlement to stop the proceedings.

**Authority:** Souter's Part III-A; the eight-Justice coalition stated in Part I-A.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court distinguishes a forum from success there. *Thunder Basin* permits constitutional claims to follow an administrative route when actual judicial review is meaningful, despite the agency's inability finally to decide constitutional law. *McNary* identifies an unavailable factual record as a real barrier; its separate surrender-to-obtain-review obstacle differs from proceedings already pending here. *Catholic Social Services* requires an actual obstacle, not a collateral label. *AT&T v. Iowa Utilities Board* concerned an announcement imposing no present conduct constraint and leaving concrete statutory review; this case presents instituted proceedings, preliminary injury findings and an identified factual-review barrier. *Your Home* presented no distinct colorable constitutional claim and does not decide this access question. *Yoshina* separates a substantial maintained claim from its ultimate failure, without supplying any individual's surviving controversy here.

Ordinary defense costs, anxiety and undifferentiated chill do not justify stopping adjudication. *Hollywood Motor Car* distinguishes a dismissal remedy from a right never to undergo proceedings, but already had a trial court capable of hearing the constitutional motion. *Younger* and *Dombrowski* distinguish good-faith process from sufficiently supported continuing suppression; their state-federal comity rationale does not independently bar this federal action. *Oestereich*'s unequivocal exemption is absent; its broader timing concurrence is not controlling.

The preliminary association-based selection, disparate-treatment and present-chill findings are more than pleadings, but do not resolve the disputed conduct and immigration arguments. *Whren* separates objective grounds from forbidden selection; *Dawson* distinguishes abstract association from relevant evidence; *Armstrong*'s indirect racial-selection discovery rule does not decide this developed association claim's complete merits. *R.A.V.* controls on overbreadth, not its separate content-selection proposal. A successful statutory defense may leave no adverse final order without deciding retaliation; that possibility proves neither a future controversy nor entitlement to an advisory declaration. The selection merits, universal citizen-alien equivalence and immigration immunity remain undecided.

### III-B. A confined construction preserves a capable constitutional forum

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1252(g) does not foreclose existing section 1331 jurisdiction over a substantial constitutional selection claim when a claimant has a concrete personal controversy, the actual immigration process cannot adjudicate that claim, and the applicable judicial-review provisions cannot receive the factual record necessary to decide it. A constitutional or collateral label, disagreement with enforcement discretion, or preference for earlier review is insufficient; this retained access does not itself entitle the claimant to an injunction.

**Authority:** Souter's Part III-B, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls by five direct joins. The same five-Justice coalition controls Part IV. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas reject this construction; Stone-Zsela also rejects present collateral jurisdiction. No narrowest-ground combination of the competing constructions is involved.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress has not unmistakably eliminated every capable forum for this substantial constitutional claim. *Webster* requires clear intent before reading a statutory restriction to exclude consideration of colorable constitutional objections. *Lincoln* preserves constitutional review separately from discretionary statutory administration. The actual-barrier reasoning of *McNary* and *Catholic Social Services*, and *Kolster*'s requirement that a retained route reach the substantial authority objection and effective relief, reinforce that distinction without automatically extending their different statutory holdings.

The contrary textual argument is substantial: this suit attacks the very actions subsection (g) names, and its notwithstanding clause and reference to any cause or claim are broad. The Court does not redescribe selection as a completed-adjudication claim. It construes the withdrawal narrowly only at the distinct point of otherwise unavailable meaningful constitutional adjudication. The statute's treatment of constitutional questions elsewhere and contemplated individual relief supply context, not jurisdiction or wholesale applicability of the permanent scheme. Section 1331 and the existing constitutional action against federal officers supply the original basis.

Here the agency cannot adjudicate selection, the applicable judicial record limitation excludes its necessary factual record, and the proposed transfer does not cure that defect. The preliminary findings of present association-based coercion make postponing even access to the only competent forum inappropriate merely because a statutory defense might succeed. The Court does not adopt every lower merits ground or treat every First Amendment injury as automatically irreparable. *Reyes-Hernandez* retains its named-action boundary; this decision answers its reserved constitutional-access issue at the stated narrow scope. Ordinary channeling remains where a capable route exists. Whether the Constitution would itself require the same access absent a permissible construction, or where an adequate later route actually exists, remains open.

### IV. Retained jurisdiction requires separate reconsideration of each injunction

**Holding and operative rule:** The confined jurisdictional result is affirmed, but the appellate judgment's unqualified preservation of preliminary relief is set aside for individualized reconsideration of continuing controversy, entitlement and scope; the existing District Court orders remain temporarily effective, without enlargement, pending lower-court action on remand. The Six's refusal-to-dissolve posture and the Two's later initial injunction must be analyzed separately, and individual relief must be modified or dissolved if its continuing requirements are absent.

**Authority:** Souter's Part IV; the five-Justice coalition stated in Part III-B.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court preserves the existing orders during reconsideration because constitutional jurisdiction remains and the record contains preliminary findings of present injury. This is a bounded interim consequence, not final approval of those findings or automatic relief from a jurisdictional label. *Webster* preserves ordinary equitable limits; *Catholic Social Services* requires relief fitted to concrete claimants and barriers.

The January 5, 1994 order protects the Six against further deportation proceedings. The Government's later resubmission of already available evidence does not alone reopen every preliminary issue as an initial application. But new statutory law, status changes, continuing controversy, actual ongoing injury and the need to restrain proceedings rather than afford narrower protection require consideration. The April 1996 order separately grants the Two's renewed initial application; their own showing, disputed conduct and immigration grounds must support that relief. The Six's modification posture cannot supply the Two's missing predicates.

Hamide and Shehadeh are permanent residents facing association/material-support charges; the Six's original grounds concern status violations. Barakat and Sharif obtained temporary resident status and cannot be deported on their original status grounds. That does not establish permanent residence, universal immunity or a new charge. Respondents' view that the injunction protects against new charges and their allegation of no governmental disavowal establish neither a particular charge nor an imminent individual threat. The lower courts must identify any actual surviving controversy; temporary preservation supplies no adjudicated protection against hypothetical charges.

The remand permits narrowing, modification or dissolution on the supported record. It does not require new discovery, a hearing, permanent protection, a fixed deadline, a removal order or a scheduled return here. Neither statutory deportability, specific intent, final discrimination nor a universal equivalence of citizen and alien rights is decided.

## Law After Decision

Effective February 24, 1999, the named-action withdrawal and old-proceeding transitions remain in force. They now operate with the confined section 1331 construction for a concrete, substantial constitutional selection claim whose necessary factual adjudication is unavailable in the actual statutory channel. Agency inability alone, a constitutional label and a preference for immediate review remain insufficient. The proposed section 2347 route cannot supply this missing factual review under the applicable hearing and record provisions; the separate availability of constitutional legal arguments on a proper agency record is left intact.

Neither subsection (g)'s constitutionality nor the selection merits is decided. The two existing injunctions survive only provisionally and without enlargement while the lower courts address their distinct procedural and individual requirements. No habeas rule is added, no immigration status is granted, and no valid substantive deportation ground is immunized. The competing limited constitutional-exclusion inquiry and dismissal position are noncontrolling; neither enlarges the Court's construction.

## Holdings
#### Actual protected policy selection remains outside the FTCA waiver

**Operative rule.** Section 2680(a) bars claims requiring adjudication of governmental choice susceptible to protected policy judgment, even if the discretion is abused. It does not protect violation of a specifically prescribed course or an act merely because choice exists or the subject is military.

**Authority.** Thomas's Part I has eight direct joins: Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. It controls the protected-policy proposition and the agreed treatment of actual policy-selection allegations; Stone-Zsela agrees with their dismissal on a different ground.

**Controlling explanation.** Berkovitz requires attention to whether a binding direction leaves lawful choice; Gaubert then asks whether permitted discretion is of the kind the exception protects. Gaubert rejects an automatic planning-versus-operations division. The Court therefore preserves immunity for actual entrusted choices about military protective substances, combinations and policy warnings without declaring every act of storage, administration or warning execution protected.

Aktepe requires the same attention to the claim actually presented and supplies no categorical immunity for military subject matter. Neither a hypothetical safety instruction nor an allegation labeled operational establishes an actionable violation. The Justices disagree about whether the remaining execution allegations in these complaints can be separated from policy selection. Part I settles the legal distinction, not that disputed application, and finds no negligence, causation or entitlement to compensation.

#### No common rationale controls dismissal of all remaining allegations

Five Justices affirm the whole dismissal, but only four adopt Part II's complete section 2680(a) application. O'Connor and Stone-Zsela rely on Feres; Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer reserve that question. Four others reject a bar based merely on military causal genesis and seek limited further examination of execution allegations.

These grounds are not nested rules. A protected policy choice may be unrelated to military service, while Feres may bar a service-incident claim involving conduct that violated a binding direction and therefore fails section 2680(a)'s test. Marks does not yield a five-Justice rule from them. The judgment binds these parties; it creates no general Feres rule for all injuries to military families and no controlling determination that every alleged execution act here is protected policy.

## Precedent Treatment
- **Berkovitz v. United States, 486 U.S. 531:** Applied to distinguish a specifically prescribed course from permitted choice; a possible directive is not a proved directive.
- **United States v. Gaubert, 499 U.S. 315:** Applied to require protected policy discretion and to reject an automatic planning/operations boundary. The full application to the remaining allegations lacks five joins.
- **Aktepe v. United States:** Applied for claim-specific examination of policy dependence; its reservation of Feres remains intact.
- **Feres v. United States; Stencel Aero Engineering Corp. v. United States; United States v. Shearer; United States v. Johnson:** Existing holdings remain. The Court adopts no majority extension to all civilian personal injuries traceable to military service.
- **United States v. Brown, 348 U.S. 110:** Its distinction involving a separate postservice wrong is not displaced or made sufficient to establish such a wrong here.
- **Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188:** Supplies no common rule where the judgment-supporting grounds are not logical subsets.

## Law After Decision
The children's dismissal stands. The Court applies the existing discretionary-function distinction to actual policy selection without adding a categorical military exception. The divided application to the remaining allegations and the proposed Feres extension establish no broader majority rule. Ordinary state-law prerequisites and the FTCA's other exceptions retain their independent force.

## Judgment & Remedy
**Affirmed, 5-4.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.
