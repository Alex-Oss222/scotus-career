SOURCE 27: mcclellan_v_cablevision_merits_1999-01-07.md
## Holdings

### The asserted federal damages action

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 531(e) of the Cable Act does not, by itself and in the statutory setting presented, create the asserted implied private damages action against Cablevision. A prohibition protecting public-access programming does not establish congressional intent to authorize that judicial damages remedy; independently authorized forms of relief must be identified and tested under their own sources.

**Authority.** Souter's unanimous Court opinion controls this rule, its damages-only review scope and the confined remand. All nine Justices join the full explanation.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the statutory command and the authority to recover damages present different questions. Cort v. Ash, Touche Ross and Transamerica require an inquiry into Congress's intent to authorize the particular action. Cannon permits contextual implication, so the concrete protection of programmers matters and an express remedy elsewhere is not automatically conclusive. But the differentiated provisions here and the limited judicial background do not support this new damages action. Section 532(d) expressly provides a particular leased-capacity action; section 531(c) addresses enforcement of franchise requirements. Neither is a general public-access damages authorization.

The strongest contrary argument combines the protected speakers, the direct editorial-control prohibition, the possible gap in franchise enforcement and Congress's later amendments. Those amendments followed only a few district-court recognitions, not the settled received-action background on which Curran and the existing securities action rest. The subsequent abandonment of one lower recognition is not attributed to Congress as foreknowledge. A gap in compensation does not itself supply the intended remedy, and the Court does not assume local enforcement is adequate or exclusive.

Suter preserves the separate action-creation inquiry. Franklin's appropriate-relief principle applies after a cognizable action exists; it cannot create its own antecedent. Central Bank and Musick, Peeler concern incidents of the received securities action and do not authorize importing it into the Cable Act. The supplied question concerns damages. The Court therefore decides neither every prospective remedy nor the legality of Cablevision's actual exclusion.

**Application, remedy and limits.** The Second Circuit's judgment is reversed only insofar as it permits the asserted implied federal damages action, and the case is remanded. The complaint also requested injunctions and asserted Connecticut law. Any surviving relief requires an actual authorized source and the ordinary preservation, jurisdictional and remedial requirements; no equitable action is created by the request itself. Viewer-claim status remains unresolved, no unpleaded section 1983 claim is created, and no damages are awarded.

Section 531(e) remains subject to section 544(d). Its separate permission to refuse public-access programs or portions containing obscenity, indecency or nudity is permission, not an obligation, and does not automatically extend that public-access wording to every educational or governmental use. The constitutionality and application of that permission, channel forum status, operator state action and whether the actual blanket exclusion was lawful remain undecided.



SOURCE 28: buckley_v_american_constitutional_law_foundation_merits_1999-01-12.md
## Holdings

**Common authority.** Stevens's Court opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg throughout. Those six directly support Parts I, III-A and III-B. O'Connor and Breyer additionally join Part II, giving that component eight full rationale votes. Thomas concurs in every invalidity judgment on the distinct grounds described below; his judgment votes are not full joins in the Court's methodology. The rule's limits accompany each component.

### I. Registered-voter condition

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado may not require otherwise qualified resident-adult initiative circulators to be registered voters on this record. Excluding otherwise qualified speakers because they are unregistered substantially burdens protected petition circulation, while the retained identification and enforcement mechanisms serve traceability more closely.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a person need not register to vote to engage in the protected political conversation involved in circulating a petition. Meyer v. Grant protects that interaction and the available pool of speakers. Colorado's condition excludes at least 400,000 otherwise qualified but unregistered voters. Easy enrollment does not eliminate that reduction or show that registration adequately measures residence, availability for process or honesty.

The State's strongest argument treats registration as an administrable qualification in a legally operative election process. Anderson and the governing Burdick rule permit appropriately fitted administration, but require attention to the actual burden and interest. Here the sworn identifying and residential-address affidavit, signature review, subpoenas and targeted fraud sanctions address traceability without the registration exclusion. Timmons's different candidate-nomination setting does not make this speaker restriction minor. The Court invalidates the registration condition, not the age floor, circulation period or residence rule whose validity is not presented. An affidavit cannot preserve the invalid condition merely by requiring a registration recital.

### II. Personal name displayed during solicitation

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado may not compel every circulator to display a personal name on a badge during protected face-to-face petition advocacy when the retained post-circulation identification and enforcement measures serve the asserted interests with materially less speech burden. The personal-name component is invalid; standalone paid-or-volunteer and employer-information requirements are not adjudicated.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that compelled personal attribution at the expressive encounter requires a justification fitted to that burden. McIntyre protects individual political advocacy against inadequately justified identification demands; Meyer protects petition circulation, and Riley prevents payment from stripping a solicitor of speech protection. The trial record shows deterrence from the personal badge, particularly when the subject is unpopular. That is a burden on the actual political conversation.

Even assuming fraud prevention and detection are compelling interests, Colorado has not shown the necessary fit. The identifying affidavit, signature assurances, review, protests and fraud penalties allow officials to identify and investigate circulators without exposing their names to everyone approached. The later public affidavit differs in timing and accessibility from a worn badge; the Court creates no anonymity from lawful investigation. A witness's interest in reporting misconduct does not establish the needed additional benefit of this blanket personal-name display. This holding does not decide standalone paid-status, sponsor or employer attribution, nor invalidate lawful post-circulation verification.

### III-A. Final individual-circulator reports

**Holding and operative rule.** The challenged final-report requirements publishing each paid circulator's identity, address and individual compensation fail the required substantial relationship to Colorado's asserted informational and enforcement interests on this record. Disclosure of initiative proponents and aggregate spending remains distinct; the decision creates no categorical exemption from initiative-finance disclosure.

**Controlling explanation.** Buckley v. Valeo permits campaign-finance disclosure substantially related to important governmental interests. That inquiry requires attention to what the information reveals. Proponent identity and aggregate expenditures reveal who finances an initiative and the extent of paid efforts. The individual messenger is not, for that reason, a financing source or a candidate contributor. Colorado has not demonstrated the added benefit necessary to justify publishing every paid circulator's identity and compensation, without a threshold separating consequential financing from any paid messenger.

The State argues that payee information permits tracing and checking spending beyond aggregate totals. That is a legitimate subject of inquiry, but the retained sponsor, aggregate, amount-per-signature and verification mechanisms make the asserted incremental need insufficient here. Final reporting is less immediate than a badge; it still requires its own disclosure justification. Kentucky Right to Life's payer-identification rule concerns a different financing function and does not answer this demand for every messenger's information. A narrower individual-reporting rule supported under the governing scrutiny remains open.

### III-B. Monthly individual-circulator reports

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado's challenged monthly requirements publishing each paid circulator's identity, residential and business addresses and individual amounts paid or owed are likewise invalid on this record. The Court decides that individual-information package, not the independent validity of every other monthly-report field.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court separately considers publication during the circulation campaign. Timely information can expose abuse and inform voters before qualification; that argument gives monthly reporting work that a final report cannot always perform. But timing does not cure the insufficient relationship between identifying each messenger and the State's demonstrated financing or enforcement need. It also exposes the individual while the political effort is underway. The retained sponsor and aggregate disclosures and enforcement mechanisms remain material alternatives. The decision does not hold that referendum disclosure has no informational value or that every individual challenge requires proof of reprisals.

The Tenth Circuit invalidated the monthly subsection as a whole. The Court affirms the reviewed invalidity of individual circulator disclosures and leaves the independent validity of its other prescriptions, including proponents and measure identity, undecided. It neither orders those fields conclusively reinstated nor independently declares them unconstitutional. Any ordinary remedial or severability implementation must respect the existing judgment and the scope actually decided.

**Common limits and remedy.** The reviewed registration, personal-name and individual-disclosure invalidations stand. Age, circulation timing, verification affidavits, fraud and signature-review mechanisms are outside the reviewed invalidations and remain governed by their own terms. This decision does not invalidate standalone paid-status or sponsor attribution, create a replacement initiative code, grant damages, destroy public records, authorize fraud or decide every future disclosure rule. Unreviewed requirements receive no automatic new constitutional endorsement.



SOURCE 29: el_al_israel_airlines_v_tseng_merits_1999-01-12.md
## Holdings
#### Part I — Local law cannot avoid the Convention's conditions within its covered field

**Operative rule.** When a passenger's personal-injury occurrence falls within Article 17's temporal and substantive field, Article 24 subjects an action against the carrier, whatever its legal foundation, to the Convention's conditions and limits. Failure to satisfy the accident or bodily-injury condition does not itself remove the occurrence from that field or permit an independent local damages action; occurrences outside that field remain outside this holding.

**Authority.** Part I of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court has the eight direct joins identified in Opinion Topology and controls this rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation.** Article 24 makes the Convention's conditions apply to actions founded on local law as well as the treaty. Reading a failed condition as permission to escape that condition would deprive it of its limiting force. The reference to Article 17 confines the rule to the personal-injury field of covered international carriage; it does not make liability under the Convention a prerequisite to the operation of its restrictions.

Saks distinguishes the injury from the external event causing it. Floyd enforces Article 17's bodily-injury condition while leaving this exclusivity question open. Zicherman permits domestic law to identify compensable losses and beneficiaries subject to treaty conditions; it does not permit domestic law to create liability when those conditions fail. The absence of a uniform treaty damages code therefore does not establish a separate local source of recovery. Uniformity supports this reading but does not enlarge the treaty's field.

Tseng's search occurred in the operations of embarking for her international flight, not merely somewhere in an airport. The accepted absence of qualifying bodily injury defeats the reviewed personal-injury claim. The Court need not decide whether the search was an accident. It does not categorically exclude every physical manifestation of emotional harm, decide events outside embarking, carriage or disembarking, or alter the baggage award. Part I leaves Article 25's operation open.

#### Part II — The Article 25 argument does not change the result on this record

**Operative rule.** The asserted Article 25 route cannot alter the disposition where the trial's finding of no treaty willful misconduct remains undisturbed. Intentional touching alone does not establish that misconduct; this application decides neither contested preservation nor Article 25's full effect when its misconduct predicate is proved.

**Authority.** Part II has the seven direct Associate joins identified in Opinion Topology. Stone-Zsela does not join it.

**Controlling explanation.** Assuming that Tseng may present her Article 25 argument despite the disputed preservation objection, its factual predicate is absent. The trial court found no treaty willful misconduct, and this record supplies no basis for disturbing that finding. The deliberate character of a search does not itself prove the greater culpability required by the asserted treaty provision. The Court therefore need not determine how Article 25 affects exclusions or limitations when willfulness is established, and does not restrict that provision to monetary ceilings. It also does not infer that intentional conduct can never be an Article 17 accident.



SOURCE 30: city_of_west_covina_v_perkins_merits_1999-01-13.md
## Holdings
### 1. Notice of the seizure does not require an individualized guide to published return procedures

**Holding and operative rule:** Due process requires timely notice reasonably calculated to identify the seized property and its responsible custodian so that an owner may seek its return. When the owner receives that notice, due process does not additionally require personalized instructions describing adequate state return procedures reasonably ascertainable from generally available statutes and judicial decisions; publication alone does not establish meaningful access to an inadequate, concealed, misleading, obstructed or practically inaccessible remedy.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion of the Court is joined in full by the other members of the seven-Justice coalition of Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. They adopt both holdings and the procedural-due-process explanation. Scalia and Thomas agree with the judgment and the negative rule rejecting the additional legal-instruction duty, but withhold assent to deriving notice duties for criminal warrant execution from civil procedural-due-process decisions. The seven complete joins independently establish controlling authority; no fractured-decision synthesis is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that notice must inform the owner what the government has done and where to seek the property. Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co. supplies that practical notice principle. It does not require an official to provide a personal explanation of the law an owner may use. Memphis Light, Gas & Water Division v. Craft involved an operative utility-dispute procedure that a customer could not discover simply by consulting generally available law. California's judicial return procedures present a different question. Mathews v. Eldridge requires attention to the actual interests and procedures; the modest expense of adding instructions to a receipt does not alone make those instructions constitutionally necessary. United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property preserves an independent due-process inquiry despite a warrant, but concerned process before civil seizure of real property. It neither settles notice after criminal seizure of movable evidence nor requires a guide to judicial remedies. The receipt here identified the property, agency, issuing judge and court, relevant dates and officer contacts. The Court rejects the additional legal-guide requirement without independently adjudicating every aspect of California's procedures or their operation. Withholding information actually necessary to invoke a remedy, or misleading or obstructing the owner, would present a different properly preserved claim.

**Limits:** The holding does not validate an unlawful initial seizure, unreasonable retention, a failure to disclose the seizure or custodian, or an inaccessible return route. It adopts no comprehensive notice checklist, universal deadline or rule that a warrant satisfies every later process obligation. California Penal Code §1536's custody subject to court order, the nonstatutory return motion recognized in People v. Icenogle, and §1540's separate restoration conditions remain distinct; this case does not merge them or decide that every owner satisfies any one route.

### 2. The additional-notice judgment must be reversed without deciding an unproved obstruction claim

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's requirement of individualized instructions about published return procedures, and its direction to enter judgment for Perkins on that ground, must be reversed. The omitted warrant number does not sustain that judgment because the District Court found that respondents had not established the number was necessary to bring a return motion; the reported difficulties do not establish a different obstruction ground.

**Authority:** The seven-Justice opinion of the Court supplies the controlling explanation. Scalia and Thomas also support this application and its reservations through their concurrence in the judgment; all nine support reversal and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Ninth Circuit held the available California procedures constitutionally adequate but demanded additional instruction about using them. This Court rejects that legal demand; it does not independently endorse every procedure or its practical administration. Perkins reported difficulty finding the warrant, the issuing judge's absence, a mistaken number and a sealed warrant. Some officer statements were disputed. A public index connected the searched address to the warrant number, and the District Court's unrefuted finding was that necessity of that number had not been established. These circumstances do not permit the Court to replace the lower finding with a determination that officials withheld an indispensable identifier. Nor does rejection of the instruction theory extinguish a distinct, properly preserved challenge to actual obstruction or accessibility. Such a claim must rest on its own record and procedural basis. Return of most property in June 1994 does not itself dispose of a supported claim concerning an earlier deprivation. The judgment on individual qualified immunity is not reopened, and the Court adjudicates neither entitlement to the reportedly stolen starter pistol nor damages.

**Remand limit:** The lower courts must reject the federal individualized-instruction theory. Any preserved adequacy, accessibility, obstruction or operation issue remains subject to ordinary procedural and substantive requirements; no new claim, fresh hearing or exploratory discovery is ordered.
