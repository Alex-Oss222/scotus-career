### Central State University v. American Association of University Professors, Central State University Chapter, No. 98-1071

Summary merits decision, March 22, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Following the separate certiorari grant of the same date, the Court decides the case without oral argument.

The Ohio Supreme Court invalidated Ohio's exclusion of public-university faculty workload standards from collective bargaining on the reviewed federal equal-protection ground. The question is whether that occupational distinction has a rational basis. The university seeks reversal; independent Ohio constitutional protections remain outside the federal holding.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded on the federal equal-protection question, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens, who opposes summary resolution and takes no final position on the federal merits.

The Ohio courts may address surviving independent state-law questions. The Court does not adjudicate existing agreements, other statutory bargaining rights or retaliation for protected association.

#### Opinion Topology

The per curiam opinion is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stevens dissents from summary disposition.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Faculty workload administration supplies a rational basis for this distinction

**Holding and operative rule.** Ohio may exclude public-university faculty workload standards from collective bargaining to promote classroom teaching without proving that bargaining caused each earlier reduction in teaching. An ordinary occupational classification must rationally serve a legitimate purpose; a challenger cannot establish irrationality merely by identifying a lack of empirical proof or a different possible means.

**Authority.** The eight Justices joining the per curiam adopt this rule, its application and the stated limits in full.

**Controlling explanation.** Beach supplies ordinary rational-basis review without an empirical-certainty requirement. Nordlinger permits reasonable generalization, while Heller's distinct holdings require a rational connection to the actual disadvantage and preserve legislative choice among rational arrangements. Faculty classroom workloads are the subject Ohio seeks to administer. Removing those workload standards from bargaining can enable universities to increase or standardize classroom teaching, whether or not bargaining caused every past decline. The fact that other public employees retain bargaining rights does not defeat that subject-specific connection. Equality Foundation requires examination of the law's actual reach; it does not equate this defined management measure with a sweeping exclusion from ordinary civic protection. The presented record supplies no forbidden-purpose or independently protected-right ground that defeats the rational connection. The Court decides neither independent Ohio constitutional limits nor other bargaining and association rights.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Beach is applied: ordinary economic and occupational classifications do not require empirical proof of the legislature's proposed causal account.
- Nordlinger is applied: reasonable generalization may sustain a classification despite imperfect fit.
- Heller's separate holdings are preserved: review examines the actual disadvantage without converting legislative choice into a necessity or empirical-certainty test.
- Equality Foundation is distinguished: the workload exclusion has a defined management relationship and does not enact the sweeping civic disability addressed there.

#### Law After Decision

Existing rational-basis doctrine governs this faculty-workload classification and sustains it against the presented federal challenge. No new scrutiny tier, general immunity for anti-union action or rule overriding independent state protections is adopted.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens dissents from the Court's summary treatment. He would deny review or, after the grant, permit plenary consideration of the occupational distinction and the state court's rationale. His Beach and Nordlinger positions require attention to the actual comparison between faculty and other workers; a general management objective should not obscure that inquiry. He acknowledges the possible force of the teaching-management connection and does not decide that Ohio's exclusion ultimately fails federal equal protection. His objection does not adopt an empirical-proof requirement or establish a separate academic-freedom holding.

#### Source Notes

The reviewed decision is reported at 83 Ohio St. 3d 229, 699 N.E.2d 463. The public case record appears in the [official United States Reports](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/526bv.pdf), at 526 U.S. 124–134. The Court's ruling is confined to the federal equality ground and does not determine other bargaining protections or existing agreements.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review of the federal question is complete. The cause returns to the Ohio courts for proceedings consistent with that ruling, including any properly surviving independent state-law question. No particular state-law outcome, new hearing or deadline is directed.

**End of entry: Central State University v. American Association of University Professors, Central State University Chapter, No. 98-1071, summary merits decision, March 22, 1999.**

### Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael, No. 97-1709

Merits decision, March 23, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 7, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, 131 F.3d 1433. The District Court excluded the tire-failure expert's testimony and granted defense summary judgment, maintaining both after reconsideration. The Court of Appeals reversed because it treated experience-based testimony as outside Daubert's reach. The questions are whether Rule 702's reliability obligation extends to technical and other specialized knowledge, how flexibly its reliability considerations apply, and what follows for this exclusion and judgment.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Formal disposition of the Eleventh Circuit judgment | Reversed and remanded, 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Stevens, who would vacate and remand | The categorical exemption for experience-based testimony is rejected; the actual remand instruction is separately stated below |
| Permissibility of rejecting the unsupported defect inference | Eight Justices support permissible exclusion; one reserves application | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Stevens reserves application for the Court of Appeals | The District Court was entitled to reject the unsupported analytical bridge; this proposition does not resolve the distinct remand instruction |
| Operative remand | Conforming disposition of defense summary judgment required, 7–2 | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Stone-Zsela and Stevens | On remand, the Eleventh Circuit must conform its disposition to the permissible exclusion and resulting defense summary judgment on this record |

All nine Justices adopt the common Rule 702 and appellate-review rules. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela would require the Eleventh Circuit to review the reliability ruling for abuse of discretion under the complete Rule 702 framework and reinstate summary judgment if no admissible evidence supports defect and causation. Justice Stevens would vacate for that court's first application of the correct framework. Those proposed remedies do not control the mandate.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Unanimous rejection of the categorical experience exemption and adoption of the governing reliability and review rules | Common Rule 702 duty; adapted inquiry; abuse-of-discretion review |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III-A | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Eight-Justice support for the District Court's entitlement to reject the unsupported defect inference | Permissible exclusion and the common unsupported-inference ground; no adoption of the distinct mandate |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III-B | Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Directs the conforming disposition of summary judgment | Fuller record application supporting direct disposition and the operative remand |
| Concurrence in Parts I–II and III-A and in reversal; dissent from Part III-B | Stone-Zsela | Author alone | Would reverse and require appellate abuse-of-discretion review, with conditional reinstatement of summary judgment | Joins Parts I–II and III-A's permissible-exclusion and unsupported-inference ground; does not join Part III-B's direct-disposition analysis or conforming mandate |
| Concurrence in Parts I–II and dissent from Part III and the judgment | Stevens | Author alone | Would vacate and remand for appellate application of the correct framework | Joins the common rule and review allocation; does not join direct resolution of actual exclusion and summary judgment |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Rule 702 requires reliable specialized knowledge, whatever the field

**Holding and operative rule:** Original Rule 702 requires the trial judge to assess the reliability and fit of scientific, technical and other specialized expert testimony. Experience may supply relevant knowledge, but the expert's professional label does not excuse a reliable connection between the method used and the proposition offered.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Breyer's Opinion of the Court. All nine directly adopt this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Rule addresses knowledge that will assist the factfinder and expressly includes technical and other specialized knowledge. Its reliability requirement cannot turn on whether the witness describes the same practical inference as scientific or experiential. Daubert supplies the preliminary duty to examine foundation and fit; its scientific setting does not establish a categorical exemption for technical testimony. Joiner supplies the requirement that the offered conclusion have an adequately supported inferential connection. Experience can provide that connection, but credentials alone cannot replace it. The Eleventh Circuit therefore erred in excluding experience-based tire analysis from the reliability inquiry. Cross-examination remains available for admissible evidence; it does not eliminate the threshold foundation required by the Rule. This holding does not decide ultimate defect or causation.

##### II. The reliability inquiry must fit the proffer and receives abuse-of-discretion review

**Holding and operative rule:** A trial judge may use Daubert's nonexclusive reliability considerations when they help evaluate the particular expertise, method and application, but need not demand every consideration in every field. Appellate abuse-of-discretion review governs both the reasonable choice of how to assess reliability and the ultimate admission or exclusion, while categorical legal error remains independently correctable.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Breyer's Opinion of the Court, adopted by the same nine Justices identified for Part I.

**Controlling explanation:** Daubert's considerations include testability and testing, peer review and publication, meaningful error information and operational standards, and acceptance. Their usefulness depends on the question and the proposed expertise. A technical practice may yield reliable knowledge without laboratory testing or publication; conversely, the assertion that a method rests on experience does not establish its reliability. The judge must select considerations that examine the method's actual work and explain its connection to the conclusion. Flexibility permits a suitable inquiry, not abandonment of screening. Joiner's allocation of appellate review preserves correction of legal error and deference to permissible evidentiary judgments; an exclusion's effect on summary judgment does not change that allocation. The inquiry concerns preliminary foundation, not proof of the ultimate tort claim. The Court requires no invariant checklist, identical independent study, expert appointment or new hearing, and permits no exclusion merely because the judge disagrees with a well-supported conclusion.

##### III-A. The District Court was entitled to reject the unsupported defect inference

**Holding and operative rule:** Rule 702 permitted the District Court to reject Carlson's defect inference because he did not reliably explain how his inspection distinguished defect from abuse or why the observations supported the asserted cause. This permissible-exclusion proposition does not itself settle the distinct instruction governing appellate review and summary judgment on remand.

**Authority:** Part III-A of Justice Breyer's Opinion of the Court, supported by the eight-Justice coalition identified above. Stone-Zsela joins this bounded application and its unsupported-inference ground; Justice Stevens reserves application for the Court of Appeals. Part III-B's fuller direct-disposition discussion and mandatory conforming remand have a separate coalition.

**Controlling explanation:** Carlson's experience was relevant, but experience alone did not establish the connection between his observations and a defect rather than abuse. Assuming that separation caused the blowout did not supply that further inference. Joiner permits the judge to examine the analytical connection without deciding ultimate causation. On this proffer, the District Court was entitled to reject an unsupported bridge between inspection and conclusion. The Court holds rejection permissible, not compulsory for every reasonable judge, and does not discredit visual tire inspection as a category or authorize exclusion merely because a judge disagrees with a well-supported conclusion.

##### III-B. The remand must conform to the exclusion and resulting summary judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** On the reconsidered proffer, the case is remanded for a disposition sustaining the exclusion and resulting defense summary judgment, because the record supplies no remaining evidence of defect. The mandate does not require a new hearing, appointed expert or another appellate determination whether to sustain this exclusion.

**Authority:** Part III-B of Justice Breyer's Opinion of the Court, supported by the seven-Justice coalition identified above. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justice Stevens oppose this conforming mandate and retain their distinct appellate-review remedies.

**Controlling explanation:** Carlson proposed to exclude overdeflection unless at least two of four physical signs were significant, then infer a manufacturing or design defect. He acknowledged shoulder wear, a bead groove, discoloration, rim-flange marks and inadequately repaired punctures, and explained why he regarded the symptoms as insufficient. Those explanations did not require acceptance of his significance threshold or the resulting elimination of misuse. After reconsideration, the District Court recognized Daubert's flexibility and possible accepted uses of visual inspection; its remaining objection concerned the analysis connecting this inspection to defect. Thus the initial concern about an inflexible checklist does not describe the ruling under review. Joiner's deferential allocation permits the Court to resolve the supported exclusion now; the Court need not leave that application for another appellate determination. Because no other defect evidence remains on this record, the exclusion carries the stated summary-judgment consequence. This direct disposition does not establish the accident's actual cause.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Original Rule 702 governs. The Court adopts no later Rule amendment, universal technical checklist or substantive finding that misuse rather than defect caused the accident. The ruling establishes neither automatic admissibility of experiential evidence nor categorical unreliability of visual inspection.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Daubert supplies flexible examination of foundation and fit. Its duty extends to scientific, technical and other specialized knowledge under original Rule 702, while its nonexclusive considerations remain adaptable, not a mandatory checklist. The reconsidered ruling follows that flexibility; visual inspection and other experience-based methods are not categorically excluded.
- Joiner supplies uniform abuse-of-discretion review for a reasonable screening method and the ultimate evidentiary ruling, while preserving correction of legal error. Its permission to reject an unsupported inferential connection applies to this technical proffer and, under Part III-B, to the reconsidered ruling and resulting summary judgment. It does not make admissibility a decision on ultimate causation.

No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

Scientific, technical and other specialized testimony receives reliability-and-fit screening suited to the actual proffer. The judge's reasonable screening method and evidentiary ruling receive abuse-of-discretion review, subject to correction of legal error. This particular exclusion and the resulting summary judgment are sustained; experience-based tire analysis as a category remains available when adequately supported.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins Parts I–II and Part III-A's permissible-exclusion proposition and unsupported-inference ground, and concurs in reversal, but dissents from Part III-B's operative remand. Rule 702 addresses the knowledge offered rather than the expert's professional label. Carlson's experience was relevant, but he did not reliably explain how his inspection distinguished defect from abuse or why the observations supported the asserted cause; the District Court was entitled to reject that unsupported analytical bridge. Stone-Zsela nevertheless would direct the Eleventh Circuit to review the reliability ruling for abuse of discretion under the complete Rule 702 framework and reinstate summary judgment if no admissible evidence supports defect and causation. His position preserves flexible reliability screening without mandatory peer review or testing in every technical field, displacing the jury on reliable disputed evidence, or excluding a well-supported conclusion merely because the judge disagrees with it.

Justice Stevens joins Parts I–II but dissents from Part III and the judgment. The common Rule 702 obligation defeats the Eleventh Circuit's categorical exemption, but that court should first apply the correct framework to the complete technical proffer. As in his Joiner position, agreement on appellate review and inferential fit does not settle the actual exercise of discretion. He would vacate and remand for appellate application, without ordering admission, reopening every foundation submission or requiring a new hearing.

#### Source Notes

The objective chronology, proffer and lower-court rulings are reported in [526 U.S. 137](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/526bv.pdf#page=248), including the descriptions of Carlson's deposition and the District Court's reconsideration. The assumed separation and blowout premises do not establish defect causation. The present decision concerns original Rule 702, Daubert and Joiner; it does not apply later amendments to the Rule.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Eleventh Circuit must conform its disposition to the sustained exclusion and resulting defense summary judgment on the record presented. The Court requires no new foundation hearing or expert appointment and makes no finding of the accident's actual cause.

**End of entry: Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael, No. 97-1709, merits decision, March 23, 1999.**

### South Central Bell Telephone Co. v. Alabama, No. 97-2045

Merits decision, March 23, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 19, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Alabama. The judgment under review left franchise-tax assessments in place through its preclusion treatment and disposition of the federal challenge. The questions concern this Court's review of the state judgment, authority to bind taxpayers through earlier litigation, discrimination against foreign corporations, the asserted compensatory tax and the necessary relief.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The same coalition supports each component. Alabama's courts must provide constitutionally adequate relief, choosing among lawful refunds, corrective assessments or other permissible state mechanisms under the conditions stated below. The Court selects no remedial formula, exact refund or assessment against a particular domestic corporation.

The Eleventh Amendment objection is rejected, the preclusion and Commerce Clause judgments are reversed, and the case is remanded for constitutionally adequate state relief.

#### Opinion Topology

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela delivers the Opinion of the Court, Parts I through V, joined in full by Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine join the holdings on review of the state judgment, nonparty preclusion, the discriminatory tax structure, both independent defects in the compensatory defense, and the constitutionally adequate state remedy.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The Eleventh Amendment does not bar review of Alabama's own judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** The Eleventh Amendment does not prevent this Court from reviewing under 28 U.S.C. § 1257 the federal questions decided by Alabama's own courts. Such review is distinct from initiating a new federal damages action against the State.

**Authority:** Part I of the unanimous Opinion of the Court, joined in full as stated above.

**Controlling explanation:** Alabama's immunity objection concerns the character of this proceeding. The taxpayers seek review of the federal judgment entered in litigation in Alabama's courts. McKesson recognizes both this Court's authority to review such federal questions and the State's obligation to administer constitutionally adequate relief when required. Treating appellate review as a new action would insulate the state court's decision of federal law from the review Congress authorized. The Court rejects that objection without deciding an unpresented immunity question concerning a separate federal suit or awarding damages against the State.

##### II. Shared taxpayer interests do not authorize binding these nonparties

**Holding and operative rule:** Claim or issue preclusion cannot bind these taxpayers to earlier litigation merely because they share the earlier taxpayers' interests or were aware of the public dispute. Binding requires constitutionally sufficient consent, control, genuine privity, adequate class or other legally accountable representation, or another recognized relationship consistent with due process; the asserted common-interest rationale supplies none of those prerequisites.

**Authority:** Part II of the unanimous Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Richards requires lawful authority to bind the person before the State may extinguish that person's own claim by another litigant's judgment. The earlier litigation concerned different taxpayers; shared objectives, vigorous advocacy and the public importance of a tax question do not establish representative authority over these taxpayers' personal liabilities. Awareness of another action creates no general obligation to intervene. Describing the proposed bar as issue preclusion rather than claim preclusion does not cure the missing relationship. The Court rejects the relationship asserted below without inventing a factual finding that every conceivable ground of control or representation has been disproved. Ordinary party preclusion, actual control, adequate class representation, fiduciary representation and authorized public representation retain their lawful operation. A prior decision may also supply precedent without conclusively binding a stranger to its judgment. The tax merits therefore require independent consideration.

##### III. Alabama's franchise-tax bases discriminate against foreign corporations

**Holding and operative rule:** Alabama's franchise-tax structure discriminates against interstate commerce by taxing foreign corporations on actual capital employed in the State while allowing comparable domestic corporations to use a par-value base that may be set well below actual value. The favorable domestic base cannot be justified by comparing nominal rates or tax labels while disregarding the actual competitive burden.

**Authority:** Part III of the unanimous Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Oregon Waste requires scrutiny of an express geographic tax preference, and West Lynn Creamery requires examination of linked economic operation. Fulton applies those principles to corporate tax classifications. Here, the ability of domestic corporations to employ the favorable par-value base places a materially heavier franchise-tax burden on comparable foreign corporations. Calling both charges franchise taxes does not equalize their bases. Nor does the State's collection of revenue from domestic corporations in other forms establish that the challenged distinction is evenhanded. Tracy's different regulated-service comparison supplies no exemption for this domestic/foreign classification on the comparable-business facts presented. The Court decides this discriminatory structure, not the validity of every regulation with differing economic effects or every tax imposed on corporations with some common product.

##### IV. The domestic shares tax does not establish a compensatory defense

**Holding and operative rule:** A compensatory-tax defense requires an identified actual intrastate burden, an interstate charge that roughly approximates and does not exceed that burden, and substantially equivalent taxable events capable of functioning as mutually exclusive proxies. Alabama's domestic shares tax reaches a different event and does not establish the equivalence needed to compensate for the discriminatory foreign-corporation franchise tax.

**Authority:** Part IV of the unanimous Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Oregon Waste and Fulton make these requirements cumulative. A domestic tax does not compensate for a foreign-business disadvantage simply because the two taxes relate economically to corporate activity or raise revenue for the same government. The shares tax concerns a different taxable event from the challenged franchise burden, and Alabama has not established substantially equivalent events serving the claimed compensatory purpose. That failure independently defeats the defense. The Court does not hold that different nominal taxpayers always prevent compensation or prohibit genuinely complementary charges. It requires the substantive equivalence that the asserted defense has not demonstrated here.

**Independently sufficient alternative holding:** Alabama also fails to establish an actual matching domestic burden and a roughly equivalent, nonexcessive interstate burden by invoking other domestic taxes or an aggregate fiscal balance. This separate deficiency defeats the compensatory defense even apart from the absence of equivalent taxable events.

**Authority for the alternative holding:** The same unanimous Part IV coalition.

**Controlling explanation for the alternative holding:** Fulton distinguishes a demonstrated matching burden from a general description of corporate taxation. The present justification does not establish that the domestic burden actually corresponds to the disadvantage imposed on comparable foreign corporations. An assertion that the overall system balances revenue cannot replace that showing. The Court supplies no numerical ratio, new formula or finding that every conceivable tax comparison necessarily fails; it holds the asserted comparison insufficient on this record.

##### V. Alabama must provide adequate relief through lawful state remedial choices

**Holding and operative rule:** The judgment is reversed and the case remanded for constitutionally adequate relief under state law. When payment was required before an adequate opportunity to contest the tax, McKesson requires meaningful backward-looking correction; independently, a State may not withdraw a clearly held-out postpayment remedy after payment and substitute an unpursued prepayment route.

**Authority:** Part V of the unanimous Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** McKesson permits a lawful refund, constitutionally permissible retrospective equalization, or a permissible combination when backward-looking relief is required. Reich separately protects the postpayment remedy the State clearly held out, including after voluntary payment; ordinary proof, reasonable previously announced conditions and prospective changes with fair notice remain possible. Fulton preserves those rules while leaving actual payment, preservation, state procedures and severability for lawful state administration. Alabama may choose a remedy within those constraints. Prospective correction may govern future taxation, but cannot by itself replace backward-looking relief when the stated conditions require that relief. A genuinely adequate prepayment system presents a different remedial setting. The Court does not select a replacement tax base, order assessments against particular domestic corporations, fix a refund amount or declare every taxpayer entitled to recovery. Those matters depend on the actual claims and lawful state choices, which must cure rather than preserve the unconstitutional disadvantage.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No exact refund, replacement assessment, severability ruling or particular taxpayer's satisfaction of payment and preservation conditions is decided. Ordinary valid preclusion and genuinely equivalent compensatory taxes remain possible under their own prerequisites. The Court does not reopen otherwise binding judgments or decide a new federal damages action.

#### Precedent Treatment

- McKesson supplies the distinction between reviewing federal questions in a State's own judgment and initiating a new federal action. It also requires meaningful backward-looking correction when payment was required before an adequate opportunity to contest the tax.
- Richards supplies the due-process prerequisite for binding nonparties through claim or issue preclusion. It defeats the asserted common-interest bar while preserving lawful representative adjudication, ordinary party preclusion and the use of prior decisions as precedent.
- Oregon Waste supplies scrutiny of origin-based tax discrimination; West Lynn Creamery requires examination of linked economic operation; Fulton applies the practical comparison to corporate tax preferences. Those rules govern Alabama's unequal tax bases.
- Oregon Waste and Fulton require all three compensatory predicates: an actual intrastate burden, a roughly equivalent and nonexcessive interstate burden, and substantially equivalent taxable events. The event-equivalence requirement and Fulton's independently sufficient actual-burden and approximate-equivalence ground defeat this defense. Genuinely complementary taxes remain possible; fiscal correlation alone is insufficient, and no universal calculation is established.
- Tracy's actual comparison of distinct regulated services is distinguished, not displaced; it does not control the domestic/foreign classification presented here.
- Reich independently prohibits withdrawal of a clearly held-out postpayment remedy after payment, including voluntary payment. It preserves ordinary proof, reasonable previously announced conditions and prospective changes with fair notice.
- Fulton also preserves the distinction between invalidating a discriminatory tax and selecting its precise lawful remedy. McKesson's conditional correction duty and Reich's independent no-withdrawal rule govern the remand.

No Supreme Court precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

The discriminatory domestic/foreign franchise-tax structure cannot stand on the asserted preclusion or compensation grounds. Federal review and nonparty hearing protection remain separate from tax liability and remedy. Alabama retains lawful remedial choices, but must supply backward-looking relief when its governing conditions apply; prospective correction alone cannot discharge that duty. The Court establishes no automatic refund or replacement tax base.

#### Source Notes

The argument and delivery dates, state-court posture and described tax structure are reported in [526 U.S. 160](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/526bv.pdf#page=271). The decision does not establish each taxpayer's payment, the amount of any refund or a new factual basis for binding a nonparty; those matters remain subject to their applicable legal requirements.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to Alabama's courts for proceedings consistent with the invalidity of the discriminatory structure and a constitutionally adequate remedy. Payment, preservation, state-law severability where relevant and lawful implementation remain for those proceedings; no particular recovery is fixed here.

**End of entry: South Central Bell Telephone Co. v. Alabama, No. 97-2045, merits decision, March 23, 1999.**

### Jones v. United States, No. 97-6203

Merits decision, March 24, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 5, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit's 1997 judgment, 116 F.3d 1487 (table). The question is whether the serious-bodily-injury and death provisions of the governing 18 U.S.C. § 2119 define aggravated offenses or sentencing factors for the judge.

The December 1992 offense is governed by the original firearm-possession enactment: fifteen years for the basic offense, twenty-five years if serious bodily injury results, and up to life if death results. The indictment did not allege serious bodily injury, and the jury's general verdict did not establish it; the judge's injury finding supplied the asserted aggravated sentencing authority. The earlier Oliver appeal supplies the record recitation, not the judgment now reviewed. Death is relevant to the statute's structure, not a finding about this offense's result.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed in the challenged aggravated-sentencing respect; sentence vacated and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Kennedy and Breyer, who would affirm against the presented classification challenge.

On the existing carjacking indictment and verdict, punishment may not exceed the fifteen-year basic maximum. This is not an order to impose fifteen years, an aggregate-sentence calculation, an acquittal or automatic authorization of a new aggravated prosecution. Any further prosecution must satisfy its own procedural and constitutional requirements. The Court does not reinstate an earlier sentence or decide unrelated sentencing errors.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Exact scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Dissent | Kennedy | O'Connor, Breyer | Would affirm against the presented classification challenge | Complete-offense and penalty-clause construction; independent constitutional defense of this injury allocation |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Serious bodily injury and death define aggravated carjacking offenses

**Holding and operative rule:** The version of 18 U.S.C. § 2119 governing the December 1992 offense creates a basic carjacking offense and aggravated offenses when serious bodily injury or death results. Serious bodily injury is an element of the grade carrying a twenty-five-year maximum, and death is an element of the grade carrying a maximum of life imprisonment; the basic offense carries a fifteen-year maximum.

**Authority:** Part I of the six-Justice Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court reads the additional injury and death conditions as facts defining aggravated crimes. The numbered clauses prescribe sharply different limits on lawful punishment and use harm to the victim, a traditional distinction among robbery offenses, to select the applicable grade. The Government emphasizes the complete opening description of firearm carjacking, the common punishment command, dependent clauses and penalty headings. Those features support its reading, but they do not establish that the additional facts merely guide sentencing: aggravated offenses may share the basic offense's elements and add the fact that permits greater punishment. Almendarez-Torres supplies a statutory analogue in which placement within a punishment provision did not dispense with an aggravated element. The fairly available graded-offense reading also avoids serious constitutional doubt about assigning to a judge a current-offense fact indispensable to punishment beyond the charged verdict's authority. Gaudin protects the jury's determination of actual elements; neither it nor Almendarez-Torres already establishes a universal rule classifying every sentencing fact. Monge permits renewed proof of its particular noncapital serious-prior qualification for double-jeopardy purposes and expressly reserves independent charging and jury claims. It does not resolve this statute's construction. The Court decides the original enactment, without adding the later intent-to-cause-serious-harm language or death-penalty amendment.

##### II. The existing indictment and verdict authorize only the basic carjacking range

**Holding and operative rule:** The aggravated injury or death element must be alleged in the federal indictment; absent a valid admission as an element of the charged offense or a valid waiver of jury determination, the Government must submit it to the jury and prove it beyond a reasonable doubt. An ordinary factual admission does not itself cure an indictment that omits the aggravated element. Because injury was neither charged nor established by Jones's verdict, the judge's later injury finding cannot authorize the aggravated range; the aggravated sentence must be vacated, and sentencing on the existing carjacking indictment and verdict may not exceed the fifteen-year basic maximum.

**Authority:** Part II of the same six-Justice Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Hamling requires the federal charge to contain the offense's elements, and Gaudin places their determination beyond a reasonable doubt with the jury. Almendarez-Torres applies those safeguards to a statutory aggravator and holds that an admission to a fact cannot repair its omission from the charge. Here the indictment and general verdict establish only the basic carjacking offense. No valid admission or waiver supplies the missing aggravated charge and verdict. The judge's injury finding therefore cannot perform their work. Witte and Watts permit relevant-conduct findings to select punishment within authority supplied by the conviction; they do not supply additional statutory authority or remove an actual element from the jury. The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit's authorization of the injury-based aggravated sentence, vacates that sentence and remands for lawful proceedings. The fifteen-year ceiling concerns this carjacking count on the existing charge and verdict. It neither fixes the sentence at fifteen years nor determines an aggregate sentence. No acquittal, harmless-error finding or automatic entitlement to another aggravated prosecution is entered; any further prosecution must satisfy its own procedural and constitutional requirements. Monge's completed-insufficiency and renewed-proof question is absent.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court's ground is statutory construction and the protections of the resulting elements. It does not decide a general constitutional rule for all maximum-increasing facts, bare prior-conviction status, mandatory minima within an unchanged maximum, every Guidelines classification or capital sentencing. McMillan's unchanged-maximum holding and Walton's capital rule are not overruled. The death grade is part of the statute's construction, not a finding that Jones caused death.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Almendarez-Torres supplies the statutory aggravated-element and charging analogue, including the distinction between admitting a fact and properly charging the aggravated offense. It establishes no universal constitutional rule for maximum-increasing facts.
- Hamling requires a federal indictment stating the offense's elements. Gaudin requires the jury's determination of actual elements beyond a reasonable doubt; it does not itself classify every sentencing fact.
- Monge retains its particular noncapital serious-prior double-jeopardy classification and its express reservation of independent charging and jury claims. No renewed-proof or other independent remedy is drawn from it here.
- Witte and Watts retain their within-authority sentencing permission. They do not authorize punishment beyond the charged conviction's statutory range or remove an actual element from the jury.
- Holloway's attention to enacted offense conditions is consistent with this reading, but its construction of later statutory language does not amend the law governing this offense.
- McMillan's unchanged-maximum holding and Walton's capital rule are not overruled. Custis's limits on collateral attack remain undisturbed.

#### Law After Decision

The original section 2119 creates aggravated injury and death offenses whose added elements receive ordinary federal charging and jury-proof protections. The existing basic conviction cannot authorize the injury range. The Court adopts no general constitutional maximum-fact rule, prior-conviction exception, new mandatory-minimum rule or capital-sentencing rule. The distinct constitutional positions in the concurrences retain their stated, noncontrolling scope.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Souter, joined by Stevens, Scalia and Ginsburg.** These four join the Court's sufficient statutory ground and also conclude that a fact about the commission or consequences of the charged offense, indispensable to a greater statutory maximum than the charged elements and verdict otherwise allow, performs the constitutional function of an element. The federal charging, jury and reasonable-doubt safeguards follow, subject to valid admission and waiver rules; those rules do not repair the omitted charge by implication. Their Monge position treated an additional, previously unestablished personal-conduct qualification as an element for the finality question. They now apply that functional reasoning to injury in the present offense, with Gaudin supplying jury allocation. This is their constitutional conclusion here, not a claim that Monge already decided the jury question. Ordinary factfinding within existing conviction-authorized punishment remains distinct. The concurrence leaves bare prior-conviction status, unchanged-maximum minima, general Guidelines classification and capital sentencing unresolved. Four Justices adopt this independent constitutional ground; it is not the Court's holding.

**Stevens, joined by Scalia in Part I.** Their broader position protects facts that change the legislatively prescribed punishment range, including facts increasing the minimum, and recognizes no categorical exclusion merely because the fact is a prior conviction. Stevens relies on Winship's proof requirement, Mullaney's protection against shifting the burden on a fact determining punishment, his objections to Patterson's treatment of offense definition, and Duncan's protection of jury adjudication. Scalia agrees that the legislature cannot avoid the protection by naming a range-determining fact a sentencing consideration. His narrower Monge element-function ground supports that conclusion without having already established this universal range principle. Neither ordinary relevant-conduct sentencing under Witte and Watts nor an existing conviction's validity is the same question as whether a new fact legally authorizes a different punishment range. A charged prior-status element may be established by adequate admission or other lawful proof without relitigating the conviction's validity; Custis's collateral-review limits remain. These positions do not decide a mandatory-minimum application or overrule McMillan here. In Part II, Stevens alone also places facts necessary for death eligibility within his constitutional principle and favors reconsideration of contrary Walton reasoning in an appropriate case. No capital issue is decided, and Scalia does not join that recommendation.

**Stone-Zsela.** The Chief Justice joins the statutory construction and remedy as sufficient to decide this case. He maintains that a fact increasing authorized punishment must receive the jury protection owed an element, but does not make that broader position an additional constitutional ground of this disposition or join Souter's independent constitutional concurrence. Almendarez-Torres supplies a statutory analogue, not an already controlling universal constitutional rule. His maintained position preserves his view while the Court's decision rests on the enacted aggravated offenses. It supplies no decision of the separate questions concerning prior status, unchanged-maximum minima or capital sentencing.

**Kennedy, joined by O'Connor and Breyer.** The dissent reads the opening firearm-taking paragraph as a complete offense and the dependent injury and death clauses as graded punishment for its consequences. The sharp increases and the traditional use of injury to grade robbery support the Court's reading, but do not overcome that offense-first structure in the dissent's view. Patterson preserves a bounded legislative role in defining an offense; Gaudin protects the jury's determination once an actual element is identified. The dissent directly defends treating injury during this completed offense as a punishment condition, acknowledging that present victim injury differs from recidivist character. Monge accepted both an unproved personal-conduct qualification and a higher ceiling without treating those features as conclusive of offense status, while leaving the independent jury question open. The dissent therefore does not claim that Monge already resolved this claim or that the qualification was previously proved. Almendarez-Torres's statutory reentry holding remains binding but supplies no general constitutional rule either permitting or forbidding this allocation. McMillan's unchanged-ceiling setting and Witte's and Watts's within-authority sentencing do not themselves resolve the increased maximum. The dissent would sustain the challenged injury-based sentencing authority against this classification objection, without adopting unlimited legislative relabeling, resolving unrelated sentencing errors or directing reinstatement of an earlier sentence.

[United States v. The cause returns to the Ninth Circuit for proceedings consistent with vacatur of the aggravated sentence and the limits of the existing indictment and verdict. No fixed replacement sentence, new hearing requirement or automatic further-prosecution entitlement is ordered.

### Minnesota v. Mille Lacs Band of Chippewa Indians, No. 97-1337

Merits decision, March 24, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 2, 1998. On certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, whose judgment, 124 F.3d 904, recognized continuing treaty hunting, fishing and gathering rights subject to lawful conservation regulation.

Minnesota asks whether the 1850 Executive Order, 1855 Treaty, statehood or later events terminated the rights reserved on the ceded territory in the 1837 Treaty during the President's pleasure. The Court considers each asserted terminating act separately.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–3, preserving recognition of the surviving treaty rights and the declaratory and injunctive framework within the questions reviewed.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, each relying on the three independent extinction grounds described in the dissents.

The treaty rights remain subject to lawful, necessary nondiscriminatory conservation measures. No particular regulation, harvest allocation, private-land-access claim or right outside the ceded territory is decided.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Stevens delivers the Opinion of the Court, Parts I through IV, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. All six join the combined-order, 1855-treaty, statehood and bounded-remedy holdings. Justice Scalia dissents, joined in full by Justices Kennedy and Thomas, on three independent extinction grounds confined to their party- and treaty-specific scope. Justice Thomas also dissents separately on the treaty-specific scope of state regulation; no other Justice joins that additional objection.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The combined removal order did not terminate the reserved use rights

**Holding and operative rule.** An unauthorized compelled-removal order does not terminate reserved treaty use rights when its revocation and removal commands are inseparable parts of the same purpose and operation. The 1850 order therefore did not extinguish these rights; the Court does not decide the full extent of an independently exercised presidential power under the treaty's pleasure clause.

**Authority.** Part I of Justice Stevens's Opinion of the Court, adopted by the six Justices identified above. All six join the bounded combined-order rationale and its reservation of independent presidential revocation authority.

**Controlling explanation.** The Removal Act's consent structure did not authorize the compelled removal described by this record. That unlawful removal command could not cancel the separately reserved use rights. The order connected its revocation command to that same removal purpose and operation; treating revocation as a standalone measure would create a materially different executive action rather than preserve an independently chosen lawful termination. Minnesota's strongest argument is that the treaty separately permitted revocation during the President's pleasure. Even assuming an independently lawful revocation could occur, that possibility does not make this combined order severable or validate its unauthorized removal command. The Court decides this order's supported relationship and operation. It adopts neither a prohibition of every independent presidential revocation nor a rule that subsequent nonenforcement restores rights already lawfully extinguished.

##### II. The 1855 land cession did not surrender these independent use rights

**Holding and operative rule.** A later land cession does not extinguish separately retained treaty use rights unless the agreement, read in its setting and as the tribal parties reasonably understood it, clearly relinquishes those rights. The 1855 agreement did not surrender the Band's retained hunting, fishing and gathering privileges.

**Authority.** Part II of Justice Stevens's Opinion of the Court, adopted by the same six Justices.

**Controlling explanation.** Dion requires clear evidence that the disputed right was considered and relinquished, without imposing an invariable formula of express words. Menominee and Fishing Vessel protect retained rights against casual implication. Here the supported negotiations concerned a land purchase and did not address surrender of the independent use privileges. The broad cession language must be read in that setting. Klamath's reservation-dependent exclusive rights do not make every land transfer a surrender of a separately retained use right. Bourland likewise distinguishes regulation based on a power to exclude from personal treaty uses that remain after the relevant land change. The Court therefore rejects Minnesota's claim that comprehensive conveyancing words alone settle this distinct right. No cession by a nonsignatory Band or surrender under another agreement is inferred.

##### III. Statehood did not itself extinguish the treaty rights

**Holding and operative rule.** Minnesota's admission to the Union did not itself terminate otherwise surviving federal treaty use rights. Those rights can coexist with lawful, necessary nondiscriminatory conservation regulation; the treaty's presidential-pleasure condition does not make state admission the terminating event.

**Authority.** Stevens's Part III has the same six-Justice coalition.

**Controlling explanation.** The Supremacy Clause preserves enforceable federal treaty rights after state admission. Winans, Tulee and Fishing Vessel demonstrate the compatibility of reserved uses with lawful conservation authority. Race Horse addressed a differently conditioned right on unoccupied lands. Its treaty-specific construction does not establish that this pleasure-qualified reservation ended at statehood. To the extent Race Horse treats equal footing alone as incompatible with surviving treaty use rights, the Court rejects that proposition. Minnesota's resource-management interest remains substantial, but it can operate through the governing conservation standards rather than an inference of wholesale extinction. The Court does not decide every concrete regulation or displace Race Horse's separate construction of its own treaty condition.

##### IV. The affirmance preserves the rights at their actual scope

**Holding and operative rule.** Recognition of the surviving rights within the ceded territory remains in force against the presented extinction claims, subject to the applicable treaty and conservation standards. Survival does not itself decide private access, unrelated criminal conduct, numerical allocations or rights beyond the treaty's territory.

**Authority.** Stevens's Part IV has the same six-Justice coalition.

**Controlling explanation.** None of the presented terminating acts extinguished the rights, and no supported later event supplies another termination premise. That resolves the reviewed judgment. It does not require reopening every resource-management ruling in the larger proceedings, granting an unrestricted harvest or conferring a veto over every state rule. The declaration and related injunctive framework therefore remain in force within their lawful scope, without a new allocation, title transfer or retrospective award.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Dion is applied: clear abrogation is required, but no mandatory form of express words is imposed.
- Menominee and Fishing Vessel are applied: independently reserved rights are not extinguished by casual implication.
- Klamath is distinguished: reservation-dependent exclusive rights do not determine the fate of these retained use privileges.
- Bourland is distinguished and preserved: exclusion-derived nonmember regulation and retained personal uses remain different rights.
- Hagen and Yankton retain their instrument-specific inquiry into actual federal displacement; land or ownership change alone does not decide every retained right.
- Race Horse is limited: its broader equal-footing incompatibility premise cannot terminate otherwise surviving rights merely at statehood; its distinct treaty-condition construction is not decided here.
- Winans and Tulee are applied with Fishing Vessel: treaty uses and lawful conservation regulation can coexist.

#### Law After Decision

The Band's reserved uses survive the three asserted extinction grounds. The 1850-order, 1855, statehood and bounded-remedy holdings each control through the same six direct joins. No combination of separate opinions is needed to identify the governing rules. Race Horse no longer supports extinction solely from equal-footing incompatibility at statehood.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

The common dissent of Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would find the rights extinguished on three independent grounds. The treaty's revocation authority gives the revocation command independent operation even if removal was unauthorized. The 1855 comprehensive cession, in their view, reaches the retained use interest; negotiation silence does not overcome that text. Race Horse's temporary-right analysis also applies to a privilege conditioned on presidential pleasure. They reject the Court's removal-purpose, land-purchase-context and conservation-coexistence answers, while confining their 1855 ground to the agreement's parties and rights and declining to nullify every tribal right at statehood.

Thomas separately questions whether survival alone establishes that Minnesota's regulatory authority is limited to conservation necessity. He considers the treaty's pleasure-qualified privilege distinct from the perpetual usual-and-accustomed-place language in other fishing cases and would require a treaty-specific examination of regulatory scope. His objection decides no particular state regulation and receives no additional join.

#### Source Notes

The record concerns the 1837 and 1855 Treaties, the 1850 Executive Order and the Eighth Circuit judgment reported at 124 F.3d 904. The public materials are reproduced in the [official United States Reports](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/526bv.pdf), 526 U.S. 172–226. The holding is confined to the supported terminating acts and does not establish unreported subsequent events or new findings about particular regulations.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review of the presented extinction claims is complete. The judgment recognizing the rights and its related framework remain in force, subject to applicable treaty and conservation standards. The Court directs no new allocation, hearing, deadline, compensation or adjudication of unpresented management claims.

**End of entry: Minnesota v. Mille Lacs Band of Chippewa Indians, No. 97-1337, merits decision, March 24, 1999.**

### Brower v. Washington, No. 98-1510

Merits decision, March 25, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

On certiorari to the Washington Supreme Court, whose December 24, 1998 judgment is reported as Brower v. State, 137 Wash. 2d 44, 969 P.2d 42.

The legislature referred stadium-financing legislation through a publicly administered statewide special election, conditioned on the team affiliate's agreement to reimburse the election's costs. The campaign lasted less than sixty days. The questions are whether that condition unconstitutionally allocated electoral access by wealth, whether the compressed calendar and unequal resources impaired opposition speech, and whether the affiliate's consent was an additional or disproportionately powerful electoral vote.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed on each presented federal component, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Court grants no injunction, new election, changed tally, damages or unwinding of reimbursement. The distinct state constitutional rulings remain undisturbed and are not adopted as federal law.

#### Opinion Topology

Kennedy delivers the unanimous Opinion of the Court. All nine Justices join the three holdings, their stated qualifications and the affirmance.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The reimbursement condition does not establish the claimed exclusion or transfer of electoral authority

**Holding and operative rule.** A legislature may refer this particular public-private proposal subject to the beneficiary's undertaking to reimburse public election costs when the arrangement preserves equal franchise, viewpoint neutrality and meaningful legal participation. On this record the condition neither charges citizens for voting or ordinary petition access nor transfers electoral administration or the public's approval power to the beneficiary.

**Authority.** Kennedy's unanimous Court opinion controls this and the following two holdings, including each stated limit.

**Controlling explanation.** Anderson and Burdick require examination of the actual injury, the State's precise interests and how those interests require the burden. Severe restrictions require narrow tailoring to a compelling interest; reasonable nondiscriminatory administration receives proportionate review. Here the legislature selected the proposal and its financial contingency. The affiliate has a real choice whether to assume the expense, but voters need not pay to vote and public officials administer the election. Requiring this proposal's beneficiary to bear its special-election costs serves the identified fiscal function without making wealth a citizen qualification. The strongest objection is that the State has conferred private gatekeeping over electoral machinery. That objection requires examining what the private consent actually controls: the undertaking to finance this particular arrangement, not the citizen franchise, tally or public approval. Ordinary petition routes also remain available. Their availability supports this access analysis but would not cure a proven discriminatory referral practice or actual exclusion. The Court establishes no general right to purchase an election, categorical immunity for private funding or presumption that fiscal savings justify any burden.

##### II. The supported timing and resource-disparity claim establishes no right to delay or equalize the campaign

**Holding and operative rule.** The short campaign and opposition's inability to match the beneficiary's resources do not, on this record, establish the asserted abridgment of political expression or a right to postpone the election or equalize advocacy. A supported practical inability to organize or communicate, or a deliberately disabling calendar, would require analysis of that actual burden and the State's precise scheduling justification.

**Controlling explanation.** ACLF protects political conversation against inadequately justified direct restraints, while Reform Party rejects the presented entitlement to equal campaigning resources without excusing actual exclusion or suppression. The claim here establishes no separate restriction on the opposition's speech, fundraising, spending or organization. The lower court found no support for the alleged suppressive purpose. That record assessment does not make hostile intent indispensable: a practically prohibitive calendar can require scrutiny without it. Neither public control of counting nor the absence of a voter fee would answer such a burden. But duration and resource disparity alone do not establish that different record. The Court does not supply an emergency rationale for the calendar, find that opponents raised enough money, establish a minimum campaign period or declare every period shorter than sixty days adequate. The State's administration and financing interests support this arrangement at its demonstrated scope; they are not a sufficient answer to every possible restriction.

##### III. Financial consent is not an additional governmental electoral vote

**Holding and operative rule.** The affiliate's agreement to reimburse costs is an antecedent financial condition of this legislative proposal, not an allocated vote in the public electorate's decision. Because it gives the affiliate no ballot and changes no citizen's voting weight or the public tally, it does not violate the asserted federal equal-vote rule.

**Controlling explanation.** Reynolds protects equality in governmental electoral choice. Brower argues that an entity able to prevent the election by withholding reimbursement has more consequential power than an ordinary voter. The condition indeed affects whether this arrangement proceeds, but legal consequence alone does not turn private consent to an expense obligation into an electoral ballot. The legislature controls the referral; citizens approve or reject it; the affiliate controls acceptance of its own financial undertaking. No citizen's vote is multiplied or diminished, and no private choice displaces the public result. White's district-dilution setting is different, but absence of districts is not itself an exemption from equal-vote review. The Court rejects this characterization after examining the actual allocation of authority. It leaves separate access and speech claims to their own analysis and does not authorize private control of counted votes or any different arrangement conferring unequal governmental electoral power.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Anderson and Burdick supply the actual-burden and justification inquiry; Burdick's write-in application creates no categorical presumption for private reimbursement.
- Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation, decided January 12, 1999, supplies protection against direct restraints on political conversation. Its invalid registration, badge and disclosure restrictions are not facts of this case.
- Citizens for a Constitutional Convention v. Yoshina, decided January 21, 1999, supplies attention to actual legal effect and preserves review of fundamentally unfair administration. State-law authorization alone remains insufficient to answer a federal injury.
- National Committee of the Reform Party v. Democratic National Committee, decided March 17, 1999, rejects the presented resource-parity entitlement while preserving actual suppression and discriminatory administration. That limit is retained here.
- Gable v. Patton remains a threshold disposition, not a controlling election-financing merits rule.
- Reynolds v. Sims supplies equality in governmental voting weight; it does not classify this financial undertaking as an electoral vote.
- White v. Regester is distinguished because its district-dilution setting differs; equal-vote review is not categorically confined to district elections.

#### Law After Decision

This reimbursement arrangement and compressed campaign survive the presented federal challenge. Courts must keep financing consent, electoral access, actual expressive burdens and citizen voting weight distinct, and assess their actual legal operation. Private payment, public administration and unequal resources are each relevant facts, not categorical answers. No precedent is overruled and no general private-financing or campaign-duration safe harbor is established.

#### Source Notes

The posture, reimbursement condition, short campaign and separate federal claims appear in [Brower v. State, 137 Wash. 2d 44](https://openjurist.org/137/wash2d/44/brower-v-state). [RCW 36.102.802](https://apps.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=36.102.802), originating in Laws of 1997, chapter 220, section 606, confirms the condition's cost-reimbursement function. The Court addresses the supported operation of that provision; it does not convert the lower court's separate state-law emergency discussion into a federal finding justifying every possible calendar.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review of the presented federal claims is complete. The state judgment stands within that scope. The Court orders no new election, tally change, reimbursement unwinding, damages, additional factfinding or continuing supervision.

**End of entry: Brower v. Washington, No. 98-1510, merits decision, March 25, 1999.**

### United States v. Rodriguez-Moreno, No. 97-1139

Merits decision, March 30, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 7, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit, which vacated the §924(c) conviction for improper venue, 121 F.3d 841. The question is whether venue lies in a district where a continuing kidnapping occurred even though the defendant used the firearm in another district. The kidnapping continued through New Jersey; the firearm was used against the victim in Maryland.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens and Scalia.

The §924(c) conviction and its sentence are restored subject to any genuinely unresolved, properly available claim. The case returns for proceedings consistent with that conditional restoration and lawful New Jersey venue. No such claim is resolved by this decision; no other conviction or sentence is adjudicated. Stevens and Scalia would affirm the venue vacatur, which would neither acquit the defendant nor command or prohibit a new prosecution.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Thomas delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Justice Scalia dissents, joined by Justice Stevens.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Continuing kidnapping supplies essential conduct for venue

**Holding and operative rule:** When the charged §924(c) firearm offense is committed during and in relation to a continuing kidnapping, venue lies in any district where that kidnapping continued, even if the firearm was physically used in another district, because commission of the ongoing kidnapping is essential conduct incorporated into the firearm offense. The Government must still prove the distinct firearm act and its required relation to the kidnapping; the predicate's status as a necessary element does not make every related circumstance a venue-conferring act.

**Authority:** Thomas's Opinion of the Court has the complete seven-Justice coalition identified above. All seven adopt this rule, its New Jersey application and its limits, supplying direct majority authority.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court identifies the crime Congress enacted and the conduct constituting it. United States v. Anderson supplies that inquiry; Article III, the Sixth Amendment and Rule 18 tie trial to the charged offense, while §3237(a) permits venue where a continuing offense begins, continues or ends. Section 924(c) requires both firearm use or carriage and commission of the violent crime during and in relation to which that act occurs. Here the defendant continued confining the victim in New Jersey. That continuing kidnapping is incorporated conduct of this charged offense, not a geographical fact borrowed from an unrelated or completed crime. Cabrales forbids locating completed laundering transactions solely by the earlier criminal origin of their proceeds. Its distinction between conduct and circumstance supports, rather than defeats, examining what this different statute makes part of the offense. The contrary construction would require the firearm act and kidnapping to occur simultaneously in the venue district. The statute's incorporation of the continuing kidnapping and §3237(a) permit venue where that essential conduct occurred, although the firearm act occurred elsewhere. This does not dispense with proof of either requirement or make every statutory element a geographical act. It authorizes no venue based solely on remote effects, preparatory acts or evidentiary circumstances, or on a predicate completed before the firearm conduct began.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide the Government's broader effects theory or establish a universal prohibition governing every effects-based venue statute. It decides no different conspiracy, aiding-and-abetting, transportation or possession charge, no number-of-violations question, no independent sentencing issue and no other conviction. Cabrales's separate charging reservations remain open.

#### Precedent Treatment

- United States v. Anderson, 328 U.S. 699, supplies the inquiry into the nature of the offense and the acts constituting it; counting statutory verbs alone is insufficient.
- United States v. Cabrales, decided June 1, 1998, is applied for its charged-conduct distinction and distinguished because these allegations incorporate the defendant's ongoing kidnapping rather than the earlier criminal origin of property. Its completed-laundering holding and reserved alternative charges remain intact.
- Smith, Bailey and Muscarello retain their distinct constructions of firearm conduct and its relation to the predicate. Those requirements remain independently necessary; none is converted into a prior holding on this venue question.

#### Law After Decision

The §924(c) charge predicated on this continuing kidnapping may be tried in New Jersey because incorporated kidnapping conduct continued there. The decision adds this application of essential-conduct venue while preserving Cabrales's limit on using completed criminal provenance to relocate a separate offense. Every necessary element must still be proved; no independent effects-only venue rule is adopted and no precedent is overruled.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Stevens, would affirm. Section 924(c) punishes firearm use or carriage occurring during and in relation to the predicate crime. Both acts are elements, and the firearm offense may continue through multiple districts when those acts coincide there. But the unarmed portion of a kidnapping does not extend that distinct firearm offense to every place the victim was held. Cabrales's attention to the particular charged conduct reinforces that limit. The continuing nature of kidnapping therefore does not answer where the combined firearm offense occurred. The dissent does not adopt a rule identifying conduct from statutory verbs alone, disregard the predicate element or resolve how many violations may arise from one predicate. Its venue vacatur would not be an acquittal or a direction concerning a new prosecution.

#### Source Notes

### Conn v. Gabbert, No. 97-1802

Merits decision, April 5, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 23, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 131 F.3d 793. The District Court granted prosecutors Conn and Najera summary judgment on qualified immunity; the Court of Appeals reversed in part on Gabbert's Fourteenth Amendment right-to-practice-law claim. The question is whether arranging execution of a search warrant while his client testified before the grand jury deprived Gabbert of that occupational liberty.

The reported conduct occurred March 21, 1994. While Gabbert was searched by a special master, his client twice sought consultation, invoked her privilege and the grand jury recessed. Deliberately obstructive timing is assumed for the present legal analysis, not adjudicated as a historical fact. The separately pleaded Fourth Amendment claim is outside the question under review.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

Gabbert's presented Fourteenth Amendment occupational-liberty claim is resolved for Conn and Najera. The separately pleaded Fourth Amendment claim retains its existing procedural position; any genuinely preserved question concerning that claim remains for the ordinary proceedings available to it. No search-validity ruling, new hearing, discovery, damages award or revival of an ungranted claim is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reversal and remand | Entire opinion: occupational-merits holding and confined search-source explanation |
| Same Court opinion | Stone-Zsela | Stevens | Reversal and remand | Occupational-merits holding, disposition and reservations; not the additional case-specific search-source explanation |
| Concurrence in part | Stevens | Author alone | Reversal and remand | Independent-protection qualification explaining the limited join |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### This interruption does not establish deprivation of occupational liberty

**Holding and operative rule:** Liberty to pursue a profession does not guarantee freedom from government interruption on every occasion for providing professional services. This search-related interruption of consultation, with no prohibition on practice, impairment of professional qualifications or supported consequential interference with Gabbert's legal practice, does not establish deprivation of his Fourteenth Amendment occupational liberty, even assuming the prosecutors deliberately timed the search to obstruct consultation.

**Authority:** All nine Justices directly join this occupational-merits portion of Stone-Zsela's Court opinion and its record-limited application. It is the sufficient ground for reversal.

**Controlling explanation:** Roth recognizes liberty to pursue a vocation, not a personal guarantee of every opportunity to perform a professional service. Collins requires an identified constitutional deprivation; its separate workplace-omission holding does not itself answer affirmative interference with a lawyer. The reported record supplies no evidence of adverse effects on Gabbert's income, reputation, clientele or professional qualifications. It separately supplies no real evidence or allegation of substantial prejudice to the client. Those descriptions do not erase the interrupted consultations or establish lawful search execution. They identify the limited professional interference shown here. Gabbert's strongest argument is that deliberately obstructing consultation at the moment counsel is needed can injure professional activity without lasting financial harm. The Court does not reject that possibility categorically, but the asserted vocation guarantee and this record do not establish the professional deprivation claimed. Duration alone is not decisive, and permanent exclusion, lost fees, reputational harm and client prejudice do not become mandatory elements for every occupational-liberty case. Soldal and James Daniel Good preserve genuinely independent constitutional protections; Albright supplies no controlling broader displacement rule or palpable-consequence threshold. Lewis's emergency-pursuit standard does not govern this planned investigation, and Haddle's statutory employment-injury rule does not create a constitutional guarantee of uninterrupted practice. The Court decides Gabbert's own occupational claim, not his client's constitutional rights.

**Confined source explanation and authority:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer also join the explanation that, to the extent Gabbert objects that the search itself was unreasonable because of its execution or timing, the Fourth Amendment supplies the governing standard under Graham. Lanier preserves that actual-coverage rule without excluding every independent due-process injury. This is a distinction of constitutional sources, not a second sufficient ground that absorbs the entire occupational allegation; the occupational interest is resolved by the preceding holding. Stevens does not join this additional case-specific explanation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The decision does not hold the search lawful, extinguish a genuinely independent protected interest, foreclose preserved privilege or attorney-client objections, or decide the separately pleaded Fourth Amendment claim. It creates no immunity based on prosecutorial office or possession of a warrant. Because the asserted occupational deprivation is not established, the Court does not decide whether that asserted right was clearly established when the prosecutors acted. No mandatory merits-first sequence, absolute-immunity ruling or new client-right holding is adopted.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Board of Regents v. Roth supplies the recognition of vocational liberty; it does not establish an entitlement to every occasion for professional service.
- Collins v. City of Harker Heights, decided February 26, 1992, supplies the need for an actual constitutional deprivation. Its workplace-omission rules are not extended automatically to affirmative investigative interference.
- Graham v. Connor supplies the specific-protection inquiry for the search objection. United States v. Lanier, decided March 31, 1997, preserves that inquiry while rejecting categorical exclusion of independent due-process injuries.
- Albright v. Oliver, decided January 24, 1994, retains its fractured judgment without a controlling combined rationale. No broader displacement rule or palpable-consequence requirement is extracted from it.
- Soldal v. Cook County and United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property preserve genuinely independent protections despite possible constitutional overlap. City of West Covina v. Perkins, decided January 13, 1999, preserves that distinction in the criminal-warrant setting without deciding occupational liberty.
- County of Sacramento v. Lewis, decided May 26, 1998, remains confined to its supported emergency-pursuit setting; its culpability rule does not decide this planned investigation.
- Haddle v. Garrison, decided December 14, 1998, remains a statutory witness-conspiracy injury decision, not a constitutional entitlement to uninterrupted employment or professional practice.

#### Law After Decision

The described interruption does not establish Gabbert's claimed occupational deprivation. The ruling creates neither a categorical rule that every temporary interference is harmless nor a requirement of permanent exclusion or financial loss. An objection to this search as unreasonable belongs under its specific constitutional standard, while a genuinely independent professional deprivation requires its own analysis. The search claim and immunity law are otherwise unchanged; no precedent is overruled.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Stevens concurs in part. He joins the occupational-merits holding and reversal because the reported record does not establish deprivation of Gabbert's own professional liberty. He does not join the additional case-specific classification of the search objection. The possible existence of Fourth Amendment protection cannot extinguish an otherwise established, independent due-process violation: Soldal and James Daniel Good preserve that distinction. The absent evidence of professional effects and the separate absence of substantial client prejudice explain his conclusion on this record; they are not universal financial-loss or permanent-exclusion requirements. He finds no occupational violation here and does not seek a ruling on the separate search claim, the client's rights or qualified immunity.

### Mitchell v. United States, No. 97-7541

Merits decision, April 5, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 9, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, 122 F.3d 185 (1997). The questions concern whether a guilty plea waives the privilege at sentencing and whether silence may support the disputed drug quantity.

Mitchell pleaded guilty without an agreement to conspiracy and three distribution counts, expressly reserving attributable quantity. The colloquy described the right to silence at trial. At sentencing, the court credited cooperating witnesses and used her failure to contradict them as support for finding at least five kilograms, producing a ten-year minimum. Counsel disputed the testimony's reliability. The Third Circuit affirmed on waiver; a separate concurrence considered any error harmless because other evidence supported quantity.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed in the sentencing respect; sentence vacated and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, who would affirm against the inference challenge while rejecting automatic plea waiver.

The District Court must determine quantity from affirmative evidence admissible under the governing sentencing standards without treating silence as evidence against Mitchell. No replacement term, automatic minimum, plea withdrawal, acquittal or jury hearing is ordered. Consequential adjustments to other sentence components must follow governing law; no independent ruling automatically eliminates supervised release or the assessment.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Exact scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Retained privilege and the limited effect of the plea and colloquy |
| Opinion of the Court, Parts II–III | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | No adverse inference concerning crime facts; prejudice and bounded sentencing relief |
| Concurrence in Part I and dissent from Parts II–III and the judgment | Scalia | O'Connor, Thomas | Would permit this inference and affirm without adopting waiver |
| Separate dissent | Thomas | No other Justice | Additional willingness to reconsider Griffin and Carter in an appropriate case; no present overruling |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The plea did not waive privilege concerning reserved quantity

**Holding and operative rule:** A guilty plea does not itself waive the privilege against compelled self-incrimination concerning disputed sentencing facts while testimony creates a genuine risk of additional criminal punishment. This accepted plea, its limited admissions and its trial-silence warning did not waive protection concerning expressly reserved quantity.

**Authority:** Part I of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Estelle preserves protection against sentencing incrimination. Brown prevents a voluntary witness from disclosing a subject selectively while refusing relevant cross-examination; it does not make a plea's limited factual inquiry consent to compelled testimony on unresolved quantity. Rule 11 permits inquiry into a plea's factual basis, and refusal to supply necessary information may leave that basis inadequate. Here the court accepted the plea and reserved quantity. Neither awareness of possible punishment nor admission of the offense establishes the missing waiver. The plea and actual voluntary admissions remain usable. When sentence is fixed, conviction final and further testimony presents no remaining adverse criminal consequence, the privilege has no continuing incriminating risk to protect; finality alone does not extinguish another genuine criminal exposure. Immunity-backed testimony is not decided.

##### II. Protected silence cannot prove the crime's circumstances at sentencing

**Holding and operative rule:** A sentencing court may not draw an adverse factual inference from a defendant's protected silence in determining the circumstances and details of the crime. The Government must establish disputed quantity through lawful affirmative evidence; permission for judicial sentencing findings does not authorize this source of proof.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg: six Justices including Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** Griffin prevents using protected silence to supply criminal proof, and Estelle preserves the privilege when punishment remains unresolved. Their protection cannot depend on whether admitted guilt precedes determination of the crime's remaining punitive facts. Baxter's civil inference and Woodard's discretionary-clemency setting differ from this initial criminal sentencing determination. Witte and Watts permit certain sentencing information within lawful authority but retain independent constitutional limits; Dunnigan permits consequences for established willful material perjury, not an inference from giving no testimony. This holding concerns the source of proof, not whether every sentencing fact is an element or must go to a jury. It does not bar voluntary statements. Remorse and actual acceptance-of-responsibility claims remain undecided. The ruling does not prevent denial of an affirmative acceptance reduction for failure to carry a lawful burden; it creates no new burden or entitlement and decides no particular reduction ruling.

##### III. Other potentially sufficient evidence does not establish harmlessness here

**Holding and operative rule:** The Government must establish beyond a reasonable doubt that this constitutional error did not contribute to the affected sentencing determination. Because the judge expressly relied on silence in crediting disputed quantity testimony, the finding cannot be preserved merely because the remaining evidence could support it; the sentence must be vacated and quantity reconsidered without that inference.

**Authority:** Part III, joined by the same six Justices as Part II.

**Controlling explanation:** Chapman asks whether error contributed, not whether an appellate court could independently assemble sufficient evidence. The judge used silence to support the disputed testimony and indicated that a contrary privilege ruling could warrant closer examination. No independent conclusive quantity determination unaffected by that use is established. Wright independently requires a lawful sentencing basis established in the sentencing findings rather than a substituted appellate ground; its Guidelines holding is not converted into a universal constitutional rule. The District Court must reassess quantity from affirmative evidence admissible under the governing sentencing standards. The Court neither declares the witnesses unreliable nor guarantees a lower sentence. The pleas and admitted offenses remain undisturbed, and any lawful consequential sentencing adjustments remain for remand.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Estelle supplies continuing protection against incrimination at sentencing. Brown supplies the scope-based rule for a voluntary witness's waiver; neither makes every plea statement inadmissible nor establishes a permanent privilege.
- Griffin's no-inference protection now applies to proof of crime facts at criminal sentencing; its established trial operation remains. Estelle supplies the continuing privilege that this application protects.
- Baxter's civil-inference rule and Woodard's discretionary-clemency setting are distinguished; neither authorizes this initial criminal-sentencing inference.
- Witte and Watts preserve lawful sentencing findings within lawful authority. Dunnigan preserves consequences for established willful material perjury. None removes independent constitutional safeguards or permits an adverse inference from protected silence here.
- Chapman supplies the contribution-based constitutional harmlessness standard. Wright separately requires a lawful sentencing basis in the sentencing findings; its Guidelines holding is not converted into a universal constitutional rule.
- Jones and Monge supply no quantity classification or universal mandatory-minimum rule here.

No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

The privilege survives this plea concerning reserved punitive facts, and protected silence cannot supply adverse proof of the crime's circumstances at sentencing. Express reliance requires relief unless the Government establishes constitutional harmlessness. Lawful sentencing allocation, proof requirements and voluntary-statement use otherwise remain; no universal jury rule, mandatory-minimum classification or right to an acceptance reduction is adopted.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Scalia, joined by O'Connor and Thomas.** The three join Part I because the plea did not waive privilege on reserved quantity, but distinguish the right to refuse compelled testimony from an ordinary evidentiary inference drawn alongside credited evidence. They accept Griffin and Carter at their established guilt-phase scope and decline the sentencing extension. Baxter and Woodard illustrate the distinction between inference, compelled answers and an automatic penalty for invocation; sentencing's different evidentiary rules, recognized in McMillan, remain relevant. They dispute the majority's separation of offense circumstances from other sentencing information. Their position permits neither compulsory testimony, treating the plea as an admission of reserved quantity, an automatic privilege penalty nor proof by silence alone. Scalia retains his broader Jones penalty-range position without deciding a new quantity-classification claim here. They find no error and therefore do not reach harmlessness as a ground for affirmance. Conditional acceptance of lawful relief under the Court's contrary rule adds no vote for reversal.

**Thomas.** Thomas joins Scalia's opinion but separately questions the constitutional foundations of Griffin and Carter: an ordinary inference does not itself compel testimony, and a statutory no-comment policy does not necessarily establish a constitutional prohibition. He would consider reconsidering those decisions in an appropriate case. He casts no vote to overrule them here, and no other Justice joins this prospective recommendation. Scalia instead preserves their established operation.

#### Source Notes

The chronology, reserved quantity, sentencing reliance and separate lower-court harmlessness ground are recited in [526 U.S. 314](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/526bv.pdf#page=425); the judgment below is reported at 122 F.3d 185. The published recitation establishes neither a new credibility finding nor an independent quantity determination free from the challenged reliance.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Third Circuit for resentencing consistent with the Court's ruling. Quantity must be determined from affirmative evidence admissible under the governing sentencing standards, with no adverse inference from protected silence. The guilty pleas remain intact; the Court fixes no new sentence.

**End of entry: Mitchell v. United States, No. 97-7541, merits decision, April 5, 1999.**

### Murphy Brothers, Inc. v. Michetti Pipe Stringing, Inc., No. 97-1909

Merits decision, April 5, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 1, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, 125 F.3d 1396. The District Court denied Michetti's motion to remand. On certified review under 28 U.S.C. §1292(b), the Court of Appeals reversed and directed remand because it treated receipt of a courtesy complaint copy as starting the removal period.

Michetti faxed Murphy a courtesy copy of the filed complaint before formal service. Murphy removed more than thirty days after receiving the fax but within thirty days after formal service. The question is whether that pre-service receipt began the original-pleading period under §1446(b). This is review of a certified denial of remand, not an appeal from a covered district-court remand order.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

The federal district court must treat the notice of removal as timely and address subject-matter jurisdiction and any other preserved remand ground. No contract merits or other removal defect is decided. Scalia and Thomas would sustain the appellate direction to remand to state court because receipt of the fax began the period under their construction.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Ginsburg delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer. Justice Thomas dissents, joined by Justice Scalia.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Formal process and the statutory pleading events govern the original removal period

**Holding and operative rule:** Informal receipt of a courtesy copy before formal service, waiver or legally equivalent process has subjected the defendant to the court's authority does not by itself begin §1446(b)'s original-pleading removal period. Under its first branch, thirty days run when that process requirement and receipt, through service or otherwise, of the initial pleading setting forth the claim for relief have both occurred; the separate branch runs thirty days after service of summons if the initial pleading has then been filed in court and is not required to be served on the defendant, whichever period is shorter.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court has the complete seven-Justice coalition identified above. All seven directly adopt both statutory branches, their limits and the application to Murphy's removal.

**Controlling explanation:** Receipt of useful information and an obligation imposed by court process are different events. Section 1446(b) operates within the system in which formal service, waiver or a legally equivalent process subjects the defendant to judicial authority. The phrase permitting receipt otherwise than through service has work to do: pleading information may arrive separately from formal process. If the complaint arrives first, later process starts the first branch; if process precedes the required pleading receipt, that branch awaits receipt. The separate summons branch applies on its own stated conditions and does not invariably require actual receipt or service of the complaint. The dissent correctly observes that Congress expressly referred to receipt in the first branch and summons service in the second. Reading the paragraph as a whole, however, those different information events operate within the formal-process framework, rather than making an unsolicited advance copy a compulsory forum-election deadline. Henderson supports attention to service's actual procedural role without answering this statute or erasing its commands. Caterpillar preserves prospective removal requirements; no completed-trial cure is invoked here. Murphy had no obligation to appear or face default when the fax arrived, and its removal was within thirty days after the subsequent formal service. The notice was therefore timely under the statutory rule, not an equitable extension. This construction does not hold that Congress could never enact an earlier deadline or impose a universal clear-statement requirement.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Both original thirty-day periods and the shorter-period direction remain operative. A validly begun period is not extended, and service-evasion gamesmanship is not excused. The Court decides no unpresented waiver's precise timing or other process sequence, does not prohibit removal before service, and does not apply the separate later-removability paragraph. Timeliness supplies no subject-matter jurisdiction. Other preserved remand grounds, contract merits and fees are not decided.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Henderson v. United States, decided May 20, 1996, supplies source-specific attention to service's procedural function. Its Suits in Admiralty Act holding does not itself define this removal trigger, repeal other service statutes or authorize a discretionary extension here.
- Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis, decided December 10, 1996, preserves the prospective duty to comply with removal requirements. Its bounded protection of an actually jurisdictionally sound completed judgment is distinguished; this case has no such judgment and applies no cure.
- Things Remembered, Inc. v. Petrarca, decided December 5, 1995, and Quackenbush v. Allstate Insurance Co., decided June 3, 1996, retain their covered-remand-review boundaries. Review here arose from the certified denial of remand, not appellate reconsideration of a district-court remand order protected by §1447(d).
- Omni Capital International, Ltd. v. Rudolf Wolff & Co. preserves the distinct role of authorized service in establishing personal jurisdiction. It does not independently fix the statutory time to elect removal.

#### Law After Decision

A courtesy complaint copy received before process does not itself start the original removal clock. The first branch requires both the legally relevant process status and pleading receipt; the summons branch operates independently when its filed-pleading and no-service-required conditions are met, with the shorter statutory period controlling. The rule preserves timely statutory compliance without confusing the removal opportunity, duty to appear and subject-matter jurisdiction. No precedent is overruled.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Thomas, joined by Justice Scalia, would affirm the appellate remand direction. The first statutory branch expressly makes receipt of the filed initial pleading the triggering event and permits receipt otherwise than by service. Formal service is a means of receipt under that language, not an additional condition. The second branch expressly requires summons service when the pleading has been filed and need not be served, and the shorter period remains controlling. General service principles cannot add the second branch's process condition to the first. Congress may require an election of a federal forum before state process imposes a duty to answer; an expired removal period neither authorizes default without service nor establishes personal jurisdiction. Because Murphy removed more than thirty days after receipt of the fax, the dissent finds the removal untimely. It adopts no waiver, bad-faith or equitable-extension finding.

#### Source Notes

The procedural route, argument date and fax/service sequence are reported in [526 U.S. 344](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/526bv.pdf) and [the public opinion account](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/97-1909.ZO.html). That published text also reproduces the applicable first paragraph of §1446(b), including the summons alternative, its filed-pleading and no-service-required conditions, and the shorter-period proviso. The decision concerns that event-date provision and the established intervals, not a later statutory version or an unpresented service event.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for otherwise authorized federal proceedings. The district court must reject the courtesy-fax timeliness ground, treat the removal notice as timely, and address subject-matter jurisdiction and any other preserved remand ground. The Court directs no merits judgment, fees or extension of another deadline.

**End of entry: Murphy Brothers, Inc. v. Michetti Pipe Stringing, Inc., No. 97-1909, merits decision, April 5, 1999.**

### Wyoming v. Houghton, No. 98-184

Merits decision, April 5, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 12, 1999. On certiorari to the Wyoming Supreme Court, 956 P.2d 363, which suppressed evidence from a passenger's purse because probable cause directed to the driver and vehicle did not authorize searching her separate effects.

The officer observed driver David Young's syringe and received his admission that he used it to take drugs. Vehicle-level probable cause to search for drugs is conceded. The question is whether the automobile exception reaches a purse found on the rear seat and identified as the passenger's before it was opened. The officer learned of the false-name discrepancy only after opening the purse's wallet; later discoveries cannot supply earlier facts.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, who would affirm the reviewed suppression judgment.

The passenger-ownership ground cannot sustain the judgment under the automobile rule applied here. The remand does not direct unconditional reinstatement of a conviction, decide every later intrusion or erase properly available independent issues.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice O'Connor delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer. All five join the detached-container rule, its application, the person-and-effect limits and the bounded remand. Justice Stevens dissents, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Souter and Ginsburg. All four would affirm the suppression judgment under their proposed qualification for known passenger effects, including its effect-or-passenger connection requirement and independent-search exceptions.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Vehicle probable cause reaches this detached passenger-owned purse

**Holding and operative rule.** When police have probable cause to search an automobile for drugs, the automobile exception permits searching a detached passenger-owned purse found inside the car if it can conceal the drugs. Known passenger ownership does not alone remove that container from the object-based search; this holding does not authorize searching the passenger's person or decide the treatment of an effect worn or carried on the person.

**Authority.** The five Justices identified above adopt Justice O'Connor's Opinion of the Court in full.

**Controlling explanation.** Labron applies the automobile exception without a separate exigency requirement and measures its container scope by the object sought. Ross and Acevedo likewise permit a properly supported search of places and containers capable of concealing that object. Wellons recognizes a personal privacy interest in luggage but preserves otherwise valid search exceptions. Those propositions coexist: a protected interest does not defeat an established justification for searching it. Houghton's purse was a container within the searched vehicle and could conceal the drugs. Its acknowledged ownership does not change that capacity or establish an exception to the rule. Di Re's protection of the passenger's person remains distinct; the Court does not equate a detached purse with the passenger's body. Nor does it presume Houghton guilty or engaged in a common criminal enterprise. Young's syringe and drug-use admission supplied the conceded vehicle probable cause; that existing authority justifies the initial purse intrusion. The false-name discrepancy and later discoveries play no role in justifying the initial intrusion. Searches of clothing or attached personal effects remain outside the decision.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Labron/Kilgore is applied without modification: actual vehicle probable cause supplies object-based container authority without an additional exigency requirement.
- Ross and Acevedo are applied: a capable container within a justified automobile search is not exempt solely because a passenger owns it.
- Wellons is preserved: separate personal privacy and an applicable search exception are distinct questions.
- Di Re is distinguished: authority over a vehicle does not by itself authorize a bodily search.
- Knowles retains its rule that a citation alone supplies no general search authority; the independent probable cause conceded here presents a different ground.
- Carter/Johns retains its personal-right, justification and causal limits; the Court makes no new independent-source finding.

#### Law After Decision

The existing automobile exception reaches this detached passenger purse. The four dissenting Justices support the same proposed ownership qualification and suppression judgment; that qualification is not adopted. Known ownership neither eliminates personal privacy nor, by itself, defeats the applicable exception. Person searches and attached effects remain separate questions.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Stevens, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Souter and Ginsburg, would qualify Labron for a closed personal effect known before intrusion to belong to a passenger when vehicle probable cause derives from someone else. Location and capacity alone would not authorize the search. They would require particularized facts connecting the effect or the passenger to the object sought, establishing probable cause reaching the effect, or another independently applicable search ground. Passenger-related facts can supply that connection; the evidence need not originate from the effect itself. Independently supported probable cause reaching the passenger's effect, consent and other established exceptions remain available.

Wellons supplies the distinct privacy interest, and Di Re supports refusing to transfer suspicion merely from the car to the passenger; neither already establishes their proposed exception. They acknowledge Ross, Acevedo and Labron as the principal contrary authority. A uniform container rule, in their view, gives insufficient protection to an identified person's private effects. They answer the concealment and administrability concerns by requiring concrete indicators of transfer, shared ownership or common concealment rather than presuming those circumstances from companionship. The qualification preserves ordinary vehicle compartments, unclaimed or shared containers and independently justified searches. It creates no blanket passenger immunity, universal luggage-warrant rule or separate automobile-exigency test.

Young's syringe and admission supplied vehicle probable cause but no reported fact tied Houghton's effect or her participation to the drugs before entry. Her claimed lack of identification did not by itself create narcotics probable cause; the false identity was discovered through the intrusion requiring justification. The later pouch, track marks and admission about the black container likewise cannot justify the first search retrospectively. All four would adopt the stated limited modification and affirm the reviewed suppression judgment. They make no independent-source finding and propose no acquittal or unqualified disposition of every later intrusion.

#### Source Notes

The reviewed judgment is reported at 956 P.2d 363. The public search record is reproduced in the [official United States Reports](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/526bv.pdf), 526 U.S. 295–313. The operative chronology places Houghton's ownership claim before the initial purse intrusion and the false-name discovery afterward.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Wyoming courts for proceedings consistent with the controlling automobile-search rule. No independent-source finding, new trial, acquittal or unconditional conviction-reinstatement order is entered. Properly available issues outside the resolved passenger-ownership ground retain their ordinary procedural and remedial treatment.

**End of entry: Wyoming v. Houghton, No. 98-184, merits decision, April 5, 1999.**

### Barnes v. American Tobacco Co., No. 98-1489

Merits decision, April 7, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit, whose November 12, 1998 judgment, 161 F.3d 127, sustained denial or decertification of a proposed statewide smoker medical-monitoring class under Rule 23(b)(2). The question is whether that proposal may be certified despite individual issues of addiction, causation and limitations. The proposed Pennsylvania class also presents materially varied exposure, monitoring need and substantive defenses. Five named claims received separate limitations judgments; the sixth received a judgment for lack of a monitoring need. Those individual merits dispositions are distinct from the class-certification question before the Court.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed as to the reviewed certification denial, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The five limitations judgments and the sixth plaintiff's lack-of-monitoring-need judgment remain undisturbed. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The proposed monitoring class does not satisfy the applicable classwide-relief requirement

**Holding and operative rule:** Rule 23(b)(2), together with Rule 23(a), requires a supported showing that generally applicable defendant conduct makes final injunctive or corresponding declaratory relief appropriate for the proposed class; common allegations of tobacco conduct and a shared request for monitoring do not establish that showing by themselves. This proposal fails because the materially different exposure, addiction-related, causation, monitoring-entitlement and limitations issues cannot be resolved by the proposed classwide ruling while preserving meaningful individual proof and substantive defenses; the Court imports neither Rule 23(b)(3)'s predominance and superiority requirements nor a categorical prohibition based on individual differences.

**Authority:** Breyer's unanimous Opinion of the Court controls the complete rule, application, limits and remedy. All nine participating Justices directly adopt it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies the requirements of the class device actually requested. Falcon requires examination of the applicable prerequisites. Castano requires an actual account of governing law and proof, and Amchem confirms that the attractiveness of aggregate relief cannot replace Rule 23's conditions. Their Rule 23(b)(3) applications do not create a predominance or superiority test under (b)(2). Here a common product or marketing campaign does not establish that every smoker needs the same screening, presents the same risk or faces the same limitations response. The stated differences concern entitlement to monitoring and preservation of substantive defenses, not merely convenient administration of an already established common remedy. Calling differential personal medical treatment one injunction cannot itself answer those questions. The proposal therefore lacks the cohesion necessary for the classwide relief it seeks on this record. Proponents correctly maintain that individual differences do not invariably defeat a common monitoring program. That proposition does not cure these demonstrated entitlement problems. Nor would an unexplained appeal to predominance or management difficulty suffice to deny a different supported (b)(2) proposal. The Court adopts no additional universal indivisibility requirement beyond the actual rule, decides no new medical fact or Pennsylvania claim element, and does not design a replacement monitoring program.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Medical-monitoring classes are not categorically barred, and tobacco deception receives no immunity. Rule 23(a)'s requirements remain applicable, but the Court makes no independent adequacy-conflict holding drawn from Amchem's different settlement facts. It decides no separate named claim, individual standing question, new limitations rule or medical-necessity rule. The class ruling creates no assumed jurisdiction over individual substantive merits. A different proposal would require an available procedural route, appropriate actual representatives and satisfaction of every applicable prerequisite; neither its availability nor its result is decided.

#### Precedent Treatment

- General Telephone Co. of the Southwest v. Falcon supplies rigorous attention to the actual Rule 23 prerequisites; the Court applies that inquiry without turning certification into a prediction of ultimate merits success.
- Castano v. American Tobacco Co., decided January 14, 1997, supplies the actual governing-law and proof inquiry and preserves its rejection of categorical novelty or size barriers. Its (b)(3) predominance and superiority rules remain confined to their proper setting; its unappealed (b)(2) denial did not decide this medical-monitoring question.
- Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor, decided June 25, 1997, supplies the requirement to satisfy applicable Rule 23 conditions and preserve lawful representation. Its settlement-specific conflicts and (b)(3) predominance application are not adopted as Barnes facts or requirements. Its limited antecedent nonmerits sequencing does not permit assumed jurisdiction to decide individual substantive claims.
- In re Rhone-Poulenc Rorer, decided February 26, 1997, preserves lawful substantive defenses. The Court does not extend its distinct jury constraints into a new Seventh Amendment holding about this proposed equitable relief.

#### Law After Decision

Courts must connect the differences among proposed monitoring-class members to the actual Rule 23(a) or (b)(2) requirement at issue. This proposal fails (b)(2)'s classwide-relief requirement because the stated individual issues affect entitlement and defenses. Individual differences, medical monitoring, class size and the equitable label are not categorical substitutes for that inquiry. Rule 23(b)(3)'s distinct requirements and the substantive law governing the named claims remain unchanged. No precedent is overruled.
