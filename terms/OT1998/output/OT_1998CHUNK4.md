## Decisions and Dispositions

### Citizens for a Constitutional Convention v. Yoshina, No. 98-671

Merits decision, January 21, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; *Bennett v. Yoshina*, 140 F.3d 1218, decided March 27, 1998, amended June 23, 1998.

The question is whether Hawaii's post-election construction counting blanks in the approval denominator violated due process or the First Amendment, and whether the November 1998 referendum ended the controversy. In 1996 the question received 163,869 yes votes, 160,153 no votes, 45,245 blanks and 90 overvotes. The Hawaii Supreme Court construed Article XVII, §2 to include submitted blanks in the denominator and distinguished the language governing amendment ratification. The district court ordered a new referendum; plaintiffs sought certification that the 1996 proposal passed. The Ninth Circuit rejected the federal claims, vacated the new-election injunction and its stay, and dismissed the dependent cross-appeal and intervention appeals in light of that merits resolution.

Act 131 placed another convention question on the November 1998 ballot. It received 140,688 yes votes, 244,753 no votes, 26,784 blanks and 295 overvotes and failed under either denominator. Petitioners nonetheless maintained their demand to recognize asserted 1996 approval. The later vote is therefore addressed as a change in remedial circumstances, not as a decision of the earlier federal claim.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The rejection of both federal constitutional claims and the vacatur of the district court's new-election injunction and associated stay stand. Certification of 1996 approval is denied because the asserted federal violation is not established. No third referendum is ordered. The Court does not vacate the merits judgment for mootness, award a new fee amount, or independently decide intervention entitlement. Any still-pending collateral matter retains its actual procedural posture.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer in full; Stone-Zsela in Parts I and II and the remedial disposition | Supports affirmance | Parts I and II: live controversy, federal review and due process; Part III: First Amendment; disposition: affirmance and denial of the requested constitutional relief |

Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment rejecting the First Amendment claim, without joining Part III or adding a ground. No separate opinion is filed.

#### Holdings

##### I. A live federal claim survives the second referendum

**Holding and operative rule:** A later referendum does not moot a substantial federal claim seeking recognition of an asserted entitlement created by the earlier vote when that relief remains capable of addressing a continuing injury. These plaintiffs maintain that claim; their ultimate failure to establish it does not eliminate jurisdiction.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, controls this threshold holding and Part II. Part III has the different coalition stated there.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the November 1998 vote does not dispose of the maintained demand to give legal effect to the asserted 1996 approval. Act 131 submitted a new question without expressly extinguishing that asserted entitlement. *Church of Scientology* separates the possibility of meaningful relief from ultimate entitlement to it; *Spencer* requires an actual surviving interest, which the maintained certification demand supplies here. *Steel Co.*, *NAACP v. Jones* and *Wisconsin Right to Life v. Paradise* require jurisdiction before contested merits and distinguish a substantial but unsuccessful claim from an absent controversy. Fees alone, hypothetical damages and an unproved collateral ruling supply no jurisdictional premise.

The federal plaintiffs were not parties to the state action, and the two organizations' participation as friends of the court did not make them parties. The bar against lower federal appellate review of a state-court loser's judgment therefore does not foreclose their own federal claim. The authoritative state construction remains binding as state law. Under *Ex parte Young*, a claim for future implementation of a federal right against responsible officials is not a demand for retrospective damages; under *Pennhurst*, it cannot become an instruction to follow a different federal construction of state law. This permits consideration of the constitutional claim without deciding the precise certification order available had a violation been proved. No mootness-based vacatur follows.

##### II. The record does not establish fundamental unfairness

**Holding and operative rule:** A state court's neutral interpretation of an ambiguous election provision does not violate due process merely because election officials earlier gave a contrary advisory interpretation. Federal relief remains available for fundamentally unfair administration, but the dissemination, reliance and voting circumstances here do not establish the pervasive deprivation needed to overturn the 1996 legal result or certify approval.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion is joined by all eight other Justices; it states the Court's controlling due-process rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that official misinformation and authoritative construction of state law require distinct examination. A legally possible construction does not by itself answer a claim of actual official inducement, and informal guidance is not irrelevant merely because it lacked power to amend the Constitution. The polling-place sheet explained yes and no choices but said nothing about blanks. The contrary fact sheet had limited distribution. Statements to proponents and allegations of individual reliance matter, but campaigning reliance and a voter's inability to cast an effective choice are different injuries. Every submitted ballot was counted; voters had ready yes and no choices; and the record does not establish the election-wide frustration asserted.

*Burdick* requires consideration of the actual burden and the State's precise interests. Requiring affirmative support among those submitting ballots serves Hawaii's legitimate decision whether to convene a constitution-making body. Its highest court's distinction between convention and amendment-ratification language is not facially arbitrary. *Griffin v. Burns* and *Roe v. Alabama*, persuasive lower-court decisions, concern materially different changes affecting ballots cast through or withheld from officially described absentee procedures; neither makes every administrative mistake a federal violation. *Smiley* preserves the difference between interpretive authority and displacement of settled guidance; it supplies no universal immunity for retrospective change.

The Court does not find that no individual relied, that every abstainer was indifferent, or that deliberate misleading directions can never justify relief. It rejects the constitutional premise for the relief sought on this record without recounting the election under a different rule of state law.

##### III. A denominator rule does not compel political speech

**Holding and operative rule:** A neutral referendum-approval threshold may include blank ballots in the denominator without compelling voters to espouse opposition when blanks remain accurately counted and reported as blanks and the rule does not discriminate among identifiable voters. The legal consequence of abstention does not itself supply the asserted First Amendment violation.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those eight Justices supply a controlling majority. Stone-Zsela agrees only with rejection of this claim and adds no ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that determining how much affirmative support a convention requires differs from attributing a political message to an unwilling voter. *Gordon v. Lance* permits nondiscriminatory heightened approval requirements; *Burdick* rejects a general entitlement to use the official ballot as an expressive tally. Here the State did not relabel blanks as affirmative no votes, alter recorded yes or no choices, require a public statement, or suppress advocacy outside the ballot. *Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation* concerns an actual requirement of personal identification during political solicitation and therefore does not govern this denominator rule. The claim that abstention makes approval harder does not establish compelled speech. The Court grants no general permission falsely to attribute political views to nonconsenting voters.

#### Precedent Treatment

- *Church of Scientology of California v. United States* is applied to distinguish possible meaningful relief from success on the merits; it retains that distinction.
- *Spencer v. Kemna* is applied to require a concrete continuing interest; the maintained 1996 recognition claim supplies it here.
- *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, *NAACP, Los Angeles Branch v. Jones* and *Wisconsin Right to Life v. Paradise* are applied to keep Article III injury and substantial merits failure separate; none permits jurisdiction to be assumed.
- *Rooker v. Fidelity Trust Co.* and *District of Columbia Court of Appeals v. Feldman* are distinguished because these federal claimants were not losing parties in the state action; the Court does not reopen state-law construction.
- *Ex parte Young* is applied to the prospective federal claim against officials; *Pennhurst State School & Hospital v. Halderman* retains its bar against directing state officials on a federal court's contrary view of state law.
- *Burdick v. Takushi* is applied to actual burdens and justified administration and to the absence of a general expressive-ballot entitlement. Its contextual inquiry remains intact.
- *Smiley v. Citibank* is distinguished from an automatic answer to an election-reliance claim; its reservation concerning displacement of settled guidance remains.
- *Gordon v. Lance* is applied to nondiscriminatory heightened approval requirements; no new discriminatory threshold is approved.
- *Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation* is distinguished because its personal-attribution burden is absent here.
- *U.S. Bancorp Mortgage Co. v. Bonner Mall Partnership* remains undisturbed: the equitable vacatur question premised on subsequent mootness does not arise for this continuing merits controversy.

#### Law After Decision

As of January 21, 1999, this record supplies no federal right to certify the 1996 convention proposal as approved or require another vote. Due process still reaches fundamentally unfair election administration; lawful state interpretive authority alone does not immunize materially different official inducement. The First Amendment distinguishes neutral approval thresholds from compelled attribution. A subsequent election does not automatically extinguish a concrete maintained claim to the legal effect of an earlier vote. No state constitutional counting rule is rewritten, and no broader rule concerning deliberately misleading directions is established.

Yoshina*, 84 Haw. 374, 935 P.2d 89, supplies the authoritative state construction. The record does not establish a later disposition of the reported collateral motions. No separate Supreme Court oral-argument date is established.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Ninth Circuit's rejection of federal relief remains effective, including its direction for judgment for the officials and vacatur of the new-election injunction and stay. The dependent proceedings yield no separate entitlement to certification or a fresh election. No additional merits remand or scheduled Supreme Court act is required. Collateral motions, if still pending, remain for disposition under their actual posture and ordinary law. The record establishes no later disposition of those motions, and the Court awards no new fee amount.

### AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Board and consolidated cases, Nos. 97-826, 97-829, 97-830, 97-831, 97-1075, 97-1087, 97-1099 and 97-1141

Merits decision, January 25, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 13, 1998.

On writs of certiorari to the Eighth Circuit; *Iowa Utilities Board v. FCC*, 120 F.3d 753, with its amended rehearing decision, and *California v. FCC*, 124 F.3d 934.

The Telecommunications Act of 1996 imposed interconnection, resale and unbundled-access duties to open local telephone markets. The FCC's First Report and Order implemented those duties through national rules addressing pricing methodology, state proceedings and required elements. The Eighth Circuit rejected federal authority over several subjects, vacated rules on that and other grounds, and sustained other portions. Its October 1997 amended combination ruling vacated Rule 51.315(b)–(f), adding existing combinations to its earlier affirmative-combination decision. The separate California judgment vacated intraLATA toll-dialing-parity rules on the authority ground and treated the numbering-cost challenge as unripe.

The questions concern national implementing authority, the actual statutory access criteria, the network-element definition and ownership prerequisite, existing and new combinations, superior quality, individual agreement arrangements and the separate threshold questions. AT&T expressly sought reconsideration of the feasibility presumption, superior-quality duties and affirmative combinations under the correct authority standard. The two judgments and eight dockets remain one consolidated matter with distinct component mandates.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Holdings

##### I. National implementing rules coexist with state determinations

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 201(b) authorizes FCC rules implementing the local-competition obligations added to the Communications Act by §§251–252. That authority includes national standards governing the statutory tasks while state commissions retain the negotiations, arbitrations, individual rates and other determinations Congress assigned to them.

**Authority:** Scalia's Parts I–II are joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg. Six Justices control the general-authority and associated remand propositions; O'Connor, Thomas and Breyer dissent on their distinct allocation grounds.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress's insertion of the local-competition provisions into the Communications Act brings those provisions within its implementing grant. Section 251(d)(1) directs implementation; §252(c) requires state commissions to apply §251 and the Commission's rules. National methodology and state determination of particular rates can coexist. Section 152(b) does not remove the new federal duties from the regulatory authority the amendments create. Louisiana Public Service Commission prevents a general grant from regulating matters Congress left wholly intrastate; it does not disable implementation of newly enacted federal obligations. MCI requires an actual delegation and preservation of operative limits, not a rule that every delegation permits only minor action. The contrary reading gives proper attention to the state role but treats methodology and particular determinations as mutually exclusive when the statute does not. The holding decides authority, not the substantive lawfulness of TELRIC, a confiscatory rate or every instruction the Commission might issue.

##### II. The rural-exemption and agreement rules require review within the correct allocation

**Holding and operative rule:** The categorical absence-of-federal-authority grounds for vacating Rules 51.405 and 51.303 and displacing otherwise lawful federal rules from state implementation cannot stand. Unresolved substantive objections return for review with the actual statutory rural-exemption, agreement-approval and state-law preservation limits intact.

**Authority:** The same six-Justice coalition controls Part II. The remand does not adopt a new rule resolving the returned substantive objections.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the general implementation rule does not adjudicate every asserted federal instruction. Section 251(f)'s exemption predicates remain for the actual assigned determinations; §§251(d)(3) and 252 retain their preservation and approval limits. MCI requires those specific statutory conditions to retain their force even when a general delegation exists. The lower court therefore must examine any unresolved objection to the rural burden-of-proof rule or preexisting-agreement requirements on its own substance. Rejection of exclusive state regulatory authority supplies no blanket validation of either rule and no permission for state commissions to disregard otherwise lawful federal requirements. The remedial disposition removes the categorical jurisdictional ground and returns the residual questions without selecting a new proof burden or deciding a particular agreement.

##### III. A network element need not be a detachable physical object

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 153(29)'s network-element definition includes features, functions and capabilities supplied through facilities or equipment, as well as the physical facilities or equipment themselves. Eligibility under that definition does not establish that §251(d)(2) requires unbundled access to every eligible function.

**Authority:** Scalia's Part III is joined by all seven other Associate Justices. Those eight supply a controlling majority; Stone-Zsela agrees only in the judgment on the independent definition component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that operational-support systems, operator and directory services, and vertical-switching functions are not excluded merely because they are not detachable physical components or can also be described as services. The statutory definition itself supplies the answer. The physical-component-only construction would omit its express functional language. But definition and compulsory access are separate inquiries: the Commission must still apply the proprietary-necessity and general-impairment criteria to identify required elements. No general requirement to unbundle every technically available service follows.

##### IV. Necessity and impairment must perform independent limiting work

**Holding and operative rule:** In selecting required network elements under §251(d)(2), the FCC must give independent limiting effect to necessity for proprietary elements and impairment for network elements generally, considering realistically available alternatives outside as well as inside the incumbent's network. Rule 51.319 is set aside because the adopted comparison and impairment standard did not perform that task.

**Authority:** Scalia's Part IV is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, giving the complete rule seven votes. Stone-Zsela additionally joins the invalidity holding that the rule must make element-specific judgments giving necessity and impairment independent constraining force, and joins vacatur and agency remand; he does not supply a separate join in the more specific outside-alternatives requirement. Souter dissents from invalidation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that any increase in cost or decline in quality cannot by itself establish the required impairment while external alternatives are categorically ignored. MCI preserves operative statutory predicates before agency deference. The agency's competitive-entry expertise remains substantial, but it cannot make every purchasing disadvantage a sufficient statutory obstacle. The proprietary standard of significant impairment or thwarting is not invalid simply because it is less than absolute indispensability; its defect includes the cramped alternative comparison limited to the incumbent's nonproprietary elements. The Court does not require access only to an irreplaceable natural monopoly, invent a numerical threshold or choose a replacement list. On remand the agency may explain real entry impairment, actual availability, costs, quality, service requirements and workable selection criteria under the enacted standard. The strongest contrary argument invokes the competitive-entry purpose and danger that theoretical alternatives will require disabling duplication. The Court accepts that real commercial availability matters; that is why an actual inquiry is required rather than either automatic access or theoretical replication.

##### V. The Act imposes no freestanding own-facilities prerequisite

**Holding and operative rule:** A requesting telecommunications carrier need not own some local facilities merely to qualify to seek unbundled access under §251(c)(3). Its entitlement to particular elements remains governed by §251(d)(2) and the lawful conditions of access and combination.

**Authority:** Scalia's Part V is joined by all seven other Associate Justices. Stone-Zsela agrees only in rejection of the independent own-facilities prerequisite, without a ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that §251(c)(3) reaches any requesting telecommunications carrier and contemplates combining elements into a service. The separate resale route in §251(c)(4) does not insert a minimum-property requirement into the unbundling route. Combining or buying elements entails obligations and commercial consequences distinct from buying a finished retail service for resale; the existence of both routes does not make one redundant. This holding promises no entrant the incumbent's whole network regardless of necessity and impairment and decides no assumed difference in particular rates.

##### VI. Existing combinations need not be dismantled

**Holding and operative rule:** Rule 51.315(b) lawfully prohibits an incumbent from separating requested network elements already combined in its network unless the requesting carrier asks for separation. Unbundled availability and valuation do not require physical destruction of an existing combination.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Scalia's Parts VI–VII; those parts control the existing-combination and individual-arrangement rules with their stated limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that §251(c)(3)'s direction to make elements available in a manner permitting the requester to combine them does not require the incumbent first to dismantle a configuration already joined. The Commission reasonably implements nondiscriminatory access by preserving that configuration subject to the requester's option to separate. The contrary reading confuses separately available elements with mandatory physical separation. This holding restores paragraph (b), not the distinct affirmative new-combination duties in paragraphs (c)–(f), and does not require an unestablished configuration to be built. Those duties receive their own disposition. Particular requests remain subject to the Act and valid technical and operational requirements.

##### VII. An entrant may select an individual agreement arrangement with its pertinent conditions

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 252(i) permits Rule 51.809's access to an individual interconnection, service or network-element arrangement in an approved agreement on the same pertinent rates, terms and conditions. The rule preserves proof of greater provision costs or technical infeasibility and limits availability to a reasonable period after public filing.

**Authority:** The same nine-Justice coalition joins Part VII and controls this rule and its qualifications.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statutory reference to any interconnection, service or network element supports selection of an individual arrangement, not a requirement to accept the entire agreement. The entrant may not take an isolated favorable price while discarding its relevant obligations. The incumbent may establish that provision to this requester costs more or is technically infeasible, and the reasonable availability period protects against indefinite use of stale arrangements. The challengers argue that selective access undermines negotiated tradeoffs; the agency reached a contrary practical judgment with nondiscrimination and these safeguards in view. That disagreement does not establish that Congress commanded whole-agreement adoption or that the agency's incentive assessment is irrational. No unrestricted cherry-picking right results.

##### VIII. Dialing-parity authority and numbering-cost ripeness remain distinct

**Holding and operative rule:** The categorical absence-of-FCC-authority ground for vacating intraLATA toll-dialing-parity rules is rejected, with remaining objections returned for review. The separate Rule 52.17 numbering-cost challenge remains unripe because its asserted disadvantage depends on particular cost-recovery rate decisions not before the Court.

**Authority:** Scalia's Part VIII parity-authority ground is joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg. O'Connor agrees on the narrower §251(b)(3)/§251(d)(1) implementation ground stated in her separate opinion. Stone-Zsela agrees in the parity disposition without an additional ground; the five-Justice lead rationale independently controls. All eight Associates join Part VIII's separate numbering-cost ripeness holding; Stone-Zsela agrees only in that disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that federal implementation of §251(b)(3)'s substantive parity duty is authorized. The general implementing grant supports that conclusion, and the narrower substantive-duty ground also supports the parity disposition without resolving the separate state-pricing dispute. Section 271 and other actual statutory limits remain operative. The separate numbering issue concerns who will bear costs under future particular rate decisions. Reversal of the authority ground neither resolves those rates nor creates an accrued numbering injury. The California judgment therefore receives distinct dispositions rather than complete reversal by inference from the general-authority holding. No particular carrier's liability or cost share is fixed.

##### IX. Technical feasibility cannot replace the required access criteria

**Holding and operative rule:** The partial vacatur of Rule 51.317's technical-feasibility presumption and Order paragraphs 278 and 281 is affirmed because the presumption and its actual exceptions fail to preserve the required necessity and impairment limits. The separately sustained definition of technical feasibility and other unaffected portions of the rule remain outside that invalidation.

**Authority:** Scalia's Part IX is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those seven control the substantive presumption holding. Stone-Zsela agrees only in the partial invalidation; Souter would remand this component without a final validity ruling.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that technical feasibility may inform element selection: §251(d)(2)'s minimum criteria do not prohibit consideration of additional relevant matters. For elements beyond Rule 51.319's list, once technical feasibility is found, Rule 51.317 permits the state commission to decline unbundling only if either (1) the element is proprietary or would reveal proprietary information, and the requester could offer the same proposed service through other nonproprietary unbundled elements within the incumbent's network; or (2) denial would neither reduce service quality nor increase its financial or administrative cost compared with providing that service through other unbundled elements in that network. Those restrictions reproduce the limiting-standard defect in the required-element list. They do not allow realistic outside alternatives to perform their proper role. The result rests on this independent substantive flaw, not on the mistaken premise that feasibility can never matter. A corrected-delegation remand cannot preserve the existing defective criteria without agency reconsideration. The Court does not forbid every lawful presumption or invalidate all of Rule 51.317.

##### X. Superior-quality duties return for review under the correct framework

**Holding and operative rule:** The invalidation of Rules 51.305(a)(4) and 51.311(c) is vacated and remanded for reconsideration of the actual conditions and agency justification under the correct implementing authority. The Court does not decide their ultimate validity or order unconditional reinstatement.

**Authority:** All eight Associates join Part X's corrected-standard remand and its confined review directions. Thomas and Breyer do so under the Court's governing authority framework while retaining their dissent from Part I. Stone-Zsela agrees in the remand without adding a ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that §251(c)(2)'s requirement of at-least-equal quality establishes a floor; the absence of an express superior-quality command does not alone show that an implementing rule can never require more. The lower court's existing-network and substantive-conflict objections nevertheless remain for review. Rule 51.305(a)(4) concerns requested, technically feasible interconnection superior to that supplied to the incumbent, affiliates or other interconnection recipients. Rule 51.311(c) separately concerns the element and access superior to the incumbent's own, upon request and to the extent feasible, placing technical-infeasibility proof before the state commission on the incumbent. Lesser quality at the requesting carrier's sole request remains permitted. On remand the actual improvement, statutory relationship and agency explanation must be examined. Compensation alone supplies no missing authority, and nondiscrimination alone does not command every upgrade. The lower court's allowance for modifications necessary to accommodate lawful access is preserved; neither an unconditional superior-facilities right nor a categorical ban on necessary modifications is adopted.

##### XI. Affirmative new-combination duties require separate reconsideration

**Holding and operative rule:** The invalidation of Rule 51.315(c)–(f) is vacated and remanded for review under the proper implementing-power standard. Section 251(c)(3)'s direction to enable requesting carriers to combine elements does not unambiguously assign every physical combination task exclusively to the requester; the particular affirmative duties and burdens remain undecided.

**Authority:** Scalia's Part XI is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Breyer's agreement is a remand under the governing authority framework, not a Part I join. Seven Justices control this remand; Stone-Zsela agrees only in its disposition, and Thomas dissents on his independent task-allocation ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the lower construction treated enabling access as an exclusive assignment of all combining labor without adequately addressing the actual delegation, network control, conditions and agency explanation. That differs from preserving existing combinations, which the Court separately upholds. Subsection (c) addresses requested configurations the incumbent does not ordinarily make, only when technically feasible and not impairing other carriers' access or interconnection. Subsection (d) addresses combination with the requester's own elements in a technically feasible manner. The other-carrier condition expressly belongs to (c), not automatically to (d). Subsections (e) and (f) place the relevant infeasibility or other-carrier-impairment showing before the state commission on the incumbent. MCI preserves specific statutory allocations, but the contested sentence does not alone establish the exclusive task allocation the lower court found. The remand neither approves every proof burden nor compels a particular configuration. Voluntary combination and necessary accommodation remain distinct from a mandated new combination.

##### XII. The general section 208 announcement is not ripe for review

**Holding and operative rule:** A definite agency announcement of a possible enforcement forum is not ripe merely because the position is complete and the dispute predominantly legal. Because this announcement directs no present change in primary conduct, determines no carrier-specific liability and permits meaningful review of a concrete application, the general challenge must be dismissed without prejudice, without deciding enforcement authority.

**Authority:** All eight Associate Justices join Scalia's Part XII, supplying a controlling majority. Stone-Zsela agrees only in vacatur of the premature enforcement-merits adjudication and dismissal without prejudice as unripe; he adds no enforcement or ripeness ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that finality, standing, fitness and hardship are distinct; it decides ripeness, not independent standing or final-order questions. Ohio Forestry and Toilet Goods distinguish settled policy from presently consequential application. Paragraphs 126–128 definitively assert complaint authority even where a carrier complies with a state-approved agreement, and separately contemplate enforcing agreement terms; a later expedited-procedure rulemaking does not make that assertion tentative. Yet the announcement changes no substantive carrier duty, identifies no operative agreement conflict, revises no state determination and orders no act or liability. A concrete complaint would distinguish statutory enforcement, agreement enforcement and a carrier proceeding that effectively revisits state action.

Footnote 236 says the Commission might decline financial penalties in some state-authorized circumstances; it establishes neither immunity nor a required penalty. The statement that judicially decided issues are unlikely to be revisited likewise creates no absolute preclusion rule. The absence of an absolute safe harbor is not itself withdrawal of an established protection or present liability. Bennett's operative legal-protection change and Franklin's completed determination are therefore different.

Section 208(a) supplies complaint, notice, opportunity to satisfy or answer and investigation under its conditions; §208(b)(3) makes an order concluding a covered investigation appealable under §402(a). Concrete application permits the authority objection in its actual setting. The prospect of defending a future complaint and uncertainty about forum allocation do not alone establish present primary-conduct hardship. Lee preserves review of presently burdensome civil duties, and Pic-A-State concerns an existing business actually constrained by a law; neither condition is established by this instrument. NAACP and Paradise require examination of the actual claim, not an automatic further inquiry into hypothetical applications. The Court creates no universal exhaustion rule, promises no future filing's standing or timeliness, and does not defer every challenge until penalties are collected. A presently binding or burdensome statement remains a different case. The Court decides neither carrier statutory-violation authority, agreement enforcement, review of state determinations under §252(e)(6), nor state-failure authority under §252(e)(5); no carrier complaint, agreement, state order or penalty is adjudicated.

#### Precedent Treatment

- *MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. AT&T* is applied to identify actual delegation and preserve enacted limiting conditions; it is distinguished from a categorical ban on consequential implementing rules. Its tariff-filing holding is not changed.
- *Louisiana Public Service Commission v. FCC* retains its protection of matters Congress left wholly intrastate; the Court distinguishes the newly enacted federal local-competition obligations from those matters.
- *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC* supplies review within actual delegation and statutory limits, not authority to remove necessity or impairment from the selection inquiry.
- *Allentown Mack Sales & Service v. NLRB* remains authority for administering the actual announced lawful standard; no judicial substitution of an economic rule is authorized.
- *Ohio Forestry Association v. Sierra Club* and *Toilet Goods Association v. Gardner* are applied to the difference between settled policy and presently consequential application, and the fitness/hardship inquiry.
- *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner* retains its fitness-and-hardship framework; a presently burdensome rule remains distinct from this forum announcement.
- *Lee v. Harcleroad* is distinguished because present civil duties can create hardship without criminal penalties; the announcement here itself imposes no such duty.
- *Pic-A-State PA, Inc. v. Reno* is distinguished because its claimant's existing business was actually stopped by the challenged law and real enforcement risk.
- *Franklin v. Massachusetts* and *Bennett v. Spear* are distinguished as completed determinations carrying concrete legal consequences; finality alone supplies no section 208 ripeness holding.
- *NAACP, Los Angeles Branch v. Jones* and *Wisconsin Right to Life v. Paradise* require attention to the actual jurisdictional claim and remedy; neither commands another factual inquiry after this instrument's threshold status is ascertainable.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Congress authorized federal implementation of §251's express substantive access duties, but §252(d)'s state-pricing assignment does not authorize comprehensive federal replacement of that choice. Rural-exemption and agreement-approval determinations also retain their specific state allocation. National methodology and local rates can sometimes coexist, but that observation cannot itself supply the particular displacement she finds here. She accepts the other rule-specific judgments and joins the lead's other eligible parts. In the California matter, §251(b)(3)'s substantive dialing-parity duty and §251(d)(1)'s implementing instruction support reversal independently of the broader authority rationale. **Souter, dissenting in part.** The Act makes unbundled access an instrument of competitive entry rather than a last resort after facilities replication. Increased costs and lost quality affect the requested service; the higher proprietary threshold and nonproprietary alternative give necessity and impairment distinct work. Under Chevron and the contextual delegation analysis of his MCI dissent, the agency reasonably declined a categorical external-source requirement that could demand commercially disabling duplication. He would sustain Rule 51.319 against this statutory attack. He does not demand access to every technically available element. The separate Rule 51.317 presumption still requires review of its burdens and explanation: feasibility may be an additional consideration, and its actual exceptions prevent treating feasibility as its only condition. He would vacate the partial presumption invalidation and remand under the proper authority standard, without unconditional reinstatement or a final validity decision. He joins the rest of the Court's opinion, including the section 208 ripeness disposition.

**Thomas, dissenting in part, joined in the authority and parity grounds by Breyer.** Section 152(b), the interstate context of §201(b), and the Act's particular assignments prevent converting an implementation deadline into general FCC regulatory jurisdiction over every state-assigned task. Louisiana Public Service Commission distinguishes federal substantive obligations from a federal agency's jurisdiction to enforce them by regulation. The new provisions impose national duties but specifically assign pricing and other decisions to state commissions. Detailed federal methodology can replace lawful local policy choices rather than simply establish statutory boundaries. They would retain the identified authority and intraLATA-parity vacaturs, without making substantive parity optional. Both agree with the superior-quality remand under the Court's contrary governing framework and with the section 208 unripe disposition; those agreements do not become general-authority joins.

Thomas alone would also retain invalidation of Rule 51.315(c)–(f). Congress's direction to enable the requesting carrier to combine elements, reinforced by the separate resale route, assigns that affirmative undertaking to the requester. MCI requires the particular allocation to constrain a general implementing grant. The contrary reading stresses that the sentence speaks of enabling rather than exclusive labor, but compensation and feasibility safeguards cannot provide an affirmative duty the statute withholds. Preserving an existing combination requires no new combining work and is therefore different. Necessary accommodation and voluntary new combinations remain available. Breyer does not join this ground and accepts the Court's new-combination remand.

**Breyer, concurring.** He joins the Court's Rule 51.319 criterion holding and adds that the agency has not convincingly explained its treatment of available alternatives, administrative costs and investment incentives. Allentown Mack respects agency expertise when exercised through a reasoned application of its actual lawful standard; it does not supply that explanation here. This additional objection imposes no essential-facilities-only rule, numerical cost threshold or judicial replacement list. It does not withdraw his contrary general-authority position or his conditional remedial agreement on superior-quality and new-combination review.

### Department of Commerce v. United States House of Representatives; Clinton v. Glavin, Nos. 98-404 and 98-564

Merits decisions, January 25, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 30, 1998; decided January 25, 1999. Direct appeals under Pub. L. 105-119, §209(e)(1), from three-judge District Courts: District of Columbia, 11 F. Supp. 2d 76 (August 24, 1998), and Eastern District of Virginia, 19 F. Supp. 2d 543 (September 24, 1998). Both courts entered summary judgment and permanent injunctions against using statistical sampling for interstate House apportionment, without deciding the constitutional challenge. The appeals concern each action's independent standing, the relationship between 13 U.S.C. §§141 and 195, and the Enumeration Clause's application to the proposed nonresponse follow-up and Integrated Coverage Measurement methods. Postal Vacancy Check sampling is outside the challenge.

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. A supported threat to personal representation permits Glavin review

**Holding and operative rule:** A voter may challenge an announced census method before the final apportionment when summary-judgment evidence sufficiently supports a concrete threat to that voter's representation, causally attributable to the method and likely redressable by relief against responsible officials. Hofmeister's Indiana representation claim meets that standard; neither statutory authorization alone nor an unsupported prediction would suffice.

**Authority:** Souter's Part I, joined by all eight other Justices, controls.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Weber's comparison of Bureau projections and measured differential undercounts identifies a particular marginal Indiana seat at risk. Wetrogan's updated projections and Thompson's objections concerning redesigned methods, uncertainty and overseas personnel qualify the forecast but do not eliminate the supported threat attributable to sampling. *Lujan* requires evidence appropriate to summary judgment, not merely pleading a possible injury; it does not require certainty of the eventual allocation. *Franklin* connects representation injury to relief directed at census officials without requiring a completed census before prevention may be sought. Section 209 makes the plan reviewable and provides expedition, but does not manufacture injury. The Court adopts no finding of conceded or certain seat loss and need not decide county-funding, intrastate-districting or feared invalid-census/reversion theories. Each would require its own personal and causal showing.

##### II. The House has not established its own institutional injury

**Holding and operative rule:** Statutory authorization for the House to sue does not establish Article III injury where the asserted harm is disagreement with the lawfulness of a future census methodology, without withheld information or a demonstrated present impairment of institutional power. This House action must be dismissed without prejudice, and its judgment and injunction vacated.

**Authority:** Souter's Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg, supplies six controlling rationale votes. Stone-Zsela supplies a seventh judgment vote only. Stevens and Breyer dissent from this component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the authorized House is a different plaintiff from the individual legislators in *Raines*, which distinguishes authorization from the existence of a concrete constitutional injury. The House will receive an apportionment report; the dispute concerns the legality of its preparation, not the withholding of particular information for an established use. *Akins* protects actual deprivation of statutorily required electoral information and does not convert every methodology dispute into informational injury. The House retains 435 seats, while the composition theory concerns later electorates and membership without present nullification of its votes or demonstrated impairment of concrete work. The House's continuing identity and statutory authorization therefore do not cure the missing injury. The Court adopts no categorical bar on institutional suits and reserves actual information refusals, subpoena enforcement, property injury and complete vote nullification for their own records. Standing in Glavin cannot be transferred to this separate plaintiff.

##### III. The two proposed sampling methods are statutorily permissible

**Holding and operative rule:** Sections 141 and 195 permit the proposed nonresponse follow-up and Integrated Coverage Measurement methods, as components of the actual enumeration effort described in this record, to contribute to the population tabulation used for interstate House apportionment. The Act does not require the Secretary to select these methods, and the statutory injunction against their use cannot stand.

**Authority:** Souter's Part III-A has five direct joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The shared proposition concerns these two designs, not every possible sampling plan. Part III-B's general construction has only Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; Breyer supplies a distinct, narrower statutory explanation in his separate opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statute allows the challenged methods in the described census. That conclusion has five votes, but the five do not adopt a common general construction of the apportionment exception. The plurality reads the exception as removing a duty while preserving discretion; Breyer permits the methods because they supplement the actual enumeration effort. Their distinct grounds are set out below. Neither the general discretion theory nor Breyer's proposed ban on replacing enumeration with a sample census becomes a majority holding. The common permission does not establish a fixed percentage safe harbor, compel sampling when feasible for apportionment, or dispose of a materially different design. Section 141(e)(2)'s separate mid-decade restriction is not the §195 exception and remains undisturbed.

##### IV. The Constitution permits this explained use of statistical supplementation

**Holding and operative rule:** The Enumeration Clause requires a genuine population-counting effort reasonably related to enumeration and population-based representation, rather than an exclusively headcount method. The proposed follow-up and coverage-measurement design satisfies that requirement on its explained geographic and methodological record; materially different, unsupported, discriminatory or unauthorized implementation remains reviewable.

**Authority:** Souter's Part IV is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer and controls with five votes. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas resolve Glavin on statutory prohibition and do not decide constitutional validity.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an actual enumeration may combine direct collection with reasoned statistical correction of its limitations. *Franklin* rejects a literal physical-presence requirement without itself deciding sampling. *Wisconsin* supplies the reasonable-relationship standard, respect for an explained methodological choice and the need to examine geographic accuracy and uncertainty; its reservation left this question open. The plan combines initial collection, tract-based follow-up and coverage measurement directed at differential omissions. Its explanation distinguishes random block error from systematic bias, examines state and congressional-district accuracy, and addresses aggregation and uncertainty. The challengers correctly observe that better national totals alone would not establish better representation. The actual geographic explanation meets that objection sufficiently to establish the required reasonable relationship; the Court does not find that every State or district will improve or that error is impossible. Neither conventional enumeration nor statistical supplementation receives automatic superiority. The decision requires no particular census method, fixes no permissible error rate, and gives no advance approval to unexplained later changes. The presented constitutional objections therefore supply no basis to preserve the Glavin injunction or require a remand for those same objections.

#### Precedent Treatment

- *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife* is applied at the summary-judgment stage: a supported threatened personal injury is required; legislative authorization cannot replace it.
- *Franklin v. Massachusetts* is applied to representation injury, the official remedial chain and rejection of a literal physical-presence rule. Its actual-reasons review remains distinct from constitutional reasonableness, and no presidential APA or coercive-relief question is decided.
- *Raines v. Byrd* is applied by distinguishing the authorized institution from individual legislators while independently requiring concrete injury. Its reservation of institutional suits is preserved; this particular House claim fails.
- *Federal Election Commission v. Akins* is distinguished: actual withheld information for a concrete electoral use differs from disagreement over lawful preparation of a future report. Its informational-injury rule remains effective.
- *Wisconsin v. City of New York* is applied to explained census choice, geographic accuracy and uncertainty. Its reserved constitutional sampling question is answered for these methods; its reasonable-relationship standard is unchanged, and neither it nor this decision commands adjustment.
- *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, *NAACP, Los Angeles Branch v. Jones* and *Wisconsin Right to Life v. Paradise* are applied to decide each plaintiff's actual threshold before contested merits. No jurisdiction is assumed to obtain either docket's result.

#### Law After Decision

As of January 25, 1999, the two proposed apportionment methods are permitted by the Census Act and satisfy the Enumeration Clause on this design and explanation. Five Justices support that statutory permission and the constitutional rule, but only four adopt the general permission-versus-duty interpretation of §195. No controlling prohibition on every replacement sample census or general percentage safe harbor is established. The six-Justice House-standing rationale requires an institution's own concrete injury and leaves other institutional claims open. Review of actual implementation, geographic reliability and compliance with independent statutory limits survives. No sampling mandate or new exemption from judicial review results.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Souter's statutory plurality, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Ginsburg (Part III-B).** Section 141(a)'s express sampling authorization, together with §141(b)'s use of that tabulation for apportionment, leaves discretion to sample. Section 195 removes apportionment from the command to authorize sampling when the Secretary considers it feasible; it does not prohibit sampling. The exception retains work because the Secretary need not sample for apportionment even when feasible. The retained language and earlier statutory setting are substantial contrary arguments, but do not turn a changed command into an unexpressed prohibition. Section 209 provides review and unsampled preparations, not substantive authorization. The plurality recognizes neither a duty to sample nor immunity for unconstitutional methods. This general statutory construction has four votes.

**Breyer's separate opinion.** Breyer agrees that these methods are lawful, but reads §195 to distinguish replacing enumeration with a sample census from supplementing an actual enumeration effort to correct its omissions. Integrated Coverage Measurement compares and adjusts the initial count. Nonresponse follow-up requires separate analysis because it estimates remaining housing units: direct information from at least ninety percent of a tract's housing units, practical collection difficulty and the Secretary's explained accuracy-and-cost judgment keep this design within permissible supplementation, though it approaches the discretionary boundary. The denominator is housing units, not all persons. Breyer adopts neither a fixed percentage safe harbor nor a cost exception to an express prohibition, and declines Part III-B's broader reading. He joins the constitutional holding. In the House portion, joined by Stevens, he finds a concrete continuing institutional function concerning the apportionment report and lawful representation. Their *Raines* positions distinguish interference with constitutional work from personal legislative disappointment, and *Akins* connects an information entitlement to actual use. Authorization alone is insufficient, but the House's legal continuity permits protection of this function before the membership changes. They would reverse the House judgment on the merits rather than dismiss its action.

**O'Connor's partial concurrence and dissent.** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas read §195's specific retained apportionment restriction to qualify §141(a)'s general methods language. The 1976 change to a feasibility-qualified sampling command expanded required use elsewhere without removing that boundary; §141(b)'s report requirement creates no contrary grant. They accept that an exception from a command is not invariably a prohibition but find the continuous statutory structure decisive here. O'Connor and Kennedy also accept legislative-history corroboration; Scalia and Thomas do not join that ground. All four would affirm the Glavin statutory judgment and injunction as to the challenged apportionment methods, without deciding constitutional validity, banning all statistical work or treating feasibility as an unconditional duty.

**Scalia's partial concurrence and dissent, joined by Kennedy and Thomas.** These Justices additionally find serious constitutional doubt concerning the ordinary historical meaning of enumeration and Congress's long practice, supporting the available restrictive statutory construction. *Franklin*'s residence ruling and *Wisconsin*'s express reservation did not resolve that doubt. This is a supplementary construction argument, not a constitutional holding, an assertion that every estimate is forbidden or a literal census-day physical-presence rule. They join O'Connor's text-and-structure ground, not her legislative-history discussion.

The two district-court opinions supply the reviewed statutory judgments. Glavin's appendix supplies its actual order; the House appellee brief quotes the House order at jurisdictional appendix 67a, supporting its apportionment scope and its Department, Secretary, Bureau and Director addressees. The latter is a published quotation of the order, not an independently reproduced original. The statutory appendix supplies §§141, 195 and 209. #### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Glavin returns to the Eastern District of Virginia following reversal of its statutory judgment and vacatur of its injunction. No injunction survives on the rejected categorical statutory or constitutional objections, and the same design-level constitutional challenge is resolved. The House judgment and injunction are vacated and its action returns to the District of Columbia with instructions to dismiss without prejudice for lack of standing. ### Hughes Aircraft Co. v. Jacobson, No. 97-1287

Merits decision, January 25, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 2, 1998.

On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 105 F.3d 1288, January 23, 1997, amended October 23, 1997 and clarified January 23, 1998. The district court dismissed the original six ERISA claims under Rule 12(b)(6), without discovery or leave to amend; the Court reviews their legal sufficiency, not facts found at trial. The plan is a contributory defined-benefit arrangement with an alleged surplus of approximately one billion dollars. Employer contributions ceased while employee contributions continued. A 1989 amendment offered an early-retirement incentive to eligible active employees; a 1991 amendment added a noncontributory structure, permitted existing active participants to choose, and closed the contributory structure to new entrants. Retirees sought surplus distribution, an injunction against using assets for the alleged separate plan, a neutral trustee, restoration and fees. The questions concern anti-inurement, fiduciary and cofiduciary duties, accrued benefits, termination distributions, prohibited transactions and enforcement of the plan's fair-administration clause. Later merger allegations in an amended complaint are outside the reviewed original judgment.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Ninth Circuit's revival of the six original claims is reversed. On remand those theories shall be dismissed on the independent grounds stated in the opinion. No surplus distribution, neutral trustee, asset restoration, benefit recalculation or new fee award is ordered. A genuinely independent administration, misrepresentation, asset-diversion or nonpayment claim remains governed by its own elements; later amended-complaint merger allegations are outside this judgment.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer in full; Stone-Zsela in the sponsor-capacity and preservation-of-independent-duties rule of Part II, the no-surplus-ownership/no-accrued-benefit-reduction rule of Part III, and the continuing-plan nontermination holding of Part IV | Reversal and dismissal of the six original theories | Parts I–VI state the separate statutory and plan grounds; Stone-Zsela does not join the additional implementation-insufficiency ground in II, the distinct statutory computation/distribution analysis in III–IV, or Parts I, V and VI |

Stone-Zsela agrees in the judgment on the remaining components without adding a ground. No separate opinion is filed.

#### Holdings

##### I. Genuine payments within the continuing plan do not inure to the employer

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 403(c)(1) does not prohibit payment of genuine benefits to participants under the amended single plan merely because those benefits reduce future employer contributions or payroll costs. Surplus remains plan property, and the anti-inurement rule independently governs an actual diversion, unlawful reversion or payment outside the lawful plan.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's Part I, joined by the seven other Associate Justices, controls; Stone-Zsela agrees in dismissal of the anti-inurement claim without adding a ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that adding a benefit structure and eligible participants does not, on the acts alleged, create a second legal plan to which assets were transferred for Hughes's separate obligations. Both benefit structures draw from the continuing defined-benefit fund. A contribution holiday does not itself distribute cash to the employer. John Hancock separates asset status from a particular unlawful use, while Spink preserves anti-inurement as an independent claim even when adoption of the design was not fiduciary conduct. The retirees correctly insist that employee-derived surplus is protected plan property; that premise does not establish that genuine payments to the plan's participants are an employer diversion. The Court rejects the separate-plan and incidental-employer-benefit theories rather than creating an exception to §403 or declaring the surplus Hughes's property. A different actual recipient or unlawful transfer would require its own statutory analysis.

##### II. Amendment design is distinct from fiduciary implementation

**Holding and operative rule:** Design and adoption of these pension amendments are sponsor functions, even though employees contributed to the plan; those acts alone create no fiduciary breach under §§404–405. Actual asset management or disposition and administration remain subject to §3(21)(A)'s functional requirements, including authority or control over disposition without an additional discretion requirement.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join the sponsor-capacity rule and its preservation of independent duties. The eight Associates additionally join the claim-specific conclusion that these original implementation and derivative cofiduciary theories identify no independent breach after their surplus-ownership and separate-plan predicates fail; Stone-Zsela agrees in that disposition without supplying an additional implementation ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that employee contributions do not make participants co-sponsors entitled to control plan design. Spink directly separates amendment adoption from implementation and requires independent claims to receive their own analysis. Varity preserves responsibility for actual administration and misleading benefit communications; it does not turn this design decision into administration. John Hancock keeps plan assets, fiduciary function and breach distinct. Assuming fiduciary implementation of the genuine payments, the original claims identify no independent imprudent management or disloyal diversion beyond the failed surplus-ownership and separate-plan theories. The cofiduciary label supplies no underlying breach. The holding neither adds a discretion condition to asset-control responsibility nor immunizes misrepresentation, investment misconduct, discriminatory administration or an actual diversion. Dismissal follows from the particular pleaded acts and failed legal predicates, not from treating everything Hughes did as sponsor conduct.

##### III. Accrued benefits are protected without individual ownership of all surplus

**Holding and operative rule:** Participants in this defined-benefit plan have enforceable rights to promised and statutorily protected accrued benefits, including the employee-contribution floor, but no present individual ownership of all actuarial surplus. Because the original theory identifies no reduction of those protected benefits, use of pooled surplus for lawful plan benefits establishes neither forfeiture nor an anti-cutback violation.

**Authority:** The nine Justices join the no-surplus-ownership and no-reduction holding. The statutory distinction between required and voluntary contributions explains the eight Associates' rejection of the original employee-contribution theory; no benefit amount is calculated.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that §§203(a)(1) and 204(c) protect accrued employee-derived benefits without converting all actual investment appreciation into a separate participant account. For mandatory contributions, the statutory measure uses accumulated contributions with the enacted interest treatment and expresses the result as an annual benefit beginning at normal retirement age; another benefit form requires actuarial equivalence. The contribution conditions are employment, plan participation or obtaining employer-derived benefits. Section 204(c)(4)'s separate treatment of voluntary contributions remains intact; the original complaint describes mandatory payroll contributions. The Court decides no disputed computation, applicable rate or amount under those provisions. John Hancock identifies protected plan assets and fiduciary functions; it does not give each retiree title to a share of an ongoing defined-benefit plan's overfunding. No promised benefit or statutory floor was reduced by the theories alleged here. Actual underpayment, a reduction below the statutory floor or violation of a greater benefit promised by the plan remains actionable under its own elements.

##### IV. Continuing benefit obligations do not trigger termination distribution

**Holding and operative rule:** Closing the contributory structure to new entrants while continuing the plan and its benefit obligations is not a statutory termination triggering §4044 distribution. A common-law characterization as a wasting trust cannot replace §4041's standard-or-distress termination process or PBGC proceedings under §4042.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join the nontermination holding on these continuing-plan facts. The eight Associates join the separate statutory distribution analysis; no general rule for every partial termination is decided.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the 1991 amendment changed benefit structures within an ongoing plan; it did not terminate the plan merely because new employees could no longer enter its contributory structure. The plan continues to owe and pay benefits. Sections 4041–4044 govern termination and distribution. When their distribution trigger occurs, plan liabilities and the statutory allocation order must be respected, including protection of remaining assets attributable to employee contributions. Employer reversion also requires satisfaction of liabilities, consistency with law and authorization under the plan, and remains subject to the enacted timing and employee-residual protections. The Court applies none of those residual-distribution rules to authorize a present reversion or calculate a distribution here. Protecting employee contributions does not require a court to invent an earlier termination event. Genuine termination, mass exclusion and another event satisfying an enacted termination rule remain outside this holding. The deleted lower-court common-law discussion supplies no alternative trigger.

##### V. Incidental employer advantage does not make these benefit payments prohibited transactions

**Holding and operative rule:** Under §406(a)(1)(D), genuine pension payments under an otherwise lawful plan do not become prohibited transfers merely because they save the employer money. Nor does §406(b)(2), which bars a fiduciary from acting in a transaction for an adverse party, establish a violation merely from the sponsor's adoption of a benefit design advantageous to itself.

**Authority:** All eight Associates join Part V, which controls both statutory grounds; Stone-Zsela agrees only in dismissal of the prohibited-transaction component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the alleged recipients are participants of the continuing plan, not Hughes or strangers renamed as beneficiaries. Spink's genuine-benefit-payment rule applies after the separate-plan, unlawful-benefit and premature-distribution predicates fail. Its rule assumes fiduciary implementation for that inquiry and preserves sham transfers, kickbacks, independently unlawful plans and other prohibited transactions. Souter's and Breyer's earlier reservation about a technical release-payment question does not establish that these genuine pension payments are prohibited; they apply the controlling rule to the narrower original allegations here. The distinct adverse-party prohibition of §406(b)(2) does not turn sponsor adoption itself into a fiduciary transaction for an adverse party. There is no blanket benefit-payment exemption, no net-benefit defense, and no reliance on §408 to excuse an otherwise established violation. A later merger transaction or separately pleaded implementation wrong is not decided.

##### VI. Fair administration does not promise a later incentive to ineligible retirees

**Holding and operative rule:** The plan's fair-and-equitable administration, interpretation and application clause does not give every retiree a contractual right to an incentive later offered to specified active employees. The original sixth claim fails independently under §502(a)(1)(B) because no benefit due under the operative eligibility terms is withheld, and under §404(a)(1)(D) because the asserted difference is lawful eligibility design rather than unequal administration among eligible participants.

**Authority:** All eight Associates join Part VI and its distinct statutory and plan grounds. Stone-Zsela agrees only in dismissal of this separate plan-enforcement component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Article V, §5.2 has work in governing administration, interpretation and application of plan terms, but does not require identical benefit design for existing retirees and active employees whom the sponsor seeks to induce to retire. Spink defeats fiduciary liability resting solely on creation of the eligibility class. That classification alone would not answer a benefits-enforcement claim: the separate answer is that the administrative clause promises no incentive to persons outside the class and no due benefit is alleged to have been withheld. Curtiss-Wright preserves both actual amendment procedures and authorized adoption; these six original theories identify no separate unauthorized-adoption defect. Mertens does not bar an otherwise proper §502(a)(1)(B) claim and is not the basis for dismissal. Specific statutory benefit, allocation, anti-discrimination or amendment restrictions remain effective. A fairness clause gives no free-standing judicial power to redesign an eligibility scheme.

#### Precedent Treatment

- *Lockheed Corp. v. Spink* is applied to sponsor design and genuine benefit payments while its independent-claims directions are enforced. The present dismissal rests on separate statutory and plan answers, not categorical sponsor immunity.
- *John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank* is applied to separate plan property, function and breach; it establishes no participant ownership of every dollar of actuarial surplus.
- *Varity Corp. v. Howe* is preserved for function-specific administration, disloyal communications and appropriate relief. No conduct of that kind is immunized by amendment authority.
- *Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen* retains its distinction between a sufficient amendment procedure and actual authorized adoption; no separate adoption defect is decided here.
- *Mertens v. Hewitt Associates* retains its §502(a)(3) equitable-category limitation. It is distinguished from the §502(a)(1)(B) claim, which fails for lack of a promised due benefit rather than a categorical bar on benefits enforcement.

#### Law After Decision

As of January 25, 1999, employee contributions do not convert amendment design into a fiduciary act or establish individual ownership of an ongoing defined-benefit plan's surplus. Plan-property restrictions, employee-derived accrued benefits, functional fiduciary obligations, genuine termination requirements and contractual benefit promises remain independently enforceable. These six original theories fail after those distinct predicates are examined. No blanket immunity for implementation, new distribution entitlement or general rule disposing of a later merger claim is established.

### Suster v. Marshall, No. 98-737

Merits decision, January 28, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

The case was decided after plenary post-grant merits consideration; no separate oral-argument date is established. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, 149 F.3d 523, July 30, 1998. The question is whether the mandatory $75,000 common-pleas expenditure ceiling in former Canon VII(C)(6)(d) survives the First Amendment expenditure rule because the candidates seek judicial office. Review concerns preliminary protection under the former canon, with continuing disciplinary exposure and reelection interests. The later population-based canon and state-law count remain for proceedings below. The distinct nonjudicial-account transfer ruling is outside the grant.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The preliminary injunction continues for plaintiffs, plaintiff-intervenors and their respective opponents under the October 22, 1996 consent extension, including Maloney's later protection. It is not a final statewide decree. The amended-canon and state-law remands remain intact. The transfer ruling is left undisturbed outside the grant; its merits are not adjudicated.

#### Opinion Topology

Breyer delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. No separate opinion is filed.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Judicial-campaign expenditures and the preliminary protection

**Holding and operative rule:** A mandatory ceiling on the total amount of judicial-campaign advocacy receives the demanding First Amendment review applicable to campaign expenditures. Ohio's interest in impartial adjudication and its appearance does not justify this source-independent $75,000 ceiling on the present record; the candidates' likelihood of success supports the existing preliminary expenditure injunction at its actual, consent-extended scope.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion for the unanimous Court supplies the complete rule, application and remedial limits. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer join every controlling proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the judicial office's distinctive function does not remove campaign advocacy from the expenditure rule. Buckley v. Valeo distinguishes the quantity of a candidate's expression from a donor's contribution. City of Cincinnati v. Kruse applies that distinction to a source-independent total ceiling while reserving materially different institutional restrictions. The Court now considers judicial impartiality directly: judges must decide cases impartially, and public confidence in that function is a weighty interest. The lower court's suggestion that corruption is the only interest worthy of consideration is not adopted.

The asserted interest nevertheless does not establish this ceiling's necessary fit. The record concerns contribution influence, adjudicative commitments and bias; the total limit also forbids further personally financed and qualifications-related advocacy without identifying the corresponding danger. That a candidate can communicate adequately below the ceiling does not explain why additional advocacy may be prohibited. A concern about interested donors does not make every expenditure a contribution restriction. Colorado Republican preserves that transaction-specific distinction rather than immunizing transfers or other financial arrangements.

Separate contribution, solicitation, disclosure and recusal measures address different dangers. Their relevance does not establish that every such measure is valid or invariably sufficient, and no proof of a completed bribe or compulsory empirical waiting period is required. The candidates' continuing exposure under the old canon gives the preliminary protection practical legal effect despite the completed election and amendment. The amended canon remains for consideration below on its own text and record. The Court affirms the confined preliminary protection; it neither enters a final statewide decree nor adjudicates the separate transfer ruling.

**Application and limits:** Protection continues for the plaintiffs, plaintiff-intervenors and their respective opponents under the October 22, 1996 consent extension, including Maloney's later preliminary protection. It does not extend to every Ohio judicial candidate. The removed $18,750 primary sublimit and its separate consent decree are outside this decision. Ordinary recusal and disqualification, distinct ethics restrictions and lawful donor-contribution controls are undisturbed. The state-law remand is preserved, and no new general preliminary-injunction test is adopted.

#### Precedent Treatment

- *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1, is applied to the direct restraint on the quantity of campaign expression. Its expenditure/contribution distinction remains controlling; the judicial-office setting does not supply an exemption from demanding expenditure review.
- *City of Cincinnati v. Kruse*, December 16, 1998, is applied to this source-independent ceiling after separately examining the judicial-impartiality interest it reserved. Its rule against justifying a total speech cutoff by sufficient remaining speech remains effective, and its reservation of materially different institutional restrictions survives.
- *Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. Federal Election Commission*, June 26, 1996, is applied for its distinction between political expression and actual candidate-resource transactions. Its actual-connection requirements and limitations remain intact; neither its party-specific transfer exemption nor its treatment of coordinated activity supplies a ruling on Ohio's unreviewed transfer canon.

#### Law After Decision

As of January 28, 1999, the demanding expenditure rule applies to mandatory judicial-campaign spending ceilings. Judicial impartiality and its appearance receive independent consideration, but do not justify the challenged source-independent $75,000 limit on this record. The existing preliminary injunction stands at its actual scope. The Court makes no final adjudication of the amended canon, no statewide decree, no decision on the nonjudicial-account transfer restriction and no general invalidation of judicial ethics or contribution rules.

### Wright v. Ernst & Young LLP, No. 98-783

Merits decision, February 4, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, 152 F.3d 169 (August 6, 1998), affirming dismissal of the amended complaint. The question concerns primary §10(b) and Rule 10b-5 responsibility for an issuer's statement privately approved by an outside auditor but not publicly attributed to it, together with the separately preserved affirmative-assistance theory.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary-speaker theory based solely on private approval of BT's unaudited, unattributed January 1996 release | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | This confined ground of dismissal remains in effect. |
| Preserved affirmative-assistance theory concerning the January release | Vacated and remanded, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | Apply Central Bank to the existing amended complaint and properly presented independent pleading defenses. |
| Separate 1995 prospectus and later duty-to-correct theory | Dismissal left intact, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | No new claim, amendment entitlement, or revival follows from the assistance remand. |

No fraud, damages, discovery entitlement, or final pleading sufficiency is adjudged.

#### Opinion Topology

Thomas delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The opinion supports all three judgment components.

#### Holdings

All three holdings are adopted in the complete Opinion of the Court by the nine Justices identified in Opinion Topology. Each has direct majority authority; no combination of separate rationales is required.

##### I. Private approval does not itself make the auditor the public speaker

**Holding and operative rule:** An auditor's private review and oral approval do not, on these allegations, make an issuer's expressly unaudited release the auditor's own public certification when no auditor representation was communicated or attributed to investors. A general market expectation about an outside auditor's involvement cannot supply that missing representation; the Court does not adopt a universal public-name requirement for every deceptive act, agency relationship, scheme, or indirect communication.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that responsibility for one's own statement and responsibility for helping another's fraud are distinct. Central Bank preserves that distinction in recognizing a confined assistance action. First Jersey imposes primary responsibility for proved hands-on deception, not for office, ownership, or assistance alone. BT's January 30 release did not identify Ernst & Young or convey its assurances and expressly described the figures as unaudited. The pleaded January 22 oral approval therefore does not turn BT's statement into the auditor's public certification. The contention that investors assumed an auditor's involvement does not identify an assurance the auditor actually communicated through this release. This conclusion addresses the primary-speaker theory presented; it neither immunizes a culpable assistant nor decides responsibility for an auditor's own report, independently attributed statement, or other direct deception.

##### II. The preserved assistance allegations require application of Central Bank

**Holding and operative rule:** A preserved allegation of knowing or severely reckless affirmative substantial assistance to an underlying securities fraud cannot be dismissed merely because the assistant's conduct was undisclosed or the public statement was not attributed to it. The defendant must know or severely recklessly disregard both the fraud and its assisting role; severe recklessness requires an extreme departure from ordinary care presenting a specific danger of deception known or so obvious that the defendant must have appreciated it, and the plaintiff must establish the underlying fraud, qualifying assistance, legally sufficient reliance on that fraud, causation, and recoverable loss.

**Controlling explanation:** Central Bank requires no separate disclosure duty for affirmative assistance, but excludes negligence, professional status, valuation concerns alone, ordinary services without the necessary culpability and assisting effect, and mere duty-free silence. Investors need not rely on concealed assistance itself; that rule creates no automatic reliance presumption. Wright's amended complaint alleges affirmative approval after discovery of a specific accounts-payable under-accrual, and substantial participation was argued below. These are existing allegations to test, not a new claim supplied by the Court. The release's unaudited label bears on primary attribution but does not license knowing assistance to deception. Conversely, knowledge that figures will reach investors is not knowledge that they are fraudulent. Later discovery of accounting irregularities, alleged failures to follow auditing standards, correction of the reported profit, and a subsequent price fall do not themselves establish the required January mental state or the other elements. The strongest independent objection—that the alleged conduct shows only ordinary professional error—must be considered under the correct rule. The categorical rejection of assistance cannot decide it. The Court vacates that ground and remands for ordinary application of the elements and applicable pleading requirements, without finding that the complaint satisfies them or prescribing discovery, amendment, or recovery.

##### III. The separate prospectus branch remains dismissed

**Holding and operative rule:** Correction of the categorical assistance rule for the pleaded January 1996 conduct does not revive the separately deficient 1995 prospectus theory or insert a later duty-to-correct claim into the amended complaint. That dismissal remains intact, and the remand creates no entitlement to amend.

**Controlling explanation:** The prospectus branch has its own pleading and preservation defects, including the absence of the duty-to-correct claim later pressed in opposition papers and deficient allegations concerning the earlier scienter, this class's reliance, and causation. Wright declined leave to replead before final judgment. Those circumstances differ from the affirmative January approval already alleged and argued. Central Bank corrects the governing law for the preserved assistance claim; it neither supplies missing allegations nor erases the distinct prospectus defects. The Court therefore leaves that dismissal in effect while returning only the January assistance branch for consideration under the proper rule.

Actual auditor reports, independently attributed statements, direct market deception, SEC enforcement, and nonfederal professional liability remain separate. The Court decides no settlement credit or ultimate recoverable amount.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Central Bank of Denver v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, April 19, 1994: applied without expansion; its affirmative-assistance action, demanding culpability, underlying-fraud reliance, causation, and loss requirements remain controlling and distinct from primary authorship.
- Securities and Exchange Commission v. First Jersey Securities, September 28, 1998: applied for the distinction between proved direct deceptive conduct and office, ownership, or assistance alone; its preservation of Central Bank remains effective, and its enforcement remedy is not transferred to this private pleading.
- United States v. O'Hagan, June 25, 1997: preserved in its separate source-deception setting; its duty, deception, and transaction requirements supply no shortcut from professional status to liability here.

#### Law After Decision

The private securities action continues to distinguish an actor's own deception from culpable affirmative assistance to another's fraud. Unattributed private approval of this unaudited release does not establish the primary-speaker theory, but nonattribution does not defeat the distinct assistance action. The existing complaint must be tested under Central Bank's actual elements. The separate prospectus dismissal remains effective. No new negligence action, universal attribution test, or remedy is created.

#### Source Notes

The [Second Circuit opinion, 152 F.3d 169](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/152/152.F3d.169.97-9241.html), supplies the allegations and lower procedural history; the [petition record](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40386003_1171) supplies the presented primary-liability question. The [1998 Rule 10b-5 text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-1998-title17-vol3/pdf/CFR-1998-title17-vol3-sec240-10b-5.pdf) supplies the applicable regulation. The alleged accounting error, approval, correction, and price decline are not findings of fraud. No separate Supreme Court argument date is established.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Second Circuit for proceedings on the preserved January affirmative-assistance allegations consistent with the Court's holdings. Properly presented independent pleading defenses remain available. The other dismissal components stand. The Court orders no damages, trial, discovery, or amendment and decides no effect of the BT settlement on any eventual recovery.

**End of entry: Wright v. Ernst & Young LLP, merits decision, February 4, 1999.**

### Hart v. Gwadosky, No. 98-676

Threshold decision after post-grant consideration, February 11, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

On writ of certiorari to the Maine Supreme Judicial Court, reviewing Hart v. Secretary of State, 1998 ME 189, 715 A.2d 165, decided July 28, 1998. No oral-argument date is established. The granted question concerns whether Maine may require initiative-petition circulators to reside in the State; the Court first considers the effect of the initiative's subsequent qualification.

The Law Court sustained the residence requirement and expressly reserved registration. Its original qualification dispute involved a 4,622-signature shortfall after the factual-residency remand. On December 23, 1998, the Secretary accepted 47,406 signatures against a new 42,101 threshold and found the resubmitted petition valid for submission to the Legislature. The rejection categories overlap. The new administrative qualification, rather than arithmetic from the original shortfall, supplies the present posture.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 9–0.** The federal controversy is moot. The Maine Supreme Judicial Court's reviewed judgment is vacated, and the cause is remanded for such proceedings as state law provides.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The mandate does not direct dismissal of the state action under Article III. Residence, registration and signature validity are not decided. Administrative qualification remains undisturbed; no new counting or ballot-certification order issues.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Nine-Justice threshold rationale and unanimous nonmerits disposition | Qualification-based mootness, absence of a supported surviving injury or recurrence, and nonreach of the constitutional merits. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | O'Connor | Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Eight-Justice equitable rationale; unanimous vacatur and bounded state remand | Distinct equitable judgment treatment and preservation of Maine's state-law procedural authority. Stone-Zsela agrees only in this remedial judgment. |

No separate writing is filed. Each controlling proposition has a direct majority.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Qualification ends this federal controversy

**Holding and operative rule:** A challenge seeking relief from an initiative's failure to qualify becomes moot when the initiative qualifies and the claimant shows neither a surviving redressable injury nor a reasonable expectation of renewed exposure to the challenged restriction. The exception for a dispute capable of repetition yet evading review requires both duration too short for complete review and reasonably expected renewed exposure of the same claimant; the continuing existence of an election rule alone does not satisfy it.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by all eight other Justices. These nine directly support the claimant-specific mootness holding and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the December 23 qualification removed the ballot-access injury presented for review. The Secretary accepted 47,406 signatures against the new threshold of 42,101 and found the petition valid for legislative consideration. The determination also states that previously accepted signatures already exceeded the new threshold. Petitioners' asserted future harm concerned further collection to secure this same November 1999 effort; acceptance removes that need. The record supplies no further initiative plan, surviving individual-capacity damages claim or other collateral injury redressable by the requested federal ruling.

*Spencer v. Kemna* requires a concrete continuing injury and both elements of the recurrence exception. *Norman v. Reed*, *Chandler v. Miller* and *Libertarian Party of Illinois v. Rednour* preserved review because their records established continuing electoral consequences or actual future exposure. They establish no general exception for election disputes. The persistence of Maine's rules and petitioners' repeated work on this measure do not supply the missing post-qualification injury. Nor does possible legislative rejection revive a circulation obstacle the initiative has already overcome. *Arizonans for Official English* prevents treating a general declaratory request against an official-capacity defendant as an unpleaded surviving damages claim.

*DeFunis v. Odegaard* requires the Court to resolve federal mootness even when reviewing a state judgment. *Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation* reserved residency and supplies neither a residency holding nor jurisdiction here. The Court reaches no residence, registration or signature-validity merits. Qualification is not enactment, a completed election, repeal of the restrictions or a finding that petitioners will never circulate another initiative.

##### II. Equitable vacatur leaves state proceedings to Maine

**Holding and operative rule:** When a changed qualification threshold removes this controversy while petitioners continue seeking qualification and review, section 2106 permits equitable vacatur of the reviewed constitutional judgment and remand for proceedings allowed by state law. Vacatur is justified here by those circumstances, rather than by an automatic entitlement whenever review becomes moot; Article III does not require an order directing dismissal of the state action.

**Authority:** Part II of O'Connor's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; those eight Associates directly support its equitable rationale. Stone-Zsela agrees in the vacatur and bounded state remand without joining Part II's additional equitable explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that mootness and the proper treatment of the judgment require separate decisions. *U.S. Bancorp Mortgage Co. v. Bonner Mall Partnership* preserves a just disposition under section 2106 while rejecting automatic vacatur; its settlement rule is not a bar to relief whenever a litigant continues trying to obtain the practical benefit sought. The gubernatorial election changed the qualifying threshold. Petitioners' resubmission pursued qualification while seeking review; it was neither a settlement, consent to the adverse constitutional judgment nor abandonment of review. *Kentucky Right to Life v. Stengel* supports clearing a constitutional merits determination that intervening circumstances prevent the reviewing loser from obtaining review of, subject to the actual equities.

Leaving the judgment intact is a substantial alternative because this is a state proceeding and petitioners voluntarily resubmitted. But the external threshold change and continued pursuit of review justify vacating the reviewed judgment here. *DeFunis* supplies the bounded state-remand form. *Arizonans* concerned lower federal judgments and does not require importing its federal-action dismissal direction into Maine's courts. Maine determines the status of the remaining pleadings under its own law.

The judgment is vacated and the cause remanded for such proceedings as state law provides. This neither declares the judgment void when entered nor reopens abandoned factual-residency challenges. The administrative qualification remains undisturbed. The Court orders no new signature count, validates no otherwise defective signature and determines no future legislative or electoral outcome.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Spencer v. Kemna, March 3, 1998:** Applied to continuing personal injury and both requirements of the recurrence exception; neither is relaxed for this election dispute.
- **Norman v. Reed, January 14, 1992:** Distinguished because its continuing party-qualification consequences are absent here; its supported-election-continuity rule remains intact.
- **Chandler v. Miller, April 15, 1997:** Distinguished because its uncontested future candidacy supplied renewed exposure; no comparable further initiative plan is established here.
- **Libertarian Party of Illinois v. Rednour, September 21, 1998:** Distinguished at its continuing nomination-classification scope; completion of one election and disappearance of this initiative's qualification injury present different circumstances.
- **Arizonans for Official English v. Arizona, March 3, 1997:** Applied to the need for an actual surviving claim and the unavailability of a manufactured official-capacity damages residue; its direction to dismiss a federal action is not extended to a state action.
- **U.S. Bancorp Mortgage Co. v. Bonner Mall Partnership, November 8, 1994:** Applied to equitable section 2106 relief; no automatic-vacatur rule or new settlement exception is adopted.
- **Kentucky Right to Life v. Stengel, September 14, 1998:** Applied to separate jurisdictional mootness from equitable judgment treatment after changed circumstances; its independent live-claim rulings are unaffected.
- **DeFunis v. Odegaard, 416 U.S. 312:** Applied to federal mootness on review of a state judgment and remand for appropriate state proceedings; state-law authority is not displaced by Article III.
- **Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation, January 12, 1999:** Its express reservation of residence requirements is preserved. Its Colorado registration holding is neither extended to Maine residency nor reconsidered.

#### Law After Decision

The continuing-injury and equitable-vacatur requirements remain in force. This qualified initiative supplies no live federal controversy on the presented record, and the reviewed constitutional judgment is vacated. Maine's authority over further state proceedings is preserved. Residence and registration restrictions, signature validity and the initiative's later legislative or electoral fate remain undecided. ### Boring v. Buncombe County Board of Education, No. 97-1835

Merits decision, February 17, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 9, 1998.

On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit, sitting en banc, 136 F.3d 364 (February 13, 1998), as amended March 18, 1998. The court affirmed final dismissal of the First Amendment claim under Rule 12(b)(6); its earlier panel decision was vacated.

The questions are whether a public-school teacher's selection, production, and direction of curricular material pursuant to assigned teaching responsibilities is the teacher's protected personal speech or institutional curricular expression, and, if a personal interest remains, what constitutional standard governs changes to curriculum or related adverse employment action. Review is confined to that First Amendment claim; federal and state due-process, liberty, and North Carolina constitutional claims are outside the grant.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas.

**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The dismissal of the granted First Amendment claim remains in force. The Court orders no further remedy and does not reopen claims outside the grant.

#### Opinion Topology

Stone-Zsela delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Souter filed a dissent, joined in full by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer, that would vacate and remand.

#### Holdings

##### Assigned instructional production and the asserted personal speech right

**Holding and operative rule:** A public-school teacher's selection, production, and direction of material as part of the assigned school instructional program constitute institutional curricular expression subject to the school system's lawful control; the artistic discretion entrusted to the teacher does not, on this claim, create a personal First Amendment right against curricular and related employment control. This rule concerns the assigned school production, and does not authorize punishment of separate citizen speech or an independently unconstitutional classification or exclusion.

**Authority:** The complete Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas, supplies five direct rationale votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the personal right asserted must be identified before deciding whether an employer's justification defeats it. Boring selected and directed *Independence* through her assigned advanced acting class and an interscholastic competition for which the school bore institutional responsibility. Artistic judgment and public controversy do not turn that instructional choice into a personal right to control the institution's expression. Hazelwood distinguishes personal student expression from school-sponsored activity and recognizes legitimate educational control; it does not give the employee administering that activity a parallel personal editorial entitlement. Settle protects a student's otherwise permissible perspective within an assignment against categorical religious exclusion disguised as pedagogy. Its constitutional limit remains, but the student's claim does not establish a teacher's personal right over the school's selected program. Jeffries protected an unaffiliated address expressly outside assigned work; Brewster addressed a particular attendance-error report, not selection of instructional content.

The strongest contrary argument is that Boring challenges retaliation after approval, not the school's final power to choose a play. The Court accepts the pleaded approval, compliance, and retaliatory motivation at their proper status. Those allegations do not create the missing personal curricular interest or make approval an irrevocable constitutional delegation. This conclusion does not establish that officials actually acted for legitimate reasons. Settle and the independently applicable constitutional prohibitions prevent treating every act labeled curricular as lawful by definition. But the granted claim identifies no separate citizen expression or independent forbidden classification supplying the personal right asserted here. Waters and Mt. Healthy regulate justification and cause once protected conduct is established; they do not supply that antecedent right. Dismissal therefore stands on this confined ground.

The conditional second question is not reached because the asserted personal interest in this assigned curricular act is absent. The Court decides no standard for a distinct protected interest outside assigned curricular responsibilities, no rule for every duty-related statement of every public employee, and no materially different university academic-freedom question. Statutory, contractual, tenure, and state-law limits remain separate. The Court finds neither insubordination nor lawful actual motive nor a same-decision defense.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Hazelwood School District v. Kuhlmeier: applies the distinction between personal student expression and school-sponsored educational activity; its student-speech rule is not converted into an instructor's personal control over institutional content.
- Settle v. Dickson County School Board, June 25, 1996: preserves genuine curricular authority and the prohibition on categorical religious exclusion masquerading as pedagogy; distinguishes the student's limited interest within an assignment from the teacher's asserted interest in administering the program.
- Jeffries v. Harleston, November 17, 1997: distinguishes the unaffiliated citizen address expressly outside assigned official work; its protection, actual-cause rule, and limited mandate remain intact.
- Brewster v. Board of Education, August 17, 1998: distinguishes the particular internal attendance-error report and conduct-date immunity question; no general rule withdrawing protection from workplace reports follows.
- Waters v. Churchill, May 31, 1994: preserves its limited majority propositions on genuine lawful grounds and mistaken accounts of protected employee speech; supplies no personal curricular entitlement or universal investigation procedure.
- Mt. Healthy City School District Board of Education v. Doyle: preserves the claimant's protected-conduct and substantial-or-motivating-factor showing and the employer's separate preponderance same-decision defense; no defense is found proved here.
- Pickering v. Board of Education, Connick v. Myers, and Givhan v. Western Line Consolidated School District: retain contextual public-employee speech protection, including private speech; the Court adopts no categorical workplace or private-concern exclusion.
- Epperson v. Arkansas and Keyishian v. Board of Regents: preserve independently applicable constitutional limits on educational authority; the curricular label does not establish immunity from them.

No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

The teacher's delegated selection and direction of this school instructional production do not supply the personal speech entitlement claimed against curricular and related employment control. That limited institutional-content rule leaves student expression, separate citizen speech, independent constitutional prohibitions, and other sources of employment protection in place. The decision adopts no universal rule for government employees' assigned speech and no constitutional safe harbor based on any conceivable pedagogical reason.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Souter, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer, would vacate the dismissal and remand. The dissent accepts the school's final authority over instruction but recognizes a limited personal expressive interest against adverse action unrelated to genuine legitimate pedagogy. A teacher need not have a right to insist on a chosen play to object that officials punished an allegedly approved expressive contribution for an impermissible nonpedagogical reason. The dissent would extend Hazelwood's legitimate-pedagogical-relation approach to this claim, informed by Settle's distinction between real educational authority and constitutional immunity. It does not assert that either precedent already decided a teacher's entitlement.

Officials could alter content and take related personnel action reasonably related to legitimate pedagogical concerns. They would not have to satisfy strict scrutiny, prove actual disruption in every case, open a forum, or guarantee Boring's preferred production. But approval, compliance with the requested edits, and punishment for unpopular expression are pleaded; insubordination or a merely conceivable legitimate reason cannot replace an actual supported ground. Waters and Jeffries preserve that genuine-ground and causal distinction. Brewster reinforces attention to the actual employee claim and individualized defenses without treating every internal disagreement as protected.

The dissent would permit ordinary adjudication of protection, actual pedagogical grounds, and cause. Boring would have to show protected conduct was a substantial or motivating factor; defendants could prove by a preponderance that they would have made the same decision without it. Liability, municipal attribution, and individual immunity would remain distinct; immunity notice would be judged as of the 1991–1992 conduct. The proposed remand orders no play, reinstatement, damages, discovery, or trial and permits another supported lawful disposition. These four Justices reject the majority's absence-of-personal-interest conclusion while preserving broad final school control.

#### Source Notes

The [Fourth Circuit en banc opinion, 136 F.3d 364](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/136/136.F3d.364.95-2593.html), supplies the allegations, final dismissal, and opinions below; the [petition record](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40386006_1533) supplies the review materials. Approval, compliance with edits, policy coverage, and retaliatory motivation remain allegations or pleading assumptions. No trial established actual motive, insubordination, or the auditorium-floor incident as an independent lawful cause.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Fourth Circuit's judgment remains effective as to the granted federal First Amendment claim. No remand or additional proceedings are ordered. Claims outside the grant are not revived, and the Court determines no liability, damages, or entitlement under a different source of law.

**End of entry: Boring v. Buncombe County Board of Education, merits decision, February 17, 1999.**

### John Crane, Inc. v. Abate, No. 98-873

Merits decision, February 18, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

On writ of certiorari to the Court of Special Appeals of Maryland, *ACandS, Inc. v. Abate*, 121 Md. App. 590, 710 A.2d 944 (1998), after the Maryland Court of Appeals denied discretionary review.

The question is whether the consolidated asbestos trial denied Crane due process by combining many claims and defendants, selecting five plaintiffs for trial, and using product-category verdicts. The five trial plaintiffs proceeded to final judgments. Common findings also concerned approximately 1,300 other claimants, whose exposure, disease, causation and damages remained for later individual proceedings. The jury's common negligence and defect findings would not ordinarily be retried in those proceedings.

The trial separated common and individual liability issues, designated cross-claims, and punitive damages into phases, while receiving overlapping evidence together. Crane contends that the resulting complexity and broad category findings prevented a meaningful defense. The lower court examined the instructions, identification of represented parties, differentiated verdicts, product evidence and retained individual elements. It upheld consolidation while separately correcting particular errors and ordering claim-specific relief. The present review concerns the due-process objection as it bears on Crane's final judgments.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed insofar as challenged by Crane on the consolidation and product-category due-process ground, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The lower court's separate reversals for insufficient evidence, damages-cap reduction, vacaturs for contractor-instruction proceedings and corresponding judgment-share adjustments remain intact. The Court orders no additional trial, damages calculation or universal asbestos-liability determination. It does not reopen other defendants' judgments or finally adjudicate the approximately 1,300 untried individual claims.

#### Opinion Topology

Breyer delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. The unanimous opinion controls all three holdings, their applications and reservations, and the remedial limits.

#### Holdings

##### I. Common issues may be tried together only with a meaningful defense

**Holding and operative rule:** A State may decide genuinely common issues together when each defendant has a meaningful opportunity to contest the evidence and every material defense to those issues, while the remaining claimant-specific elements must still be proved. Neither the number of parties nor the absence of brand names on a verdict form alone establishes a due-process violation; efficiency cannot replace a legally required individual showing.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, controls this holding and Holdings II and III at their stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the constitutional question concerns what the procedure actually decides and whether the affected party can defend against it. *Richards v. Jefferson County* requires a lawful, meaningful opportunity to litigate before a judgment binds a person; shared interests alone cannot supply one. Crane was an actual party and could contest the common evidence. That distinction does not excuse eliminating a material product defense merely because an individual damages hearing will follow.

*Amchem Products v. Windsor* requires the prerequisites of the procedural device actually used and protection of material differences. Its requirements for a federal settlement class do not govern consolidation under Maryland Rule 2-503. The State's authority to consolidate common questions does not alter substantive exposure, product connection, causation, injury or damages requirements. Five trial plaintiffs cannot statistically supply those different facts for every other claimant. Nor does a category label establish that materially different products share the characteristic at issue. A lost defense requires relief fitted to the affected adjudication, however useful aggregation might otherwise be.

The Court adopts no numerical ceiling, brand-name-verdict requirement, efficiency exception to due process, or rule binding strangers without lawful representation. The controlling inquiry remains the actual common issue, evidence, opportunity to defend, and elements left for individual proof.

##### II. The reviewed Crane judgments satisfy that requirement

**Holding and operative rule:** The consolidated procedure and product-category findings do not deny Crane due process on this record because the actual common evidence, differentiated verdicts and retained individual elements preserved a meaningful defense. This conclusion does not extend a common finding to a materially different product or defense that was not fairly adjudicated.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the concrete record defeats Crane's asserted structural inference. Lawyers identified their clients; explanations and instructions distinguished earlier liability from contested liability; verdicts identified defendants; and the five trial plaintiffs illustrated issues without supplying extrapolated recoveries for the untried claimants. Receiving overlapping evidence across phases did not itself prevent Crane from disputing its significance.

The product-category finding rests on testimony connecting visible asbestos dust with excessive exposure, the absence of directed contrary proof for the products implicated in this appeal, and the continued requirement to prove actual exposure and resulting harm. The lower court acknowledged that products differ. It did not determine that a nonemitting or materially different product could never exist.

The jury distinguished Crane packing from Crane gaskets. Crane argues that Theis and Glensky described gaskets and thus received inconsistent judgments. The admitted catalog identified sheet packing from which gaskets could be cut; testimony described cutting sheets and the resulting dust. That evidence supports the packing finding despite the witnesses' description of the cut article as a gasket. The verdict did not simply impose liability on the separately exonerated gasket product.

The corrected starting dates for Stebbing and Hampshire, set-aside Harbison-Walker findings, and reversals of unsupported fiber-drift exposure findings are real errors. They do not establish that Crane lost a material defense or that the differentiated remaining findings resulted from an incapable jury. Conversely, appellate correction is no universal cure for unconstitutional aggregation. The present affirmance rests on the particular opportunity and evidence supporting Crane's judgments, applying the fair-adjudication requirement of *Richards* and the protection of substantive differences preserved in *Amchem*.

##### III. The affirmance preserves individual proof and distinct remedies

**Holding and operative rule:** Affirmance of the reviewed due-process ruling leaves the state appellate court's independent reversals, reductions, vacaturs and remands in force. Later proceedings may apply only the common findings lawfully resolved; each claimant must still prove individual exposure, disease, sufficient causation and damages, and any extension to a materially different product or defense remains open to challenge on its actual record.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that rejection of the categorical attack does not erase specific errors or create final recoveries for claimants whose individual cases remain untried. The lower mandate separately reverses specified judgments against U.S. Mineral, Stebbing and Hampshire for insufficient evidence, requires reduction of Ciotta's judgment under the damages cap, directs proceedings on specified Hampshire contractor instructions, and adjusts judgment shares accordingly. Those dispositions retain their own grounds.

The common negligence and defect findings will not ordinarily be retried in later individual proceedings. That makes the scope of the original opportunity to defend essential; later mini-trials could not cure a material defense already foreclosed. The findings upheld here carry only the scope supported by the actual common evidence. The Court neither rewrites Maryland causation law nor authorizes averaging different exposures, products or injuries. No separate federal jury-trial claim is decided. The due-process judgment concerns Crane's preserved challenge to the actual final judgments before the Court, not every possible future application of the common findings.

#### Precedent Treatment

- *Richards v. Jefferson County* is applied to preserve a meaningful opportunity to litigate and lawful grounds for binding a party. It does not prohibit an actual defendant's fairly conducted common-issue trial or permit binding strangers solely because their interests resemble those of participants.
- *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor* is distinguished as a federal settlement-class decision and applied for its protection of substantive differences and the requirements of the actual procedural device. Its Rule 23 prerequisites do not become rules for Maryland consolidation; its rejection of procedural shortcuts remains intact.

#### Law After Decision

Common-issue consolidation remains available subject to a meaningful defense on every material common issue and separate proof of the individual elements. Trial size and category labels alone do not invalidate it, and efficiency alone cannot sustain it. These Crane judgments survive the asserted due-process challenge. The separate state appellate corrections remain effective. No universal asbestos finding, extrapolated injury award, or permission to expand a common verdict to unadjudicated material differences follows.

#### Source Notes

The full *ACandS, Inc. v. Abate*, 121 Md. App. 590, 710 A.2d 944, opinion, including its finality discussion, Part I and mandate, supplies the trial structure, product evidence and distinct appellate remedies. The petition, opposition and reply preserve the competing descriptions and requested relief. The Maryland review-denial order establishes denial of discretionary review; inconsistent typography concerning its precise 1998 date remains unresolved and supplies no finding of untimely filing. The five final trial claims are distinguished from the approximately 1,300 claims awaiting individual adjudication. No exact Supreme Court argument date is established.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Maryland judgment stands within the scope reviewed. Its independent corrective mandates proceed on their stated grounds; this decision does not require another trial of Crane's reviewed claims. Untried individual claims retain their remaining proof requirements and the lawful scope of common findings. No additional Supreme Court event is scheduled.

**End of entry: John Crane, Inc. v. Abate, merits decision, February 18, 1999.**

### National Collegiate Athletic Association v. Smith, No. 98-84

Merits decision, February 23, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 20, 1999.

On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit, 139 F.3d 180 (1998).

Smith played two seasons of volleyball at St. Bonaventure, graduated and pursued further study at Hofstra and Pittsburgh. An NCAA rule allowed athletes with remaining eligibility to compete during specified further study at the same institution, subject to the applicable five-year or ten-semester limit. Smith alleges that the NCAA discriminatorily denied her waivers. She seeks monetary and declaratory relief.

The district court dismissed her Title IX claim and denied amendment as moot. The Third Circuit held that ordinary dues from federally assisted members could make the NCAA a recipient of assistance. On that basis it vacated the Title IX dismissal, reversed the denial of amendment and remanded, directing reinstatement of the supplemental contract claim. It affirmed dismissal of the antitrust claim. The question here is whether dues alone support Title IX recipient status. No court has found discrimination or awarded damages on the claim before the Court.

#### Judgment

**Dues-based Title IX disposition vacated and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The lower courts must reject ordinary membership dues alone as sufficient to establish the NCAA's receipt of federal financial assistance. Properly preserved alternative coverage and pleading questions remain for ordinary consideration. No coverage finding, leave to amend, discrimination finding or award is ordered. The concluded antitrust disposition is undisturbed; supplemental contract jurisdiction depends on the resulting federal-claim posture.

#### Opinion Topology

O'Connor delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The unanimous opinion controls both holdings and their reservations.

#### Holdings

##### I. Ordinary membership dues do not alone establish receipt of federal assistance

**Holding and operative rule:** An association does not become a recipient of federal financial assistance under Title IX merely because it receives ordinary membership dues from institutions receiving such assistance. Assistance may reach a recipient through an intermediary, but an ordinary downstream payment by a recipient is not, without more, assistance extended to the payee.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, controls this holding and Holding II at their stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that receipt and economic benefit are different. *Grove City College v. Bell* establishes that federal student aid intended for payment to a college can make the college an indirect recipient. *Department of Transportation v. Paralyzed Veterans of America* distinguishes a recipient accepting assistance from another entity benefiting economically from assisted operations. Ordinary membership dues fall on the latter side of that distinction unless additional facts establish a transfer of assistance.

Section 1687 enlarges the operations covered once assistance reaches part of the identified entity. Its provision for an entity established by two or more listed institutions still requires that some part receive assistance. The Civil Rights Restoration Act did not eliminate that condition. The complete definition in 34 C.F.R. §106.2(h) likewise requires assistance extended directly or through another recipient. Its following reference to an educational activity receiving or benefiting from assistance does not erase the first requirement.

The NCAA's role in collegiate athletics and its relationship to member institutions do not make every membership payment federal assistance. Neither the amount nor the percentage of dues supplies an exemption or threshold. The private action retains the recipient requirement; its implied character does not extend coverage to every beneficiary. *Kelley* governs equal athletic opportunity at covered institutions, and *Gebser* addresses attribution of discrimination to an established recipient; neither supplies receipt here. This holding does not relieve funded members of their own duties or decide independently sufficient coverage based on actual assistance or delegated control.

##### II. The unadjudicated alternatives and pleading questions remain for the lower courts

**Holding and operative rule:** The dues-based Title IX disposition must be vacated and the case remanded; properly preserved theories of actual assistance through the National Youth Sports Program or its Fund and independently sufficient delegated control remain open. Dismissal alone does not make an amendment motion moot, but the remand grants neither leave to amend nor a finding that an alternative is preserved or sufficient.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the lower judgment's stated basis fails without deciding issues the lower courts did not independently resolve. The Third Circuit's description of the NCAA as an athletics surrogate supported its dues rationale. It was not a completed adjudication of either NYSP assistance or a control-only theory. Those alternatives require their own proper presentation and factual and legal support; the existence of a contention proves neither.

*Foman v. Davis* supplies the ordinary amendment principles: futility, undue delay, bad faith, dilatory motive and prejudice remain available grounds under the applicable procedure. An allegation relying only on ordinary dues cannot cure the recipient defect. The lower courts must consider the actual pleading history, preservation and the Marshall affidavit's procedural significance before determining any permissible amendment. The Court does not independently grant or reject the proposed addition of the universities.

*Steel Co.* distinguishes constitutional jurisdiction from disputed statutory liability. This decision concerns statutory coverage at the pleading stage; it does not declare an Article III defect. Expiration of the athletic seasons does not by itself eliminate Smith's compensation demand, but recipient status, responsibility, discrimination, causation, compensable loss and entitlement to prospective relief remain unproved. Contract-jurisdiction consequences follow the federal claim's resulting posture, and the antitrust judgment is not reopened.

#### Precedent Treatment

- *Grove City College v. Bell* is applied to preserve indirect receipt where assistance is intended for the recipient; §1687 broadened covered operations without erasing receipt.
- *Department of Transportation v. Paralyzed Veterans of America* supplies the analogous distinction between receipt of assistance and economic benefit, which ordinary dues do not overcome.
- *Kelley v. Board of Trustees* remains the rule for equal athletic opportunity within covered institutions; it establishes no association coverage from dues.
- *Gebser v. Lago Vista Independent School District* remains concerned with attribution and liability at an established recipient; its grounds and coalition limits do not decide these alternative coverage theories.
- *Foman v. Davis* governs ordinary amendment discretion; dismissal alone does not moot the request, and no amendment is guaranteed.
- *Steel Co.* preserves the distinction between Article III jurisdiction and the statutory recipient element.

#### Law After Decision

Title IX reaches actual recipients through direct or indirect delivery of assistance; ordinary payments from assisted institutions do not alone extend coverage to their associations. Institution-wide coverage under §1687 begins with receipt somewhere in the identified entity. The NCAA is neither automatically covered nor categorically exempt. Its possible NYSP assistance, independently sufficient delegated control, properly preserved pleadings and any ultimate liability remain for their own adjudication.

#### Source Notes

The Third Circuit's opinion, 139 F.3d 180, supplies the pleading history, dues rationale, separate view of the original pro se pleading and mandate. The parties' filings supply the disputed alternative theories; they do not establish the NYSP funding mechanics or legally sufficient delegated control. The governing texts are 20 U.S.C. §§1681 and 1687 and 34 C.F.R. §106.2. The statutory exceptions unrelated to the recipient question are not adjudicated. The decision rests on the dues theory and does not determine the merits of Smith's discrimination allegations.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Third Circuit must proceed on the basis that ordinary membership dues alone are insufficient. Further proceedings may address properly preserved alternative coverage and amendment questions under the applicable procedure. No particular alternative, amendment, monetary award or prospective relief is directed. The antitrust disposition remains intact.

**End of entry: National Collegiate Athletic Association v. Smith, merits decision, February 23, 1999.**

### Your Home Visiting Nurse Services, Inc. v. Shalala, No. 97-1489

Merits decision, February 23, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 2, 1998.

On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit.

Your Home disputed $48,890 in owners' compensation on four agencies' 1989 Medicare cost reports. After the intermediary issued reimbursement notices on March 29, 1991, the provider did not take the statutory appeal within 180 days. On March 28, 1994, it requested reopening based on salary-comparison material it described as newly discovered. The intermediary refused on April 21, concluding the request did not establish a qualifying error, inconsistency or new material evidence. The Provider Reimbursement Review Board dismissed the ensuing appeal for want of jurisdiction. The district court dismissed, and the Sixth Circuit affirmed.

The questions concern Board jurisdiction over that refusal and the asserted federal-question, Administrative Procedure Act and mandamus routes. Review of the Board's jurisdictional conclusion is distinct from jurisdiction to review the underlying reopening refusal or calculate reimbursement. The provider exhausted the available routes concerning that refusal; it did not timely appeal the original amount determination. The claimed compensation error remains unadjudicated.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

All nine support denial of the Board, federal-question, independent-APA and mandamus routes presented. The Court orders no reimbursement, reopened report, Board merits hearing or additional remand. Agreement with that disposition does not establish agreement with every supporting rationale.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer in full; Stone-Zsela in the limited portions stated here | Supports the whole affirmance | The eight Associate Justices adopt Parts I through V. Stone-Zsela joins I-A's statutory ground and V's disposition and reservations, but not I-B's regulatory-deference rationale or the grounds in II through IV. |

Stone-Zsela agrees only in the judgment on the federal-question, APA and mandamus components in Parts II through IV. No separate opinion is filed.

#### Holdings

##### I. Refusal to reopen is not a new appealable reimbursement determination

**Holding and operative rule:** A fiscal intermediary's discretionary refusal to reopen a final reimbursement determination is not a new final determination as to the amount of total program reimbursement under 42 U.S.C. §1395oo(a)(1)(A)(i). Judicial review of the Board's final jurisdictional ruling under §1395oo(f)(1) permits deciding that question; it does not give the Board jurisdiction over the refusal or authorize direct judicial calculation of reimbursement.

**Authority:** Part I-A of Kennedy's opinion is joined by all eight other Justices. Part I-B's additional regulatory construction and deference ground is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; Stone-Zsela does not join that ground. Each stated rationale commands a majority at that scope.

**Controlling explanation — I-A, statutory ground:** The Court holds that the refusal leaves the earlier amount determination in place. Congress supplied a 180-day appeal period; the regulation supplies a distinct, ordinarily three-year opportunity to request reopening. Treating every refusal as a new amount determination would allow a provider to renew the statutory appeal simply by requesting reconsideration. The provider's contrary argument—that refusal finally leaves payment unchanged—confuses the effect of preserving an earlier decision with a new decision about its amount. The Board's authority under §1395oo(d) over matters in a cost report operates once the hearing conditions are met; it does not remove them. The credibility of the asserted cost error supplies no missing jurisdiction.

**Controlling explanation — I-B, regulatory support:** Section 405.1885(c) gives reopening jurisdiction exclusively to the body that last decided the matter; §405.1889 separately recognizes review when actual reopening produces a revision in the reimbursement amount. The Secretary's considered reading respects that distinction. *Auer* requires a fair and considered regulatory interpretation consistent with the text, and *Thomas Jefferson University* applies that bounded deference to Medicare regulations. The manual explains this arrangement rather than creating jurisdiction or independently amending law, consistently with *Guernsey*. The interpretation leaves the regulation's mandatory reopening commands intact. An agency's jurisdictional label is not conclusive, and deference does not replace the statutory analysis.

##### II. The claim cannot proceed directly under federal-question jurisdiction

**Holding and operative rule:** Incorporated §405(h) bars the asserted 28 U.S.C. §1331 route because this request seeks reopening for additional Medicare reimbursement and derives its standing and substantive basis from the Medicare scheme. Describing the requested order as procedural does not remove that claim from the statute.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part II, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, commands eight votes. Stone-Zsela agrees only in the judgment on this component. This same eight-Justice coalition controls Holdings III and IV.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the requested reopening is a means of obtaining payment under Medicare. *Heckler v. Ringer* supplies the arising-under inquiry and does not allow a claimant to escape statutory channeling by requesting a preliminary procedural step. *Michigan Academy* recognizes a presumption of review in its different Part B setting; it does not promise a new district-court route whenever the claimant has not used an original statutory appeal. This scheme offered an original Board appeal followed by judicial review. The missed deadline matters to that arrangement, but it is not treated as nonexhaustion of the separate, later refusal claim. No distinct colorable constitutional claim is presented, and no independently authorized constitutional channel is foreclosed.

##### III. The APA supplies no independent jurisdiction

**Holding and operative rule:** The Administrative Procedure Act does not independently confer subject-matter jurisdiction over this refusal to reopen. Its review standards do not supply jurisdiction missing from the asserted statutory routes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that *Califano v. Sanders*, which rejects the APA as an independent jurisdictional grant, governs. The provider expressly asks the Court to reconsider that rule based on Medicare's corrective-adjustment command. That command regulates lawful reimbursement; it does not amend the APA's jurisdictional character or create an exception when §1331 is barred. Section 1395oo(f)'s use of APA standards in an authorized action likewise does not establish that every proposed action is authorized. The Court neither decides the reimbursement claim nor adopts a separate factual conclusion about the salary evidence. No distinct colorable constitutional claim is presented; the treatment of such a claim is not decided away.

##### IV. No clear duty to reopen these determinations supports mandamus

**Holding and operative rule:** Assuming without deciding that incorporated §405(h) does not universally preclude mandamus under 28 U.S.C. §1361, mandamus fails because no clear nondiscretionary duty to reopen these final determinations is established. The denial rests on the absence of that duty, not on failure to exhaust the available avenues concerning the refusal.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that lawful reimbursement duties do not automatically require reopening closed reports. *Ringer* requires a clear nondiscretionary duty and exhaustion of other avenues. The provider exhausted the refusal claim. The reasonable-cost and comparable-compensation requirements govern reimbursement; *Good Samaritan* places corrective adjustments within authorized cost methods, and *Regions Hospital* distinguishes correction of historical inputs for future payments from reopening final annual payments. Neither supplies the requested duty.

Section 405.1885(a) permits reopening on a timely motion and ordinarily requires a request within three years of notice; reopening afterward is barred except under paragraphs (d) and (e). New evidence or alleged error alone does not command reopening. Paragraph (b) requires reopening and revision when HCFA, within those three years, notifies the intermediary that its determination or hearing decision is inconsistent with applicable law, regulations, or general instructions issued by HCFA in accordance with the Secretary's agreement with the intermediary. Paragraph (d) requires reopening and revision at any time when a party procured the determination or decision by fraud or similar fault. Neither trigger is established. A provider's allegation is not HCFA notification or proof of fraud.

Paragraph (e) applies paragraphs (a) and (b) to periods ending on or after December 31, 1971, and the three-year limit to earlier periods reopened after May 27, 1972; it changes nothing for 1989. Section 405.1887 requires written notice of reopening, an explanation when revision follows, and a reasonable opportunity for additional evidence or argument in the reopening. Section 405.1889 separately permits review of an actual post-reopening revision in reimbursement. Those provisions do not require granting every request. The manual's new-material-evidence, clear-error and inconsistency considerations cannot override the distinct regulatory commands. The compensation claim's possible accuracy therefore does not establish a clear present reopening duty.

##### V. The affirmance decides the presented routes and preserves their limits

**Holding and operative rule:** The Sixth Circuit's judgment is affirmed without a reimbursement award, reopening order, Board merits hearing or further remand. Timely initial appeals, actual reopening followed by a new determination, genuinely presented regulatory procedural violations and independently authorized constitutional channels retain their own requirements and are not decided here.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part V's stated disposition and reservations. The conclusions about the independent routes remain controlled by the distinct coalitions stated above.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds only that the routes presented do not authorize relief on this record. An established mandatory reopening trigger, actual amount revision or independently supported constitutional claim would present its own question under the lawful channel governing it; none receives automatic review or relief. The possible absence of the intermediary as a party supplies no independent dismissal ground. Nor do four constituent reports establish an unraised amount-in-controversy or grouping defect. The decision does not determine the proper compensation amount, compel payment of a claimed cost, or declare all reopening-related actions unreviewable.

#### Precedent Treatment

- *Califano v. Sanders* is followed: the APA supplies no independent subject-matter jurisdiction. Its treatment of genuine constitutional claims is not displaced.
- *Heckler v. Ringer* supplies the Medicare arising-under analysis and mandamus's clear-duty requirement. Its reservation about universal §405(h) preclusion of mandamus remains open.
- *Bowen v. Michigan Academy of Family Physicians* is distinguished: its review presumption does not create a new route around the original provider-appeal scheme.
- *Auer v. Robbins* and *Thomas Jefferson University v. Shalala* support the eight-Justice regulatory rationale within their text-consistency and considered-interpretation limits; neither makes agency labels conclusive.
- *Shalala v. Guernsey Memorial Hospital* preserves the distinction between a lawful manual explanation and an amendment of binding law.
- *Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala* is applied to keep corrective adjustments within authorized reimbursement methods; it supplies no general duty to reopen final cost reports.
- *Regions Hospital v. Shalala* is distinguished and preserved: correcting a historical input for future payments does not reopen a closed annual payment or impose a reciprocal reopening duty here.

#### Law After Decision

A bare discretionary reopening refusal does not renew the original Board appeal. The courts may review the Board's jurisdictional ruling without acquiring authority to review the underlying refusal or calculate reimbursement through that channel. These asserted federal-question, independent-APA and mandamus routes fail separately. Actual revisions after reopening, mandatory HCFA-notification and fraud-or-similar-fault triggers, properly presented procedural violations and independently authorized constitutional claims retain their distinct terms and channels; the decision promises no automatic remedy for them. The original reimbursement remains undisturbed without a decision approving its substantive amount.

#### Source Notes

The provider's briefs expressly present the APA and mandamus alternatives. The controlling period texts are 42 U.S.C. §§1395oo, 1395ii and 405(h), and 42 C.F.R. §§405.1885, 405.1887 and 405.1889. Manual instructions are explanatory material, not independent legislation. The record establishes neither HCFA notification under paragraph (b) nor fraud or similar fault under paragraph (d), and it does not adjudicate the correct reimbursement amount.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Sixth Circuit's judgment stands. The Board need not hear the reopening refusal, and no reimbursement, reopened report or further remand is ordered. The original amount determinations remain in place; their substantive correctness is not adjudicated. No further Supreme Court event is scheduled.

### Reno v. American-Arab Anti-Discrimination Committee, No. 97-1252

Merits decision, February 24, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 4, 1998.

On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 119 F.3d 1367 (1997).

The question is whether section 1252(g) withdraws jurisdiction over this selective-enforcement challenge to commencement and adjudication of deportation proceedings, including its application to pending cases and the claimed need for present constitutional review. There is no final deportation order. The Government seeks dissolution of preliminary district-court relief and use of the immigration review process.

Eight individuals remain operative claimants. Hamide and Shehadeh are permanent residents; the other six originally held temporary visas. The original advocacy-of-world-communism charges were dropped in April 1987. The Six faced status grounds, while the Two faced amended association and later material-support grounds. The January 5, 1994 injunction protected the Six; the April 1996 order denied its dissolution and separately granted the Two's renewed preliminary-injunction motion. The Ninth Circuit sustained jurisdiction and relief. The Six's refusal-to-dissolve posture differs from the Two's initial injunction.

The lower courts made preliminary findings of purposeful association-based selection, disparate treatment and present chill. Their specific-intent treatment and the Government's conduct and immigration objections remain disputed; those findings are not final deportability or constitutional merits adjudications. Barakat and Sharif later obtained temporary resident status and are no longer deportable on their original status grounds. No replacement charge or concrete new threat is established. Organizational standing had been rejected and supplies no operative claimant in this decision.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Holdings

##### I-A. The withdrawal reaches the named acts in these pending proceedings

**Holding and operative rule:** From April 1, 1997, section 1252(g) reaches earlier, pending and future proceedings, and its named-action withdrawal concerns claims arising from the Attorney General's identified decisions or actions to commence proceedings, adjudicate cases or execute removal orders, rather than every legal challenge to completed adjudication. This selection challenge attacks commencement and the requested restraint of adjudication; neither its constitutional label nor its possible attachment to a later final-order petition moves it outside the named actions, and continuing proceedings is not a fourth statutory act.

**Authority:** Souter's Part I-A, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls by eight direct joins. The same eight-Justice coalition controls Parts I-B, II and III-A below. Stone-Zsela joins no part of the opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies *Yang*, which distinguishes commencement of the new rule from the proceedings it reaches, and *Reyes-Hernandez*, which separates the named executive actions from specified legal challenges to completed adjudication. This suit seeks to restrain the former. Those decisions preserved the additional constitutional-access question; they did not create a general exemption for old proceedings or constitutional claims.

Corrected section 309(c)(1) generally saves the former procedure, subject to its exceptions. Paragraph (2) permits election of the new procedure only before the evidentiary hearing has begun and on at least thirty days' notice; paragraph (3) permits termination and reinitiation only before a final administrative decision. Paragraph (4)'s transitional review rules require a final exclusion or deportation order entered more than thirty days after September 30, 1996. No election or final order is established here. The permanent scheme, including section 1252(b)(9), cannot be imported wholesale.

As *Salazar-Haro* and *Kolster* explain, section 306(c)(2) protects an actual judgment or order entered under former section 106, as amended, against paragraph (1)'s invalidation or compelled reconsideration. It grants no jurisdiction, reopens no merits review and does not bar ordinary review of a judgment's correctness. These collateral injunctions are not automatically qualifying section 106 judgments. The express transitions therefore defeat a blanket pending-case exemption without resolving the distinct question in Part III-B.

##### I-B. The individual-relief exception does not grant jurisdiction

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1252(f)(1)'s exception for application of the designated provisions to an individual alien against whom proceedings have been initiated limits that subsection's prohibition on lower-court injunctive relief; it does not independently confer subject-matter jurisdiction. Section 1252(f)(2)'s clear-and-convincing-evidence condition concerns enjoining removal pursuant to a final order and does not govern this case, which presents no such order.

**Authority:** Souter's Part I-B; the eight-Justice coalition stated in Part I-A.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court rejects the Ninth Circuit's reliance on subsection (f) as a jurisdictional answer. An exception to a restriction on remedies does not establish the underlying power to hear a claim. The availability of individual relief may inform the surrounding statutory construction, but the original jurisdiction retained in Part III-B rests on section 1331 and the constitutional action against federal officers. Subsection (f)(2) addresses a different remedy at a different stage; its condition cannot be substituted for subsection (f)(1) or applied as though a final deportation order already existed. No categorical conclusion about every possible injunction application is necessary.

##### II. The proposed factual transfer does not make this final-order channel adequate

**Holding and operative rule:** Former section 1105a(a)'s incorporation of chapter 158 for final-deportation-order review does not authorize the proposed district-court development of this selection claim's necessary extra-record evidence: paragraph (4)'s administrative-record restriction, subject to the nationality-transfer exception in (5)(B), and section 2347's hearing conditions must both be satisfied. A required deportation hearing cannot become an action taken without a required hearing merely by treating the earlier charging decision as the action reviewed while using the final order as the jurisdictional trigger.

**Authority:** Souter's Part II; the eight-Justice coalition stated in Part I-A.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the proposed route lacks the statutory capability attributed to it. Section 2347(a) uses the agency record when a hearing was held. Subsection (b) first requires that no hearing preceded the action reviewed. It then requires agency remand if a hearing was legally required; decision on pleadings and affidavits if none was required and no genuine material factual dispute exists; or district-court transfer if none was required and such a dispute exists. These conditions are not a general permission to develop any missing record.

The final deportation order follows a required hearing. The immigration judges and Board cannot adjudicate selection, but their inability does not erase the separate record-only command. The express nationality mechanism confirms that a general extra-record transfer cannot simply be assumed. Nor can the developed district-court record be imported merely because it exists.

Subsection (c) separately permits additional agency evidence upon materiality and reasonable grounds for the earlier omission. Any permanent or transitional prohibition of that subsection requires its own trigger; excluding (c) does not make (b)(3) available. The parties' accounts of a deleted Senate proposal are not adopted as legislative facts. The Government's proposed screening, explanation and material-dispute procedure, Rule 48, or a comparable future procedure cannot create missing authority. Its advocacy is neither a jurisdiction-conferring stipulation nor a concession that every selection claim constitutionally requires a forum.

This ruling concerns the necessary selection evidence, not every constitutional legal argument cognizable on a proper administrative record or a future constitutional remedy. *Kolster* preserves conditional section 2241 authority against AEDPA section 440(a); it does not decide subsection (g), establish custody, respondent, forum, exhaustion, filing, factual-review scope, stay or relief here. No new discovery or trial is required merely because the present statutory mechanism fails.

##### III-A. Access, statutory defenses, constitutional merits and interim protection are distinct

**Holding and operative rule:** A substantial constitutional selection claim cannot be rejected merely because the actual immigration charges may be lawful or a statutory defense may avert deportation; those questions do not necessarily adjudicate selection, permit its necessary proof or repair an independently supported present injury. The developed preliminary findings make this access contention substantial, but establish neither ultimate discrimination, a general right to immediate review nor an automatic entitlement to stop the proceedings.

**Authority:** Souter's Part III-A; the eight-Justice coalition stated in Part I-A.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court distinguishes a forum from success there. *Thunder Basin* permits constitutional claims to follow an administrative route when actual judicial review is meaningful, despite the agency's inability finally to decide constitutional law. *McNary* identifies an unavailable factual record as a real barrier; its separate surrender-to-obtain-review obstacle differs from proceedings already pending here. *Catholic Social Services* requires an actual obstacle, not a collateral label. *AT&T v. Iowa Utilities Board* concerned an announcement imposing no present conduct constraint and leaving concrete statutory review; this case presents instituted proceedings, preliminary injury findings and an identified factual-review barrier. *Your Home* presented no distinct colorable constitutional claim and does not decide this access question. *Yoshina* separates a substantial maintained claim from its ultimate failure, without supplying any individual's surviving controversy here.

Ordinary defense costs, anxiety and undifferentiated chill do not justify stopping adjudication. *Hollywood Motor Car* distinguishes a dismissal remedy from a right never to undergo proceedings, but already had a trial court capable of hearing the constitutional motion. *Younger* and *Dombrowski* distinguish good-faith process from sufficiently supported continuing suppression; their state-federal comity rationale does not independently bar this federal action. *Oestereich*'s unequivocal exemption is absent; its broader timing concurrence is not controlling.

The preliminary association-based selection, disparate-treatment and present-chill findings are more than pleadings, but do not resolve the disputed conduct and immigration arguments. *Whren* separates objective grounds from forbidden selection; *Dawson* distinguishes abstract association from relevant evidence; *Armstrong*'s indirect racial-selection discovery rule does not decide this developed association claim's complete merits. *R.A.V.* controls on overbreadth, not its separate content-selection proposal. A successful statutory defense may leave no adverse final order without deciding retaliation; that possibility proves neither a future controversy nor entitlement to an advisory declaration. The selection merits, universal citizen-alien equivalence and immigration immunity remain undecided.

##### III-B. A confined construction preserves a capable constitutional forum

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1252(g) does not foreclose existing section 1331 jurisdiction over a substantial constitutional selection claim when a claimant has a concrete personal controversy, the actual immigration process cannot adjudicate that claim, and the applicable judicial-review provisions cannot receive the factual record necessary to decide it. A constitutional or collateral label, disagreement with enforcement discretion, or preference for earlier review is insufficient; this retained access does not itself entitle the claimant to an injunction.

**Authority:** Souter's Part III-B, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls by five direct joins. The same five-Justice coalition controls Part IV. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas reject this construction; Stone-Zsela also rejects present collateral jurisdiction. No narrowest-ground combination of the competing constructions is involved.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress has not unmistakably eliminated every capable forum for this substantial constitutional claim. *Webster* requires clear intent before reading a statutory restriction to exclude consideration of colorable constitutional objections. *Lincoln* preserves constitutional review separately from discretionary statutory administration. The actual-barrier reasoning of *McNary* and *Catholic Social Services*, and *Kolster*'s requirement that a retained route reach the substantial authority objection and effective relief, reinforce that distinction without automatically extending their different statutory holdings.

The contrary textual argument is substantial: this suit attacks the very actions subsection (g) names, and its notwithstanding clause and reference to any cause or claim are broad. The Court does not redescribe selection as a completed-adjudication claim. It construes the withdrawal narrowly only at the distinct point of otherwise unavailable meaningful constitutional adjudication. The statute's treatment of constitutional questions elsewhere and contemplated individual relief supply context, not jurisdiction or wholesale applicability of the permanent scheme. Section 1331 and the existing constitutional action against federal officers supply the original basis.

Here the agency cannot adjudicate selection, the applicable judicial record limitation excludes its necessary factual record, and the proposed transfer does not cure that defect. The preliminary findings of present association-based coercion make postponing even access to the only competent forum inappropriate merely because a statutory defense might succeed. The Court does not adopt every lower merits ground or treat every First Amendment injury as automatically irreparable. *Reyes-Hernandez* retains its named-action boundary; this decision answers its reserved constitutional-access issue at the stated narrow scope. Ordinary channeling remains where a capable route exists. Whether the Constitution would itself require the same access absent a permissible construction, or where an adequate later route actually exists, remains open.

##### IV. Retained jurisdiction requires separate reconsideration of each injunction

**Holding and operative rule:** The confined jurisdictional result is affirmed, but the appellate judgment's unqualified preservation of preliminary relief is set aside for individualized reconsideration of continuing controversy, entitlement and scope; the existing District Court orders remain temporarily effective, without enlargement, pending lower-court action on remand. The Six's refusal-to-dissolve posture and the Two's later initial injunction must be analyzed separately, and individual relief must be modified or dissolved if its continuing requirements are absent.

**Authority:** Souter's Part IV; the five-Justice coalition stated in Part III-B.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court preserves the existing orders during reconsideration because constitutional jurisdiction remains and the record contains preliminary findings of present injury. This is a bounded interim consequence, not final approval of those findings or automatic relief from a jurisdictional label. *Webster* preserves ordinary equitable limits; *Catholic Social Services* requires relief fitted to concrete claimants and barriers.

The January 5, 1994 order protects the Six against further deportation proceedings. The Government's later resubmission of already available evidence does not alone reopen every preliminary issue as an initial application. But new statutory law, status changes, continuing controversy, actual ongoing injury and the need to restrain proceedings rather than afford narrower protection require consideration. The April 1996 order separately grants the Two's renewed initial application; their own showing, disputed conduct and immigration grounds must support that relief. The Six's modification posture cannot supply the Two's missing predicates.

Hamide and Shehadeh are permanent residents facing association/material-support charges; the Six's original grounds concern status violations. Barakat and Sharif obtained temporary resident status and cannot be deported on their original status grounds. That does not establish permanent residence, universal immunity or a new charge. Respondents' view that the injunction protects against new charges and their allegation of no governmental disavowal establish neither a particular charge nor an imminent individual threat. The lower courts must identify any actual surviving controversy; temporary preservation supplies no adjudicated protection against hypothetical charges.

The remand permits narrowing, modification or dissolution on the supported record. It does not require new discovery, a hearing, permanent protection, a fixed deadline, a removal order or a scheduled return here. Neither statutory deportability, specific intent, final discrimination nor a universal equivalence of citizen and alien rights is decided.

#### Law After Decision

Effective February 24, 1999, the named-action withdrawal and old-proceeding transitions remain in force. They now operate with the confined section 1331 construction for a concrete, substantial constitutional selection claim whose necessary factual adjudication is unavailable in the actual statutory channel. Agency inability alone, a constitutional label and a preference for immediate review remain insufficient. The proposed section 2347 route cannot supply this missing factual review under the applicable hearing and record provisions; the separate availability of constitutional legal arguments on a proper agency record is left intact.

Neither subsection (g)'s constitutionality nor the selection merits is decided. The two existing injunctions survive only provisionally and without enlargement while the lower courts address their distinct procedural and individual requirements. No habeas rule is added, no immigration status is granted, and no valid substantive deportation ground is immunized. The competing limited constitutional-exclusion inquiry and dismissal position are noncontrolling; neither enlarges the Court's construction.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Kennedy and Thomas.** They join Parts I, II and III-A: the actual transition and named-action rules, subsection (f)'s remedial limits, rejection of the proposed statutory factual-transfer mechanism, and the separation of substantiality, selection merits and interim relief. They reject Part III-B because subsection (g) expressly reaches the action challenged. *Webster* permits choosing among lawful constructions, not removing an expressly covered class merely because access would otherwise fail. *McNary* and *Catholic Social Services* construed narrower statutory objects.

They would vacate the judgment sustaining original selection-claim jurisdiction and set aside both existing injunctions. The Six's refusal-to-dissolve posture cannot insulate an injunction from a newly operative jurisdictional withdrawal, and the Two's initial order needs jurisdiction independently. This does not declare the preliminary findings false or direct removal.

They would nevertheless retain the already presented substantial contention that applying the review exclusion here is unconstitutional and therefore ineffective. Section 1331 permits that limited present inquiry in this case. Scalia and Thomas's *Thunder Basin* formulation and the three Justices' *Kolster* reservations support confronting the actual exclusion question instead of certifying a promised future remedy. Kennedy joins this confined rule, which does not announce a universal formula for every asserted exclusion. Jurisdiction to decide exclusion's constitutional effectiveness is distinct from jurisdiction over the underlying selection merits.

The remand would examine the missing factual route, timing, actual injury and asserted need for a forum, together with individual continuing controversy. An incidental interim request could be considered only upon an established source and the required equitable showing; the existing orders do not automatically convert into such relief. They decide neither that exclusion is constitutional nor that every alien must await a final order. They would not dismiss the entire action while this identified question remains unanswered.

**Stone-Zsela, dissenting.** He would vacate the injunctions and remand with instructions to dismiss the collateral action for want of jurisdiction. Section 1252(g) applies to the pending action and reaches its effort to restrain commencement and adjudication. It remains confined to the three named executive acts; detention, evidence gathering, adjudicative procedure and legal errors do not fall within it merely because they occur during removal. Applying that channel changes forum and timing rather than imposing new liability.

In his assessment, this record establishes neither permanent loss of meaningful review nor an independently irremediable injury requiring immediate freestanding adjudication. The constitutional theory can be preserved while the statutory proceedings continue. He does not adopt an identified section 2347 mechanism, pronounce an unfiled habeas proceeding adequate or erase the lower courts' preliminary findings. His conclusion is that those materials do not establish the constitutional necessity of this present collateral forum. He therefore also rejects the three-Justice proposal to retain the separate exclusion challenge now. Whether the objection may later be heard on final-order review or through another constitutionally indispensable channel on materially different facts remains open. He reaches no retaliation merits, approves no elimination of every adequate review route and adds no removal direction.
