## Event

Maine Green Party v. Maine, First Circuit No. 98-1309. Merits decision, May 20, 1999, on review of the First Circuit's March 2, 1999 judgment, 173 F.3d 1, affirming summary judgment for Maine. The question concerns the five-percent presidential-vote condition for retaining statewide party recognition and the asserted right to organize locally. ## Holdings

### A refined legal argument does not itself forfeit the original associational claim

**Holding and operative rule:** A party that initially challenged the presidential-vote condition and asserted its right to choose its organizational structure does not lose that legal organizational-autonomy objection merely by sharpening it later. That conclusion neither proves an exclusive local philosophy nor permits new summary-judgment facts to be assumed; the national-organizing affidavit and the factual basis of the as-applied theory retain their ordinary evidentiary significance.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, controls this proposition. Breyer, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, would enforce the presentation limit and affirm.

**Controlling explanation:** Yee permits a different legal argument supporting a properly presented federal claim while preserving distinct limits on facts and the question presented. Here the original challenge identified the same presidential-vote condition and the asserted freedom to organize as the members chose. The later legal formulation therefore is not wholly new merely because it gives that associational objection greater precision. The contrary position correctly identifies a possible difference between inability to build a national party and a philosophical refusal to do so. That difference prevents a court from assuming the asserted philosophy; it does not itself erase the originally presented legal objection. Thomas v. Arn permits reasonable, nonjurisdictional presentation rules, but its failure-to-object setting does not automatically decide an argument raised in objections. The district court's silence establishes neither a new fact nor an express waiver ruling. This conclusion makes no factual finding about exclusive-local intent and resolves no constitutional merits.

### The preserved associational claim returns for ordinary examination

**Holding and operative rule:** Because the First Circuit affirmed on forfeiture without deciding the preserved organizational-autonomy objection, its judgment is vacated and the case remanded for ordinary consideration of that claim's actual factual basis, the electoral burdens on the party, Maine's precise interests and the feasible alternatives within sections 301, 302 and 303. The Court finds no exclusive-local intent, neither sustains nor invalidates the retention condition, and orders no recognition, injunction, new election, permanent party status or public funding.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, controls the judgment and remand. Stone-Zsela's separate measure-of-support view has no other vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The First Circuit majority rested its affirmance on forfeiture and did not resolve the merits; Campbell would have affirmed on the merits, but that ground did not decide the judgment below. With the legal objection preserved, Anderson and Burdick require attention to the actual associational burden, the precise interests Maine advances and the fit between them. Norman confirms that support requirements are measured within the electoral system they actually govern. This record does not establish the cumulative cost of requalification, the timing of the available routes or the party's actual organizing aims, and nominal statutory access does not by itself establish its real burden. Maine's routes are distinct: section 301 governs retention, section 302 permits formation around a consenting qualifying nonparty candidate, and section 303 provides petition qualification measured by the last gubernatorial vote. Timmons's fusion-nomination holding and Reform Party's support-sensitive funding rule do not decide this retention condition. ## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring:** He joins the Court's opinion in full. The initial organizational-autonomy argument and Eu and Tashjian citations preserve the legal objection, while the affidavit prevents an unsupported finding of an exclusively local philosophy. Statewide administrative privileges may depend on demonstrated statewide support, and Maine may require that support to be renewed. Presidential results may be one available measure of that support, but they cannot be the exclusive price of recognition when a party can otherwise show relevant statewide support. Burdick looks to whether a remaining route is effective, not merely nominal, and Norman measures support within each electorate without letting a shortfall elsewhere cancel support shown where it is required. Section 301 is not invalid merely because it counts presidential results. Whether the alternative routes give a party with genuine statewide support a workable means of showing it, so that presidential performance is not in practice the only measure, is for the remand; the party's 1994 qualification through a nonparty gubernatorial candidate shows that section 302 has operated, but the record does not establish the present burden of either route. This is not automatic deference to the statewide label, a duty to nominate a presidential candidate, a right to recognition on local success alone, permanent party status or public funding.

The routes remain distinct: section 301 governs retention, with caucus, convention and certification conditions; section 302 permits formation through a consenting qualifying nonparty candidate, together with enrollment, caucuses in all sixteen counties and a convention; section 303 provides petition qualification. Its approved form, verified and certified signatures and legal addresses equal to five percent of the last gubernatorial vote, and filing before 5 p.m. on the 180th day before a primary are not another presidential-vote condition. Section 302's March 20 caucus-notice date is separate. None receives blanket constitutional approval.

**Breyer, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, dissenting:** These Justices would affirm because an as-applied case based on practical difficulty building a national organization differs materially from one based on an exclusive philosophical commitment to local politics. Yee protects new legal arguments, not assumed new facts. The affidavit describing national organization efforts makes the change consequential. The State's undeveloped objection and the district court's unexplained adoption do not prevent enforcement of ordinary presentation requirements. Arn supports reasonable nonjurisdictional management but does not make every omitted theory jurisdictionally barred. They reach no constitutional merits, approve no universal presidential-support proxy and find no alternative route necessarily adequate.
