##### L1: reverse the exclusive-eligibility plan on the preserved legal question

The opportunity reserved to old holders to acquire the reorganized debtor is property received under the plan because they hold the old interest. When the impaired senior unsecured class is not paid in full and objects, section 1129(b)(2)(B)(ii) forbids that preferred allocation. The proposition does not depend on proving that the Bankruptcy Judge undervalued the real property or misstated the contribution's present value.

## Holdings

### Old equity cannot reserve this valuable investment opportunity because of its former ownership

**Holding and operative rule:** When an impaired senior unsecured class objects and is not paid in full, §1129(b)(2)(B)(ii) forbids a plan from reserving to old equity alone the opportunity to contribute new capital and acquire reorganized ownership where old ownership supplies that exclusive eligibility. The opportunity is property received under the plan on account of the old interest, even when the proposed investment purchases new ownership and the underlying valuations are unchallenged.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, eight votes for the complete rule, application, reservations and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court identifies the right the plan distributes. New money explains payment for new ownership; it does not explain why only former owners receive the chance to acquire it. Ahlers recognizes economic ownership rights and confines equitable treatment to the Code. The promised $6.125 million over five years, worth about $4.1 million presently, does not erase the separate eligibility preference. The Court finds no numerical valuation error and presumes no higher outside bid. Section 1121 governs proposal exclusivity, while §1129 governs a resulting plan's substantive acceptability. The lawful, unappealed proposal period does not independently make an ownership preference confirmable. Noland and Reorganized CF&I preserve actual statutory priorities and exceptions; Nobelman illustrates why valuation and the legal treatment of rights are different inquiries, without deciding this Chapter 11 question itself. Stevens's contrary construction gives decisive weight to the new consideration and accepted confirmation findings. Those facts do not answer why old ownership alone confers the opportunity. The preserved priority objection therefore succeeds without reopening the unappealed procedural order or claim classification. The Court reserves whether a general new-value corollary survives and its complete conditions. It prescribes no auction, bidder, automatic termination of exclusivity or universal valuation method, and approves no future plan. The same exclusive allocation cannot sustain this confirmation; a changed proposal or process must satisfy the Code through lawful further proceedings.

## Precedent Treatment

- Norwest Bank Worthington v. Ahlers recognizes economic ownership rights and limits equity to the Code; its reservation concerning actual new capital remains open.
- Case v. Los Angeles Lumber Products identifies the historical new-capital distinction; the Court neither recognizes nor rejects every modern new-value plan.
- United States v. Noland and United States v. Reorganized CF&I Fabricators preserve statutory ranking and genuine exceptions rather than judicially preferred priorities.
- Nobelman v. American Savings Bank distinguishes valuation from the legal rights protected by statute; its Chapter 13 mortgage rule is not extended wholesale to this Chapter 11 dispute.

The exclusive old-equity investment opportunity defeats this plan under the absolute-priority rule. Accurate asset or contribution valuation and lawful proposal exclusivity do not cure that distinct statutory preference. General new-value survival, complete conditions and the confirmability of a different plan remain unresolved. No precedent is overruled.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the Court and would require genuine market exposure before old equity can receive this ownership opportunity in a later plan. Its value cannot be established merely by calling the cash contribution substantial. In his view termination of exclusivity, competitive bidding or another process must test whether the contribution reflects market value and the opportunity carries no residual old-interest preference, while satisfying all statutory requirements. He prescribes no single auction format and neither approves nor rejects every new-value plan. This additional market-testing instruction does not command the Court's majority.

Justice Stevens would affirm. Los Angeles Lumber's capital distinction and Ahlers's reservation support treating the new ownership as acquired for the new contribution. The property valuation and other confirmation findings are unchallenged, and the lawful proposal-exclusivity ruling is not before the Court. He does not regard the plan's investment eligibility as a separate distributed asset requiring another exchange or a compulsory competitive process. Noland and Reorganized CF&I demand fidelity to enacted conditions rather than an additional judicial condition. He acknowledges that exclusivity does not immunize every plan and that eligibility may have economic value; he rejects the Court's legal conclusion here without finding this opportunity valueless, treating the installment promise as immediate payment or approving every proposed new-value arrangement.
