## Holdings

### The preliminary-period authority controversy remains live

When a preliminary restraint is challenged as beyond the court's legal power during the period before judgment, later success on the underlying contract and conversion to a permanent injunction do not necessarily extinguish that distinct controversy. Here the asserted preliminary-period injury and existing bond preserve a consequential authority question, while actual bond liability and damages remain undecided.

**Authority:** Scalia's Part I is joined by all nine Justices and controls at this limited level of generality.

Camenisch distinguishes a surviving case from a surviving preliminary-injunction issue. The Court applies that distinction rather than treating the mere presence of a bond as conclusive. The investors' success on their contract may justify relief after judgment without establishing authority to restrain an unsecured debtor beforehand. GMD's acceptance of the permanent conversion therefore does not resolve whether the earlier restraint was legally authorized. A ruling on that distinct question can matter to the asserted loss during the preliminary period. This is not review of the final money judgment or an adjudication of the separate turnover appeal. Whether a proper bond claim ultimately proves wrongful restraint, causal loss and an amount, and whether independent defenses defeat it, remains for the appropriate proceedings. No universal rule preserves every appeal involving an expired injunction and a bond.

### General equity does not supply this unsecured-creditor asset freeze

A federal court may not use general equitable authority, at the request of a general unsecured creditor suing solely for money damages, to freeze a defendant's unencumbered assets before judgment when the creditor has no lien, equitable interest, statutory authorization or other recognized source of prejudgment control. Likely success and threatened inability to collect do not supply the missing power.

**Authority:** Scalia's Part II is joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas; five Justices adopt the complete rule, explanation and remedy.

The historically conferred equity jurisdiction permits flexibility within its sources and objects. It does not authorize creation of a security interest for an unadjudicated general debt. The judgment and proprietary prerequisites protect the debtor-creditor relationship; merger of law and equity did not erase them. Rule 65 regulates injunction procedure, and Rule 64 preserves authorized prejudgment security routes. Neither makes the inadequacy of collection itself a new source of power. The bondholders sought to secure payment from assets that were not the subject of their contract claim. The strong findings favoring them therefore address the usefulness of a restraint, not its legal availability.

Deckert involved equitable rescission and preservation in that setting. First National City Bank involved federal tax collection and statutory authority. Their preservation reasoning does not create the omitted private-creditor predicate. De Beers confirms that preliminary relief must remain within recognized equitable authority related to the available final remedy. Expanding general equity here would supply security and affect other creditors outside enacted attachment and bankruptcy arrangements. The Court does not restrict injunctions protecting specific property in dispute, statutory freezes, independently authorized fraudulent-transfer remedies, bankruptcy orders, Rule 64 attachment or postjudgment enforcement. Nor does it decide bond recovery, create a lien, establish fraud or adjudicate the rights of absent Mexican creditors.

- De Beers Consolidated Mines v. United States: applies its limit connecting preliminary relief to recognized authority and the permissible final remedy; it supports rejection of this unsecured-creditor freeze.
- Deckert v. Independence Shares Corp.: distinguishes its equitable rescission setting; its preservation authority remains intact within that setting.
- United States v. First National City Bank: distinguishes its federal-tax and statutory predicates; personal jurisdiction and preservation language do not alone establish private unsecured-creditor power.
- Mertens v. Hewitt Associates: preserves its statutory equitable-category rule; it does not itself govern every private injunction or prohibit all equitable monetary remedies.
- Varity and Peacock: preserve their ERISA rules concerning appropriate individual equity and an independently violated obligation; neither supplies this private-creditor power.
- Department of the Army v. Blue Fox: preserves the distinction between an equitable label and an actual legal entitlement within its sovereign/APA setting.
- West v. Gibson: leaves intact the EEOC's specifically enacted compensation authority; it creates no general private-creditor injunction power.

## Law After Decision

General federal equity supplies no prejudgment security freeze on the stated unsecured, purely monetary claim without an independent recognized source of control. Equity's flexibility, legally authorized attachment and statutory or proprietary remedies retain their respective scopes. Postjudgment enforcement remains distinct. A surviving preliminary-period authority issue may remain reviewable despite later final relief, but neither a bond nor success on the contract automatically determines bond liability.

## Separate Writings

Ginsburg, joined by Stevens, Souter and Breyer, concurs in the live-controversy ruling and dissents from reversal. General equity's traditional preservation function, in their view, permits this provisional restraint given near-certain contractual recovery and the concrete threat that asset disposition and insolvency would defeat a judgment. They accept that the particular freeze was not historically granted but regard equitable function as adaptable without creating ownership or a lien. Deckert and First National City Bank support preservation by analogy despite their different predicates; De Beers does not foreclose relief directed to making the eventual money judgment effective. They would affirm this order without adopting the Second Circuit's entire preliminary-injunction test, allowing routine freezes for ordinary debts, treating necessity alone as authority, or resolving other creditors' rights and the separate final orders.
