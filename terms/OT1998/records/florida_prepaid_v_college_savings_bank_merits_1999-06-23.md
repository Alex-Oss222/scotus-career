**Section 5:** Section 5 authorizes Congress to prevent and remedy unconstitutional State deprivation of patent property through legislation congruent and proportional to that constitutional injury. Congress need not establish a fixed numerical count of prior violations, produce formal findings in a prescribed format, restrict every preventive measure to proven violators, or require each plaintiff to prove a constitutional tort. Patents are protected property, but infringement is not itself deprivation without due process. The Act imposes ordinary infringement liability and the full patent remedies on every State without differentiating infringement from unconstitutional deprivation, and patent-property status, exclusive federal infringement jurisdiction, and possible limitations on State remedies do not establish an adequately supported preventive relationship for that full liability and remedial exposure. National uniformity and equal competition are Article I objectives that do not themselves supply Section 5 power. ## Holdings

### Congress unmistakably authorized this federal patent action against States

**Operative proposition:** Sections 271(h) and 296 expressly subject States and their instrumentalities to private patent actions in federal court for covered violations on or after October 28, 1992. Section 296(b) makes the otherwise available patent remedies available on their own statutory terms; that authorization does not itself establish constitutional power, State consent, infringement, or entitlement to a particular award.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Part I, joined by all nine Justices, directly adopts this statutory proposition and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress did not leave State coverage to inference from a general reference to persons or to federal-question jurisdiction. Section 271(h) identifies States and instrumentalities as persons subject to the patent obligations. Section 296(a) expressly addresses immunity, private claimants, and the federal forum; subsection (b) separately identifies available remedies. Seminole distinguishes this enacted authorization from the constitutional source of amenability, and Welch does not make general statutory coverage sufficient consent to federal suit. These provisions satisfy the distinct clarity inquiry. They still require a valid basis for proceeding against the State and proof of every element and remedial condition. No infringement, compensable injury, enhancement, fee award, or injunction follows from the text alone.

### Patent property and unconstitutional deprivation remain distinct

**Operative proposition:** A patent is an established exclusive property interest; federal creation does not disqualify it from Fourteenth Amendment protection. Recognizing that property and alleging infringement do not alone establish deprivation without due process, compensable injury, or entitlement to an award; process, remedies, alleged conduct, and any applicable culpability requirement require separate treatment.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this precise distinction in Part I. No additional patent-negligence rule receives majority support; the §5 conclusion is stated in Part II.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 261 and the established patent-property authorities recognize an exclusive interest, rather than a mere expectation of continued competitive success. But a statutory infringement claim and a constitutional deprivation claim have different requirements. Available process and remedies cannot be assumed adequate or inadequate from the property's existence. Neither the allegation of knowing or willful infringement nor the request for damages adjudicates those additional matters. Whether Daniels's prison-negligence rule governs patent deprivations commands no majority, and Part II does not rest on it. This common distinction does not itself answer the §5 question, which Part II decides.

### Appropriate respondent alternatives are legally available within their limits

**Operative proposition:** A respondent may defend an unchanged favorable judgment on an appropriately supported alternative that neither enlarges its rights nor reduces the adversary's rights under that judgment. CSB's posture permits consideration of its preserved defenses of the immunity denial, but preservation and briefing alone do not expand the §5 question or establish an alternative's merits.

**Authority:** Part I has all nine Justices' joins at this precise level. Their actual decisions on the alternative grounds remain distinct.

**Controlling explanation:** X-Citement Video permits an appropriately supported defense of an unchanged favorable judgment, while Izumi, Madsen, and Lebron preserve the boundary between included legal grounds and independent questions. Murphy leaves its two presented but ungranted questions undecided, while preserving the respondent-defense route for an unchanged judgment. Kolstad's inclusion of attribution within punitive relief and Olmstead's inclusion of the statutory duty's fundamental-alteration limit illustrate why the actual question and legal relationship matter; neither automatically includes a different constitutional source. CSB seeks to retain the denial of immunity, not obtain expanded relief. That posture permits consideration of properly supported alternatives without compelling every Justice to decide each one or establishing factual predicates by assumption. No general Article I reach rule, automatic consent finding, or exception for any preserved issue follows.

### Section 5 does not sustain the Patent Remedy Act at the scope presented

**Operative proposition:** Section 5 authorizes Congress to prevent and remedy unconstitutional State deprivation of patent property through legislation congruent and proportional to that constitutional injury. Congress need not establish a fixed numerical count of prior violations, produce formal findings in a prescribed format, restrict every preventive measure to proven violators, or require each plaintiff to prove a constitutional tort. Nevertheless, a statute enforcing ordinary federal patent liability against States without differentiating infringement from unconstitutional deprivation must have an adequately supported preventive relationship to the constitutional wrong. For this enactment, patent-property status, federal patent jurisdiction, and possible limitations on State remedies do not sufficiently establish that the statute's full infringement liability and remedial exposure are a congruent and proportional response to unconstitutional State deprivations. The Patent Remedy Act does not independently qualify as Section 5 legislation at the scope presented. Congress is not forever barred: a differently supported or fitted measure remains possible.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas, states a holding of the Court. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer dissent from Part II.

**Controlling explanation:** Fitzpatrick establishes Congress's authority to subject States to suit when it enforces the Fourteenth Amendment. Boerne requires congruence and proportionality between the constitutional injury and the means adopted. Enforcement legislation may prevent as well as remedy, and it may reach some conduct that is not itself unconstitutional. Patents are protected property. The inquiry is structural and substantive. It asks whether the enacted liability is fitted to the constitutional wrong. It does not ask whether Congress assembled a numerical count of violations, made findings in a prescribed form, confined prevention to proven violators, or required each plaintiff to prove a constitutional tort.

The constitutional wrong is not infringement; it is deprivation of property without due process. Daniels, Parratt, Hudson, and Zinermon distinguish injury to property, the State's conduct, the process available, and unconstitutional deprivation. Sections 271(h) and 296 instead impose ordinary federal infringement liability, with the full patent remedies, on every State and every infringement, without differentiating infringement from unconstitutional deprivation. A measure of that reach needs an adequately supported preventive relationship to the constitutional wrong.

For this enactment, three circumstances are offered: patent-property status, exclusive federal patent jurisdiction, and possible limitations on State remedies. They show at most that a State's infringement can injure protected property and that State redress may be limited. They do not establish that the Act's full infringement liability and remedial exposure are a congruent and proportional response to unconstitutional State deprivations. National uniformity and equal competition are Article I objectives and do not supply Section 5 power.

The partial dissent relies on exclusive federal jurisdiction and uncertain State remedies. Those are the circumstances weighed here, and they leave the Act's undifferentiated reach unexplained as prevention. Boerne's rejection of a findings quota does not dispense with fit. The Act therefore does not qualify as Section 5 legislation at the scope presented. A differently supported or fitted measure remains open to Congress. Part II does not decide the judgment, which rests on Part III.

### Article I sustains the expressly authorized action

**Operative proposition:** Congress may authorize private enforcement against States when the underlying obligation and enforcement measure fall within its Article I authority and Congress unmistakably authorizes the State's exposure to suit. Congress possesses power under the Patent Clause and the Necessary and Proper Clause to impose the federal patent obligation on covered States and to authorize ordinary private federal enforcement when the authorization is unmistakably clear. Sections 271(h) and 296 satisfy the clarity requirement. The action may proceed without actual State consent. The availability of suit decides no validity, infringement, remedies, defenses, or amounts. This ground sustains the judgment.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Part III, joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, states the holding of the Court that decides the judgment. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas dissent from Part III.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress created a national patent system, concentrated infringement adjudication in federal court, and expressly included States in §§271(h) and 296. Uniform patent rights lose important content if a State may exploit a patented invention while withholding the ordinary federal remedy. The Patent Clause and Necessary and Proper Clause authorize enforcement of this valid federal obligation through the expressly enacted action.

Union Gas's retained five-vote constitutional conclusion supplies the Article I foundation. The Court extends that conclusion into the enforcement principle stated here without treating every proposition in its plurality rationale as binding. Seminole distinguished unmistakable statutory authorization from constitutional amenability and set aside the State-dismissal direction without a controlling constitutional rationale. It established neither a categorical Article I prohibition nor an overruling of Union Gas, and required no abrogation-before-officer sequence. The present holding resolves the constitutional question it left open.

The Eleventh Amendment limits the citizen-State diversity grants, not jurisdiction over this federal question. CSB's out-of-State citizenship does not change the claim's federal source. Hans does not establish an immunity that Congress cannot displace in this setting. The structural objection therefore cannot defeat an enforcement measure within Congress's enumerated authority and an unmistakable choice to permit suit.

Because Part II holds that Section 5 does not sustain the Act at the scope presented, this ground decides the judgment. X-Citement Video permits its consideration because CSB, the prevailing respondent, defends the same immunity-denial judgment without enlarging its relief. The holding is claim-specific: it removes immunity from this authorized action, finds no infringement or damages entitlement, and awards no relief. It does not depend on commercial participation constituting consent.

### Continued commercial participation does not supply consent to this suit

**Operative proposition:** A State's continued commercial participation after Congress authorizes suit does not constitute consent: Florida Prepaid did not consent to federal patent suit by continuing its prepaid-tuition program after §§271(h) and 296 took effect. Actual consent through a distinct State choice, such as an express waiver or voluntary invocation of federal jurisdiction, remains available. Whether Florida Prepaid's litigation conduct waived immunity need not be decided, and no waiver finding is made.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Part IV, joined by all nine Justices, applies College Savings Part III and states these limits.

**Controlling explanation:** College Savings Part III, announced earlier the same day, holds that a State's continued operation of an otherwise lawful commercial undertaking does not unequivocally submit it to federal jurisdiction merely because Congress clearly and prospectively subjects that activity to private federal enforcement, even when the undertaking is realistically optional. CSB's commercial-condition alternative rests on that inference: enacted notice, covered post-enactment activity, and Florida Prepaid's practical choice to keep marketing its competing tuition-prepayment program. Those circumstances establish Congress's intent, not Florida's voluntary submission. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer did not join College Savings Part III, but they accept it as controlling; no changed premise or special justification supports departing from it here.

Express waiver and voluntary invocation of federal jurisdiction remain distinct State choices that can supply actual consent; properly conditioned federal benefits and interstate compacts keep their own rules. CSB separately contends that Florida Prepaid waived immunity by appearing, seeking transfer under §1404(a), and requesting declarations of invalidity, noninfringement, and unenforceability. No Justice decides whether any of that conduct waived immunity, and no waiver finding is made.

### Limits and questions not reached

The Court makes no finding of patent validity, infringement, willfulness, Florida-remedy adequacy, constitutional injury, or entitlement to relief. Section 296(b) retains its own conditions and any properly presented award-specific constitutional limits. Neither the Article I holding nor the judgment preapproves any compensation, injunction, fee, or enhanced award. Kolstad's Title VII punishment rules do not determine patent enhancement. The Court reserves the adequacy of any Florida state-law remedy, on which the record lacks the facts, and, as outside the question presented, any federal constitutional damages cause, punitive or enhanced awards, and Section 5 for a different statute with a more closely demonstrated predicate. Whether Daniels's prison-negligence rule governs patent deprivations commands no majority: the four Justices who join Part II's negative conclusion use it in their fit analysis, the four who dissent from Part II reserve it, and Part II rests on the Act's failure to differentiate and states no negligence rule.

The appearance, transfer request under §1404(a), and request for affirmative declarations of invalidity, noninfringement, and unenforceability remain distinct litigation-consent contentions. The Court finds no waiver from any of them. Schacht does not settle their effect. No actual-violation exception, willfulness exception, State-remedy exhaustion, new constitutional damages cause, or Just Compensation Clause justification is adopted.

The action is not converted into an officer suit. Young and Coeur d'Alene require a connected officer, ongoing federal violation, proper cause, and genuinely prospective relief; Edelman and Pennhurst preserve retrospective-compensation and state-law boundaries. No Florida claims-bill or takings remedy is adjudged adequate or inadequate, and State Street supplies no invalidity finding about this different patent.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Union Gas:** Extends its retained five-vote Commerce-power constitutional conclusion into the Article I enforcement principle applied to the Patent Clause and Necessary and Proper Clause here. Its statutory holding and private third-party damages posture remain intact; its four-Justice complete rationale and absence of a Parden-waiver ground are not enlarged into historical majority holdings.
- **Seminole Tribe; Hans:** Seminole’s separation of explicit State-action authorization from constitutional amenability remains intact. Its set-aside of the State-dismissal direction established no controlling constitutional rationale, categorical Article I prohibition, abrogation-before-officer sequence, or overruling of Union Gas. The Court resolves the reserved power question here and limits Hans: its immunity does not prevent Congress from authorizing this federal-question action within the Article I enforcement principle.
- **Welch; Parden; Hilton:** Preserve the displacement of federal-forum consent inferred from general FELA/Jones Act coverage and Hilton's distinct specialized state-court FELA holding, including retained statutory construction and compensation reliance. Howlett's competent-state-court enforcement rule remains. Welch's fractured discussion did not adjudicate every later express prospective condition. Part IV does not reopen Hilton's federal action.
- **Alden v. Maine:** Announced earlier the same day, its holding that valid federal legislation may displace nontextual immunity from an expressly authorized private action in a competent state court is consistent with Part III. Alden supplies no federal-court amenability rule; Part III does not depend on it, and it enters no law here.
- **College Savings Bank:** Part IV applies its Part III holding, announced earlier the same day, that continued ordinary commerce is not unequivocal consent to federal suit, even under a clear prospective condition and a realistically optional undertaking. Its preservation of actual voluntary consent and its nonreach of litigation-waiver acts remain.
- **Fitzpatrick; Boerne:** Fitzpatrick’s Section 5 power to subject States to suit when enforcing the Fourteenth Amendment is preserved, and a differently supported or fitted patent measure remains possible. Boerne’s congruence-and-proportionality rule is applied to reject the Section 5 rationale for this Act at the scope presented: ordinary infringement liability imposed on every State without differentiating infringement from unconstitutional deprivation lacks an adequately supported preventive relationship to the constitutional wrong. Prevention may still reach some conduct that is not independently unconstitutional. The holding adds no numerical findings requirement, prescribed findings format, restriction to proven violators, per-plaintiff constitutional tort, geographic restriction, or sunset requirement.
- **Daniels; Parratt; Hudson; Zinermon:** Preserve the distinction between injury to property, the State's conduct, available process, and unconstitutional deprivation. Whether Daniels's negligence rule governs patent deprivations commands no majority. The Court adopts no wholesale patent-negligence rule, no automatic willfulness exception, and no finding about Florida remedies. Hartford-Empire and Consolidated Fruit-Jar support patent property without deciding every infringement's constitutional character.
- **X-Citement Video; Izumi; Madsen; Lebron; Murphy:** Apply the unchanged-judgment respondent-defense route with its actual scope limits; that route is how Part III reaches Article I. Preservation, briefing, priority, or usefulness alone does not enlarge review. Kolstad and Olmstead supply included-ground comparisons, not transferred immunity or patent-remedy rules.
- **Schacht:** Preserves claim-specific immunity and possible waiver while leaving distinct litigation-waiver acts unresolved; it decides none of the acts asserted here.
- **Hess; Lane; Cedar Rapids; West; Blue Fox:** Retain their separate predicates of actual entity status, federal remedy-specific consent, accepted funding obligations, express federal compensation, and enacted remedial authority. None already resolves this State's commercial consent or eliminates its admitted arm status.
- **Puerto Rico Aqueduct:** Applies immediate review of a conclusive immunity denial without treating appealability as immunity, consent, or infringement.
- **Young; Coeur d'Alene; Edelman; Pennhurst:** Preserve the connected-officer, ongoing-federal-violation, genuinely prospective-relief, retrospective-compensation, and state-law boundaries; no officer claim is created.
- **State Street:** Its determination of the complete claims in that case establishes no categorical business-method exclusion or adjudication of Patent No.4,722,055.
- **Reserved:** The adequacy of any Florida state-law remedy; any federal constitutional damages cause; punitive or enhanced awards; and Section 5 for a different statute with a more closely demonstrated predicate.

## Law After Decision

This immunity-denial judgment stands and the covered patent action proceeds to ordinary merits adjudication, without a new constitutional-tort prerequisite imposed by the mandate. The express federal-forum and remedy provisions satisfy the distinct statutory-clarity inquiry, and patent property remains distinct from unconstitutional deprivation.

Section 5 does not sustain the Patent Remedy Act at the scope presented. Under Boerne, a statute enforcing ordinary federal patent liability against States without differentiating infringement from unconstitutional deprivation needs an adequately supported preventive relationship to the constitutional wrong. Patent-property status, federal patent jurisdiction, and possible limits on State remedies do not establish that relationship for the Act's full liability. No numerical count, prescribed findings, restriction to proven violators, or plaintiff-by-plaintiff constitutional tort is required, and a differently supported or fitted measure remains possible.

Article I sustains the action. Lower courts apply the rule that Congress may authorize private enforcement against States when the underlying obligation and enforcement measure fall within its Article I authority and Congress unmistakably authorizes State exposure to suit. Union Gas’s constitutional conclusion is extended to that principle, and Hans supplies no immunity Congress cannot displace in this setting. Under Part IV, for all nine Justices, continued commercial participation after Congress authorizes suit is not consent, as College Savings Part III holds; actual consent through a distinct State choice, such as express waiver or voluntary invocation of federal jurisdiction, remains available, and whether Florida Prepaid's litigation conduct waived immunity remains undecided. Hilton’s state-forum holding and actual-waiver and officer-relief doctrines retain their distinct boundaries. The action proceeds without an actual-waiver adjudication as a prerequisite, and all merits and award-specific conditions remain for ordinary adjudication.

## Separate Writings

**Stevens, joined by Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, concurs in part and dissents in part, joining Parts I, III, and IV and the judgment and dissenting from Part II.** The four would hold that Section 5 also independently sustains this action. Fitzpatrick and Boerne permit Congress to protect existing patent property through preventive enforcement. Exclusive federal infringement jurisdiction, uncertain State remedies, the difficulty of proving purposeful appropriation, and completed loss that prospective relief cannot repair supply the substantive relationship to the threatened deprivation. The Act enforces an existing defined right rather than replacing a constitutional standard as RFRA did. Prevention may reach conduct that is not independently unconstitutional, and covering some innocent infringement does not by itself make the remedy disproportionate. National uniformity alone would not supply Section 5 power. The opinion finds no State remedy inadequate, reserves whether Daniels's negligence rule governs patent deprivations, and preapproves no award.

**Kennedy, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, and Thomas, concurs in part and dissents in part, joining Parts I, II, and IV and dissenting from Part III and the judgment.** On Part II, ordinary liability's reach to unknowing infringement and to States that furnish adequate process confirms the lack of fit. The dissent joins Part IV.

The dissent would vacate and remand for ordinary treatment of the preserved actual-submission contentions and any other properly available current-law ground before final immunity dismissal. Appearance, transfer, and affirmative relief are not interchangeable or already adjudicated consent. The dissent opposes the Court’s Article I extension: substantive Patent Clause authority and unmistakable statutory intent do not, on its structural view, establish power to impose a nonconsensual direct-State suit. All four would reject that power and overrule Union Gas if the question required decision; their shared limited remand leaves that overruling undecided. The surviving Union Gas conclusion cannot be silently displaced through the mandate; actual submission may resolve the case without that further constitutional decision. Fitzpatrick’s Section 5 power, actual consent, United States enforcement, and proper Young relief remain distinct. The dissent orders no discovery, hearing, new proof, amendment, or constitutional-infringement trial, and decides no patent merits.

## Procedure After Action

The Federal Circuit's immunity-denial judgment is affirmed on the Article I ground. The pending action returns for ordinary adjudication of validity, infringement, causation, defenses, and remedies for covered post-enactment conduct. The Court grants no substantive patent relief and requires no new constitutional-tort or actual-litigation-waiver adjudication as a condition of proceeding. Any award must satisfy the governing patent provisions and properly presented award-specific limits.
