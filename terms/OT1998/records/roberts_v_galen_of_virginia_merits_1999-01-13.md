## Controlling holdings

#### 1. Stabilization has no additional improper-motive element

**Holding and operative rule:** A claim under 42 U.S.C. §1395dd(b) does not require proof that a participating hospital acted with an improper financial or other invidious motive. Liability nevertheless requires proof of the enacted stabilization or transfer duty and the applicable civil-action predicates; removing the extra motive requirement establishes neither breach nor injury.

**Authority:** The per curiam opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt both holdings and their stated limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the stabilization provision means what its conditions say. When an individual comes to a hospital and the hospital determines that the individual has an emergency medical condition, subsection (b)(1) requires further examination and treatment within the available staff and facilities to stabilize the condition, or transfer in accordance with subsection (c). The text does not make that obligation depend on why the hospital acts. Subsection (a)'s separate requirement of an appropriate screening examination cannot insert an additional condition into subsection (b). National Organization for Women, Inc. v. Scheidler supplies the analogous principle that a court may not add a motive requirement while Congress's actual liability conditions remain intact. Concern that EMTALA should not become general federal malpractice law does not permit rewriting those conditions. Its determination, stabilization, transfer, injury and remedial requirements continue to distinguish a statutory violation from negligent care. Cleland's screening analysis therefore cannot sustain the additional motive element imposed on this stabilization claim. The Court does not decide whether subsection (a) contains a motive requirement, how long this hospital's duty continued, or whether the patient proves its violation.

**Operative qualifications retained:** Subsection (b)(2) deems the treatment requirement satisfied when the hospital offers the required examination and treatment, informs the patient or representative of their risks and benefits, and that person refuses; the hospital must take all reasonable steps to obtain written informed refusal. Subsection (b)(3) separately addresses refusal of a transfer offered in accordance with subsection (c), after risk-and-benefit information, with the same reasonable-efforts duty concerning written informed refusal. Neither provision is a finding about this patient.

For an unstabilized emergency condition, subsection (c)(1) separately requires either a written transfer request made by the individual or legally responsible representative after receiving information about the hospital's EMTALA duties and the risks of transfer, or a physician's signed certification, based on the information available when transfer occurs, that the expected medical benefits of appropriate treatment elsewhere outweigh the increased transfer risks, including risks to the unborn child in labor; it also requires an appropriate transfer. If no physician is physically present in the emergency department, a medical person qualified under the Secretary's regulations may sign after consultation with a physician who makes the required determination and later countersigns. The certification must summarize its supporting risks and benefits. Appropriate transfer requires risk-minimizing treatment within the transferring hospital's capacity; available receiving space, qualified personnel and acceptance with appropriate treatment; transmission of all emergency-condition records available at transfer, including the condition, observed signs or symptoms, preliminary diagnosis, treatment, test results and written request or certification, together with the name and address of an on-call physician who refused or did not appear within a reasonable time to provide necessary stabilization; qualified personnel and transportation equipment, including necessary and medically appropriate life support during transfer; and any further applicable regulatory conditions. None is declared satisfied or violated here.

Subsection (d)(2)(A) permits the person directly harmed by a participating hospital's violation to sue that hospital for personal-injury damages available under the law of its State and appropriate equitable relief. The separate limitation is two years after the violation. This decision creates no personal action against an individual physician and no automatic liability for later deterioration.

#### 2. The motive-based summary judgment must be reversed; other predicates remain open

**Holding and operative rule:** Summary judgment rejecting the stabilization claim because the guardian failed to establish improper motive rests on an element the statute does not contain and must be reversed. The hospital's actual-knowledge and completed-screening defenses remain open for lawful consideration on remand; neither is accepted or rejected on its merits by this decision.

**Authority:** The same unanimous per curiam opinion controls this confined reversal and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court of Appeals recognized a genuine dispute about stabilization but nevertheless sustained judgment because the evidence did not sufficiently connect financial pressure to the physician's discharge decision. Eliminating that erroneous element removes its ground for judgment. Evidence concerning the patient's infection, the safety of transfer before the antibiotic response and culture results could be assessed, and the possible role of later care does not establish the remaining statutory elements in either party's favor. The hospital argues that it lacked actual knowledge of an emergency condition and that proper screening ended its duty. Those arguments must be assessed on their own legal and factual predicates. The Court neither converts the stabilization dispute into an undisputed absence of knowledge nor holds that screening necessarily ends every stabilization obligation. The preserved ability of a respondent to defend a favorable judgment on a supported alternative does not supply missing findings. The separate state-law ostensible-agency ruling and other claims are not reopened. No liability judgment, damages award or mandatory trial is ordered.

**Questions not reached:** Whether an emergency medical condition existed, what the hospital determined or knew, duration of any duty, stabilization, authorized transfer, causal injury, damages and ultimate liability remain undecided. The treatment-refusal, transfer-refusal and transfer-certification provisions remain distinct; no exception is presumed.

## Precedent treatment and present force

- **National Organization for Women, Inc. v. Scheidler (January 24, 1994):** Applied by analogy for refusal to add an omitted motive element while preserving every enacted liability condition. Its RICO construction remains distinct from EMTALA.
- **Cleland v. Bronson Health Care Group, 917 F.2d 266 (6th Cir. 1990):** The extension of its screening-motivation analysis to impose an extra element under subsection (b) is rejected. The Court does not decide subsection (a)'s screening-motive question.
- **United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc. (November 29, 1994):** Its favorable-judgment defense rule is respected without treating an unestablished alternative factual premise as proved. Neither alternative defense is conclusively adjudicated here.

## Resulting law and continuity

EMTALA stabilization litigation proceeds without an additional improper-purpose element. The statutory determination, treatment or lawful-transfer conditions, direct personal harm, causation, permitted remedies and limitations period retain their independent force. Screening, stabilization and the separate state-law agency claim remain distinct. The judgment changes no general medical-negligence rule and awards no relief beyond reversal and remand of the appealed stabilization disposition.
