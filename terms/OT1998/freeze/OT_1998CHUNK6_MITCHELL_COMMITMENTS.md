## 2. Entering law and independent issue paths

### A. Plea, testimony and remaining exposure

The retained-privilege path distinguishes admission of guilt and waiver of trial from compelled testimony about a still-disputed fact capable of increasing punishment. Estelle v. Smith rejects the proposition that adjudicated guilt alone extinguishes the privilege against incriminating sentencing use. Brown v. United States permits relevant cross-examination after voluntary testimony; it does not automatically extend a limited disclosure to a distinct later proceeding and a expressly reserved issue. Here the reservation and warning identify the actual scope of assent. No plea agreement or express sentencing-testimony waiver supplies a different basis.

The strongest waiver path is that a voluntary plea under oath admits the charged crime and opens the defendant to examination about its details, particularly when the charge includes a quantity allegation and the defendant knows the potential sentence. That path must still confront the court's express decision to defer attributable quantity and the trial-limited warning. Neither an indictment allegation nor knowledge of a possible life maximum establishes a knowing relinquishment of silence on the reserved fact. Mezzanatto's limited consensual impeachment waiver, Hyde's accepted-plea rule and Bousley's informed-plea rule do not supply a missing agreement or expand its scope. No Justice below treats silence as a right to lie or a plea as revocable at will.

### B. Retention does not alone decide evidentiary use

One path applies Griffin's prohibition to using the defendant's silence as substantive proof of the circumstances of the crime when a criminal court fixes punishment. The different sentencing burden does not answer whether this particular source of proof is permissible. Estelle protects against sentencing incrimination. Baxter addresses civil prison discipline, and Woodard's assumed-inference ground addresses discretionary civil clemency rather than determination of the initial criminal punishment. Those distinctions can preserve ordinary civil inferences while forbidding the present use.

The competing path retains the testimonial privilege but distinguishes compelled answers, an automatic penalty for invoking it and an ordinary inference from failure to meet probative evidence after guilt is admitted. Baxter and Woodard show that retention of a privilege does not always make silence evidentially unusable. Griffin expressly concerns proof of guilt; Estelle involved compelled statements. Witte, Watts and Edwards recognize sentencing factfinding with rules different from adjudication of guilt. On that view an inference supporting otherwise credited testimony need not compel testimony or create a separate sanction. Its strongest objection is that this hearing remains part of a criminal case and the inference helps determine imprisonment, rather than merely executive mercy or civil discipline. A sentencing label cannot alone answer that objection.

Jones's controlling opinion is statutory. It does not establish a new universal sentencing-element rule or decide sections 841, 846 and 860. Its published separate positions are nevertheless material to individual commitments: Stevens and Scalia expressly protect facts changing a statutory minimum; Souter and Ginsburg join the narrower maximum-based constitutional ground, which leaves unchanged-maximum minima open. Kennedy, O'Connor and Breyer defend the particular Jones injury allocation. Thomas joins the statutory opinion without those constitutional proposals. None of these positions independently resolves whether the Fifth Amendment bars the inference.

### C. Error, harmlessness and bounded remedy

If the inference is lawful, rejection of the lower court's categorical waiver reasoning need not disturb its judgment. If the inference is unconstitutional, Chapman supplies the direct-review harmlessness inquiry: the Government must establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the sentencing determination. The contrary remedial path, represented by Judge Michel, relies on the sufficiency of the remaining quantity evidence. Sufficiency alone does not establish absence of contribution. The sentencing judge expressly relied on silence in crediting the contested testimony and said a contrary privilege ruling could lead to closer examination of that testimony. The record supplies no independent conclusive determination unaffected by the inference.

Current-term Wright, joined by every Associate, independently cautions against preserving a sentence through an appellate alternative not established as a lawful independent sentencing ground. Its section 3E1.1 holding does not decide this constitutional question, but its actual-record remedy is pertinent. Peguero's collateral causation rule supplies no replacement for direct-review harmlessness; the distinct Brecht and O'Neal categories are not generalized. None of these paths authorizes a new appellate quantity finding, an acquittal, withdrawal of the pleas, a prescribed replacement sentence, automatic acceptance credit or automatic elimination of every other sentence component.

## 3. Justice-specific commitments

### Justice Stevens

**Disposition and ground.** Reject categorical plea waiver; hold the inference impermissible; vacate the challenged sentence and remand for sentencing without adverse use of silence. The express quantity reservation leaves a live punitive issue. He would apply Griffin to the unresolved crime facts used to determine punishment, without confining the protection to formal adjudication of guilt. His published Witte dissent rejects the suggestion that a broad ceiling erases the real punitive consequence of an additional fact; his Watts dissent distinguishes receipt of information from legal authority for a mandatory increase. His additional Monge dissent treats the substantive punitive adjudication seriously even when labeled sentencing. His Jones concurrence supplies an additional personal premise: facts changing the statutory minimum receive element protection.

**Counterpaths and limits.** His Woodard join accepts ordinary civil inferences and defeats an indiscriminate rule against every consequence of silence. Initial criminal punishment is materially different. Brown's voluntary-testimony rationale does not turn reserved quantity into admitted testimony, and knowing the maximum is not consent to the Government's quantity account. He would not preserve this sentence on Judge Michel's sufficiency rationale; his Wright join and Chapman require attention to the actual adverse contribution. The permitted remedy reopens the challenged sentencing determination, without deciding a newly invented jury-allocation or section 841 classification claim. He may preserve his broader Jones position separately, but it is not an already controlling Court rule or a basis for directing an unpresented new jury proceeding. No remorse or acceptance-of-responsibility rule is necessary.

**Join boundary.** He can join the rule barring adverse inference about the facts and circumstances of the offense at this criminal sentencing. He rejects a rationale that all facts below a maximum necessarily lack constitutional protection. A separate writing is useful only to preserve the broader range principle if the disposition's explanation otherwise appears to reject it.

### Justice O'Connor

**Disposition and ground.** Reject automatic waiver by the plea but affirm against the presented adverse-inference challenge. The limited colloquy does not establish a voluntary agreement to surrender sentencing silence. She nevertheless distinguishes the right not to be compelled to answer from an evidentiary consequence of declining to rebut probative sentencing evidence. Her Woodard opinion expressly recognizes that distinction in its assumed civil-inference ground. Her Witte opinion treats relevant-conduct sentencing as determination of punishment for the convicted offense rather than a new prosecution, while preserving independent safeguards; her Monge and Jones positions resist treating every additional punishment fact as an offense element. She would extend the inference distinction to this postplea sentencing record, where the judge used silence with credited testimony rather than imposing a standalone sanction for silence.

**Counterpaths and limits.** The strongest objection is that Baxter and Woodard are civil and this quantity finding produces a criminal mandatory floor. She accepts that distinction exists, but does not regard it as sufficient to extend Griffin's evidentiary prohibition beyond adjudication of guilt when no compelled testimony, contempt sanction, automatic privilege penalty or separate compulsory waiver is established. Estelle remains controlling against compelled sentencing statements. This is an affirmance on the independent inference ground, not an adoption of the Third Circuit's waiver rationale, not a finding that the witnesses were necessarily truthful, and not approval of every sentencing use of silence. She does not convert her Jones dissent into a controlling general constitutional allocation rule.

**Remedy and join boundary.** Because she finds no impermissible inference, harmlessness is unnecessary to her judgment. Conditionally, if an inference violation controls, she accepts that the judge's express reliance prevents affirmance merely because other evidence was sufficient; Wright supplies no invented independent alternative. She could then accept a bounded sentencing remand without joining the error holding. She rejects a general equivalence between inference and compelled testimony, and leaves remorse and acceptance-credit issues open.

### Justice Scalia

**Disposition and ground.** Reject plea waiver and the adverse inference on this record; vacate the challenged sentence and remand. His sufficient inference ground is narrower than a categorical rule governing every sentencing consideration: the Government used silence to establish a disputed current-offense quantity necessary for a higher statutory minimum. In Jones he expressly joined Stevens's position that facts changing the legislatively prescribed range, including its minimum, receive element protection. His Gaudin opinion assigns actual elements their constitutional safeguards; his Monge dissent treats a new personal-conduct qualification by its constitutional function. For this range-determining quantity, Griffin's protection cannot be avoided by placing the factual determination after a plea that expressly reserved it. No general plea or sworn colloquy supplied an admission of that quantity or an express waiver of silence.

**Counterpaths and limits.** His Woodard join establishes that an ordinary civil inference is not necessarily compulsion. His Watts concurrence protects otherwise lawful consideration of sentencing information and rejects an unsupported categorical Commission exclusion. Those sources furnish the strongest opposing path. They do not, for him, make this reserved statutory-floor fact an ordinary discretionary sentencing consideration: Watts expressly preserved actual-element limits, and his subsequently published Jones minimum-range position supplies the relevant individual classification. This commitment neither attributes that proposal to the Court nor holds that Jones overruled McMillan. He may decide the presented inference question consistently with his own published constitutional premise without granting relief on an unpresented independent jury claim.

**Remedy and join boundary.** He joins privilege retention and the no-inference application to this reserved range-determining quantity. He withholds assent to a broader proposition that Griffin necessarily prohibits every ordinary inference concerning all facts used within an already authorized range; the civil/evidentiary distinction remains legally significant for him. Chapman and his Wright join foreclose the lower concurrence's sufficiency-only harmlessness alternative on this expressly affected record. The remand removes adverse use of silence and leaves independently presented procedural and sentencing requirements intact; it directs neither automatic application of the ten-year floor nor a new jury proceeding. A distinct concurrence can explain this limited ground if the lead rationale extends beyond it. No author or final opinion topology is assigned here.

### Justice Kennedy

**Disposition and ground.** Reject plea waiver and adverse use of silence to determine the facts and circumstances of the crime at sentencing; vacate the challenged sentence and remand without that inference. His Balsys opinion locates the privilege in protection against domestic criminal use, a concern directly present here. His Dunnigan opinion permits a consequence for independently found willful perjury, not a presumption against a person who gives no testimony. His Woodard concurrence and join preserve genuine compulsion and criminal-use limits while treating mercy as distinct. His Witte join and Jones dissent permit particular sentencing allocations; they do not permit the Government to obtain its disputed proof through protected silence.

**Counterpaths and limits.** He confronts the strongest inference-permitted argument: sentencing differs from trial, retained privilege can coexist with an adverse inference in civil proceedings, and guilt has been admitted. The reply is specific to use in this criminal proceeding: the remaining quantity affects actual punishment and the judge used silence to credit the Government's account. Estelle defeats categorical extinction at conviction, while Griffin bars making that silence part of the prosecution's proof. He does not derive the result from a universal jury-allocation rule, and his Jones dissent remains consistent with requiring lawful evidence when a judge may find a fact. Brown does not expand the express reservation into an unrestricted testimonial account.

**Remedy and join boundary.** He supports the crime-facts no-inference rule and rejects a rule that privilege prevents every evidentiary disadvantage in civil proceedings. His Wright join requires lawful independent sentencing grounds; the present judge's express adverse reliance defeats the offered sufficiency-only harmlessness ground under Chapman. He supports reconsideration on lawful evidence without choosing quantity or a replacement sentence. Remorse, acceptance of responsibility and universal sentencing burdens remain outside this disposition.

### Justice Souter

**Disposition and ground.** Reject plea waiver and the adverse inference; vacate the challenged sentence and remand. He separates what the plea establishes from what was expressly left for adjudication. His joins in the actual-element rule of Gaudin, the independent-safeguards language of Watts and the limited waiver rule in Hyde support preserving the remaining issue's distinct legal requirements. His Jones concurrence emphasizes that a conviction does not silently supply authority based on a new unresolved fact. Its constitutional maximum-based rationale is not enlarged into a holding about every minimum; the Fifth Amendment ground here does not require that enlargement.

**Counterpaths and limits.** His Woodard and Balsys joins provide the competing boundary: not every pressure to answer constitutes prohibited compulsion, and the privilege's scope depends on protected criminal use. He would distinguish optional civil mercy from the prosecution's use of silence to establish crime facts in the pending criminal sentencing. Witte and Watts permit lawful relevant-conduct proof; they do not supply that proof or override its source-specific constitutional limit. The plea's oath and awareness of penalties do not overcome the reservation under Brown's scope principle. He does not treat the quantity as admitted or independently order jury adjudication.

**Remedy and join boundary.** He accepts a rule forbidding adverse inference about the circumstances and details of the offense at this sentencing, with no implication for remorse or acceptance credit. His Wright join and Chapman reject harmlessness based merely on sufficient other testimony when silence expressly influenced its crediting. The bounded remand leaves lawful evidence and unresolved sentencing matters to the lower court and grants no particular term. His narrower Jones reservation must not be represented as agreement with Stevens's general minimum-range proposal.

### Justice Thomas

**Disposition and ground.** Reject categorical plea waiver but affirm on the independent ground permitting this adverse inference. His Mezzanatto opinion enforces the limited voluntary waiver actually made; it supplies no plea agreement or sentencing waiver here. His Edwards opinion recognizes ordinary judicial relevant-conduct findings within lawful authority, and his Monge opinion classifies the particular recidivist proceeding without equating all punitive facts with offense elements. His Woodard join recognizes an ordinary inference as distinct from an automatic penalty or compelled answer. He would preserve that distinction here where guilt is admitted and the court uses silence with other credited quantity evidence.

**Counterpaths and limits.** Griffin's evidentiary prohibition and Estelle's sentencing protection provide the strongest opposing argument. He would retain Griffin for proof of criminal guilt and Estelle for compelled sentencing testimony without converting a defendant's refusal to rebut testimony in ordinary sentencing into compelled testimony itself. The mandatory-floor consequence gives the claim force, but Jones's statutory holding decides another enactment, and Thomas did not join its broader constitutional concurrences. The record supplies no independently presented section 841 element-classification ruling that he must adopt. He does not authorize compulsion, infer a new admission, treat a charge as proof, permit an automatic punishment for invoking the privilege or decide that silence alone establishes quantity.

**Remedy and join boundary.** He affirms against the inference objection without adopting the lower court's waiver theory or reaching harmlessness. If the controlling rule prohibits the inference, his Wright join requires a lawful sentencing response rather than preserving the term merely on appellate sufficiency; that conditional remedy does not become a join in the constitutional violation. He rejects an unqualified equation of silence inference with compelled testimony and leaves remorse, acceptance credit and broader classification questions unresolved.

### Justice Ginsburg

**Disposition and ground.** Reject plea waiver and adverse use of silence on the disputed crime facts; vacate the challenged sentence and remand. Her published Balsys statement emphasizes the person's protection from domestic official self-condemnation, while her join in Breyer's dissent treats the real penal exposure and effective protection as critical. Here exposure is domestic and immediate: the unanswered quantity question controls punishment. Her Woodard join remains a civil-process distinction; her Watts and Witte joins preserve independent protections despite sentencing flexibility. Her Jones join addresses actual charging authority and a narrower constitutional maximum-based position, not an already established universal minimum rule.

**Counterpaths and limits.** She accepts that Brown prevents a voluntary witness from presenting an untested selective account, but the plea did not offer an unrestricted account of reserved quantity at sentencing. The express trial warning and reservation defeat a general implied waiver. Baxter and Woodard show that the privilege is not an absolute guarantee against evidentiary consequences everywhere; they do not make initial criminal punishment civil. Estelle and Griffin together forbid using silence as the Government's affirmative support for the unresolved offense facts. The same conclusion would apply without declaring every sentencing fact an element.

**Remedy and join boundary.** She joins the crime-facts no-inference rule and bounded sentencing relief. She rejects reliance on sufficient other evidence as conclusive harmlessness given actual judicial reliance; Chapman and Wright address the distinct contribution question. She would not prescribe a lower term or treat the cooperating witnesses as incredible as a matter of law. The decision leaves remorse, acceptance-of-responsibility consequences and independent jury questions open; her Jones maximum-based concurrence is not silently broadened.

### Justice Breyer

**Disposition and ground.** Reject plea waiver and this adverse inference; vacate the challenged sentence and remand for lawful reconsideration. His Balsys dissent emphasizes that official compulsion must be measured against real penal exposure and a protection coextensive with the danger. His Watts concurrence accepts the Guidelines' lawful relevant-conduct system while treating information policy and constitutional permission as distinct. His Monge join and Jones dissent permit particular sentencing allocations; neither answers whether silence may supply the Government's proof of the crime's circumstances. A system permitting judicial sentencing findings can still require the prosecution to prove them without an adverse inference from protected silence.

**Counterpaths and limits.** The strongest contrary path combines Woodard's assumed civil inference with the practical difference between sentencing and trial. He distinguishes this adjudication of criminal punishment from discretionary mercy. Estelle preserves the privilege's penal-use protection after guilt, and Griffin's proof concern applies to the judge's express use of silence in resolving the contested account. Awareness of possible penalties and limited sworn plea responses are insufficient waiver of a reserved question. His approach does not adopt Stevens's general minimum-range position or undo his own Jones classification dissent.

**Remedy and join boundary.** He supports the crime-facts rule, leaving remorse and acceptance-credit questions for cases presenting them. Chapman requires more than a showing that the remaining testimony could support the sentence. His Wright join opposes appellate reconstruction of an independent lawful ground when the actual remarks intertwine it with the legal error. The quantity assessment returns without the adverse inference; credible independent evidence may still support a lawful sentence, and no exact term, automatic adjustment or new jury procedure follows.
