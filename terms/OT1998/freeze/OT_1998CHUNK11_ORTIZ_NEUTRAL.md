### Source-supported record and important corrections

Sources: [90 F.3d 963](../sources/chunk11-neutral/ortiz/lower_90f3d963.txt), especially ¶¶2–52, 53–143, 160–173 and the stated dissent passages; [sanitized 134 F.3d 668](../sources/chunk11-neutral/ortiz/lower_134f3d668_sanitized.txt), substantive majority and dissent. Judicial findings, witness predictions and legal conclusions retain their different status.

1. Fibreboard manufactured asbestos products from 1920 to 1971. It disputed broad coverage under Continental and Pacific policies lacking aggregate limits but containing per-person/per-occurrence limits. A California trial court had accepted its coverage theory; an insurer appeal remained disputed during negotiations. Coverage victory could provide substantial insurance, while loss threatened Fibreboard and claim recoveries. Neither outcome was certain (90 F.3d ¶¶2–6, 27–33).
2. The class consisted of persons with asbestos-related personal-injury claims against Fibreboard whose claims had not been brought in a lawsuit, settled or included in a settlement agreement **before August 27, 1993**. “Future” is therefore a litigation-status label, not a finding that every member was then uninjured. The class includes presently ill persons with unfiled claims and exposure-only persons; the dissent also identifies derivative future-spouse and future-child categories. Existing inventory settlements outside the class remain separate (¶¶26, 35, 98–105; dissent ¶¶238–240, 361–367).
3. The Global Settlement proposed a $1.535 billion trust: $1.525 billion from insurers and $10 million from Fibreboard. The ordinary corporate assets were not all surrendered, and shareholders retained a viable company. The trust would administer class personal-injury/death claims and releases against Fibreboard and insurers; that is not a fund size fixed by a preexisting insurance aggregate ceiling (¶¶31, 40–43, 127; dissent ¶¶176, 248–249).
4. **The trial court did make asset and depletion findings.** It heard testimony on future claims, defense costs and noninsurance value. It credited predictions that even $2 billion under the backup Trilateral settlement plus approximately $235 million of company value would be exhausted in five to nine years (¶113). The neutral correction is not “no finding of assets.” The disputed questions are the legal sufficiency and proper measurement of the asserted fund, whether negotiated insurance value demonstrates an independent limit, whether excluded value must be devoted, and what equitable treatment requires. The 1998 dissent’s report of a later acquisition price is a challenge to valuation, not a new judicial appraisal establishing collectible net assets (134 F.3d ¶30).
5. The Trilateral agreement separately settled insurance disputes and contemplated $2 billion if the Global settlement was not approved; it was not designed to settle tort claims against Fibreboard. Its Rudd approval, parties and legal effect are distinct. Rejection of the Global class does not by itself adjudicate or void all Trilateral provisions, require insurer payment directly to this class, or decide individual tort liability (90 F.3d ¶¶46, 50–51, 160–173).
6. Class members could not opt out of the Global class. The agreement provided settlement, mediation, optional binding/nonbinding arbitration, a further settlement conference, and possible tort-system proceedings **against the trust**, including a jury if requested. That back-end process retained a $500,000 cap, no punitive damages, fee limit and possible deferred judgment payments. It was not an opt-out restoring unrestricted personal claims against Fibreboard or insurers (¶¶40–43).
7. Counsel represented both present inventory claimants and the proposed class during negotiations. Present settlements included contingent back-end payments; the lower majority found phased negotiations, common interests and the later Trilateral agreement prevented material conflicts. The court appointed a guardian after the August agreement in principle, for review of fairness from the class viewpoint. The majority found vigorous, arm’s-length bargaining and adequate representation; objectors contest structural adequacy despite those findings (¶¶65–106).
8. Within the class, the identified conflicts include currently ill versus later-manifesting claimants and pre-1959 versus post-1959 exposure, with materially different insurance positions. Uniform treatment itself can be an allocation choice. The lower majority regarded common interests and eventual individualized valuation as sufficient; the dissent emphasized distinct bargaining interests in caps, conservation, insurance rights and distributions. Those are competing legal applications, not findings that every difference is automatically disqualifying or harmless.

### Lower reasoning and strongest objections

The earlier majority read the Rule’s practical-impairment language flexibly, found probable future depletion supported by evidence, and treated a mandatory settlement as an equitable alternative to wasteful insolvency. It declined to make bankruptcy the exclusive mechanism on these facts, distinguished Shutts as a money-class case, and relied on adequate representation for binding the proposed limited-fund class. It rejected the friendly-suit objection because the underlying disputes remained adverse and court approval was necessary. The 1998 majority stressed that this was (b)(1), not Amchem’s (b)(3), that individualized awards remained, and that Rule 23(a) was satisfied independently of fairness (134 F.3d ¶¶2–5).

Judge Smith’s dissents challenged creation of a limited fund by the bargain itself, retention of substantial company value, loss of in-personam tort rights and substitution of a private bankruptcy arrangement without statutory safeguards. The later dissent independently stressed Rule 23(a) conflicts, the need for representative protection rather than expert assurance of an attractive overall result, and the limits of treating desire for settlement as commonality. It also raised notice, opt-out and Article III objections. Its statements about the full effect of historical Amchem are lower advocacy and must be checked against the simulated holding. In particular, a claim that **any** minor conflict necessarily defeats certification is not the operative simulated formulation of **materially conflicting** bargaining interests.

The 1998 majority’s description of the limited-fund finding as uncontested does not erase the actual legal objections preserved and discussed in the original appeal and dissent. Neither party’s statement about economic desirability decides legal certification. Ortiz’s intervention supports review of the objections; no unsupported universal rule for appeals by nonintervening class members is needed.

### Governing law, precise Standards and open construction

**Rule text supported by the contemporaneous lower opinion:** Rule 23(a)(4) requires that “the representative parties will fairly and adequately protect the interests of the class.” Subject to Rule 23(a), (b)(1)(B) addresses the risk that separate adjudications “would as a practical matter be dispositive of the interests of the other members not parties to the adjudications or substantially impair or impede their ability to protect their interests.” The full quoted (b)(1)(B) passage is in the slice. Rule 23(a)’s numerosity, commonality, typicality and adequacy remain distinct. The Rules Enabling Act, §2072(b), prohibits rules that “abridge, enlarge, or modify any substantive right,” as quoted at 134 F.3d ¶29. The lower source and governing Amchem adequately support these propositions under Engine §14.

**Correct the supplied proposed-rule premise:** No supplied governing simulated holding already makes an independent fund limit, full asset commitment and equitable claimant treatment a conclusive multipart (b)(1)(B) test. Traditional limited-resource practice, the Rule’s language, the Enabling Act and bankruptcy distinctions support arguments for those limits. The lower majority supports a broader practical-depletion construction. Independent modeling must decide the warranted construction and application; it cannot treat a target-case rule as entering law. Neither literal risk wording nor a settlement’s own fixed cap alone settles that dispute.

**Exact Standards title: “Certification of settlement-only classes.” Present operation effective: Amchem, 1997-06-25.** The exact current rule is reproduced in the slice: a settlement-only class must satisfy Rule 23(a) and the applicable (b) requirements; no-trial manageability need not be considered, while the actual bargain may inform the remaining inquiries without relaxing them. Fairness and desirability are not substitutes; notice and opt-out rights do not cure materially conflicting bargaining interests. Amchem Parts I–II command eight participating Justices; its adequacy and (b)(3) predominance applications command six. **Exact Standards title: “Constitutional authority to bind absent litigants.” Present operation effective: Richards, June 10, 1996; Matsushita retains the representation component.** Exact current wording and component authority appear in the slice. Shared interests, vigorous advocacy and notice alone do not confer authority to bind; adequate representation, consent, control, genuine privity or another recognized constitutional relationship must supply it. Whether adequate representation may sometimes cure absent notice and the complete collateral-review boundary remain open. No separate Current wording authority is listed.

### Supported decisional paths and remedy boundaries

- **Affirm certification:** The Rule’s practical-risk wording may cover demonstrated likely depletion where disputed insurance and expensive individual litigation threaten later claimants; accept the supported findings and distinguish Amchem’s actual conflicts and (b)(3) application. The strongest objection is that negotiated limits and retained company value cannot manufacture representative authority or extinguish materially different rights without adequate independent protection. This path must satisfy Rule 23(a) and constitutional binding, not merely characterize the settlement as beneficial.
- **Set aside on (b)(1)(B):** Construe traditional limited-resource authority and the Enabling Act to require proof of a genuine limited resource and appropriate collective treatment, and find the bargained cap/retained value insufficient or require legally correct redetermination. The strongest objection is that the Rule says risk and practical impairment, the trial court did take evidence, and limiting it to a preexisting segregated fund could deny its creditor/insolvency function. The disposition must identify the actual failed requirement rather than assume no evidence existed.
- **Set aside on adequate representation:** Apply Amchem’s independent protection of materially different bargaining interests to near/far claimants, insurance differences or counsel’s inventory interests. The strongest objection is the trial court’s detailed contrary findings and the distinction between a uniform process with eventual individualized awards and Amchem’s fixed categories. Explain why structure remains inadequate despite those facts, or why a remand should permit the lower courts to apply the correct legal standard. No universal required subclass architecture follows unless independently justified.
- **Confined remand:** Vacate for proper certification review without deciding every statutory or constitutional question, if the identified legal error and remaining factual work warrant it. This is not automatic settlement approval, a command to negotiate particular terms or permission to replace Rule 23 with a fairness balance.

A certification failure prevents the present mandatory arrangement from binding through that certification. It does not adjudicate each person’s damages, impose an insurer contribution, compel a bankruptcy, approve a replacement settlement or automatically dispose of separate Rudd/Trilateral judgments. Any continuing injunction, release or remand direction must be traced to the reviewed judgment and actual scope. A certification-only rejection may leave notice, opt-out, jurisdiction over particular persons and substantive injury questions unresolved under Amchem; an affirmative merits or binding judgment cannot assume those predicates.

### Issue 1: Rule 23(b)(1)(B) limited fund and adequate representation

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor requires every settlement class to satisfy Rule 23 and holds that settlement does not relax predominance or adequate representation where present and future claimants conflict.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Settlement-only class certification under Rule 23, under Civil Procedure, supplies the operative requirement and permits narrower subclasses and individual resolution.
* **Other Governing Law:** Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23(a) and 23(b)(1)(B); Due Process Clause.
* **Material Other Authority:** Traditional limited-fund practice requires a fund fixed independently of the settlement, inadequate to pay all claims, wholly devoted to the class, and distributed through equitable treatment among claimants.
