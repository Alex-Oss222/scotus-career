## 1. Parties, questions, route, and federal jurisdiction

The petition jointly seeks review of the Minnesota judgments in Carter, 569 N.W.2d 169, and Johns, 569 N.W.2d 180. Both state opinions were filed September 11, 1997; the petitioner's jurisdictional statement reports entry of the Carter judgment September 29 and the Johns judgment October 1. It reports the petition filed December 29, 1997, and granted March 9, 1998. The full Johns state supreme court opinion is reproduced at Appendix B1–B2 and adopts Carter's grounds. The earlier statement that no companion exists, and the later source limit saying the full companion opinion was unrecovered, are superseded. (Petition and JA, Appendices A–B; petitioner merits p. 1; X01.)

The petition presents two questions: whether these invitees established their own legitimate privacy interests in the apartment, and whether the officer's unaided window observation constituted a search. The State frames the first in terms of invitation solely for illegal activity and the second in terms of a public, common, noncurtilage vantage. Those descriptions are premises of its questions, not independent factual findings. Respondents dispute the proposed limits on invited privacy and the characterization of the viewing position. The short question in the original packet does not exhaust the second question actually presented.

The reproduced omnibus order rejected the personal-interest claim and alternatively held the observation was not a search. Carter's intermediate appeal affirmed the personal-interest determination without reaching the search issue. Johns's intermediate appeal assumed an interest and sustained the observation under its reading of Crea and the investigation circumstances. Both convictions reached the state supreme court, which reversed the judgments on the residential-search issue. Other intermediate appellate branches, including Carter's sentencing issue and Johns's jury-related issue, do not become additional federal questions through this refresh. (Appendices C–E.)

The state supreme court referred to both the Fourth Amendment and Minnesota article I, section 10. Its operative personal-interest and search reasoning applies federal precedent without plainly declaring a separate adequate state-law basis independent of federal law. Michigan v. Long therefore supplies the federal-review inquiry. Dual constitutional citation alone is neither an automatic jurisdictional bar nor authority to foreclose an independent Minnesota constitutional choice on remand. The admitted review channel is under 28 U.S.C. §1257; this packet enters no new grant.

## 2. Each respondent's relationship to the apartment

The supported core is that the sole lessee, Thompson, permitted Carter and Johns to be there to package cocaine with her; they remained for about two and one-half hours and supplied an eighth of an ounce of cocaine for use of the apartment. Johns wore slippers. Invitation, duration, and the common task were the state supreme court's stated personal-interest grounds. Neither slippers nor a brief's description of them as social guests establishes residence or overnight status. The lower opinions identify an absence of evidence of a prior personal relationship, previous visits, stored effects, or continuing independent control; absence of such proof must not be rewritten as a finding that every possible residential connection affirmatively did not exist.

There is, however, a specific key proffer. The September 29, 1994 defense-counsel affidavit at JA H1–H2 asserts that Carter possessed apartment keys at arrest, that the sheriff inventoried and later released them, and that the release record had not been furnished to the defense. The State's merits brief p. 15 n.5 calls this a post-hearing allegation, denies stipulating to it, and says it was unsupported by other record evidence and not relied on by the trial or appellate courts. The proffer concerns Carter, not Johns. It is not an accepted finding of independent entry or control; it also prevents saying there is no key evidence or that Carter affirmatively had no key. No separate ruling resolving the affidavit's status or weight was found in the six recovered publications. (X08–X09.)

Fields/Crawley currently recognizes personal grounds based on sustained paid access, a key, independent entry and control, and separately on recurring expressly permitted sharing of a closed room. It protects neither mere presence nor every commercial visitor. Unlawful activity does not by itself extinguish an otherwise established privacy interest. Thus the scope of the invitation, the actual residential relationship, and any usable proof of control remain distinct from condemnation of the activity. A conclusion about Carter's key proffer cannot silently be transferred to Johns.

## 3. Exterior position and manner of observation: preserve the source levels

### Trial findings and incorporated memorandum

The express findings at E2 paragraphs 7–8 say the officer left a common entrance sidewalk, crossed a grassy common area, and stood approximately 12–18 inches from a window whose drawn horizontal mini-blinds had gaps. E9 describes a garden-level apartment, an officer standing outside and looking downward through the blinds, and observation of the table activity. The trial court did not find climbing or crouching in these passages. (X02–X03.)

E10–E12 found no fence and no bushes or trees immediately before the window forming an enclosure that prevented public access. Thompson had no exclusive right to the outside ground. The court relied on shared lawn use, children and other residents leaving the walks, bicycle storage next to the window, and absence of keep-off-the-grass signs. It also acknowledged bushes planted at an angle off the sidewalk before one window, without evidence that they were planted for privacy rather than appearance. Its legal conclusion was that this was common ground outside the apartment's curtilage and that the observation was not a search. Those ground-use findings and that constitutional conclusion must be identified separately. The court's findings did not establish a view from the ordinary entrance sidewalk. (X03.)

### Officer testimony actually reproduced in the joint appendix

At G12–G13, Thielen describes approaching from the west after parking at another building, standing outside, and looking through pulled horizontal blinds. He estimates the distance as varying between a foot and eighteen inches, says he could stand and look, and denies using a flashlight or putting his hands on the window. He watched for approximately fifteen minutes. At G27–G32 he describes a first position off the left side/in front of a projecting brick abutment, with the projection providing some cover, from which he could already see the table, three people, powder transfer, and bagging. He then moved right to obtain a closer look through the blinds and saw the same activity. These were window-side positions, not established sidewalk views. (X04–X05.)

At G43–G46, he distinguishes the buildings' two entrance walks, says there was no sidewalk access to the garden-level apartments, and describes his approach over grass from the west. His initial denial of having been on the sidewalk is followed by agreement that he crossed the other building's sidewalk on his way to these windows. This supports a grass approach away from the ordinary entrance route; it does not support an unqualified assertion that he used no sidewalk anywhere. Nor does it eliminate the difference between his approach description and the trial court's compressed statement that he left the common entrance sidewalk. Asked about shrubs, he estimates their setback uncertainly. (X06.)

At G46–G47, he denies placing his eyes six inches from the window but allows that less than a foot was possible. He explains that the marks placed on photographs indicate approximate locations and could be several inches forward or backward. The trial court's approximately 12–18 inches is therefore an approximate finding, not a proven minimum distance maintained throughout the observation. At G48–G50 he is uncertain whether the opening was between adjacent blinds or between their slats. Closed blinds with a usable opening are supported; a precise gap geometry is not settled. Respondents' formulation that the blinds were closed as tightly as possible is an advocacy characterization, not an additional trial finding. (X06.)

The informant told the officer about seeing the bagging from outside, but the reproduced testimony does not establish that the informant used the ordinary public sidewalk as the viewing position. At G9 the officer says the informant did not explain how the view was obtained; G39 describes passing a window and looking in. Neither substitutes for analysis of the officer's actual positions. (X07.)

### State appellate description and the express challenge to it

The state supreme court's narrative at A8–A9 describes going behind short bushes and standing 12–18 inches away. Its analytical paragraph at A21 adds climbing over bushes and crouching, and A22 repeats climbing and proximity as part of extraordinary measures. A20 n.10 leaves open whether the ground was a common area outside curtilage; A22 treats the legality of the officer's position as a close question unnecessary to resolve on its total-conduct reasoning. Thus a definitive curtilage holding must not be attributed to that opinion. (X10.)

The State expressly disputes the climbing/approach account in its merits brief p. 28 n.6, continuing on p. 29. It cites G44–G45 and the photographs for an approach from the west without climbing or walking around the bushes. This is a party's record-based challenge, not a replacement judicial finding. Respondents answer at p. 1 n.1 that the State failed to seek rehearing to correct the account, invoke the status of state factual determinations on federal review, and argue that the photographs undermine the officer's account. These are express review and preservation arguments; the docket fact about rehearing and their interpretation of the photographs were not independently established by the recovered publications. (X11–X12.)

No reproduced testimony located in the fully read joint appendix expressly says the officer climbed or crouched. Because the appendix contains excerpts, this does not prove that no such statement existed anywhere in the original record. The earlier neutral language calling climbing/crouching established conduct is corrected to an appellate characterization contested against the trial findings and reproduced testimony. The source operator does not resolve credibility, the proper deference to that characterization, or whether any discrepancy affects the constitutional conclusion.

### Visual limits

The reproduced exhibits show garden-level four-pane windows and blinds, brick projections, grass, entrance walks, and a bicycle beside the windows. Several exterior images are dark. I3 (exhibit 10) and the neighboring exhibits do not independently demonstrate the precise posture or route on the night in question. At F7 the investigator says he did not photograph the blinds as they were that night; G66–G67 permits that the coverings had been adjusted and notes changed interior conditions. The photographs cannot establish the exact night-of opening or prove that climbing or crouching was necessary, impossible, or actually performed. (X13.)

## 5. Personal interest, search, justification, and remedy remain separate

Recognizing a host's privacy does not establish either respondent's own interest. Rejecting a respondent's personal interest disposes of that respondent's residential-search claim without adjudicating the host's rights. Recognizing or assuming an interest requires a separate assessment of the actual observation. Common access, gaps in blinds, the purpose and manner of viewing, and close proximity bear on that assessment; no one of these facts is treated as an automatic answer.

Current Fields/Crawley holds that limited visibility to cotenants is not by itself general publication and that imperfect screening cannot independently negate subjective privacy without the actual police vantage. It did not establish a majority conclusion that every shared yard is curtilage or decide this observation as a search. Its home-entry exigency review rule likewise does not erase the separate status of historical factual findings. Pinson's technological acquisition of concealed domestic information supplies an analogy, not a categorical rule for unaided window observation. The State's own reply pp. 3–4 acknowledges that a search may occur from a legitimate exterior position; its contention depends also on the manner of observation. (X18.)

The initial viewing must be distinguished from the vehicle stop, handgun discovery, arrests, vehicle impoundment/search warrant, apartment warrant/search, and statements. The state supreme court records the State's concession that the officer lacked probable cause before looking and rejects the claimed exigency on that premise. The informant's tip and a reasonable basis to investigate do not establish a general reasonable-suspicion exception for a home search. (Appendix A23–A24; X19.)

The trial memorandum E13 relied on the observation along with the informant information for the stop. E14–E15 rejected the challenges to alleged misleading statements about an ongoing investigation and continuous surveillance because the required deliberate/reckless and material misrepresentation showing was not made; it treated the observation as central to probable cause and acknowledged the ninety-minute break in observation. E17 again describes the warrant basis as primarily the officer's window observations. The later warrants therefore cannot simply be presumed independent of that viewing. (X20.)

At JA F2 the prosecutor identified the apartment search, vehicle stop, and probable cause as live issues, described additional statements as not separately in issue, and conditionally accepted that an invalid arrest would bring down the statements and physical evidence. That recorded concession matters, but its stated condition is invalidity of the arrest; it is not an express concession that any defect in the initial observation automatically excludes every later item for both respondents. It must be read with the actual litigation and causal chain. (X21.)

Fields requires genuine independence of both the decision to seek a warrant and its issuance where independent source is invoked, and preserves actual taint and other applicable remedial inquiries. This record does not supply a conclusive independent source for all evidence, an evidence-specific harmless-error determination, or an adjudicated automatic exclusion/admission rule. A merits decision or appropriately limited remand must specify the respondent, claim, evidence, and ground it resolves. No new suppression outcome is supplied here.
