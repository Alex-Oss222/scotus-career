## Dispositions by component

Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer would vacate the statutory judgment insofar as it treats a numerical citizen-voting-age majority as categorically indispensable despite a properly supported compact, workable district's actual ability to elect, and remand for a bounded inquiry under an expressly modified first condition. They do not find that ability proved. O'Connor and Kennedy would affirm under the conventional first condition, selecting citizen voting-age population for this single-group electoral-capacity showing. Scalia and Thomas would affirm the rejection of the statutory claim on their maintained §2 dilution-noncoverage ground; they do not supply two extra rationale votes for the numerical threshold.

The six Justices who reach the operative dilution framework would reject the asserted legal requirement to show that the1990 census was wrong when conducted, find no clear error in the supported later-change demographics, and sustain the late-survey exclusion for surprise under abuse-of-discretion review. ## Entering law, exact Standard and what would change

**Section2 fragmentation, state districting choice and influence claims**, present operation effective DeGrandy,June30,1994, retains Growe's February23,1993 and Voinovich's March2,1993 components. The conventional district-formation framework requires a sufficiently large and compact district majority, cohesion and majority bloc voting ordinarily defeating the group's choice, subject to the actual special-circumstances qualification; totality remains independently necessary. Population denominator, distinct-group coalition cognizability, influence claims and modification of the first condition remain open in their stated respects. DeGrandy rejects maximization, proportional-election entitlement, conclusive numerical offsets and safe harbors. Its effective-opportunity evidence in the totality is not an existing below-majority entitlement.

Brooks,April22,1999, fits proof to the primary-runoff barrier and rejects a required general-election-winner demonstration and conclusive statewide offsets. That holding does not eliminate the conventional district-majority condition in this different at-large district-formation claim. It supplies an argument for an expressly considered adaptation, not authority to label the adaptation already controlling. The January25 Department of Commerce decision concerns national enumeration authority and the explained design before it; neither its permission nor the dissent's statutory prohibition establishes that obsolete local data must be used or that any housing estimate is sound.

Section2's enacted text protects citizens' equal opportunity to participate and elect representatives of choice under totality and expressly disclaims proportional-election entitlement. It contains no numerical percentage that alone decides the relationship between electoral capacity and the judicial first condition. The proposed functional path below is an express modification of the conventional prerequisite for the identified claim, preserving all other required showings; it cannot be introduced as a mere application of Brooks.

## Enhanced alternatives by issue

**N: retain the conventional numerical threshold.** A single protected group seeking judicial replacement of an at-large arrangement must demonstrate a compact, workable district in which that group constitutes more than half the citizen voting-age population. The first condition identifies whether the claimed loss of a group electoral opportunity is attributable to the challenged structure, distinct from the availability of occasional influence or dependence on other voters' choices. Citizenship and voting age address who can vote; total population remains a separate consideration in the district's population equality/workability. This choice resolves the denominator for this claim, not every constitutional district-population question.

The strongest counterargument is that a compact submajority with dependable crossover can possess actual electoral capacity, and §2's text protects opportunity without stating fifty percent. Path N responds that a State may voluntarily create such districts, but a judicial districting entitlement must have the conventional independent group-capacity predicate. Voinovich's open reservations do not themselves abolish that predicate, and a runaway numerical-safe-harbor analogy incorrectly moves DeGrandy's totality rule to a different prerequisite. Path N reserves distinct influence-only and different election-mechanism claims rather than announcing all are foreclosed. The accepted47.9/48.3 CVAP figures fail N; neither figure becomes a majority through turnout assumptions.

**F: expressly adapt the first condition to demonstrated ability to elect.** For a compact single protected group challenging this at-large arrangement, a workable district can satisfy electoral capacity through a group majority or demonstrated dependable crossover support giving it an actual ability to elect its preferred candidates. Mere influence, a near-fifty population, favorable turnout speculation or the race of past officeholders is insufficient. Cohesion, the relevant majority-bloc showing, causation and independent totality remain necessary. Crossover within the proposed district does not itself negate a supported majority-bloc barrier in the challenged at-large setting; those settings must be identified and proved rather than assumed identical.

F's strongest objection is departure from Gingles's administrable conventional prerequisite, together with the danger that every political alliance becomes a federal districting claim. F responds that statutory opportunity can be demonstrable without the group alone supplying a numerical majority, while retaining a district-capacity prerequisite and the independent statutory conditions. It does not guarantee a racial share of seats, entitle a group to choose a coalition partner, protect mere influence, or require maximization. This change is expressly bounded to actual ability to elect; distinct aggregation of protected minority groups is not decided.

Because the lower court stopped at the numerical first condition, F requires only reconsideration of the preserved claim under the changed legal rule. No source finding establishes reliable crossover, cohesion, majority-bloc defeat or full totality here. Missing lower findings are not findings for plaintiffs and do not themselves prove a litigant default; the trial record and ordinary preservation/supplementation limits govern. F cannot support immediate liability or a commanded district.

**X: statutory noncoverage.** Scalia and Thomas maintain their Holder/DeGrandy/SCLC position, expressly repeated in Brooks: §2 protects voting access, casting and counting rather than districting-based dilution. Their strongest objection is the governing Gingles/Growe/Voinovich/DeGrandy line and §2(b)'s opportunity language. They acknowledge that their proposed construction rejects that controlling line's incompatible coverage propositions and is not present law. They read subsection(b)'s results rule within subsection(a)'s covered practices. This is a separate legal basis to reject this claim, not support for N's numerical theory or F's actual-opportunity theory. Here the judgment already rejects relief, so their chosen mandate is affirmance on X, not a needless alteration of an already adverse result solely to imitate the different DeGrandy mandate.

**Demographics: current reliable proof versus an immutable census baseline.** The census is presumptively accurate, but proof that population materially changed after1990 is different from proof the original count was false. The lower court's accepted clear/cogent/convincing, thoroughly documented and highly accurate showing can be met by sufficiently supported housing, occupancy and growth evidence. The April1997 report—not the excluded August update—was credited. Apartment changes and growth outside the proposed district explain why adding the contiguous northern area was considered in achieving a workable population. The factual finding receives clear-error review.

The strongest challenge identifies limits of small-area estimates, projected apartment occupancy, electrical-hookup proxies and the later update. Those concerns do not establish that the credited April report is clearly erroneous when its explanations and the unchanged below-majority result are preserved. The accepted lower burden is sufficient here; no new universal federal heightened burden for every demographic dispute is promulgated. Nor is eight-percent underpopulation declared automatically constitutional: the48.3 calculation does not settle workability merely by staying below a numerical reference point. Under F, current demographics remain valid evidence but are not the whole electoral-capacity inquiry.

**Survey: sustain the surprise ruling versus a required reopening.** The survey's existence was disclosed late Friday and data late Saturday before Monday trial; it concerned a previously unidentified basis for expert testimony in a southern area. Local RuleCV-16(e) required the pertinent summaries, bases and exhibits and permitted good-cause supplementation. The defense's general view had been known since1996; the court also excluded the August defense update said to have triggered the new work. A prior deposition statement that Hispanics lived south of the proposed district did not disclose the survey methodology or data needed for preparation and cross-examination.

The strongest competing path stresses the survey's claimed importance, rebuttal purpose, late defense revision and possible accommodation. On the supplied record these points do not make the trial court's surprise ruling an abuse of discretion, especially after exclusion of the late defense update. A proper-rebuttal label does not eliminate disclosure duties. No source establishes a requested/feasible continuance or findings satisfying a four-factor test; no such findings are invented. The survey's actual accuracy and otherwise proper-rebuttal status remain open because the sufficient procedural exclusion does not decide them. Changing the substantive threshold does not retrospectively turn this exclusion into legal error or automatically admit the survey on remand.
