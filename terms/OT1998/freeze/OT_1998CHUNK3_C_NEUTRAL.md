## El Al Israel Airlines, Ltd. v. Tsui Yuan Tseng — January 12, 1999

### Posture and record

The Second Circuit's June 13, 1997 decision, 122 F.3d 99, Nos. 96-7447 and 96-7619, follows a bench trial. It reverses dismissal of the personal-injury claim and permits local-law proceedings, while affirming the separate $1,034.90 baggage award. The reviewed exclusivity question concerns the personal-injury branch; the baggage award is not a new merits component to reopen.

Tseng was searched at JFK on May 22, 1993 before boarding for Tel Aviv. After routine questioning she was designated a high-risk passenger; the airline could not explain the supposed illogic of her answers from memory or records. She was taken to a private room, removed shoes, jacket and sweater, lowered jeans to mid-hip, and underwent an external manual search by a woman including breasts and groin; the search lasted about fifteen minutes. She was then permitted to fly. The trial court accepted that the method conformed to El Al's procedures. The lower courts describe distress and treatment, but no qualifying bodily injury; physical manifestations of psychic injury were not treated as bodily injury caused by the search. This is a trial determination, not a new categorical answer about every psychosomatic condition.

The District Court found an accident under its flexible reading of Saks and found no willful misconduct: no evidence supported an inference beyond mistaken suspicion. It dismissed the personal-injury claim under the Convention. The Second Circuit regarded the routine security search as no accident and allowed state tort law because it read Article 24's covered cases as those satisfying Article 17's accident and injury conditions. It acknowledged the contrary British interpretation in Sidhu, but emphasized the treaty's limited title, drafting history, local remedies and the limits of uniformity. It also rejected the proposition that intentional conduct necessarily falls outside Article 17: Article 25, in its view, addresses a more culpable subset and does not independently create carrier liability.

Source: [Archive petition and appendix collection](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385014_0124), particularly the separate Appendix pp.1a–23a and Joint Appendix pp.30–32, 40–63. The original complaint was filed in New York state court and removed under the foreign-state removal provision; that route is documented in the lower opinion. No additional removal question is presented by this preflight.

### Entering law and competing paths

The focused slice reproduces **Zicherman v. Korean Air Lines**, January 16, 1996, and **Domestic compensable loss under the Warsaw Convention**, effective that date. All nine joined O'Connor's rule that domestic law supplies loss categories and beneficiaries **subject to the Convention's conditions and limits**. It expressly leaves general exclusivity unresolved. Neither the existence of domestic damages rules nor Zicherman's refusal to create a uniform damages code decides whether local law can supply liability when a treaty condition fails.

Surviving inherited authority: **Air France v. Saks**, 470 U.S. 392 (1985), interprets accident as an unexpected or unusual external event, applied flexibly to the circumstances, and distinguishes the event causing injury from the injury itself. **Eastern Airlines v. Floyd**, 499 U.S. 530 (1991), addresses Article 17's bodily-injury condition and reserves the independent state-law question. The authentic French treaty text and official English translation are in [49 Stat. 3000](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-49/pdf/STATUTE-49-Pg3000.pdf), especially Articles 17, 24 and 25. Use the governing original treaty; do not apply a later-effective protocol merely because it was being considered or ratified during the litigation.

* **Field-based exclusivity path:** Article 24's damages actions, however founded, remain subject to Article 17's conditions within the personal-injury field of covered international carriage. Failure of accident or bodily injury would therefore defeat this local personal-injury claim. The strongest counterargument is that the text refers to cases covered by Article 17, which can be read to incorporate all its liability predicates, and that broad exclusivity would leave some wrongs uncompensated. A defensible answer must distinguish absence of a remedy within the covered field from an event outside the treaty's temporal or substantive scope. It cannot announce immunity for every activity occurring at an airport.
* **Condition-based nonexclusivity path:** When Article 17's predicates fail, Article 24 does not displace otherwise applicable tort claims. This supports retaining the remand for local-law adjudication; it does not establish battery, false imprisonment, damages or a particular state-law defense. The strongest counterargument is that local liability could nullify negotiated restrictions on carrier liability and make absence of a treaty predicate a means of escaping those restrictions. This path must account for Article 24's broad action language and the distinction between domestic damages supplementation and independent liability.
* **Article 25 route:** Tseng's respondent brief pp.30–36 independently argues that willful misconduct removes exclusions as well as monetary limits, and disputes a United States amicus preservation objection. This is not accurately described as an uncontested absence of preservation issues. The trial finding of no willful misconduct is independently documented at JA31. A narrow resolution can preserve or assume the preservation dispute while explaining why the actual finding or the relationship between Articles 17 and 25 disposes of the offered route. It must not silently convert the allegation of intentional touching into a finding of treaty willfulness or announce that intentional acts can never be accidents.

## Department of the Army v. Blue Fox, Inc. — January 20, 1999

### Posture and supported facts

The Ninth Circuit, 121 F.3d1357, No.96-35648, August25,1997, reversed summary judgment on the Army claim and affirmed rejection of the SBA claim. Ferguson wrote for the panel; Rymer dissented as to the Army and joined the SBA result. The SBA branch is outside the supplied question.

The Army/SBA/Verdan contract concerned installation/testing of a telephone switching system and a building at Umatilla Army Depot. The Army administered and paid the contract. The original solicitation's bond condition was deleted; no payment or performance bond was obtained. The Government conceded the contract was subject to the Miller Act. Written notices reached the Army May26 and June15,1994; it paid Verdan $86,132.33 between July5 and October11,1994. It terminated Verdan for default January3,1995. Those amounts are distinct: unpaid subcontract debt, payments to the prime, unspent completion funding and replacement cost must not be conflated.

The lower opinion records that payment to the replacement contractor was authorized July27,1995, after this suit seeking to stop further payments had been filed. The neutral packet separately states no earned Verdan amount remained withheld. The Ninth Circuit reasoned that the lien attached at notice and later disbursement could not defeat it; that is its legal conclusion, not independently established claimant ownership of a presently retained res.

### Governing law, paths and limits

The focused slice supplies **Nordic Village**, February25,1992, **Lane v. Peña**, June20,1996, **Federal sovereign immunity from affirmative bankruptcy money judgments**, and **Rehabilitation Act federal-program coverage and damages consent**. Nordic Village's reservation of an actual specific-property proceeding requires that proceeding's facts and independent authority. It grants no lien here by analogy.

[APA §702](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title5/pdf/USCODE-1994-title5-partI-chap7-sec702.pdf) waives the immunity defense for relief other than money damages in its stated setting. Its saving clauses preserve other grounds to deny review or relief and forbid construing it to authorize relief another consent-to-suit statute expressly or impliedly forbids. A waiver is distinct from a substantive right, reviewability, final agency action if required, absence of another adequate remedy, and entitlement to relief. **Bowen v. Massachusetts**, 487 U.S.879 (1988), distinguishes substitute compensation from specific relief enforcing the actual entitlement. **Henningsen**, 208 U.S.404, and **Pearlman**, 371 U.S.132, concern surety/subrogation rights; **Munsey Trust**, 332 U.S.234, concerns competing governmental and surety interests. Do not silently turn those relationships or Postal Service sue-and-be-sued cases into a general Army consent.

* **No applicable waiver path:** The requested money substitutes for the private debtor's unpaid obligation, and no specific claimant property or statutory entitlement supports the asserted transfer from the Army. The absence of a payment bond does not enact compensation. The counterargument is that the sought lien is a preexisting property charge on defined contract funds, not a damages judgment, and that notice should prevent defeating it through disbursement. An answer must explain the actual entitlement and remedy, not merely equate every monetary request with damages.
* **Specific-equitable-relief path:** §702 may permit enforcement of an independently established lien against an identified fund, subject to its other restrictions. The strongest counterargument is that naming completion funds does not establish claimant ownership or consent, and recovery after the alleged res has gone may become a general substitute obligation. This route must identify the fund, the actual right, the effect of government setoff/completion claims and the statutory waiver separately. It cannot award from general Treasury assets because equity seems desirable.
* **Narrow property/remedy remand:** Available only if a legally material disputed fund or entitlement remains under a supported waiver analysis. Identify exactly what must be determined and why the existing record cannot decide it. Do not manufacture a fund, order reconstruction of payments, or reopen the SBA dismissal merely to make a remedy possible.

## Humana Inc. v. Forsyth — January 20, 1999

### Exact surviving controversy

The Ninth Circuit decision, 114 F.3d1467, May23,1997, replaced its withdrawn November1996 opinion. It concerns two classes: employer Premium Payors and employee Co-Payors. Under the represented 80/20 allocation, the insurance company allegedly received undisclosed hospital discounts while charging patients 20% of the undiscounted amount. The conduct alleged spans 1985–1988. The court permitted the Co-Payors' RICO claim concerning overpayments to proceed but retained rejection of the Premium Payors' RICO claim for lack of supported specific premium misrepresentations; it did not reach premium causation. It also retained the limitation to injury actually proved as overpayments, rather than other asserted financial injury. A lawful statutory multiplier is separate from the underlying injury amount.

This Supreme Court question concerns McCarran-Ferguson's invalidation/impairment/supersession language. The parties' merits briefs identify the other threshold propositions—RICO not specifically relating to insurance, this conduct as insurance business, and Nevada's regulatory law—as outside the granted dispute. Do not use this case to reopen ERISA, antitrust, Premium Payor fraud, injury measure or unpresented RICO elements. The lower RICO-conspiracy amendment instruction depends on the surviving Co-Payor claim and can follow the reviewed disposition without deciding conspiracy liability.

The lower court treated Nevada's prohibited insurance practices and RICO's private remedy as consistent despite different enforcement machinery. It described Nevada's administrative remedies and Commissioner jurisdiction. That description is not a holding that all Nevada civil remedies are exclusive or nonexistent. Humana's merits brief p.7 n.4 acknowledges the 1987 addition of an express private remedy to §686A.310 while disputing a pre-amendment implied remedy; respondents cite Crystal Bay and Hart for that earlier remedy. ### Entering law and two substantive paths

The focused slice reproduces **Fabe**, June11,1993, **Barnett Bank**, March26,1996, **Insurance liquidation priorities against federal priority**, and **State interference with national-bank powers and insurance agency authority**. Fabe protects state policy-performance rules and administration necessary to implement them against conflicting nonspecific federal law, but not every insurer-related creditor preference. Barnett distinguishes the insurance-specific federal-statute exception from the separate conflict question; RICO does not obtain §92's exception by analogy. Neither decision makes every additional federal remedy an impairment or reduces the question to identical statutory wording.

The operative text is [15 U.S.C. §1012(b), 1994 Code](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title15/pdf/USCODE-1994-title15-chap20-sec1012.pdf). Its insurance-specific exception and separate antitrust proviso retain their distinct functions. The RICO cause, predicate offenses, injury and remedy conditions remain independently necessary after the McCarran question.

* **Complementary federal-remedy path:** Where federal liability for this fraud neither contradicts a state command nor frustrates an actual state policy or administration, RICO may reinforce Nevada's prohibition. Different or greater recovery alone need not displace state insurance regulation. The strongest counterargument is that enforcement choices, including a regulator's authority and calibrated sanctions, can themselves be protected state policy; a court cannot declare harmony from a shared prohibition alone.
* **Impairment path:** A federal action may impair Nevada's intended regulatory scheme if its additional private enforcement or penalties displace a demonstrated choice about who may enforce or what relief is available. The strongest counterargument is the lack of demonstrated exclusivity and Nevada's own allowance of civil remedies; construing every supplemental remedy as impairment would effectively immunize insurance business from general federal law whenever any state regulation exists. Identify the actual state-law choice rather than presume it.
* **Narrow application:** A model can resolve the supported Nevada relationship without announcing that only literal contradiction counts everywhere, or that every stricter federal remedy always survives. No need to decide hypothetically exclusive state schemes, unrelated insurance fraud, criminal RICO, or other federal regulatory statutes unless essential to the adopted rationale.
