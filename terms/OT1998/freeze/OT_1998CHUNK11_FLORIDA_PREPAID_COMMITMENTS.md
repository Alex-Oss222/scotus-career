## 3. Property, constitutional deprivation and the competing §5 applications

All eight recognize the patent as an established exclusionary property interest. Section 261 and the established patent-property authorities, including Hartford-Empire and Consolidated Fruit-Jar as identified in the neutral packet, support that characterization. Federal creation does not remove Fourteenth Amendment protection. Patent infringement alone nevertheless does not prove a deprivation without due process: culpability, authorization and available process/remedies matter under Daniels, Parratt, Hudson and Zinermon. The Act covers more than knowing appropriation and does not require proof that State remedies are unavailable before every statutory action.

**The sufficient §5 ground accepted by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer:** Congress can protect this identified property through a bounded preventive remedy against State infringement. The specific exclusive right, difficulty detecting and proving purposeful appropriation, the failure of prospective injunctions to compensate completed loss, and real uncertainty about state compensation supply a substantive enforcement relationship. Covering some innocent infringement and States that might provide process does not automatically make the remedy disproportionate; prevention may address those proof and remedial gaps without redefining the property right. Their holding sustains §296(a)'s authorization of this action and does not declare every statutory infringement unconstitutional.

The strongest objection is that national uniformity and commercial equality are Article I objectives, not themselves §5 grounds, and that ordinary patent liability reaches innocent conduct and States furnishing adequate remedies. The four supporting Justices answer through the specific existing constitutional property and the practical preventive relationship, not through uniformity alone or a general congressional power to label statutory injuries constitutional. Boerne's refusal to demand individual violation findings gives Congress room to legislate against the supported class of risks; no State-remedy exhaustion prerequisite is added.

**The sufficient objection accepted by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas:** The entire ordinary patent-liability field is materially broader than intentional or otherwise constitutionally culpable State deprivation without adequate process. Applying full patent remedies to innocent infringement and to States furnishing adequate remedies lacks the necessary substantive relationship to the constitutional injury identified in this record and design. Their objection is not a demand for a numerical inventory or proof of a violation in each State. Recognized property and an enforcement benefit do not by themselves justify this degree of abrogation.

The strongest opposing point is that requiring a prior remedial failure in every suit would destroy preventive efficacy and contradict Boerne. These four do not impose that requirement. They reject this statutory fit without prescribing a universal evidence checklist or finding Florida's untested compensation routes adequate. The available evidence of State infringement is not automatically evidence of unconstitutional deprivation; conversely, lack of widespread infringement is not lack of any constitutional injury. Neither side can substitute either slogan for substantive assessment.

## 4. Commercial consent and the controlling College Savings rule

The actual prior-law boundary matters. Welch displaced the inference of federal-forum consent from general statutory coverage. Its four-Justice discussion of an expressly stated prospective condition does not already uphold such a condition. The controlling simulated Hilton opinion retains specialized FELA coverage and competent-state-court enforcement while leaving displaced the old operation-as-federal-waiver inference. Neither decision adjudicates the later explicit 1992 Patent Act terms.

**Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, on their own analysis, would accept** a confined prospective condition: Congress unmistakably subjects specified State commercial conduct to private federal-court enforcement; the State then continues a commercial undertaking it can realistically choose not to operate; consent extends to violations arising from covered post-enactment activity. Florida's competitive investment enterprise is a realistically optional means of furthering education. A public purpose alone does not make this particular business unavoidable. The condition concerns undertaking the business under stated terms, not merely a supposed choice to infringe.

This is a new confined consent development distinguished from old Parden, not a prior controlling two-part test. Congress has substantive Patent Clause authority over the underlying patent conduct; this consent ground rests on accepted conditions rather than unilateral Article I abrogation. General statutory coverage, all commerce participation, regulatory benefits, or unavoidable sovereign activity do not suffice. No signed State waiver, discretionary federal grant, contract with CSB, profit finding or exit-cost evidence is invented.

**College Savings Part III controls.** Announced earlier the same day by Stone-Zsela for five, it holds that a State's continued operation of an otherwise lawful commercial undertaking does not unequivocally submit it to federal jurisdiction merely because Congress clearly and prospectively subjects that activity to private federal enforcement, even when the undertaking is realistically optional. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer did not join it, but they accept it as controlling and do not press the confined condition here. Stevens's Neal adherence to Chapman despite his dissent and his Casey stare decisis join, Souter's Adarand concurrence and Casey join, Ginsburg's Adarand join and Allied-Bruce statutory stare decisis ground, and Breyer's Allied-Bruce opinion declining to overrule Southland and his Adarand join supply their individual stare decisis bases. No changed premise or special justification supports departing from that holding. All eight therefore conclude that Florida Prepaid's continued operation of its prepaid-tuition program after the 1992 enactment is not consent. Actual consent through a distinct State choice, such as express waiver or voluntary invocation of federal jurisdiction, remains available.

**O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas reject** consent from this conduct, applying the College Savings holding they joined. Express federal intent is not Florida's unequivocal submission to a federal forum. Continuing an ordinary lawful commercial venture, even when the State might stop, is not acceptance of a specified federal benefit or invocation of federal judicial authority. The distinction they maintain is between genuine consent and imposed amenability, not between immune governmental purposes and all private commercial purposes.

The real counterarguments are reciprocal. The proponents must answer that an expressly contested jurisdictional term cannot be inferred accepted merely from lawful activity; they rely on prospective notice and a genuinely optional defined venture rather than generic regulation. The opponents must answer that their rule makes even a clear prospective condition ineffective despite real choice; they distinguish ordinary commerce from accepted benefits and litigation submission. Statutory clarity resolves neither disputed conclusion.

**Individual support:** Stevens's complete Union Gas concurrence, his Welch dissent join at 483 U.S.517–519, his actual Hilton join and his Lane/Cedar Rapids positions support the narrower express-condition distinction without reviving the old inference. Souter's actual Hilton join, his Seminole opinion, his remedy-specific Lane opinion, and Cedar Rapids/West demonstrate why clarity and the independent choice inquiry each matter. Ginsburg's Seminole, Lane, Cedar Rapids and West joins support the same new application, with Hess's arm-status distinction preserved. Breyer's Seminole position, Lane dissent join and Cedar Rapids/West joins support meaningful prospective obligations without pretending that ordinary regulatory benefits are a federal grant.

O'Connor's Welch join left future conditions open, but her later simulated Seminole opinion distinguishes actual consent from imposed immunity loss; her funding-notice position in Cedar Rapids rests on a different accepted-assistance predicate. Scalia's own Union Gas statutory/constitutional separation, Welch concurrence, Hilton dissent and simulated Seminole join support rejection. Kennedy's own Hilton opinion and Union Gas/ simulated Seminole constitutional positions distinguish clear coverage from consent. Thomas's actual simulated Seminole join, Lane join and Schacht opinion preserve the same separation; he receives no invented earlier participation. These are individual present applications of prior positions, not claims that an earlier case decided this 1992 condition.
