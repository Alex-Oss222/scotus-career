## Event, review channel, and requested relief

Certiorari under §1254(1) reviews the Third Circuit's final judgment, 131 F.3d 353 (1997), affirming dismissal of CSB's federal false-advertising action. The distinct patent action proceeded separately and is not this judgment. CSB seeks reversal so its Lanham Act claim may proceed; Florida Prepaid seeks affirmance. Neither path decides false advertising, causation, compensable loss or amount.

The filed petitioner-merits brief identifies two questions: (1) §5 power to enact the Trademark Remedy Clarification Act's removal of State immunity for unfair-competition suits alleging false/misleading advertising of a State's own product under §43(a); (2) Parden constructive consent from continued interstate marketing after express statutory notice of federal suit. These source-verified formulations narrow the approved general paraphrase. The [sanitized docket-scope receipt](../sources/chunk11-root-scope/DOCKET_SCOPE_FACTS.md) verifies a generic grant without express restriction or reformulation and actual argument on April 20. The petition questions were independently checked and match the merits formulations in substance. No target merits opinion has been read. Litigation waiver is a separate lower issue, not one of those two questions. Direct Article I abrogation under retained Union Gas is a newly material analytical alternative, not a statement that the historical petitioner advanced that theory as available law. Whether that alternative is properly within the question/record and fairly presented must be addressed before deciding reversal on it; a necessary targeted opportunity for adversarial presentation must rest on a judicial purpose, not an operator source shortage.

## Material facts, lower grounds, and claim boundaries

CSB is a New Jersey bank selling CollegeSure CDs; Florida Prepaid sells college tuition-prepayment products in competition with CSB. The Florida instrumentality's arm-of-State status was established below and is not disputed in the supplied materials. CSB filed the separate Lanham action on August 25, 1995, alleging misleading statements in Florida Prepaid's brochures and annual reports about Florida Prepaid's own product. The claim does not allege appropriation of CSB's trademark or false statements about CSB's product. The distinction is explicit at 131 F.3d 361–62 and in the filed questions. A complaint of lost custom from a competitor's own-product advertising is not automatically loss of an exclusionary property asset.

The United States intervened to defend the statute and supports reversal in its merits filings. The Third Circuit treated §1122's clear intent as satisfied, rejected §5 as applied to this own-product advertising claim because it identified no protected property deprivation, and expressly reserved trademark infringement and false statements about a competitor's goods. It separately rejected Parden waiver on the ground that financing educational opportunity is an important governmental function, while declining to decide whether Parden was implicitly overruled. The District Court had also relied on that latter proposition. The appellate court rejected litigation waiver based on appearance, counterclaim and initial failure to invoke immunity, partly reasoning that the historical Seminole decision changed the defense's availability. The historical change premise does not apply as governing law here. That does not automatically reverse a separate litigation-waiver ruling or enlarge the grant.

CSB's merits brief describes a November 8, 1995 answer/counterclaim for alleged product disparagement or libel; the counterclaim was dismissed March 22, 1996, not appealed, and its merits are outside this petition (printed p.3 n.1). It is not an affirmative live claim to be adjudicated here. A common-law unfair-competition count dismissed below is likewise not restored merely by resolving the federal claim. These scope limits avoid deciding new claims from the same commercial history.

## Issue snapshots and strongest supported paths

**1. Statutory clarity and direct Commerce authority.** Section 1122(a), effective for violations on or after October 27, 1992, expressly removes immunity from suit in federal court for violations of the Lanham Act. Section 1122(b) makes the otherwise available legal/equitable remedies available to the same extent. Section 1125(a)(1)(B) covers specified commercial misrepresentations concerning one's own or another's goods/services/commercial activities. State conduct must still satisfy the substantive statute; clarity of the remedy does not establish that Congress may impose it constitutionally.

Retained Union Gas offers a substantial analytical basis for explicit federal commercial remedies against States; these are interstate marketing transactions rather than an unrelated local activity. The best counterargument distinguishes its exact decided scope and demands resolution of the Eleventh Amendment's express out-of-State-plaintiff setting, State immunity's constitutional source and any special justification for extension or overruling. Seminole neither categorically bars Article I remedies nor transforms any commercial activity into consent. This fork cannot be bypassed by importing historical Seminole's categorical rule. Because the petition presents §5 and Parden, the independent Article I path requires an actual review-scope/fair-presentation determination, with an appropriately confined remand or further briefing if materially necessary.

**2. Section 5 and the claimed property.** CSB's strongest argument distinguishes lawful competitive risk from false advertising and invokes its ongoing business, goodwill, economic relationships and the long recognized protection against unlawful interference. It may invoke Logan's protection of claims and the fact that intangibility is not dispositive. The State's strongest response identifies the precise alleged injury: loss of customers caused by a rival's claims about its own product, without exclusion from CSB's assets or deprivation of an established exclusive right. A newly created statutory tort is not by that fact a constitutional property entitlement. Paul, Roth and the Court's current Haddle distinction require separate statutory and constitutional inquiries; International News Service's unfair-competition/equity discussion does not itself settle the Fourteenth Amendment question.

Even if a qualifying business/property interest is identified, deprivation and due process remain independent. Boerne permits a supported preventive rule extending beyond actual violations, but does not permit Congress simply to relabel every commercial harm a constitutional violation. Both the extent of protected injury and the fit of a private remedy for own-product false advertising must be assessed. A ruling on this branch should not determine all trademark infringement or every false-advertising application. The patent companion's distinct property interest supplies no automatic outcome here.

**3. Parden, notice, voluntariness and government function.** CSB and the United States defend a knowing voluntary condition: Congress gave unmistakable notice, Florida continued the regulated conduct, and commercial investment marketing can realistically be abandoned while education continues through other means. The Government's merits brief, printed pp.24–26, distinguishes consent from unilateral abrogation and identifies clear notice and realistic voluntary choice as its proposed conditions. That is party advocacy, not an existing Court test.

The State responds that engaging in lawful commerce is not express consent to a federal forum, that a generally imposed statutory liability is compulsory abrogation rather than a bargained waiver, and that the program serves education, a central State function. Even if a residual Parden theory survives, the Third Circuit's independent government-function rationale must be confronted. CSB responds that purpose alone should not convert an investment service also supplied by private firms into an immune activity. The public Hilton holding preserves FELA coverage at its actual statutory/reliance scope and does not revive constructive federal-forum waiver. Welch demands unmistakable enacted intent and limits Parden; a statutory clear statement must not silently become a State's unequivocal consent. No broad government/proprietary classification or involuntary waiver rule is already compelled by the existing law.

**4. Litigation conduct and procedure.** The lower litigation-waiver ruling has a changed historical justification, but it is outside the filed questions as presently verified. If a modeler considers that ground outcome-material, first identify why it is properly before the Court and obtain exact preservation/scope support; do not assert that a dismissed counterclaim conclusively waives every State defense. If abrogation independently supplies amenability, no waiver holding is necessary. If §5/Parden grounds fail but Article I remains open, the disposition must address its lawful availability rather than reflexively affirming on a superseded categorical premise.

**Remedy distinctions.** Reversal removes the federal immunity dismissal and returns the claim for ordinary merits adjudication, without finding deceit or damages. Affirmance sustains the reviewed dismissal only on a legally sufficient ground within proper scope. A limited vacatur/remand or focused briefing can address an unresolved legally necessary issue without treating research limits as party default. No order reaches the abandoned counterclaim or creates a new officer suit. Officer relief requires a connected official, ongoing violation, proper claim and genuinely prospective remedy; it is not an automatic damages substitute.

Seminole's direct-State disposition lacks a controlling amenability rationale. Its majority clear-statement proposition does control. Union Gas's damages judgment, statutory holding and five-vote constitutional conclusion remain effective at their decided scope, without making every plurality sentence law. Hans and Fitzpatrick remain effective. The lower opinions' historical assertions that Seminole overruled Union Gas are descriptions of their historical premise and are displaced here. They cannot be imported as governing law.

The plaintiff is a New Jersey bank suing an admitted arm of Florida. This is an interstate private plaintiff setting within the Eleventh Amendment's words, unlike Seminole's tribal plaintiff. Extending the retained Union Gas constitutional conclusion or adopting a broader common-law account requires attention to that difference. Broad constitutional alternatives in individual Seminole writings are not controlling holdings. Stevens's public writing expressly reserved the textual out-of-state/foreign-plaintiff issue; Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer's constitutional account was conditional because their officer ground succeeded. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas publicly advocated a categorical Article I prohibition and overruling Union Gas; that proposal did not become law. These are source-specific prior positions, not predicted commitments here.

Boerne supplies the operative §5 standard through the full Kennedy opinion joined by Stevens, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg, as well as the Chief Justice. O'Connor's conditional view depended on Smith; Souter and Breyer reserved the RFRA merits. No inference that those three adopted either a universal §5 invalidity rule or deference to every statute follows. Their distinct public grounds are preserved in the exact excerpt.

Current-term boundaries: Haddle (December 14, 1998) distinguishes statutory economic injury from constitutional property entitlement; it does not constitutionalize every business tort. Sullivan (March 3, 1999) preserves statutory claims and fair adjudication while requiring identification of the actual substantive entitlement, rather than treating procedure or a challenged demand as conclusive. Blue Fox (January 20) and West (June 14) require remedy-specific enacted authority in their federal-immunity settings; neither decides state constitutional abrogation. West's express monetary authorization illustrates the independent statutory/remedial inquiry, with Kennedy/Scalia/Thomas's distinct forum-condition objection retained only as their published position. Ruhrgas (May 17) preserves the ban on deciding contested merits on assumed jurisdiction; it supplies no automatic immunity sequencing rule. State Street (January 14) decides the actual complete financial-computation claims before it and adopts no categorical business-method prohibition; it establishes no invalidity of CSB's different patent.
