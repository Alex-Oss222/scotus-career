## City of Cincinnati v. Kruse — No. 98-454

### Event, record and review scope

The reviewed judgment is *Kruse v. City of Cincinnati*, 142 F.3d 907 (6th Cir. 1998), Nos. 97-3193, 97-3194 and 97-3210. Ordinance 240-1995 caps a council candidate's or candidate committee's election-cycle expenditures at three times the office's annual compensation, then approximately $140,000. The definition covers contribution or candidate/immediate-family funds used for election or defeat advocacy. It expressly excludes independent expenditures, uncompensated volunteer services, and the listed party slate/sample-ballot publications identifying all endorsed candidates. **The Neutral packet's reference to covered independent spending must not be used:** it is candidate-campaign spending that is capped; independent expenditures are expressly excluded.

Separate contribution and disclosure provisions were enacted in November 1995 and retained with minor amendments in 1997. They are not the challenged expenditure ceiling. The lower opinion reports $1,000 individual, $2,500 PAC/committee and $10,000 party contribution ceilings, no limit on personal funds contributed to one's own campaign, and no independent-expenditure ceiling. Those background provisions receive no independent constitutional judgment here.

The challengers include a losing 1995 candidate who spent more than the cap, his committee, contributors and a potential candidate. They brought a facial challenge. Enforcement was preliminarily enjoined by agreement during the litigation. For their summary-judgment motion, challengers accepted the City's asserted facts as true. The district court nevertheless found the ceiling unconstitutional, and the Sixth Circuit affirmed. The Court must distinguish that assumption from a trial finding or a finding that each asserted interest has been proved compelling.

### Entering law, facts and competing legal grounds

*Meyer v. Grant*, 486 U.S. 414 (1988), protects petition circulation as interactive political advocacy and treats restriction of the available speakers as a speech burden. It does not remove all neutral petition administration. The *Anderson–Burdick* inquiry measures actual burden against precise interests and necessary fit; severe burdens require narrow tailoring to a compelling interest, whereas reasonable nondiscriminatory administrative burdens can receive less demanding review. The completed *Timmons* ruling concerns a distinct candidate-nomination system, not a rule that every petition-speech restriction is minor.

*McIntyre*'s April 19, 1995 controlling rule requires exacting scrutiny—narrow fit to an overriding interest—for compelled attribution on independent individual political leaflets; electoral subject matter alone does not lower scrutiny. Its reserved candidate-finance and organizational settings remain distinct. The live-solicitation badge requires an application of that rule to a different medium, not an assertion that McIntyre already adjudicated this statute.

*Kentucky Right to Life*, September 14, 1998, upheld the bounded facial payer-identification challenge in paid candidate-finance/organizational advocacy while preserving ordinary issue leaflets, materially different individual applications and demonstrated reprisal claims. That decision is a necessary comparison for identifying the actual financer versus every individual messenger. *Buckley v. Valeo* retains finance-disclosure interests and protections against demonstrated associational harm; it does not automatically equate a referendum circulator with a candidate contributor. *Talley*, *Bellotti*, *Citizens Against Rent Control* and *Riley* supply the distinct anonymity, issue-advocacy, collective-expression and paid-speaker principles at their actual scopes.

The lower trial findings, recited on appeal, include reluctance to circulate while wearing personal identification, especially on unpopular issues, and a resulting chilling burden. The lower court distinguished the greater immediacy and accessibility of a badge from later affidavit disclosure. Those are reported findings, not findings that fraud never occurs or that every disclosure produces harassment.
