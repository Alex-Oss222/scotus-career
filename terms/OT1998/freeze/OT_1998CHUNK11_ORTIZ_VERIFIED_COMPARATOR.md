## 8. Ortiz v. Fibreboard Corp. — 527 U.S. 815 (1999), No. 97-1704

**Historical disposition and topology.** June 23, 1999; Fifth Circuit reversed and remanded, seven to two. Souter wrote for Rehnquist, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Ginsburg as well as himself. Rehnquist concurred, joined by Scalia and Kennedy. Breyer dissented, joined by Stevens. All majority joins are complete, despite the additional concurrence.

| Writing/source | Historical rationale and material limits |
|---|---|
| [Souter, Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/97-1704.ZO.html), 527 U.S. 821–865 | Applicants for contested mandatory limited-fund settlement certification under Rule 23(b)(1)(B) must demonstrate a fund limited independently of their agreement and allocate it through a process protecting conflicting claimant interests. Traditional limited-fund actions feature (1) an ascertained maximum fund insufficient for the aggregate claims, (2) dedication of the entire fund to those claims, and (3) equitable treatment of all claimants sharing the recovery theory. Those characteristics are presumptively necessary; proponents must justify departures. Rule history, the Rules Enabling Act, and constitutional avoidance counsel caution, particularly over absent claimants’ jury and due-process interests. Rule 23(e) fairness does not replace the separate safeguards in Rules 23(a) and (b). Here the insurance limit lacked independent evidentiary valuation; it was taken from agreements negotiated amid conflicts because counsel represented inventory claimants whose full recoveries depended on a global settlement or insurance resolution. The class excluded substantial claimants with unsatisfied claims during the settlement negotiations. Present and future claimants, and pre-1959 insured versus later uninsured claimants, required separate structural representation; equal nominal treatment itself allocated value and did not erase their conflicts. Settlement urgency and likely cost savings did not excuse those defects. |
| [Rehnquist, concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/97-1704.ZC.html), 527 U.S. 865; Scalia and Kennedy join | Rehnquist agreed that, on a clean slate, Breyer’s solution and the district court’s efforts would have strong practical appeal. He nevertheless joined the Court because the existing Federal Rules controlled, and called for legislative resolution of asbestos litigation. Practical sympathy is not a narrower or judgment-only join. |
| [Breyer, dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/97-1704.ZD.html), 527 U.S. 865–884; Stevens joins | Breyer would defer substantially to the district court’s detailed findings and the Fifth Circuit’s affirmance in an extraordinary mass-tort setting where nominal individual litigation meant delay, high costs, and likely unpaid claims. A fund’s present value can be limited by the risk of an imminent adverse coverage decision. He considered the separate Trilateral negotiation between Fibreboard and the insurers an adequate arm’s-length benchmark, and regarded precision beyond that realistically obtainable before the coverage decision as unnecessary. He accepted the findings that qualified conflict-free counsel could not be obtained in time and that common interests, settlement terms, and circumstances adequately addressed the asserted subgroup conflicts. He also viewed transaction-cost savings exceeding Fibreboard’s retained value as substantial compliance with the whole-fund purpose. He would uphold limited-fund certification. His final part states that respondents would likely prevail on other issues, but expressly does not definitively decide standing, notice, other Rule 23 requirements, or Rules Enabling Act, bankruptcy, jury-trial, and due-process challenges. |

**Reservations that matter to comparison.** The Court treated certification as logically antecedent to the asserted Article III defects; it did not finally decide them. It did not hold that a mandatory mass-tort limited-fund class is always impossible, select between competing probability standards for fund exhaustion, settle the general notice requirement or sufficiency of this notice, or resolve the state choice-of-law questions. Fibreboard’s retention of almost all its net worth raised a third serious defect, but the Court expressly did not decide whether retention alone was fatal or whether a credit for transaction-cost savings might ever be allowed. It did not resolve how close to insolvency a defendant must be brought. An alternative insurance-dispute certification rationale was also foreclosed by existing class conflicts; the possibility of a differently structured later class was left open. The separate judgment approving the Trilateral settlement was final and not before the Court (n. 8). These are limits on the historical decision, not proposed procedural premises for the simulation.
