## 2. Claims, enacted authority and current law

## 3. Section 5: independently supported common conclusion

Each Associate would reject §5 as a sufficient basis for the present action. CSB's business, goodwill, existing assets and properly established claims can be protected interests, but losing customers because a competitor misdescribes its own product does not establish deprivation of such an asset. A statutory prohibition and compensable economic injury do not alone create a constitutional entitlement to prospective customers or truthful competitive self-advertising. Haddle's unanimous distinction between statutory economic injury and constitutional property is directly material. Sullivan requires identification of the substantive entitlement and preserves fair adjudication of an actual claim; Logan does not constitutionalize every statutory tort.

The strongest competing path identifies the ongoing business, goodwill and freedom from unlawful interference, and argues that Boerne permits preventive protection beyond direct constitutional wrongs. The objection is not that intangible property cannot exist or that Congress must prove a violation in each suit. The defect is the absence of an identified constitutional deprivation to which this particular own-product false-advertising remedy is fitted. Congress cannot create that missing constitutional right merely by creating the tort. This conclusion does not determine trademark infringement, trade-secret appropriation, palming off, false statements about another's goods or every application of §1122. It adopts no universal proposition that all constitutional property must have the exclusionary structure of a patent.

Justice-specific support is not interchangeable. Stevens's full Boerne join and Sullivan separate writing protect actual substantive property without requiring automatic payment or treating every economic harm as deprivation. O'Connor's Boerne reservation depended on Smith's disputed Free Exercise baseline; it supplies no reason to disregard the established property baseline here. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas directly adopted Boerne's enforcement/redefinition limit; Kennedy's Sullivan opinion identifies the actual entitlement. Souter and Breyer's Sullivan concurrence preserves possible individual expectations without declaring them established; their Boerne reservations did not endorse unlimited §5 authority. Ginsburg's full Boerne join and Sullivan concurrence require the same entitlement/fair-adjudication distinction. All eight directly joined Haddle's statutory/constitutional distinction.

## 4. Commercial consent: selected rules, real alternatives and joins

**Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer accept this operative rule:** When Congress unmistakably and prospectively makes specified State commercial conduct subject to private federal-court enforcement, a State that continues a commercial undertaking it can realistically choose not to operate accepts that federal-forum condition for violations arising from the covered post-enactment activity. The undertaking here is the competitive marketing of investment products, a realistically optional means of furthering education. Education's public purpose does not establish that this particular investment enterprise must be maintained. The condition concerns operating the business under stated terms, not merely a supposed choice to violate the law.

This is a new confined consent application, not an already controlling Parden test. It distinguishes the later unequivocal text and prospective voluntary choice from the displaced general-language inference. Congress's underlying power to regulate this interstate marketing comes from the Commerce Clause; the selected amenability rationale is acceptance of the condition, not a general holding that Article I unilaterally abrogates immunity. Neither statutory coverage alone, a general regulatory benefit, the heading “Waiver,” participation in any commerce, an unavoidable sovereign function nor the breadth of the commerce power suffices. No federal grant, signed State waiver, contract with CSB, unverified profit figure or exit-cost finding is invented. Hess does not erase admitted arm status.

**O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas reject that rule as applied here:** Congress's clear command proves its intention to impose liability, not Florida's consent. Continuing an ordinary lawful commercial enterprise does not unequivocally submit the State to federal jurisdiction. The practical option of ceasing that enterprise does not turn general federal regulation into an accepted federal benefit or an actual litigation submission. They reject imputed consent without endorsing the Third Circuit's categorical government-function distinction or denying Congress's power to regulate the underlying conduct.

The strongest objection to the accepted-condition path is that it infers assent to a jurisdictional term from lawful activity despite the State's denial of consent. The four proponents answer through express prospective notice, actual continued covered activity and a realistic optional undertaking, treating the condition as a permissible term of the regulated commercial venture. The strongest objection to the contrary path is that it defeats even an unmistakably stated prospective condition despite genuine choice. The four opponents answer that ordinary commerce differs from accepting a specified federal benefit or invoking judicial authority; clarity is necessary but cannot supply the missing State act of consent. Neither path may simply equate statutory intent and consent or cite a party's proposed test as governing law.
