## United States v. Rodriguez-Moreno — March 30, 1999

### Final propositions and comparison

**R1 — continuing predicate conduct supplies venue.** For this use-or-carriage charge under the applicable §924(c), the defendant's ongoing commission of the kidnapping is essential conduct incorporated by the requirement that the firearm act occur during and in relation to the violent crime. Because that kidnapping continued in New Jersey, venue for this charged offense lies there even though the firearm was used in Maryland. The gun-use or carriage requirement remains independently necessary. A predicate's mere status as a necessary element does not make every related circumstance a geographical act.

**R2 — the two kinds of conduct must coincide.** Section 924(c) punishes using or carrying a firearm during and in relation to the predicate crime; the particular firearm offense is committed where those acts coincide. Kidnapping may continue across districts, and gun use or carriage may itself continue, but the unarmed portion of the kidnapping does not extend this firearm offense to New Jersey. On the frozen allegations, venue fails there.

The historical result was reversal, with O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer joining the Court's incorporated-conduct rule; Stevens and Scalia dissented. Those eight judgment positions match the frozen commitments. The actual dissent, 526 U.S. 282–285, expressly recognizes the predicate as an element and permits a continuing firearm offense wherever the required acts occur simultaneously. It does not adopt an exclusive-verb test or treat gun use as the entire offense. R2 therefore clarifies the frozen shorthand describing kidnapping as a qualifying circumstance: these two Justices do not deny the predicate's conduct, but deny that its unarmed geographical segments locate the distinct firearm offense. The frozen two-path comparison already presented temporal overlap and the statutory relation; this clarification uses no new fact, charge or unpresented legal issue and changes no judgment.

The six R1 Justices retain the independent statutory requirement of firearm conduct and the distinction between continuing violent conduct here and completed criminal provenance in Cabrales. Historical note 4 confirms that distinction; note 2 reserves the Government's broader effects theory. Accordingly, this decision adopts no effects-only venue theory and no new universal rule prohibiting every effects-based venue statute or theory. Cabrales's own limits remain. No count, sentencing question or one-violation-per-predicate issue outside the granted venue question is decided.

## Wyoming v. Houghton — April 5, 1999

### Final propositions and comparison

**H1 — object-based automobile scope.** Given the conceded probable cause to search this automobile for drugs, police may search the detached purse found on its rear seat because it can conceal the drugs, notwithstanding its known passenger ownership. Labron's object-based scope, Ross and Acevedo supply the search authority; Wellons preserves the purse's personal privacy interest without defeating an applicable exception. Search authority does not establish the passenger's guilt or a common criminal enterprise.

**H2 — proposed qualification for identified passenger effects.** When the vehicle probable cause derives from another occupant and officers know before intrusion that a closed personal effect belongs to a passenger, that vehicle probable cause alone should not authorize opening it without probable cause connecting the effect to the contraband or another independently applicable search ground. This proposal qualifies the existing object-based container rule; Wellons alone does not already create it. It neither reinstates a universal luggage-warrant rule nor immunizes an effect reached by an independently valid search ground.

The historical result was reversal. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer joined the Court; Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg dissented. All eight frozen judgment positions are retained. The majority's historical and practical reasons support H1: ownership does not change a container's capacity to hold the sought object, while the passenger's person has distinct protection. Its discussion of possible common enterprise and concealment is not a finding about this passenger and is not needed as an individualized guilt premise. The frozen H1 rationale does not adopt the whole historical opinion as an unexamined new Fourth Amendment methodology.

The historic dissent's privacy and individualized-justification objections support H2, but its description of the passenger limitation as already settled cannot override the simulated Labron and Wellons holdings. Those holdings require the dissenters to identify the exception as a proposed qualification. Carter/Johns and Knowles reinforce separate examination of protected privacy, justification and remedy; neither already adopts H2.

**Source conflict and Breyer:** The historical concurrence, 526 U.S. 308, says ownership was not claimed until identification was discovered. The historical Court opinion, id. at 298, places the claim of ownership before the wallet opening, consistent with the frozen neutral packet. The reconciliation uses that frozen sequence and does not attribute ignorance of ownership to the officer. Breyer's reversal remains independently supported by his concurrence's first paragraph, which rejects making search authority turn on a passenger's ownership claim, and its concluding distinction between a detached purse and an item attached to the person. His reasoning does not require treating a purse as devoid of personal privacy. History informs constitutional judgment; it does not automatically decide it. A purse worn or held as part of the person presents a different, unadjudicated boundary.
