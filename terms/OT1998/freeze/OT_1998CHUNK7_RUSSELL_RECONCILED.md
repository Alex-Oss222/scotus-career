## Grounds tested against the complete lower-court record

### Governing review and the two unanimously invalidated categories

Buckley distinguishes direct contributions from independent expenditures. Contribution restrictions must fit a sufficiently important anticorruption or appearance interest while preserving meaningful political association; equality of political influence is not an independent justification. The State need not show a completed bribe. A low dollar figure, inflation comparison, successful campaign or source of self-financing is not itself a dispositive constitutional test. The lower court's evaluation of the facts is considered under the appropriate review of supported subsidiary findings; the ultimate constitutional fit remains a legal judgment.

For the seven enumerated $300 offices, the actual campaign scale and fundraising burden differ materially from the legislative examples offered to justify the low ceiling. The approximately $900,000 gubernatorial scale and the smaller relative gift shares make transposition of the legislative proof insufficient here. This is not a universal rule that a small share can never create corruption concerns, a finding that every statewide candidate has identical finances, or a constitutional entitlement to the preexisting $1,000 amount. All eight conclude that the reviewed restriction lacks adequate fit at the established scope.

For the two named appellate judicial offices, the record supplies the combined burdens of electoral geography, the prohibition on personal solicitation or acceptance, and the committee fundraising period from 180 days before the primary until 45 days after the last contested election. The district concluded that the combination prevented resources adequate for effective advocacy. Lawful alternative financing and judicial impartiality are relevant contrary arguments, but the supplied record does not establish that they cure that combination. All eight preserve the invalidation at these two offices without invalidating the solicitation canon or every judicial contribution rule. Suster's expenditure holding and recognition of impartiality do not decide this contribution case automatically.

### Remaining $100 offices

The five Associates rejecting categorical invalidation rely on the evidence of appearance and practical operation, not an assertion that there was no burden. The Maddox fundraiser, tobacco-related contributions and financial concentration were evidence of a perceived influence problem even though the district credited denials of improper access and found no actual undue influence. Agreement between donor and candidate does not itself constitute corruption. The testimony and actual special-election fundraising experience, including reported Senate fundraising of $24,000 and $40,000, support feasible effective campaigns in the demonstrated legislative setting. The limits reset by election. The hypothetical donor-count arithmetic is not treated as actual recruitment history.

The sharp reduction and the fact that up to eighty percent of prior legislative contributions would be affected are serious contrary evidence. Professional fundraisers disputed the favorable assessment, and incumbent or successful-candidate experience does not establish universal adequacy. Nevertheless, the five distinguish changed fundraising from a demonstrated generally prohibitory burden and find the actual affirmative evidence and operation sufficient for this tried challenge. They impose no new requirement of proving campaign impossibility.

The clean repair has a substantive effect: those five no longer extend the district's two-office exception to all judicial candidates. The residual ruling rejects the grounds for a blanket invalidation across the remaining covered offices; it does not transfer legislative costs or successes into findings about trial judges or every other office. Shared solicitation restrictions alone do not establish the appellate offices' combined geographic and temporal burden in every other judicial race. A genuinely different office-specific claim retains its proper legal and procedural treatment if actually presented; no such claim is invented, declared waived or presumed absent because the full transcript was not retrieved. A compulsory factual survey of every possible office is not required merely to correct the source label.

Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas reach the opposite conclusion from the same record. The broad eighty-percent impact and extensive fundraising reorganization show a substantial association burden, while the appearance evidence and limited campaign examples do not sufficiently justify this uniform restrictive amount or answer the burden on other candidates. Their general fit objection covers the residual offices without claiming all judicial candidates share the two appellate offices' particular facts. Their position does not require completed bribery, a fixed dollar minimum or expenditure scrutiny for candidate contributions.

### Ordinary-PAC receipts

The five retain reversal of invalidation of the $200 annual per-person/per-PAC ceiling. The full district opinion's paragraph 54 identifies years of operation and robust PAC candidate giving, with no appreciable demonstrated infringement on effective advocacy in the tried case. That is an actual account of the record, not an invented waiver. Arkansas's ordinary PACs lack the federal multicandidate qualifications requiring at least fifty contributors and contributions to at least five candidates; the potential candidate-transfer channel is concrete rather than a categorical assumption that every independent committee is corrupt. The limit is per recipient, not Kentucky Right to Life's single annual pool across all intermediaries.

The five therefore distinguish Day's established pooling burden and insufficient fit and Kentucky Right to Life's unresolved aggregate breadth. The annual duration, recipient function, actual operation and amount are considered together. The opportunity to support other committees does not alone cure a burden on a chosen association. No rule approves every cap on independent committees or permits realistic donor pooling to be replaced by a nominal opportunity. A materially different operational burden or recipient function remains open.

The three affirming invalidation find the annual $200 ceiling reaches ordinary PAC support too broadly in relation to the identified candidate conduit. Aggregate PAC success does not prove meaningful opportunity for each donor to associate through the chosen recipient, and the lack of federal qualifications does not by itself establish why this low blanket annual restraint is fitted. They preserve tailored earmark, coordination and conduit rules. Neither group treats contributions to PACs as automatically identical to independent expenditures or treats California Medical's four-Justice lowest-protection reasoning as a universal controlling rule.

### Small-donor differential

The five sustain this precisely defined financial classification. A committee financed exclusively by annual individual contributions of $25 or less disperses the financing source and attenuates individual control. Ordinary PACs may receive larger individual amounts and contributions from entities. Those actual differences in financial structure support differentiated candidate-giving allowances under the anticorruption interest, without a viewpoint preference or an equalization rationale. The asserted compelling anticorruption purpose and fit of this exact classification do the work; the result does not rest on rational-basis review or the bare ability to form a different committee.

The strongest contrary argument concerns the candidate's obligation to the committee that delivers $2,500: diffuse contributors do not eliminate committee-level influence. The five acknowledge that risk, but conclude the narrow annual-individual qualification sufficiently differentiates the regulated channel; constitutional fit does not require identical ceilings for materially different financial structures. They do not declare small-donor committees incapable of corruption, authorize unlimited candidate contributions, or treat their labeling as dispositive. The greater allowance remains subject to all qualifying terms.

Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas affirm invalidation because the qualification does not sufficiently answer that recipient-end risk. The committee itself can seek influence with its larger transfer regardless of dispersed intake. On their assessment, the State has inadequately justified the unequal restriction on other associations' candidate support. They do not invalidate every source classification or every multimember qualification. The ability to reorganize as a different association does not cure the inadequate fit they identify.
