## Source and actual claim

The authorized objective source is the Washington Supreme Court's December 24, 1998 opinion, [Brower v. State, 137 Wash. 2d 44, 969 P.2d 42](https://openjurist.org/137/wash2d/44/brower-v-state). The saved source's numbered paragraphs 68–72, under **One Person—One Vote**, address a federal equal-protection theory separately from the wealth-based ballot-access and compressed-campaign claims. Paragraphs 73–77 then address the distinct Washington constitutional free-and-equal-vote theory. Only this earlier lower opinion was read; no Supreme Court comparator or current modeling material was supplied.

Brower argued that the team affiliate's ability to decide whether to agree to reimburse state and local election costs itself constituted a vote. The asserted inequality concerns the legal effect of that private consent decision, not merely the affiliate's larger campaign budget or the ordinary counting of a ballot. The lower court invoked Reynolds v. Sims's protection of equal electoral participation and the equal-weight principle, with White v. Regester cited for invidious district-based dilution. It rejected the federal claim because the actual election was statewide, involved no districts, afforded the affiliate as an entity no vote, and did not weight another citizen's vote more heavily than Brower's.

The subsequent state constitutional discussion characterized reimbursement as a condition on the legislation's effectiveness, rather than a grant of a voting right. That characterization helps identify the operation being challenged. The independent state constitutional standard and its disposition do not become a new federal holding. In particular, the federal one-person-one-vote claim cannot be omitted merely because the adjacent state claim uses similar facts.

## Entering law and lawful analysis

Reynolds's protection against unequal weight in governmental electoral choice supplies the claimed federal principle. The argument must be evaluated at the actual operation challenged. The absence of district boundaries does not establish that every statewide scheme is immune from equal-vote scrutiny, but a private commercial or reimbursement decision is not automatically an electoral ballot simply because it affects whether a legislatively proposed arrangement will proceed. White's district-dilution setting must remain distinct; this source establishes no racial or political-group district dilution.

The strongest analytical formulation for Brower is that conditioning an election on a single beneficiary's assent gives that entity a legally decisive role unavailable to ordinary citizens. This is a characterization of the supported reimbursement arrangement, not a newly found private ballot, delegation of counting power, exclusion of voters, or privately altered tally. The response is that the legislature referred a proposal on a financial contingency and the public alone voted to approve or reject it; the affiliate's willingness to incur its own obligation neither added an electoral vote nor changed any citizen's voting weight. The legal character and effect of the condition must be stated rather than assumed from the word vote or from a campaign-resource disparity.

The existing **Write-in voting within the complete ballot-access system** rule, present operation effective Burdick, June 8, 1992, retains the contextual Anderson inquiry for actual electoral burdens. It does not convert every economic influence into a voting-weight disparity or make alternative petition access a universal answer to a concrete unequal-vote claim. Current-term Yoshina distinguishes the legal consequences of an election rule from falsely attributed political expression and preserves review of fundamentally unfair administration. Reform Party rejects the presented parity entitlement while preserving supported exclusion or suppression. Those holdings assist classification at their actual scope; none directly decides the reimbursement-as-vote theory.

A disposition must therefore state whether the private consent condition is a governmental voting allocation implicating the asserted equal-weight principle and, if it is, identify the actual unequal electoral authority and required justification. A conclusion that it is a financing contingency rather than a voting allocation must still preserve separate review of the other federal claims. No finding of fraud, intimidation, unequal counting, unlawful voter exclusion, an intentional suppression scheme, or ordinary voters' consent to a constitutional violation may be invented. The existing record contains no private ballot or unequal citizen weighting as found below.
