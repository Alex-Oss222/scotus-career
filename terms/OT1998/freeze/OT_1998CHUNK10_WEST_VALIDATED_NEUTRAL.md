## Identity, participation, question and scope

The Secretary of Veterans Affairs challenges the Seventh Circuit's premise that the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission cannot award compensatory damages to a federal employee under Title VII. The employee, Gibson, seeks to sustain the reinstatement of his district-court damages claim. The principal question is whether §717(b), read with §102 of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, gives the EEOC this remedial authority despite the statutory reference to an action and the jury-election provision. Whether Gibson actually exhausted the available administrative process or has another preserved answer to an exhaustion defense is distinct.

The seated Court consists of Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. ## Objective record and lower judgments

Gibson, a VA accountant, was denied promotion in 1992. His administrative complaint alleged sex discrimination and sought GS-11 backpay and a transfer. The VA rejected the discrimination complaint; the EEOC found discrimination and ordered promotion with backpay. Gibson sued on January 11, 1996, seeking enforcement and additional relief, including compensation for humiliation, mental anguish and emotional distress. Subsequent agency compliance mooted enforcement of the promotion/backpay directions.

The district court rejected compensatory damages for nonexhaustion. It separately rejected frontpay and transfer relief and retained a fee request associated with delayed compliance. The Seventh Circuit reversed the compensatory-damages component because it concluded that the EEOC could not grant such relief. Its three grounds were the judicial meaning of action, the any-party jury election coupled with the agency's lack of judicial review of an adverse EEOC decision, and strict construction of sovereign consent. Those grounds are lower-court reasoning, not controlling Supreme Court law. The separate frontpay, transfer and fee components are not automatically reopened by the question of EEOC power.

The Government characterizes compensatory damages as not requested administratively. Gibson disputes the dispositive exhaustion inference: he reports a monetary cash-award request in settlement discussions, lack of counseling about the damages remedy, and completion of the prescribed procedures. These are competing litigation positions; they establish neither actual exhaustion nor estoppel. There is no supplied finding of the amount of injury, entitlement to a particular compensatory sum, intentional official misrepresentation or any other fact needed for an affirmative estoppel ruling.

Respondent's printed pp.31–39 make two separate alternatives concrete. First, Gibson argues that a timely factual discrimination complaint, investigation and cooperation exhaust the claim without pleading each category of damages; he invokes the complaint's mandatory contents under 29 C.F.R. §1614.106(c), full-relief provisions, the reported cash-award request, and the relationship between the judicial injury and the original discrimination. He says he has not asserted a distinct harassment, retaliation or intentional-infliction claim. Second, he invokes counselors' asserted written-advice duty under §1614.105(b) and agency oversight under §1614.104(b), claiming failure to advise and investigate. He expressly cannot say whether the failure was intentional or negligent. The Government asserts a law-of-the-case obstacle to renewed exhaustion inquiry; Gibson contests that obstacle under Christianson. These are advocacy and cited regulatory propositions, not new findings or adopted rules. The requested alternative relief is affirmance or remand for further consideration.

## Enacted scheme and separate legal components

The applicable scheme is 42 U.S.C. §2000e-16(b)–(d), §1981a(a)(1), (b), (c) and definitions, together with §2000e-5(g). Section 717(b)'s grant of enforcement authority and appropriate remedies must be read with the later, express compensatory-damages authorization. Section 717(c)'s employee civil-action route and subsection (d)'s incorporation of relevant Title VII provisions are distinct from the administrative enforcement delegation.

The cleared statutory appendix gives §717(b)'s operative delegation: “Except as otherwise provided in this subsection, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission shall have authority to enforce the provisions of subsection (a) of this section through appropriate remedies, including reinstatement or hiring of employees with or without back pay, as will effectuate the policies of this section,” followed by rule, regulation, order and instruction authority. Section 1981a(a)(1) begins, “In an action brought by a complaining party under section 706 or 717,” covers unlawful intentional discrimination rather than disparate impact, requires that the complaining party cannot recover under §1981, and permits the subsection (b) damages in addition to §706(g) relief. The §1981a(d)(1)(A) definition includes a person who may bring an “action or proceeding” under Title VII. Both sets of words must receive attention; the definition does not by itself settle what proceeding can award the new remedy.

Section 717(c) separately permits the aggrieved employee's civil action within 90 days of notice of the specified final action, or after 180 days from the specified initial charge or EEOC appeal while final action remains untaken, naming the department, agency or unit head as appropriate. No new timeliness dispute is established here. The statutory appendix marks omissions and has interleaved OCR on facing pages; no omitted provision is treated as absent law, and no corrupted page sequence is quoted as enacted text.

Section 1981a's new compensatory category must remain distinct from §706(g) backpay, interest on backpay and other relief authorized there. Its intentional-discrimination trigger, statutory limits, exclusion of punitive damages against government respondents, and conditional jury election each retain their own terms. The jury provision allows any party to demand a jury when the complaining party seeks available §102 damages and prohibits informing the jury of the statutory caps. Availability of this category, authority to administer it, exhaustion, proof of actual injury, and the amount of recovery are separate issues. The 1992 conduct postdates enactment; Landgraf's preenactment exclusion does not itself defeat this claim.

The predecision statutory reproductions identified in the source map supply the text. No administrative interpretation can create federal consent that the enacted scheme fails to give; conversely, deciding how an existing express damages authorization operates within the administrative/judicial scheme is not answered merely by labeling it a new waiver. Any additional deference argument must be identified from its actual source before being attributed to a litigant or used as an independently sufficient ground.

## Supported positions and decisional paths

1. **Administrative authority from the coordinated enactments.** The Government argues that Congress gave the EEOC broad authority to enforce federal Title VII by appropriate remedies and then expressly added compensatory relief for intentional federal employment discrimination. On this reading the later statute supplies the remedial category while §717(b) supplies the administrative channel; the jury election operates when a damages claim is tried in court. This reading must answer the term action, the agency's inability to demand judicial review of an adverse administrative award, and the need for unequivocal enacted consent. Administrative efficiency or remedial purpose alone cannot supply the answer.
2. **Judicially confined damages authority.** Gibson argues that action and the any-party jury election condition the added monetary waiver on judicial adjudication. Since the agency cannot obtain judicial review of an EEOC award, an administrative award would impose the new liability without a condition Congress attached. This reading must account for §717(b)'s broad remedial delegation, the express inclusion of federal employment in the 1991 Act, and the possibility that the jury condition governs the later judicial stage without limiting every antecedent administrative remedy. Lane concerns different statutory language and does not dispose of that choice.
3. **Alternative exhaustion disposition or limited remand.** If the Court finds administrative damages authority, it must separately address the proper handling of Gibson's actual-exhaustion and estoppel arguments. The authority determination does not establish that he failed to exhaust, waived an argument, satisfied an exception, or proved estoppel. A remand for the lower court to address preserved alternatives is available where the controlling question can be decided without resolving disputed facts. An affirmance on an alternative ground requires a supported, properly presented ground; it may not rest on an invented factual finding. A decision sustaining the lower court's no-power ground likewise must state the resulting exhaustion consequence without awarding unproved damages.

These are competing supported routes, not recommendations. Neither party's preferred construction disposes of proof of compensable loss or eliminates separate statutory conditions. The Government's source account is not Gibson's concession, and respondent's alternative argument is not a finding that a counselor caused a legally sufficient failure of notice.
