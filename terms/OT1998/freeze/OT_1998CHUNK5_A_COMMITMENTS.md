## Minns v. United States — No. 98-903

### Reach, components, and bounded record

All eight accept the supplied plenary review under §1254(1) of the children's FTCA claims. The wives' separate claims and MCA finality ruling are outside this grant. All eight preserve the distinction between alleged injury and proved causation, and between a judicial immunity rule and the statutory discretionary-function exception. The lower court supplied two independently sufficient grounds; reversal of its Feres ground alone cannot reverse the judgment.

The personal-injury allegations concern military substance selection, combinations, testing, storage, administration, warnings, and alleged exposure through servicemen or through civilian contact. No manifested paternal injury is conceded to be a required element of the children's claims. No binding directive, individual officer's policy responsibility, recognized state tort, or causal mechanism is found by this handoff. The disagreement below concerns what can lawfully be concluded from these allegations at dismissal, not whether missing research materials constitute litigant default.

### Two lawful paths: Feres

**Application to the challenged military preparations.** Feres bars service-incident injuries to servicemen; Stencel prevents circumvention through a third-party claim whose necessary predicate is the same protected military relationship. Shearer and Johnson make interference with military discipline and review of service decisions relevant even when the alleged tortfeasor is civilian. O'Connor concludes that these particular claims would adjudicate the safety of inoculation and pesticide decisions made for the fathers' Gulf War service. On her proposed rule, changing the person who suffers the ultimate injury does not remove that necessary incident-to-service inquiry. A proved paternal physical injury is not necessary to that rule.

Its strongest objection is that the children seek compensation for their own bodily injuries, not the fathers' losses, indemnification, or lost companionship. The FTCA permits some civilian claims caused by military conduct, while §§2680(a), (j), and (k) protect particular governmental interests on their own terms. O'Connor accepts that civilian status alone neither establishes nor defeats immunity, but considers this direct challenge to preparation of servicemen sufficiently tied to the protected military relationship. Her commitment is confined to these alleged preparations; it is not a rule barring every civilian injury with a military causal ancestor.

**Refusal to extend Feres by causal genesis.** Stevens, Scalia, Souter, and Ginsburg would hold that a child's claim for the child's own physical injury is not barred merely because military treatment or exposure of a parent appears in the causal chain. Feres, Stencel, and Johnson do not themselves hold that every civilian personal injury arising from such conduct is derivative of a service injury. Brown demonstrates that identifying an earlier service-related event does not finish the inquiry. Ordinary state-law requirements and enacted FTCA exceptions remain independent obstacles.

The strongest objection is that adjudicating these claims may require exactly the scrutiny of military preparation that would be barred in a father's own suit. These four reject that objection as a sufficient categorical Feres rule: civilian military-tort claims can involve the same scrutiny, and Congress supplied actual exceptions. Ginsburg's Lombard writing directly supports the distinction between personal injury and derivative relational loss. Its thirty-five-year interval and possible post-service acquisition of knowledge are absent here and do no work. Her narrower commitment is against automatic genesis classification, not that every failure to warn after military exposure is independently actionable. Scalia's Johnson dissent, joined by Stevens, supplies an express refusal to enlarge the nontextual exception and preserves the work of §2680(a).

Kennedy, Thomas, and Breyer reserve the extension of Feres to these children's own injuries because their statutory ground sustains dismissal. They neither join O'Connor's affirmative Feres application nor count as votes for the four-Justice rejection. All eight leave Feres's existing service-injury holdings intact; none proposes overruling Feres in this case.

### Two lawful paths: independent §2680(a)

**Dismissal on protected policy selection.** O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas, and Breyer conclude that the pleaded selection and combined use of substances, protective measures, associated risk evaluation, and warning decisions challenge choices susceptible to military-readiness and health-risk policy judgment. Berkovitz requires compliance with a specifically prescribed course; Gaubert rejects a categorical planning/operations division and protects qualifying policy discretion even when abused. No actual binding directive removing the disputed discretion is identified. On this record these four treat the asserted storage, administration, and warning failures as allegations against how the selected protective program was implemented, without an established separable duty that can be adjudicated without selecting the disputed policy.

The strongest objection is that no-directive allegations establish only room for choice, not that every choice is protected policy. Ordinary mishandling, nonpolicy execution, or violation of an actual fixed instruction does not become immune because it occurred in a military program. These four accept that legal limitation. They conclude, as in their actual Aktepe joins, that the claims presented have not separated an independent manageable duty from the challenged policy choices. They do not find that no such instruction could exist, that any plaintiff withheld one, or that military subject matter alone proves the exception. Their application sustains this dismissal rather than ordering exploration of possible distinct claims.

**Confined partial vacatur for execution allegations.** Stevens, Scalia, Souter, and Ginsburg accept §2680(a)'s protection of the actual military choice of substances, combinations, and warnings when the choice is entrusted policy judgment. They would sustain dismissal insofar as the claim requires a court to select that policy, but vacate dismissal of the children's asserted storage, administration, and warning-execution claims insofar as the lower court treated all such conduct as policy by its military subject. On remand the courts must identify the alleged act, any actual mandatory direction, the nature of permitted discretion, and whether the discretion is of the protected policy kind. This is a claim-specific legal inquiry under Berkovitz/Gaubert, not a new presumption of negligence, a mandatory discovery order, or a requirement that every agency decisionmaker testify about subjective deliberations.

The strongest objection is that relabeling the same policy dispute as execution cannot evade immunity and that hypothetical manufacturer or safety instructions establish no binding command. These four agree. Their remand depends on the lower court's categorical treatment of distinct alleged conduct, not a finding that the supposed instructions exist or govern. Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg's Aktepe dissent demands examination of execution against design without guaranteeing a surviving claim. Scalia's Gaubert concurrence provides an additional, narrower objection: the policy character of a choice depends on the officer's assigned responsibilities as well as the subject matter. He does not infer policymaking authority for every person storing or administering a substance. Gaubert's actual bank-takeover condition supplied a policy link there that has not been established for every allegation here.

## Arizona Department of Revenue v. Blaze Construction — No. 97-1536

### Reach and competing doctrinal paths

All eight accept §1257 review of the final state appellate judgment. Legal incidence falls on Blaze, an independent BIA contractor treated as a nonmember for the affected Arizona reservations. Its Blackfeet organization and Indian ownership do not make it one of the affected tribes. The contracts at issue are federal contracts; the conceded-out housing-authority contracts are not restored to the assessment. Economic federal payment and tribal benefit do not transfer legal incidence to the United States or a tribe.

**Federal-contractor path.** New Mexico permits a nondiscriminatory state tax on an independent federal contractor unless an applicable federal immunity or controlling federal-law displacement supplies a different result. Federal payment, supervision, and the governmental importance of the work are not themselves incorporation into the United States. United States v. California preserves the incidence/economic-burden distinction. The tax here reaches Blaze's receipts from its BIA road-work contracts; no direct federal tax, incorporated federal instrumentality, or controlling exemption is established. The location and beneficiaries of the work do not by themselves turn the BIA's contractor into a tribal contracting instrumentality.

The strongest objection is that Indian preemption is not identical to ordinary federal instrumental immunity. Bracker and Ramah examine the actual federal, tribal, and state interests and can bar taxes on non-Indians. The eight accept that point and therefore address the specific asserted federal Indian policies rather than treating taxpayer identity as the answer to every preemption argument. They nevertheless reject replacing the independent-federal-contract rule with an inference of immunity from reservation location, comprehensive supervision of federally purchased work, and the reduced purchasing power of federal funds alone. Bracker involved the particular federal and tribal timber operation; Ramah involved the tribally controlled school-construction undertaking. Their holdings survive in those settings and are not converted into a categorical exemption for every BIA supplier.

**Particularized Indian-preemption path.** Blaze has a substantial argument from the close federal control of reservation-road work, tribal participation in project priorities, and Arizona's lack of planning, inspection, permitting, or maintenance of these particular projects. The inquiry could favor preemption if those features established interference with a concrete governing federal or tribal arrangement rather than simply an indirect cost. But Cotton Petroleum rejects treating an indirect economic effect as sufficient, demanding equivalence between each tax dollar and a connected service, or ignoring the State's actual general services. Here the State supplies highways and general governmental services; no finding establishes a particular reduction of road mileage or a tax-specific displacement of tribal project control. Even testing Blaze's asserted program interests on their terms, those interests do not furnish the required federal-law displacement on this record.

This alternative examination is not a new universally applicable balancing formula. The eight can join a rule confined to these ordinary federal contracts and these asserted grounds. They need not decide the treatment of a materially different federal enactment, a genuine federal instrumentality, a discriminatory tax, a direct tribal-incidence tax, or a tribal contract.

### Specific federal-policy arguments and remedy

The transaction-period §271.4(d) policy includes the tribe's option not to request a self-determination contract; §271.4(e) addresses the BIA's imposition of sanctions for contracting or not contracting. The strongest contrary argument is that different tax consequences pressure tribes to administer the program themselves. But the quoted provisions regulate the Bureau's treatment of that choice; they do not guarantee identical downstream state-tax consequences for every lawful contracting arrangement. No sanction by the Bureau is established. Their 1996 replacement does not resolve the assessment for 1986–1990.

The Buy Indian preference claim likewise receives an independent answer. Federal authority to prefer Indian contractors does not by itself exempt the successful independent contractor from an otherwise lawful gross-receipts tax, and the cited procurement provisions do not impose the claimed universal state-tax equivalence between affiliated and nonaffiliated contractors. Blaze's conceded nonmember status in these reservations matters to tribal tax immunity; it does not deny the firm's separate eligibility for a federal procurement preference. No discriminatory-tax question is decided through this conclusion.

All eight would reverse the judgment directing a blanket preemption judgment for Blaze and remand for proceedings consistent with tax permissibility on these contracts. Blaze's still-unadjudicated credits for taxes paid by subcontractors and suppliers remain available under their governing law. The Court must not fix the final net assessment, reinstate excluded tribal contracts, or decide those credits itself.

## Peguero v. United States — No. 97-9217

### Actual certificate and threshold alternatives

The July 31, 1997 judicial certificate exists and identifies ground five of the amended §2255 motion: whether omission of the Rule 32(a)(2) advice requires resentencing to restore appellate rights. The motion, request, and certificate postdate AEDPA. The unappealed counsel claim and the adverse factual findings are outside that certified and granted question. The government's former concurrence and petitioner's preservation objection cannot create subject-matter jurisdiction.

**Treat the erroneous certificate as a jurisdictional failure.** Section 2253(c)(2) allows issuance only after a substantial constitutional-right showing; a bare procedural-rule error is not necessarily constitutional. Hohn preserves that condition and does not validate an issued certificate simply by recognizing review of certificate proceedings. If absence of the required showing invalidated the certificate as an appellate-jurisdiction predicate, the proper action would be vacatur of the circuit merits judgment and remand for dismissal of the unauthorized appeal, not affirmance on collateral merits. Agreement by the parties would not cure it.

The strongest objection is statutory structure: §2253(c)(1) makes actual judicial issuance the condition for taking the appeal, whereas (c)(2) directs the issuing judge's eligibility determination and (c)(3) requires identified issues. A judicially issued certificate is an actual act admitting the appeal; the possible error in issuing it does not mean no certificate ever issued. Treating every later disagreement with the substantial-showing judgment as a retroactive absence of appellate power confuses eligibility review with the existence of the judicial authorization.

**Recognize the issued certificate and reach the bounded appeal.** All eight choose the second path on this record. The certificate was issued by the authorized district judge, identifies the actual Rule 32 question, and brought that question to the court of appeals. Section 1254(1) therefore permits review of the resulting appellate case. The proposed threshold rule is that the alleged defect in the judge's constitutional-showing assessment does not nullify this issued, issue-specific authorization for appellate-jurisdiction purposes. It does not dispense with §2253(c)(2), direct future certificates for bare rules claims, or hold that the present claim made a substantial constitutional showing.

This resolves the asserted power defect rather than assuming jurisdiction because the merits are simple. It neither relies on waiver of jurisdiction nor recharacterizes the rejected ineffective-assistance claim as part of this appeal. Hohn's majority and dissent disagreed about the uncertificated threshold proceeding; neither adopted a rule making an actually issued certificate disappear whenever its substantive eligibility assessment is later contested. The government can correctly identify an issuance error without establishing the claimed absence of appellate power. Whether and how a timely direct challenge should correct an issued certificate in a different procedural setting is not decided. No temporal uncertainty from Hohn is imported into this entirely post-AEDPA case.

### Merits alternatives and operative rule

**Automatic restoration.** Mandatory judicial advice protects a defendant's informed appellate choice. A per se restoration rule avoids unreliable retrospective knowledge hearings and ensures that a sentencing judge's omission is not excused by another person's supposed advice. Rodriquez forbids requiring a defendant actually deprived of appeal to demonstrate likely appellate success. These are the strongest grounds for resentencing to permit a new appeal.

**Causal collateral injury.** Hill and Timmreck distinguish formal violations of criminal rules from a fundamental defect or omission inconsistent with rudimentary fair procedure sufficient for collateral relief. The judicial duty remains mandatory, but where unchallenged findings establish actual knowledge of the right to appeal and a voluntary choice to pursue cooperation instead, failure to repeat the advice did not deprive this defendant of an appeal. Rodriquez concerns actual loss of the appeal, not the separate question whether this omission caused that loss. Neither potential appellate merit nor the likelihood of success is required to demonstrate causal deprivation.

All eight reject automatic relief on these findings and would affirm denial of the Rule 32 claim. This is not adoption of the Third Circuit's clear-and-convincing formulation as the universal burden for every disputed-knowledge case. No credibility issue remains open on this appeal; the Court need not decide who bears an unprovided burden or what procedure a materially disputed future case requires. The sentence remains in effect, no appeal is restored, and no resentencing or new evidentiary hearing is ordered.

## Cedar Rapids Community School District v. Garret F. — No. 96-1793

### Reach, applicable law, and relief under review

All eight accept §1254(1) review of the Eighth Circuit judgment. Garret's special-education eligibility is not reopened. His regular-class participation, educational benefit, need for trained assistance within hearing distance, and the absence of a need for a physician to perform the disputed services are the supported predicates. No final legal finding permits a cheaper unlicensed worker to disregard Iowa licensing rules. The ALJ criticized the Nursing Board's reasoning while disclaiming authority to override it; that criticism is not a judicial staffing license.

Former §1401(a)(17), the related-services definition and medical-services qualification, governs the retrospective dispute together with former §1401(a)(18)'s FAPE conditions. The 1997 reorganization preserves the relevant medical clause but does not retroactively create the reimbursement obligation. The regulations include school-health services furnished by qualified nurses or other qualified people, and describe included physician medical services for diagnosis and evaluation. The precise regulatory-definition issue and the actual agency letters must be addressed, not assumed.

The formal ALJ order reimburses school-year 1993–1994 care. Its discussion of current services is distinct from a formal award for every later year. Affirmance leaves that adjudication in place and establishes the coverage rule; it does not select employee versus contractor, require a particular RN or LPN, fix a new amount, or enter an unbounded future reimbursement decree.

### Physician rule against ordinary medical meaning

**Tatro and the statutory/regulatory distinction.** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer would affirm. Services needed during the school day to enable an eligible child to benefit from special education are supportive services even if their immediate function is maintaining health and access. Under Tatro's construction, the medical-services exception excludes physician services beyond diagnosis/evaluation; qualified nursing and other qualified-person school-health services do not enter that exclusion merely because they are continuous, complicated, or costly. Care appropriately furnished outside school hours need not be supplied at school; required physician services, actual educational necessity, and eligibility retain their separate limits.

The strongest objection is that ordinary medical services can include services performed by nurses and that the regulation expressly defines included diagnostic physician services, not every excluded service. These six answer with Tatro's actual treatment of that precise limitation: the same statutory term is used to include diagnostic/evaluation medical services and exclude other medical services; reading the provider boundary consistently is permissible, while the regulation independently includes school-health services by qualified nurses and others. The district's proposal would convert the stated medical exception into a second, undefined burden test for already included health services. Tatro's reference to cost explains the reasonableness of the boundary; it does not enact a separate school-by-school cost ceiling.

Garret's continuous need for availability does not mean that a nurse performs a medical procedure without interruption. The essential distinction nevertheless does not depend on minimizing the actual work or cost: even accepting substantial nursing responsibility, these services need no physician and permit attendance and educational benefit. Educational purpose does not require each supportive act to teach a lesson; transportation and access assistance illustrate the enacted distinction. This rule does not create a general federal entitlement to every treatment necessary to a child's life.

**Ordinary medical-service construction.** Scalia and Thomas would reject the physician/nonphysician criterion as the exhaustive statutory meaning. The exception identifies the nature and purpose of a service, and continuous respiratory and bodily care can be medical despite delegation to a nurse or trained person. The regulatory diagnostic definition identifies included physician services without necessarily exhausting all excluded care. They would disapprove Tatro's contrary general construction to the extent it makes provider identity dispositive, rather than pretending that Tatro never addressed the inference in its footnote 10. Tatro's judgment is not reopened, but its general exclusion rule would be changed under this proposed position.

The strongest objection is that this is a statutory precedent with an express, workable rule, preserved through subsequent legislation and applied alongside the regulation's express nursing inclusion. Scalia and Thomas regard the ordinary statutory limitation and the cost/competence function of the exception as sufficient reason to reject extending that construction to the intensive continuing care here. They do not describe their position as compelled by a later case or as a rule already adopted by the simulated Court. Their conclusion is an adverse federal coverage construction, not a factual finding that Garret gains too little education or that the District cannot afford the care.

### Agency letters and conditional-funding notice

The actual Anderson letter reports conflicting judicial decisions, declines a case-independent answer, and assigns the individual inquiry to the IEP team. It does not itself announce adoption of every court's multifactor burden exclusion. The government's supported reading connects individual assessment to eligibility, educational necessity, ability to use a qualified nonphysician, and the outside-school-hours limitation. Under the current Auer rule, an amicus presentation neither invalidates nor automatically establishes an agency construction; the actual text and the responsible agency's fair, considered position matter.

The six affirming Justices accept the physician rule as consistent with the regulation and the supported agency treatment, while preserving the letter's case-by-case necessity inquiry. They need not treat every sentence of the letter as a legislative rule. Scalia and Thomas agree that the letter is not an express agency adoption of the District's test, but their statutory objection precedes regulatory deference: Auer cannot make an agency's interpretation of its regulation enlarge what they read as Congress's medical exclusion. They joined Auer's actual rule and do not seek to overrule it here.

Funding notice is a separate issue. Pennhurst, the current Suter holdings, and New York preserve clear notice of the condition; Franklin, Blessing, and Gebser do not abolish notice or equate all federally funded programs. The six find notice in IDEA's express obligation to supply necessary related services, the promulgated school-health provisions, and Tatro's established construction predating the school-year costs. This is an existing IDEA service and review framework, not a newly implied §1983 right, a new damages attribution rule, or a freestanding program-adequacy injunction. The scope of Garret's care does not make an otherwise stated funding condition disappear.

Scalia and Thomas give independent weight to the absence, in their statutory reading, of clear acceptance of this continuing medical-care obligation. Their narrower Franklin judgment ground, Suter joins, Carter joins preserving actual statutory predicates, and Auer joins distinguish a valid specified obligation from an enlarged one. They do not transfer Gebser's teacher-attribution rules to IDEA or deny Congress's power to condition funds on an expressly stated service duty. The disagreement is what this statute clearly requires.

### Nature/burden alternative and Iowa law

A different lawful path would apply a nature, duration, staffing, and fiscal-burden inquiry. Its strongest support is Tatro's explanation of why Congress could exclude unduly costly services beyond school competence. Its strongest objection is that it replaces the approved statutory/regulatory boundary with uncertain local affordability and could exclude the children whose access requires the most support.

The six reject that added exclusion. Independently, merely selecting such an inquiry could not justify outright reversal here: the ALJ expressly found these costs not overburdensome in view of this District's special-education enrollment, pooled resources, and state deficit-recovery arrangements. Rowley's due-weight rule requires attention to that administrative record. Those findings create neither a national $20,000–$30,000 ceiling nor guaranteed funding for every future year. No Justice can convert the District's disputed estimates or licensing assertion into contrary Supreme Court findings.

Scalia and Thomas reject open-ended affordability balancing as the final definition too. Their proposed categorical treatment of the intensive continuing care as excluded medical services would make the ALJ's alternative favorable cost conclusion insufficient to establish the federal obligation. If the Court instead selected a genuine nature/burden test, they would not reverse the result solely because the lower federal courts used the physician rule; that path would require consideration of the ALJ's alternative and the proper review of its findings.

The ALJ separately invoked Iowa special-education/supporting-services law and least-restrictive-environment provisions. The federal courts did not adjudicate those grounds. The six affirm on federal law and need not determine whether Iowa independently requires the same services. Scalia and Thomas would vacate the federal affirmance and remand for the unresolved Iowa-law ground, including the claim of a higher state standard through IDEA, under ordinary jurisdiction and preservation rules. The District's claim that Iowa law tracks federal law, including its Buchholtz argument, must be considered there; neither side's prediction supplies a decided state rule. They would not order final dismissal of the reimbursement claim or automatically extinguish the ALJ's independent state basis.
