## Underlying controversy and interlocutory order

The plaintiffs are institutional holders of unsecured notes issued by Grupo Mexicano and guaranteed by subsidiaries. The notes rank pari passu with other unsecured unsubordinated indebtedness. GMD experienced severe financial difficulty, defaulted on an interest payment, and disclosed expected Mexican government Toll Road Notes. It assigned rights connected with those proceeds to Mexican government, employee and financial creditors. The parties disputed the extent and consequences of those dispositions; insolvency and actual threatened noncollection were addressed in the district court's findings.

On December 12, 1997, the investors sued for breach-of-contract damages and interim restraint of transfers of the Toll Road Notes. The December 23 preliminary injunction followed hearings and required a $50,000 bond. The plaintiffs asserted no existing judgment lien or proprietary equitable interest establishing ownership of the restrained assets. The case does not establish a pleaded statutory fraudulent-transfer claim or a general equitable interest merely from anticipated noncollection.

The Second Circuit affirmed. It relied on general equity, Rule 65, insolvency/dissipation, and protection of an adequate eventual remedy; it read De Beers narrowly and invoked Deckert and First National City Bank. It acknowledged contrary circuit authority and rejected a mandatory separate intent-to-frustrate element. These are the lower court's grounds, not controlling Supreme Court law. The Supreme Court petitioners' brief frames the legal objection as absence of power to restrain unrelated foreign assets before a money judgment and states that GMD did not contest the findings of irreparable harm or likely merits success in the reviewed appeal. The panel opinion nevertheless separately analyzes GMD's stated objections to the irreparable-harm reasoning. A neutral packet should preserve that distinction rather than infer a concession to legal authority.

## Later lower-court events already predating the assigned review

The April 17, 1998 district judgment awarded the investors $82,444,259, dismissed GMD's counterclaims with prejudice, ordered turnover of enough Toll Road Receivables or Notes to satisfy the judgment, and converted the preliminary injunction into a permanent injunction until satisfaction. These events are reproduced in Joint Appendix 110–112, independently of any historical Supreme Court action.

The April 20 notice of appeal identified turnover and permanent-injunction conversion. Respondents' predecision brief quotes GMD's July 1, 1998 appellate brief as abandoning the challenge to permanent-injunction conversion. The May 1 court-of-appeals order stayed turnover but expressly left the permanent injunction effective (JA 115–116). GMD's predecision merits brief describes its separate turnover appeal as pending after December 10, 1998 argument. No later disposition of that separate appeal has been supplied or imported.

The investors filed a mootness suggestion. JA 117–118 reproduces a May 4 order denying a mootness motion; respondents contest the significance of its docket numbering and distinguish the preliminary-injunction appeal from the later appeal. GMD asserts damages from the preliminary injunction's approximately four-month operation and relies on the injunction bond. Respondents argue that the permanent judgment and abandonment of its injunction challenge remove the interlocutory controversy, and that a bond issue belongs in subsequent proceedings. Those are conflicting predecision positions, not findings that damages are owed or that either appeal is moot. The merits question cannot be framed as whether simply to dissolve a still-operative preliminary injunction without addressing these later lower-court events.

## Date-eligible legal-source inventory

Federal equity jurisdiction; Federal Rules of Civil Procedure 64, 65(c), and 69; Judiciary Act antecedents; the distinction between judicial power and entitlement to its exercise; De Beers Consolidated Mines v. United States, 325 U.S. 212 (1945); Deckert v. Independence Shares Corp., 311 U.S. 282 (1940); United States v. First National City Bank, 379 U.S. 378 (1965); University of Texas v. Camenisch, 451 U.S. 390 (1981), on the preliminary/final distinction. The predecision briefs reproduce these competing constructions; no historical post-divergence Supreme Court decision becomes governing merely by being cited in a brief.

Canonical Mertens (June 1, 1993) construes the statutory category appropriate equitable relief rather than every remedy a court of equity once could award. It is not a direct holding resolving freestanding Rule65 power. The public dissent is noncontrolling. Current-term Blue Fox (January 20, 1999) addresses a differently situated federal sovereign and APA waiver, requiring attention to the actual entitlement and remedial operation. Neither establishes an across-the-board necessity test authorizing or forbidding this private-creditor restraint. The Section I phrase No remedy without necessity and source is not an exact present Standards heading and must not be cited as a freestanding current rule.
