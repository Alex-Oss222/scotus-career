### What the early sources establish

**Bills v. Aseltine, 958 F.2d 697 (March 9, 1992).** This is eligible before both conduct dates. The officers invited a private security employee to inspect a home for his employer's property, unrelated to the generator warrant; he arrived after the first warrant's object had been recovered. The court held that the warrant did not authorize that private inspection and that factual questions prevented summary judgment on the officers' unreasonable-execution theory. It separately upheld genuine private assistance identifying items covered by the next warrant, and immunity for the officer who obtained and executed that later warrant on the information he reasonably possessed. It did not grant or deny every inviting officer's immunity under a general media rule. The opinion's protection of authorized police photographs and its reservation concerning the private photographer are distinct. Its general assignment of execution issues to a jury does not displace this Court's Hunter rule for legal immunity.

**Prahl v. Brosamle, 98 Wis. 2d 130, 295 N.W.2d 768 (1980).** The court rejected the presented federal theory that filming and broadcasting an otherwise reasonable search, without more, made it unreasonable; it noted the absence of intimate, offensive or vulgar aspects. It separately permitted trespass claims against the newsman and the officer who facilitated his entry, rejecting implied media access from publicity or custom on that record. Its federal holding is relevant contrary notice evidence, but is neither blanket approval of every added intrusion nor a rule making publication essential to an entry violation. Its tort holding is not a federal damages holding.

**Dietemann v. Time, Inc., 449 F.2d 245 (1971).** The majority sustained California privacy liability and rejected a First Amendment defense to intrusive newsgathering. It expressly declined the federal constitutional/Civil Rights Act question. The separate writing favored reaching the police-agency issue while also explaining that state tort protection can exceed the federal constitutional floor. The privacy reasoning is relevant to the asserted distinction between commercial transmission and law-enforcement evidence gathering; the decision did not establish that distinction as a Fourth Amendment holding. The Ninth Circuit's later description of Dietemann cannot supply a constitutional holding the original reserved.

**Moncrief and Higbee.** The original decisions remain unrecovered. The Fourth Circuit majority characterizes them as rejecting constitutional challenges to media accompaniment. Its dissent disputes that characterization: it describes Moncrief as addressing other execution objections and Higbee as not addressing the Fourth Amendment. These conflicting attributed recitations are preserved. No commitment treats either original as read, assigns it a resolved holding, or makes either decisive. The immunity-supporting commitments below remain the same if both disputed authorities are removed from consideration.

**Other temporal limits.** Ayeni, Buonocore, Parker, Marks and the lower Wilson and Hanlon opinions cannot establish notice before their dates. Their discussion of older authorities may identify legal arguments, but their later conclusions are not proof of earlier clarity. Soldal postdates Wilson's conduct; its pre-Hanlon availability does not turn its possessory-seizure holding into a rule against all recording. Section 3105's execution-assistance language is relevant background for search warrants, not an automatic constitutional damages rule or an independently established application to Wilson's state arrest warrant. A Marshals policy, a prosecutor's agreement and customary media access are not independent constitutional authority.

## Wilson v. Layne

### Review scope and supported application

The Fourth Circuit granted the officers qualified immunity without deciding constitutionality. The homeowners seek to overturn that judgment. The district court had denied immunity on media participation; its other rulings concerning probable cause and force are not reopened by this review. Federal and local officers' damages channels remain Bivens and §1983 respectively, at their existing scope.

The supported record is an official entry to execute an arrest warrant for the Wilsons' son, accompanied by reporters invited under a ride-along arrangement. The reporters did not assist execution, observed the domestic interior and occupants, and took photographs never published. Their presence added an intrusion into the homeowners' own protected home. Neither physical rummaging beyond the officers' route nor eventual publication is necessary to identify that added intrusion. Conversely, the Court need not declare the photographs a separate seizure of a property interest to decide this claim.

**Common constitutional proposition, eight Associates:** Officers violate a resident's Fourth Amendment right when, in executing a warrant inside the resident's home, they introduce or permit invited media to enter for purposes unrelated to assisting lawful execution, without consent or another lawful basis for that added entry. Authority to execute the warrant does not itself authorize the additional intrusion. The rule does not forbid necessary or otherwise reasonable civilian execution assistance, determine every permissible documentary practice, or require a warrant to name every person who genuinely assists.

**Seven-Associate immunity proposition:** On the actual April 1992 legal landscape, the particular constitutional limit on accompanying, nonassisting media observers during an otherwise authorized execution was not established clearly enough to deny personal qualified immunity. The existing home and warrant rules supported the violation but left a materially disputed application to observation accompanying valid officers; Bills did not resolve this application, and Prahl's actual federal holding supplies relevant contrary evidence. The absence of an identical case is neither necessary nor sufficient to this conclusion. No hypothetical factual assistance is assumed.

## Hanlon v. Berger

### Review scope and separable conduct

The reviewed judgment is the Ninth Circuit's rejection of the federal officials' qualified immunity on media participation in the March 1993 ranch search. The warrant covered the ranch and appurtenant structures but expressly excluded the residence. The appellate record, read favorably to the Bergers, reports an advance media arrangement, cameras on official vehicles, media observation and recording, and Scrafford's concealed microphone transmitting conversations inside the home. The first entry with Berger's consent is undisputed; later entry, consent and residential search allegations remain disputed. The lower court's collateral-estoppel discussion distinguishes the criminal warrant challenge from this media-execution issue; no new criminal disposition follows.

The physical media-entry rule must be applied to a place in which the Bergers actually have a protected interest. The 75,000-acre title, a camera's presence on the ranch, proximity of outbuildings to the home, and the presence of a guard dog do not by themselves establish the constitutional status of every recorded area. The apparent curtilage and enclosed-building evidence cannot be disregarded either. Dunn, Oliver and the present actual-access cases require the particular space and intrusion. Mere commercial purpose does not turn otherwise unprotected open fields into a protected Fourth Amendment place.

An officer's consensual participation in a conversation, undisclosed transmission of that conversation, an additional unauthorized residential entry or search, and a physically accompanying camera crew are distinct acts. White, Hoffa and Lewis are substantial contrary authority to a constitutional recording theory; the distinction based on commercial dissemination is arguable but is not a constitutional holding of Dietemann. Neither a decision about invited media nor immunity on that theory conclusively resolves a separately preserved, independently unlawful home search. The exact scope of the officer petition remains controlling; no private-media Bivens, joint-action, Wiretap Act, state tort, conversion or injunction judgment is determined here.
