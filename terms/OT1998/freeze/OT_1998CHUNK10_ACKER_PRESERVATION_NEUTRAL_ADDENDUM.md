## Entering law on defending a favorable judgment

Relevant canonical excerpts are preserved in [targeted preservation holdings](../entering-law/OT_1998CHUNK10_ACKER_PRESERVATION_LAW.md) and [the exact Standards provision](../entering-law/OT_1998CHUNK10_ACKER_PRESERVATION_STANDARDS.md), “Presented questions, subsidiary arguments and exceptional Supreme Court review.” The favorable-judgment component operates from X-Citement Video, November 29, 1994; the distinct bounded disavowal component operates from Lebron, February 21, 1995. These are derived copies of authorized state sources.

**X-Citement Video.** A respondent may defend an existing favorable judgment on a renewed, record-supported alternative even when a lower court rejected the alternative, if it seeks neither enlargement of its own rights nor reduction of its adversary's rights under that judgment. The decision applies United States v. American Railway Express Co., 265 U.S. 425 (1924). This is the pertinent no-cross-appeal principle: a prevailing party ordinarily need not seek separate review merely to replace the reasoning while retaining the same favorable judgment. Separate review is materially different when the party seeks alteration of an adverse judgment or greater relief. The rule permits consideration; Dandridge's lawful remand option and the Court's review limits remain.

Here respondents characterize discrimination as an alternative reason to affirm the existing favorable tax judgment. If that is the actual relief sought, rejection of their theory below does not by itself create a cross-appeal requirement. But that conclusion does not dispose of every preservation question. Whether nonpursuit in the intermediate appeal counsels declining present consideration, whether the argument remains within the permissible scope, and whether the established record supports decision remain distinct inquiries. The supplied facts establish no request for enlarged relief and no actual missing cross-appeal defect; neither is invented.

**Lebron.** The Court may elect to consider an included legal argument supporting the same preserved claim when it falls within the presented question and is resolvable on the established record without a new factual theory or findings, even in the bounded setting of an earlier disavowal. That holding neither compels review of abandoned grounds nor establishes that Acker's discrimination theory is merely a new argument for the same claim. The theory's character and scope must be assessed, not assumed.

**Zafiro.** An individual's failure to pursue a claim on appeal can support prudential nonreview when no supported exceptional reason exists; that is distinct from merits rejection, a jurisdictional bar or intentional constitutional waiver. Zafiro involved a petitioner's personal claim, not this respondent alternative-ground posture, so its result cannot be transferred without accounting for X-Citement Video.

**AT&T v. Central Office Telephone (June 15, 1998).** The Court declined to construct an independent tort theory that the respondent had not advanced as an alternative appellate ground or requested remand to develop. That limits compelled reconstruction of an unadvanced theory; Acker's respondents have affirmatively renewed their discrimination argument here. Their present briefing is a real distinction, while the source's preservation and record limits remain relevant. No rule requires a new evidentiary inquiry merely because an alternative theory can be named.

## Supported analysis and remedy branches

First determine federal jurisdiction under the original packet's independent §1442 and §1341 inquiries. A conclusion that the federal forum is unavailable does not authorize an alternative merits decision on discrimination or compensation.

If merits are reachable, keep three steps separate: the legal availability of the alternative ground; whether the Court should consider it in this procedural posture; and whether it succeeds under §111 and the governing tax-discrimination principles. A response that the tax is “nondiscriminatory” cannot stand in for those inquiries, but renewed advocacy does not establish that the district court was wrong.

- **Reach the renewed alternative.** Respondents may invoke the favorable-judgment rule, the actual district-court adjudication, the ordinance/stipulated record and their present briefing. A decision to reach must explain why the intermediate nonpursuit and review-scope concerns do not require declining the defense. Then the Court must independently assess the actual source-discrimination argument and comparator differences. Neither reach nor rejection below dictates its substantive answer.
- **Decline present consideration on a supported procedural ground.** The County may rely on the reported lack of appellate challenge, ordinary preservation practice and any actual record/scope limit. The model must explain the effect of that history alongside the respondent's right to defend a favorable judgment. It must not convert the reported omission automatically into intentional waiver, jurisdictional incapacity or substantive correctness of the tax.
- **Choose a properly limited remand.** If an unresolved issue warrants lower-court treatment, the Court may define the issue and its procedural availability. It must not describe discrimination as never adjudicated, automatically reopen its adverse district-court determination, presume entitlement to new evidence, or leave the effect of a present reach/nonreach decision ambiguous. A remand option is not a finding that further factual proceedings are required.

The strongest competing procedural positions therefore are that an actually renewed, record-supported defense may sustain precisely the existing favorable judgment without a cross-appeal, and that its earlier nonpursuit may warrant prudential limitation despite that general permission. The factual note alone does not choose between them.

## Separate Compensation Clause question

The prior sanitized source establishes that the district court also relied on the Compensation Clause, while the en banc merits disposition did not reach that separate ground. Its posture differs from discrimination: the district court supported respondents on compensation but rejected discrimination, and the supplied appellate nonchallenge statement concerns discrimination. That statement cannot be transferred to the Compensation Clause theory.

If the Court rejects the intergovernmental-immunity ground supporting the appellate judgment, the remaining Compensation Clause question may require a distinct decision about its proper treatment, including a limited remand if warranted. No automatic compensation victory, tax liability, amount, exemption or reopening of every defense follows. The record supplied here establishes neither intentional abandonment of that question nor a new reason to decide it without its own scope and legal analysis.
