### Issue 1: Summary judgment on racial predominance

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Johnson v. Miller (June 29, 1995) holds that predominant racial consideration in composing a common territorial electorate does not itself establish unequal treatment or trigger strict scrutiny, even when traditional criteria are subordinated. A cognizable status, participation or other constitutional injury remains actionable under its own elements. Thus a factual contest between race and party cannot alone make the asserted racial-predominance theory viable; first identify the deprivation claimed.
* **Other Governing Law:** Equal Protection Clause; Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56.
* **Material Other Authority:** When race and party are highly correlated, supported political and racial explanations must be tested through ordinary factfinding rather than resolved against the nonmovant on a disputed record.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Material Positions and Limits:** Plaintiffs rely on race’s role in drawing the district; the State offers partisan explanations and contests both facts and the legal theory. Johnson v. Miller requires an independently cognizable unequal-treatment injury and does not make racial predominance alone actionable. The disputed motive must be material to such a claim.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

An injunction based only on predominance cannot stand under current law. A preserved independent participation or status injury may warrant ordinary evidentiary proceedings; no trial is required solely to resolve a factual dispute immaterial under the governing rule.

## Goldsmith

Correct the supplied sentence description: the court-martial imposed six years' confinement and forfeiture of $2,500 monthly for six years, not dismissal. The Air Force appellate court affirmed in 1995; Goldsmith did not seek CAAF review, and the conviction became final under the cited UCMJ provision. The proposed drop from the rolls followed the 1996 legislation and was a separate administrative act. His original extraordinary petition concerned interruption of HIV medication; he raised the drop claim later before CAAF, asserting double-jeopardy and ex-post-facto objections without reopening the expired direct appeal. The challenged CAAF injunction restrained the administrative drop. Those claims and remedial objects must not be conflated.

The statute authorizes dropping a commissioned officer who may be separated under section 1167; the latter requires a court-martial confinement sentence exceeding six months, finality of that sentence and at least six months actually served. A statutory power to act is not an adjudication that every proposed action is constitutionally valid. The writ issue concerns existing or potential jurisdiction over findings and sentence under CAAF's governing statutes; section 1651 does not itself create a new jurisdictional subject. McBryde's limited supervisory-writ holding supplies that distinction, without making an Article I military court a general court of review of military administration. Whether a lawful avenue elsewhere is available and adequate remains distinct from CAAF's jurisdiction and from the administrative action's merits. No entitlement to habeas, correction-board relief or federal injunctive relief is adjudicated by identifying a possible forum.

The live alternatives are that the injunction protects an authorized military-appellate judgment within the writ power, or that the administrative personnel act lies outside that jurisdiction and the injunction must be vacated without deciding the constitutional claims. The finality, different act and statutory review scope are material to that choice. The original packet's categorical legal conclusion is an argument, not a frozen commitment.

## White
