# Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation, Inc. — 1999-01-12

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I, III-A and III-B | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Six-Justice Court rationale; seven votes for each invalidation | Registration, final individual reports and monthly individual reports, with their distinct limits. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Eight-Justice Court rationale; unanimous invalidation | Complete bounded personal-name-badge rule and explanation. |
| Opinion concurring in the judgment | Thomas | No other Justice | Supports every reviewed invalidation | Separate strict-scrutiny grounds and alternative substantial-relation conclusion; no full lead-method join. |
| Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | O'Connor | Breyer | Joins Part II; opposes registration and both report invalidations | Part II's complete explanation; separate administrative-qualification and financial-disclosure grounds for the other provisions. |

## Holdings

**Common authority.** Stevens's Court opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg throughout. Those six directly support Parts I, III-A and III-B. O'Connor and Breyer additionally join Part II, giving that component eight full rationale votes. Thomas concurs in every invalidity judgment on the distinct grounds described below; his judgment votes are not full joins in the Court's methodology. The rule's limits accompany each component.

### I. Registered-voter condition

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado may not require otherwise qualified resident-adult initiative circulators to be registered voters on this record. Excluding otherwise qualified speakers because they are unregistered substantially burdens protected petition circulation, while the retained identification and enforcement mechanisms serve traceability more closely.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that a person need not register to vote to engage in the protected political conversation involved in circulating a petition. Meyer v. Grant protects that interaction and the available pool of speakers. Colorado's condition excludes at least 400,000 otherwise qualified but unregistered voters. Easy enrollment does not eliminate that reduction or show that registration adequately measures residence, availability for process or honesty.

The State's strongest argument treats registration as an administrable qualification in a legally operative election process. Anderson and the governing Burdick rule permit appropriately fitted administration, but require attention to the actual burden and interest. Here the sworn identifying and residential-address affidavit, signature review, subpoenas and targeted fraud sanctions address traceability without the registration exclusion. Timmons's different candidate-nomination setting does not make this speaker restriction minor. The Court invalidates the registration condition, not the age floor, circulation period or residence rule whose validity is not presented. An affidavit cannot preserve the invalid condition merely by requiring a registration recital.

### II. Personal name displayed during solicitation

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado may not compel every circulator to display a personal name on a badge during protected face-to-face petition advocacy when the retained post-circulation identification and enforcement measures serve the asserted interests with materially less speech burden. The personal-name component is invalid; standalone paid-or-volunteer and employer-information requirements are not adjudicated.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that compelled personal attribution at the expressive encounter requires a justification fitted to that burden. McIntyre protects individual political advocacy against inadequately justified identification demands; Meyer protects petition circulation, and Riley prevents payment from stripping a solicitor of speech protection. The trial record shows deterrence from the personal badge, particularly when the subject is unpopular. That is a burden on the actual political conversation.

Even assuming fraud prevention and detection are compelling interests, Colorado has not shown the necessary fit. The identifying affidavit, signature assurances, review, protests and fraud penalties allow officials to identify and investigate circulators without exposing their names to everyone approached. The later public affidavit differs in timing and accessibility from a worn badge; the Court creates no anonymity from lawful investigation. A witness's interest in reporting misconduct does not establish the needed additional benefit of this blanket personal-name display. This holding does not decide standalone paid-status, sponsor or employer attribution, nor invalidate lawful post-circulation verification.

### III-A. Final individual-circulator reports

**Holding and operative rule.** The challenged final-report requirements publishing each paid circulator's identity, address and individual compensation fail the required substantial relationship to Colorado's asserted informational and enforcement interests on this record. Disclosure of initiative proponents and aggregate spending remains distinct; the decision creates no categorical exemption from initiative-finance disclosure.

**Controlling explanation.** Buckley v. Valeo permits campaign-finance disclosure substantially related to important governmental interests. That inquiry requires attention to what the information reveals. Proponent identity and aggregate expenditures reveal who finances an initiative and the extent of paid efforts. The individual messenger is not, for that reason, a financing source or a candidate contributor. Colorado has not demonstrated the added benefit necessary to justify publishing every paid circulator's identity and compensation, without a threshold separating consequential financing from any paid messenger.

The State argues that payee information permits tracing and checking spending beyond aggregate totals. That is a legitimate subject of inquiry, but the retained sponsor, aggregate, amount-per-signature and verification mechanisms make the asserted incremental need insufficient here. Final reporting is less immediate than a badge; it still requires its own disclosure justification. Kentucky Right to Life's payer-identification rule concerns a different financing function and does not answer this demand for every messenger's information. A narrower individual-reporting rule supported under the governing scrutiny remains open.

### III-B. Monthly individual-circulator reports

**Holding and operative rule.** Colorado's challenged monthly requirements publishing each paid circulator's identity, residential and business addresses and individual amounts paid or owed are likewise invalid on this record. The Court decides that individual-information package, not the independent validity of every other monthly-report field.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court separately considers publication during the circulation campaign. Timely information can expose abuse and inform voters before qualification; that argument gives monthly reporting work that a final report cannot always perform. But timing does not cure the insufficient relationship between identifying each messenger and the State's demonstrated financing or enforcement need. It also exposes the individual while the political effort is underway. The retained sponsor and aggregate disclosures and enforcement mechanisms remain material alternatives. The decision does not hold that referendum disclosure has no informational value or that every individual challenge requires proof of reprisals.

The Tenth Circuit invalidated the monthly subsection as a whole. The Court affirms the reviewed invalidity of individual circulator disclosures and leaves the independent validity of its other prescriptions, including proponents and measure identity, undecided. It neither orders those fields conclusively reinstated nor independently declares them unconstitutional. Any ordinary remedial or severability implementation must respect the existing judgment and the scope actually decided.

**Common limits and remedy.** The reviewed registration, personal-name and individual-disclosure invalidations stand. Age, circulation timing, verification affidavits, fraud and signature-review mechanisms are outside the reviewed invalidations and remain governed by their own terms. This decision does not invalidate standalone paid-status or sponsor attribution, create a replacement initiative code, grant damages, destroy public records, authorize fraud or decide every future disclosure rule. Unreviewed requirements receive no automatic new constitutional endorsement.

## Law After Decision

Registration cannot be used to exclude otherwise qualified resident-adult circulators; personal names cannot be compelled on the challenged solicitation badge; and the reviewed final and monthly individual-disclosure packages cannot be enforced. Governing law preserves distinct authority for lawful administration, verification, fraud controls, sponsor and aggregate disclosures. The independent validity and remedial treatment of unreviewed badge and monthly fields remain outside this judgment. No fractured-opinion or narrowest-ground synthesis is needed: each Court holding has at least six direct joins, and the badge holding has eight.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** Thomas agrees that every reviewed restriction is invalid but applies traditional strict scrutiny to the direct speech and severe associational burdens. Registration reduces the Meyer-protected speaker pool without a narrowly fitted traceability justification. The personal-name badge directly intrudes on core political speech and fails narrow tailoring even assuming a compelling anti-fraud interest. The reports' effect on circulation is more attenuated; compelled disclosure independently burdens association and belief. Singling out paid circulators poorly fits general identification, and the need to expose each individual's identity and compensation has not been demonstrated. Monthly exposure is considered separately from final reporting. As an alternative, the challenged packages also fail Buckley's more forgiving substantial-relationship test. He does not displace Buckley's surviving disclosure holdings, adopt the Court's entire method, invalidate retained reports or decide reserved fields.

**O'Connor, joined by Breyer, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They join Part II's complete personal-name-badge explanation: direct intrusion into political conversation requires narrow tailoring, and the retained affidavit and enforcement tools defeat this demand on this record. They would sustain registration and the challenged final and monthly reports. Under Anderson–Burdick, readily available enrollment provides an administrable connection to the formal petition process without the severe burden found by the Court; Meyer does not make every qualification equivalent to a ban on paid circulators. Reports are indirect financial-disclosure burdens rather than badges worn during persuasion. Identifying payees and individual disbursements can permit checking campaign activity beyond aggregate sums, and contemporaneous monthly information can expose abuse before the vote. They find the relationship sufficient even under more demanding disclosure review. They acknowledge the excluded unregistered speakers and possible exposure costs, but reach different applications. They propose no automatic admission rule, universal minimum scrutiny or independent disposition of reserved fields.

# Citizens for a Constitutional Convention v. Yoshina; No. 98-671; Ninth Circuit Nos. 97-16408, 97-16543, 97-16540 and 97-16596.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer in full; Stone-Zsela in Parts I and II and the remedial disposition | Supports affirmance | Parts I and II: live controversy, federal review and due process; Part III: First Amendment; disposition: affirmance and denial of the requested constitutional relief |

Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment rejecting the First Amendment claim, without joining Part III or adding a ground. No separate opinion is filed.

## Holdings

### I. A live federal claim survives the second referendum

**Holding and operative rule:** A later referendum does not moot a substantial federal claim seeking recognition of an asserted entitlement created by the earlier vote when that relief remains capable of addressing a continuing injury. These plaintiffs maintain that claim; their ultimate failure to establish it does not eliminate jurisdiction.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, controls this threshold holding and Part II. Part III has the different coalition stated there.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the November 1998 vote does not dispose of the maintained demand to give legal effect to the asserted 1996 approval. Act 131 submitted a new question without expressly extinguishing that asserted entitlement. *Church of Scientology* separates the possibility of meaningful relief from ultimate entitlement to it; *Spencer* requires an actual surviving interest, which the maintained certification demand supplies here. *Steel Co.*, *NAACP v. Jones* and *Wisconsin Right to Life v. Paradise* require jurisdiction before contested merits and distinguish a substantial but unsuccessful claim from an absent controversy. Fees alone, hypothetical damages and an unproved collateral ruling supply no jurisdictional premise.

The federal plaintiffs were not parties to the state action, and the two organizations' participation as friends of the court did not make them parties. The bar against lower federal appellate review of a state-court loser's judgment therefore does not foreclose their own federal claim. The authoritative state construction remains binding as state law. Under *Ex parte Young*, a claim for future implementation of a federal right against responsible officials is not a demand for retrospective damages; under *Pennhurst*, it cannot become an instruction to follow a different federal construction of state law. This permits consideration of the constitutional claim without deciding the precise certification order available had a violation been proved. No mootness-based vacatur follows.

### II. The record does not establish fundamental unfairness

**Holding and operative rule:** A state court's neutral interpretation of an ambiguous election provision does not violate due process merely because election officials earlier gave a contrary advisory interpretation. Federal relief remains available for fundamentally unfair administration, but the dissemination, reliance and voting circumstances here do not establish the pervasive deprivation needed to overturn the 1996 legal result or certify approval.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion is joined by all eight other Justices; it states the Court's controlling due-process rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that official misinformation and authoritative construction of state law require distinct examination. A legally possible construction does not by itself answer a claim of actual official inducement, and informal guidance is not irrelevant merely because it lacked power to amend the Constitution. The polling-place sheet explained yes and no choices but said nothing about blanks. The contrary fact sheet had limited distribution. Statements to proponents and allegations of individual reliance matter, but campaigning reliance and a voter's inability to cast an effective choice are different injuries. Every submitted ballot was counted; voters had ready yes and no choices; and the record does not establish the election-wide frustration asserted.

*Burdick* requires consideration of the actual burden and the State's precise interests. Requiring affirmative support among those submitting ballots serves Hawaii's legitimate decision whether to convene a constitution-making body. Its highest court's distinction between convention and amendment-ratification language is not facially arbitrary. *Griffin v. Burns* and *Roe v. Alabama*, persuasive lower-court decisions, concern materially different changes affecting ballots cast through or withheld from officially described absentee procedures; neither makes every administrative mistake a federal violation. *Smiley* preserves the difference between interpretive authority and displacement of settled guidance; it supplies no universal immunity for retrospective change.

The Court does not find that no individual relied, that every abstainer was indifferent, or that deliberate misleading directions can never justify relief. It rejects the constitutional premise for the relief sought on this record without recounting the election under a different rule of state law.

### III. A denominator rule does not compel political speech

**Holding and operative rule:** A neutral referendum-approval threshold may include blank ballots in the denominator without compelling voters to espouse opposition when blanks remain accurately counted and reported as blanks and the rule does not discriminate among identifiable voters. The legal consequence of abstention does not itself supply the asserted First Amendment violation.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those eight Justices supply a controlling majority. Stone-Zsela agrees only with rejection of this claim and adds no ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that determining how much affirmative support a convention requires differs from attributing a political message to an unwilling voter. *Gordon v. Lance* permits nondiscriminatory heightened approval requirements; *Burdick* rejects a general entitlement to use the official ballot as an expressive tally. Here the State did not relabel blanks as affirmative no votes, alter recorded yes or no choices, require a public statement, or suppress advocacy outside the ballot. *Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation* concerns an actual requirement of personal identification during political solicitation and therefore does not govern this denominator rule. The claim that abstention makes approval harder does not establish compelled speech. The Court grants no general permission falsely to attribute political views to nonconsenting voters.

## Law After Decision

As of January 21, 1999, this record supplies no federal right to certify the 1996 convention proposal as approved or require another vote. Due process still reaches fundamentally unfair election administration; lawful state interpretive authority alone does not immunize materially different official inducement. The First Amendment distinguishes neutral approval thresholds from compelled attribution. A subsequent election does not automatically extinguish a concrete maintained claim to the legal effect of an earlier vote. No state constitutional counting rule is rewritten, and no broader rule concerning deliberately misleading directions is established.

## Separate Writings

No separate opinion is filed. Stone-Zsela agrees in the judgment rejecting the independent First Amendment claim without adding a ground or joining Part III.

The Court-opinion coalition is Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, each joining the opinion in full.

## Holdings

### The municipal class-specific foreclosure fails ordinary rational-basis review

**Operative rule:** Article XII's combined withdrawal of existing sexual-orientation nondiscrimination protection and prospective prohibition of ordinary municipal protection for the named class violates equal protection because its actual breadth bears no rational relationship to the asserted fiscal, associational, regulatory and local-policy interests. Municipal scope, continued general-law remedies and access to higher government do not cure that unexplained selective disability; the rule does not make every repeal unlawful or require the City to enact a particular protection.

**Authority:** The seven-Justice Opinion of the Court identified in Opinion Topology directly adopts the ordinary rationality rule, application and limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Romer requires a rational connection between legitimate objectives and the actual class-specific disadvantage. Heller's separate holdings preserve that connection while allowing reasonable generalizations, conceivable grounds and imperfect categories without formal findings, empirical certainty or least restrictive means. Moreno and Cleburne forbid bare harm and irrational prejudice; no finding about every voter's private motive is required.

Local fiscal management, regulatory restraint, private association and durable policy are legitimate subjects. Yet Article XII reaches the City's own employment and ordinary protection across housing, public accommodations and private employment, regardless of a particular protection's expense or operation. Avoiding some complaint and defense costs does not explain why this class alone becomes prospectively ineligible for all that ordinary protection. Charter authority explains who sets policy and its durability, not the missing rational connection to the full selective burden.

Hurley protects actual private expression; it does not make impersonal services or the City's own conduct categorically expressive association. Those distinct concerns cannot justify this entire public and private reach. Bowers's specified-conduct holding likewise does not explain the broader disability based on orientation or status. No particular expression or religious exemption is adjudicated and Bowers is not overruled.

The City emphasizes that Ohio institutions and general laws remain accessible. Romer already assumed generally applicable protection remained. The relevant breadth is the civic activity excluded from ordinary protection, not simply the number of governmental levels affected. Ordinary repeal remains permissible; the reported concession about repeal is not assent to prospective charter foreclosure. Coalition's distinct racial political-process inquiry supplies neither a heightened orientation standard nor a universal entitlement to favorable legislation. This decision applies ordinary rationality to Article XII's own operation.

### Equitable protection must address the discriminatory barrier without writing municipal law

**Operative rule:** The appellate direction to enter judgment for defendants cannot stand, and declaratory and injunctive protection must be restored against Article XII's unconstitutional ordinary-protection foreclosure. The lower courts must conform the injunction to that common operation and any genuinely independent lawful remainder under applicable severability law, without rewriting the charter federally, imposing a nondiscrimination code or reopening the resolved rational-basis merits.

**Authority:** The same seven-Justice Opinion of the Court directly adopts this remedial holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Removing the barrier returns ordinary lawmaking to its lawful operation; it does not require the City to adopt every requested remedy or prevent a lawful policy revision. Romer's relief followed the violation and an authoritative Colorado inseparability determination. This record supplies no corresponding final Ohio severability determination or established autonomous preference-only remainder. The Court therefore neither performs textual severance nor declares every conceivable separate quota restriction invalid.

The remand concerns fitting the injunction to the decided constitutional defect and respecting any genuinely independent lawful operation under the applicable state rule. It is not permission to continue the invalid common foreclosure or to require a new rationality showing from the plaintiffs. No damages, new ordinance, mandatory preference or adjudicated religious exemption is supplied.

### The dependent fee vacatur loses its merits foundation

**Operative rule:** Because the constitutional merits judgment is reversed and protection against the charter barrier is restored, the dependent vacatur of the plaintiffs' fee award cannot stand on loss of merits success. Their prevailing-party basis on this constitutional claim is restored, while remaining lawful §1988 issues and review of the calculation return to the lower courts; no particular amount is established here.

**Authority:** The same seven-Justice Opinion of the Court directly adopts the fee disposition and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Sixth Circuit removed the fee award as a consequence of its direction to enter judgment for defendants. The rejection of that merits direction removes the stated basis for erasing the award. It does not decide every statutory fee question or make an earlier contested calculation unreviewable. The lower courts must address those remaining matters in light of the restored constitutional success, separately from conforming the injunction.

## Law After Decision

Article XII cannot enforce its unconstitutional common withdrawal and foreclosure of ordinary municipal protection for the named class. The City remains free to make rational policy choices and lawful revisions; it is not commanded to adopt a particular ordinance or preference. Generally applicable law and actual expressive or religious claims retain their own operation. No suspect classification, general fundamental right of political access, Bowers overruling or damages remedy is created. Injunctive terms and remaining fee questions receive the separate treatment stated in the mandate.

## Separate Writings

Scalia dissents, joined by Thomas. They would affirm the merits judgment, dissolution of the injunction and dependent fee vacatur. They accept that Article XII reaches ordinary nondiscrimination protection and that municipalities remain subject to equal protection. In their view, regulatory restraint, limited resources, private association and the electorate's authority to make a local policy durable rationally support the measure despite imperfect coverage. Higher-level and generally applicable remedies remain available, and a local body's control over its own delegated policies differs from Colorado's restriction of state and local channels.

The dissent maintains its disagreement with Romer's application of ordinary rationality and proposes the municipal distinction as an additional limit. It does not claim Romer's majority adopted that reasoning or that every affected protection is a quota. O'Connor's Coalition writing supplies support for withdrawal of optional policies, while Hurley preserves actual expression; neither is treated as making every orientation protection unlawful. The dissent rejects a new racial political-process extension and finds no duty to adopt or preserve these municipal protections. Its fee position follows its merits conclusion only and supplies no separate bar to a fee award resting on an independently established surviving basis.

# Gable v. Patton / Patton v. Gable; Nos. 98-897 and 98-1072. — public reading copy

## Opinion Topology

Souter delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. The opinion controls both threshold dispositions and their complete remedial rationale. Stevens dissents, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Ginsburg and Breyer; those four would reach the merits and affirm the reviewed judgment on both granted components. No constitutional merits rationale commands the Court through this decision.

## Holdings

### I. The continuing personal controversy requires resolution

**Holding and operative rule:** When a postjudgment dispute leaves unresolved whether an election-law challenger retains a concrete personal stake in the challenged provisions, the lower courts must determine continuing exposure and redressability before deciding the constitutional merits. Supported present deterrence from candidacy or a reasonable expectation of renewed candidacy may establish that stake without formal filing; an unchanged statute and short election cycles alone cannot supply the same plaintiff's missing personal interest.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court is joined in full by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Those five Justices directly support this rule and its application; the four dissenters would find the personal showing sufficient now.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Gable's initial connection to this litigation does not settle whether the requested relief still addresses his own injury. He was a nonparticipating candidate in 1995. After the appellate judgment, the Registry asserted that he did not file for the 1999 primary. His reply relies on continuing deterrence and recurrence rather than asserting that he filed. Neither presentation is a lower-court finding resolving the present factual connection between Gable and each challenged provision.

*Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife* requires concrete personal injury, causation and redressability supported at the relevant litigation stage. *Department of Commerce v. House of Representatives / Clinton v. Glavin* applies that requirement to an actual claimant and preserves supported threatened injury without accepting an abstract interest in lawful government. Those principles permit a real deterrence claim: a person need not enter a race on allegedly unlawful terms merely to challenge those terms. They nevertheless require support for this person's continuing exposure. The short duration of elections can explain why review is difficult; it does not show that this claimant reasonably faces the same injury again.

The contrary argument properly emphasizes Gable's past candidacy and his asserted unwillingness to return under the same restrictions. It does not warrant treating the new dispute about present exposure as already resolved. The lower courts must determine whether supported present deterrence or reasonably expected renewed candidacy connects him to each provision and whether the requested relief can redress that exposure. The Court neither finds mootness nor demands a filing certificate, a particular document, a new trial, merits discovery, a special burden of proof or a deadline. The State's cross-petition supplies no substitute for a live underlying controversy. The two constitutional questions remain unanswered.

### II. Vacatur and remand are confined to the granted components

**Holding and operative rule:** The appellate judgment is vacated only as to approval of the responsive-financing trigger in No. 98-897 and invalidation of the twenty-eight-day prohibition on new personal funds in No. 98-1072, and those components are remanded for the continuing-controversy determination. If the controversy remains live, the lower courts may proceed under effective law; if it does not, they must decide the equitable treatment of the respective lower judgments in light of the actual cause and timing of mootness and the cross-petition's requested correction.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice Court opinion supplies the complete remedial rationale and both docket-specific dispositions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that uncertainty about continuing jurisdiction requires a bounded return to the lower courts, not a constitutional answer premised on unresolved personal exposure. The federal appellate channel under 28 U.S.C. §1254 permits review of the two granted components but does not dispense with Article III. Section 2106 permits the confined vacatur and remand necessary to resolve the supervening issue.

The lower courts may use existing supported materials and ordinary properly received jurisdictional submissions. A live controversy permits further consideration of the two claims under governing law. A finding that the controversy has ended instead requires separate attention to the actual equities governing the lower judgments. This order does not assume voluntary abandonment, direct automatic vacatur of every district-court ruling or require preservation of every lower judgment regardless of the cause of mootness. The State's request to restore the personal-funds restriction must be addressed at its actual procedural scope; the nonfiling assertion neither automatically defeats that request nor decides the equitable remedy.

Sponsor identification, compulsory slating and the independent validity of outside-donor restrictions lie beyond the grant and receive no new disposition. The outside-donor ruling is left undisturbed by this review without a new constitutional endorsement. The Court adopts no construction of unquoted statutory exceptions, no severability ruling, no new reporting regime and no public-payment obligation. Supreme Court review ends with this limited remand. No return date or further Supreme Court proceeding is ordered.

## Law After Decision

Personal injury, causation and redressability remain required throughout review. Supported present deterrence or reasonably expected renewed candidacy can establish a continuing personal stake without a formal filing, but the present dispute requires lower-court resolution. The appellate judgment is vacated only within the two granted components. The Court establishes no new rule on responsive financing, own-funded expression, outside contributions or severability. The existing financing and expenditure precedents retain their separate scopes, and the dissent's merits views do not govern the remand.

## Separate Writings

**Stevens, dissenting, joined by Stone-Zsela, Ginsburg and Breyer.** The dissent would reach both granted questions and affirm the Sixth Circuit on each. Gable is an identified former candidate who says these same restrictions deter his return. His nonfiling is consistent with that asserted injury. Requiring him to campaign under the challenged terms would discount the very deterrence alleged. The dissent finds his particular showing sufficient without finding a 1999 filing or granting every former candidate an indefinite right to litigate. Stevens's *Lujan* concurrence and the published standing disagreement in *Commerce / Glavin* support attention to concrete personal activity across changing electoral circumstances.

On financing, the dissent would uphold the challenged voluntary program and responsive trigger on this record. The nonparticipant retains his own money and freedom to spend; a comparative electoral disadvantage is not itself a coercive expenditure cap. Further public funds depend on the participant's additional qualifying fundraising, matched two for one after the required certification that the nonparticipant received contributions or made expenditures above the ceiling. They are not an automatic dollar-for-dollar payment matching the opponent's speech. The dissent's position follows the *Day* financing dissent's distinction between enlarging competing advocacy and restraining a speaker, while preserving challenges to genuinely coercive or punitive operation. It recognizes no numerical safe harbor, categorical subsidy immunity or unlimited public-payment obligation.

On personal funds, the dissent would affirm invalidation of the twenty-eight-day prohibition only insofar as it bars a nonparticipant from adding personal funds to finance further advocacy. Previously deposited money remains spendable, but that opportunity does not replace a later decision to finance responsive speech. *Buckley*, *Kruse* and *Suster* protect the candidate's own expression; a candidate's personal resources are not conventional donor contributions. Administration, notice and an opponent's opportunity to answer do not justify this complete cutoff. The dissent would preserve the distinction between own funds and ordinary third-party contributions, leaving the ungranted donor, sponsor-identification and slating questions open. It would not prescribe a replacement reporting code or treat every statutory branch as inseverable. These are dissenting merits views; none controls the remand.

# Holloway v. United States; No. 97-7164; OT1998.

## Opinion Topology
| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer | Affirmance | Actual conditional purpose, timing, proof, statutory argument and instruction limits. |
| Dissent | Scalia | None | Would reverse affected affirmances and remand | Ordinary statutory usage and lenity; no automatic acquittal or reversal of unrelated counts. |
| Dissent | Thomas | None | Same proposed disposition | Absence of a sufficiently settled legal convention for the asserted conditional meaning; distinct from Scalia's full linguistic account. |

## Holdings
#### A settled intent to inflict serious harm if necessary for the taking satisfies section 2119

**Operative rule.** Section 2119 is satisfied when, at the moment the defendant demands or takes control of the vehicle, the defendant actually intends to kill or cause serious bodily harm if necessary to complete the taking. The Government must prove that defendant's purpose beyond reasonable doubt; intimidation alone, an empty threat, recklessness, an accomplice's purpose alone or an intent formed only afterward is insufficient.

**Authority.** Ginsburg's opinion is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer. Seven direct joins control.

**Controlling explanation.** A person may decide to inflict harm if a victim resists while hoping compliance will make that harm unnecessary. The condition does not erase the decision. Section 2119 requires actual intent concerning death or serious bodily harm, not a desire to harm a compliant victim regardless of whether violence advances the taking.

The separate force-or-intimidation element confirms the limit. A bluff may intimidate without establishing a real purpose to inflict the specified harm. Bryan's offense-specific treatment of culpability and Bailey's insistence on enacted elements reinforce the need to prove this mental element rather than replace it with dangerousness. Traditional conditional-purpose usage corroborates the reading; the Court adopts no general Model Penal Code formula.

Congress's addition of intent therefore does real work. The Court neither treats that amendment as a drafting mistake nor permits an automatic inference from possession of a gun. Holloway's actual charge and recharge required his own serious-harm purpose if resistance made violence necessary, separately on the relevant counts and beyond reasonable doubt. They satisfy the statute against the presented objection.

The statute adds no requirement that resistance be objectively probable or that the defendant hope, believe or know it will occur. Such evidence may bear on whether the asserted purpose was real, but it is not another element. That conclusion answers the statutory argument; the Court makes no ruling on a separate instruction request whose existence and preservation are not established here. Unrelated trial errors and broader congressional-power questions remain outside review.

## Law After Decision
Actual present conditional intent satisfies section 2119's serious-harm element. The decision preserves the separate intimidation element, individual culpability, timing and burden of proof. It creates no universal rule about conditional intent in every crime, no gun-based presumption and no new probability element.

## Separate Writings
**Scalia dissents.** In ordinary usage, an unqualified intent to cause a result does not include merely planning to cause it upon resistance the actor hopes will never occur. He does not say every conceivable contingency defeats intent: remote implicit contingencies, or a likely condition the actor hopes will occur, present different ordinary-language uses. The divided state authorities do not establish a federal term of art overriding the enacted words. Congress's deterrent objective cannot erase the means it chose; any remaining criminal ambiguity favors the defendant even where the conduct is otherwise blameworthy. He would reverse the affected carjacking affirmances and return the dependent firearm judgments for lawful further proceedings, without ordering acquittal or disturbing unrelated counts.

**Thomas dissents separately.** Congress did not expressly define intent to include the asserted conditional purpose, and the cited decisions and commentary do not establish a sufficiently settled legal convention to presume that specialized meaning. Selected state statutes and the Model Penal Code are interpretive evidence, not enacted federal law. He reaches the same proposed disposition without adopting Scalia's entire linguistic classification or examples. His position likewise resolves no unpresented sufficiency, harmless-error or retrial issue.

## Holdings

### I. Demonstrated electoral support does not create a duty to equalize subsidies

**Operative rule.** The Constitution does not require equal presidential-campaign subsidies merely because parties compete or qualify for public financing. On the pleaded claim, a formula tied to prior electoral support and the alleged inability to match larger major-party resources do not establish the materially different practical suppression or discriminatory administration that remains open to challenge.

**Authority.** Parts I-III of Thomas's opinion are joined by all eight other Justices and control their respective holdings. Part IV has the distinct eight-Justice coalition in the opinion table.

**Controlling explanation.** Buckley v. Valeo, Part III, sustains support-sensitive public funding while preserving a properly supported different application. Libertarian Party of Florida v. Smith applies that distinction to the actual burden: access to electoral competition does not itself entitle a party to equal government assistance, but the funding label supplies no constitutional immunity.

The reported comparison is about $29 million for the Reform campaign and $62 million for each major-party campaign. The statutory categories use prior vote shares: at least 25 percent for a major party and at least five but less than 25 percent for a minor party. The scheme also contains a returning-candidate route and post-election eligibility for minor or new candidates reaching five percent. Those routes are not eliminated by the present decision. Section 9003(c) permits a minor- or new-party candidate's total qualified expenses to reach the major-party entitlement, with private contributions covering the excess of those expenses over public receipts; the smaller subsidy is not itself a total-spending ceiling. The Court does not find that fundraising succeeded or decide a new payment entitlement.

The Court accepts the reported disparity and inability to close it. Those allegations assert parity, not a different pleaded practical disability from the one Buckley considered. A party need not disappear or stop fielding candidates before challenging a substantially suppressive application. Viewpoint favoritism, concrete exclusionary administration and a materially different cumulative burden remain open; none is adjudicated on unreported facts here.

### II. The actual contribution-limit argument requires its own narrow answer

**Operative rule.** The alleged inability to close this public-subsidy gap under the $1,000 individual-candidate contribution restriction does not, by itself, state a materially different constitutional impairment from the parity claim Buckley rejected. This holding neither establishes a permanent dollar safe harbor nor resolves a properly supported prohibitory or cumulative contribution burden.

**Controlling explanation.** A direct contribution to a candidate differs from the candidate's own expenditure, independent advocacy and an aggregate restriction on association through intermediaries. Buckley preserves those distinctions. Kentucky Right to Life's direct-limit holding applies contribution doctrine within its actual facial scope; its separate aggregate and sourcewide-receipt holdings do not make one amount valid for every time and circumstance. Day's intermediary holding protects a different recipient class and meaningful pooling. Colorado Republican protects actual independent expression while preserving scrutiny of genuine candidate channels.

The plaintiffs' contribution argument is considered separately, not dismissed for the affiliation claim's standing defect or because public funding was voluntary. Accepting their inability-to-close allegation, equality of total resources remains the asserted constitutional demand. No finding of actual corruption, successful solicitation or consent to every contribution restriction supplies the decision. Nonrecipients do not accept the program's spending ceiling, while generally applicable contribution restrictions remain a separate question. A genuinely different burden would require its own analysis and the proper statutory tribunal.

### III. Invalidating this affiliation maximum would not redress the asserted representation injury

**Operative rule.** These plaintiffs lack standing to challenge the FEC's maximum of three members affiliated with one party because they identify no concrete exclusion caused by that maximum that its removal would likely redress. The jurisdictional dismissal decides neither the cap's constitutional merits nor a different discriminatory appointment or enforcement claim.

**Controlling explanation.** Lujan requires a concrete injury, a connection to the challenged provision and likely redress. Northeastern Florida protects the opportunity to compete free of an actual discriminatory barrier without demanding proof that the claimant would ultimately succeed. Commerce and Glavin likewise preserve concrete injury and redress without making certainty a universal requirement.

The provision requires six presidentially appointed, Senate-confirmed Commissioners and limits one party to three seats. It does not reserve seats to named major parties or prohibit Reform affiliates. The reported three-and-three practice is not the statutory command. Removing the maximum would permit the same appointees, and the supported account identifies no appointment opportunity or particular enforcement injury obstructed by this maximum. The defect is the missing cap-created barrier and remedial connection, not failure to guarantee an appointment. Financial injury from the separate allocation statute cannot supply standing against every provision governing its administrator. No Appointments Clause, separation-of-powers, equal-protection, due-process or associational merits ruling follows.

### IV. The granted claims supply no basis for bypassing or requiring a different statutory tribunal

**Operative rule.** A claim lacking Article III standing or presenting a constitutional theory plainly foreclosed by controlling law may be rejected before invoking the extraordinary statutory tribunal. A live substantial constitutional question cannot be bypassed merely because a court expects it ultimately to fail; a substantial FECA question follows section 437h, while a substantial Fund Act implementation or construction claim follows section 9011(b).

**Authority.** Thomas, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join Part IV. Stone-Zsela does not join it.

**Controlling explanation.** California Medical Association v. FEC supplies the standing and wholly-insubstantial-question distinction. It does not equate a weak ultimate claim with a frivolous question. The Court's present conclusions concern the reported legal theories, accepting their allegations, rather than an adverse factual finding or failure to produce unavailable papers.

Section 437h directs qualifying FECA constitutional questions brought by an authorized plaintiff to the court of appeals sitting en banc. Statutory eligibility, including that of a national committee or presidential voter, does not establish standing on every claim. Section 9011(b) separately provides the three-judge district court and direct Supreme Court appeal for its Fund Act suits, without requiring exhaustion of administrative or other remedies. Section 9011(a)'s D.C. Circuit review of Commission action within thirty days is another route, not a substitute for either. The financing theories as presented are foreclosed and the affiliation claim lacks standing; no statutory-tribunal remand follows. This antecedent explanation enters no independent disposition of the separate certification judgments outside the grant.

## Law After Decision

The support-sensitive formula and the asserted contribution-gap theory survive this challenge. A constitutional right to equal funding does not arise from the reported resource comparison. Different suppressive burdens, viewpoint discrimination and exclusionary administration remain reviewable on their own supported claims. The affiliation cap receives no constitutional merits judgment. Statutory eligibility to sue remains distinct from Article III standing, and the Fund Act and FECA review channels remain separate. No new subsidy amount, contribution ceiling or appointment entitlement is created.

## Separate Writings

Stone-Zsela joins Parts I-III and the judgment. He agrees that the presented subsidy and contribution-gap theory supplies no constitutional duty of parity and that the affiliation claim lacks a redressable connection to the statutory maximum. He does not join Part IV. The separate certification judgments, like the damages judgments, lie outside the specified grant; he would leave them undisturbed without the additional certification discussion. He supplies no alternative holding on their merits.
