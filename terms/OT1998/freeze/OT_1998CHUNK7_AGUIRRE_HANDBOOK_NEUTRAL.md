# Aguirre-Aguirre - additive neutral Handbook argument corroboration

**Matter:** INS v. Aguirre-Aguirre, No. 97-1754; OT1998 chunk 7; event 1999-05-03.
**Function:** Validate the newly added sources for the Ninth Circuit's Handbook-based arguments and its McMullen comparison. This is an additive argument-source handoff, not a merits decision, a new rule on treaty or Handbook authority, or a replacement of the earlier procedural freeze.
**Cutoff:** May 3, 1999. This validation reads only the newly added objective sections; earlier authorized neutral law and facts retain their existing scope.

## Newly supplied source passages

The following three sections are mechanically copied from the updated source extract. They are published recitations of the Court of Appeals' reliance and descriptions, and quoted portions of Handbook paragraphs 152 and 156. They do not certify either complete Handbook paragraph, recover the underlying Handbook edition independently, or establish the truth of every characterization as a new factual finding.

## Ninth Circuit reliance on international guidance

> In reaching the contrary conclusion and ruling that the risk of persecution should be balanced against the alien’s criminal acts, the Court of Appeals relied on a passage from the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, Handbook on Procedures and Criteria for Determining Refugee Status (Geneva, 1979) (U. N. Handbook).

## Paragraph 156 passage quoted by the source

> Paragraph 156 of the U. N. Handbook, the portion relied upon by the Court of Appeals, states that in applying the serious nonpolitical crime provision of Convention Art. I(F)(b), “it is … necessary to strike a balance between the nature of the offence presumed to have been committed by the applicant and the degree of persecution feared … .”

## Paragraph 152 language and Ninth Circuit comparison argument

> Also relying on the U. N. Handbook, the Court of Appeals held that the BIA “should have considered whether the acts committed were ‘grossly out of proportion to the alleged objective.’ … The political nature of the offenses would be ‘more difficult to accept’ if they involved ‘acts of an atrocious nature.’ ” 121 F.3d, at 524 (quoting U. N. Handbook, ¶152, at 36). The court further suggested that the BIA should have considered prior Circuit case law that “cast[s] light on what under the law are acts of [an] atrocious nature.” 121 F.3d, at 524. Citing its own opinion affirming the BIA’s decision in Matter of McMullen, see McMullen v. INS, 788 F.2d 591 (CA9 1986), the Court of Appeals stated that “[a] comparison of what the McMullen court found atrocious with the acts committed by Aguirre suggests a startling degree of difference.” 121 F.3d, at 524. It reasoned that while McMullen had involved “indiscriminate bombing, murder, torture, [and] the maiming of innocent civilians,” respondent’s “only acts against innocent Guatemalans were the disruption of some stores and his use of methods that we would all find objectionable if practiced upon us on a bus in the United States but which fall far short of the kind of atrocities attributed to McMullen and his associates.” 121 F.3d, at 524.

## Distinct arguments established by the extracts

**Paragraph 156 comparison.** The reported appellate argument compares the nature of the offense with the degree of feared persecution. The quotation supports attribution of that comparison to the portion of paragraph 156 on which the Ninth Circuit relied. It does not establish, by quotation alone, that the domestic statute incorporates that comparison, that the agency must adopt it, or that an otherwise applicable exclusion disappears whenever persecution is sufficiently severe. The excerpt's reference to Convention Article I(F)(b) identifies its subject; it supplies no new determination of treaty status or domestic legal force.

**Paragraph 152 and the political character of the offense.** The source separately corroborates the Ninth Circuit's attention to gross disproportionality between the acts and the alleged political objective, and to atrocious acts making a political characterization more difficult to accept. That is a different comparison from weighing offense seriousness against the persecution feared. The quoted language does not itself establish that atrocities are an indispensable condition of every serious nonpolitical crime, that disproportionality and atrociousness must both exist, or that the presence of either resolves every application without examining the statutory and agency framework. Nor does the extract establish an exhaustive list of required factors.

**McMullen comparison.** The source attributes to the Ninth Circuit both its description of McMullen's conduct and its view that Aguirre-Aguirre's conduct differed substantially. Those are the appellate court's comparative legal and factual characterizations, not a new stipulation or finding that revises the testimony and agency account in the frozen neutral supplement. The comparison may be an argument about consistent characterization or agency application. Its availability does not make McMullen's described acts a minimum constitutional or statutory threshold, prove that all lesser violence is political, or resolve the pending translation allegation.

The earlier neutral supplement reports that the Board's own McMullen framework compares political aspect with common-law criminal character, treating gross disproportionality or atrociousness as circumstances in which the political aspect would not predominate. The new quotations corroborate the appellate critique of that analysis; they do not prove that the Board omitted every relevant comparison. Any assertion that the Board failed to consider a factor must be tested against its actual recorded reasoning. Conversely, a framework's mention of a concept does not itself establish a reasoned or legally sufficient application.

## Supported competing legal paths

The following are neutral analytical possibilities, not additional recovered submissions or conclusions of this Court.

**Argument for the appellate approach.** The applicant can invoke paragraph 156's comparison as an interpretive reason for considering the severity of threatened persecution, and paragraph 152 plus McMullen as reasons to examine whether the reported protest violence was treated consistently with its political objective and criminal character. The strongest version must connect the guidance to the operative domestic provision and explain why the Board's actual construction or application is deficient. A comparison to more severe conduct can support scrutiny of the explanation; it does not itself establish a categorical exemption for less severe conduct. The source does not resolve whether that argument warrants affirmance, narrower legal correction or remand.

**Argument for the agency's construction and review limits.** The agency can maintain that the statutory exclusion and delegated construction must be identified before an external guidance passage becomes a mandatory judicial factor. It may defend an evaluation of political and criminal aspects that does not make atrocity the only route to a serious nonpolitical crime and distinguish the Board's comparative framework from a separate persecution-versus-offense balance. This position requires examination of the actual statute, authority and agency explanation; it cannot prevail merely by labeling the Handbook irrelevant or by ignoring a material consistency objection. The quotations establish what the appellate argument was, not that the agency either answered or failed it lawfully.

**Direct review or a bounded remand.** The Court may resolve a supported question of statutory construction and administrative-review allocation directly, while reserving another application question. If further review is necessary, a remand must identify the particular legal or explanatory defect and the actual task remaining. It cannot order a new hearing or impose additional criteria merely because the extracts do not contain the full Handbook paragraphs. Nor can it presume that a full paragraph contains an omitted exception, mandatory balancing factor, political-success requirement or safe harbor. A path depending on additional text must obtain the relevant neutral source extract first.
