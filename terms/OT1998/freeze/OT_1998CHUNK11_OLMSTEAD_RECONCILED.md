## 3. Exact propositions and join boundaries

These identify supported legal propositions, not assigned opinion sections. A Justice's acceptance of a fact, conditional observation, or ultimate remand does not imply a join in another Justice's rationale.

**A. Coverage and scope.** All eight accept *Yeskey*'s rule that Title II reaches state mental-health services and institutions; coverage does not establish discrimination, qualification, a particular modification, or a particular remedy. All preserve the question-1 and unresolved-constitutional-claim boundaries. No new §5, immunity, or benefits-adequacy holding follows.

**B. Affirmative integration rule.** Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer support this proposition: unjustified institutional isolation can itself be disability discrimination in the administration of services the State supplies. In this setting, community care is required when the State's treatment professionals determine it appropriate, the individual does not oppose it, and it can reasonably be accommodated considering available resources and the needs of others with mental disabilities. They do not require a nondisabled person eligible for the identical disability service. The disadvantage is unnecessary isolation as the condition of obtaining existing needed services, not simply disability's causal role in creating medical need. Neither clinical suitability alone nor a lower average community cost resolves the complete claim.

These five also support the two linked explanatory propositions: unjustified isolation perpetuates assumptions of incapacity or unworthiness to participate, and confinement diminishes ordinary family, social, educational, work, and cultural participation. Those explanations do not require individualized animus or proof that every institutional employee held a stereotype; neither condemns medically appropriate institutional care. Kennedy does not join this sufficient discrimination ground. Scalia and Thomas reject it on the separate statutory rationale below.

**C. Statute, regulation, and agency interpretation.** The same five read §12132 with the enacted findings, §12131's qualification/modification language, §12134's specified regulatory direction, and the integration and reasonable-modification regulations. *Choate*'s meaningful-access and neglect principles inform this construction while its no-general-adequacy limit remains. The Department's consistent, reasoned construction warrants respect. Existing *Auer* and *Haggar* require actual textual fit and delegated authority, and legal interpretation supplies no Georgia facts.

The provisional reference to a "valid integration regulation" is confined to the construction and enforcement at issue. These five do not decide a freestanding facial-validity challenge not presented or declare that full *Chevron* deference is indispensable to the result. Historical Parts I and III-A expressly reserved those matters; no changed simulated predicate requires resolving them here. This reservation does not undo the already governing *Auer* rule when its actual prerequisites are satisfied. Kennedy accepts the regulations and findings as interpretive aids within the discrimination predicate he requires; that is not a join in the five Justices' sufficient ground. Scalia and Thomas reject using the regulatory interpretation to overcome what they consider the operative statutory limitation.

**D. Individual qualification and choice.** The five integration-rule supporters retain the lower court's supported relevant-time determination that appropriate community care could meet these respondents' needs. Kennedy accepts that professional premise but finds it insufficient to establish his separate discrimination element. Scalia and Thomas accept it for resolving the legal issue without joining the affirmative duty. No one converts earlier periods of disagreement into uninterrupted professional approval, every placement into appropriate treatment, or the record into a finding about current condition. States generally may rely on reasonable professional assessments. Appropriate institutional services, including return when needs change, remain available; an unwilling individual need not accept an accommodation. The guardian provision is not authority to refuse medical necessities.

**E. Ordinary availability of the affirmative defense.** Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer accept the State's opportunity under §35.130(b)(7) to demonstrate that the necessary modification would fundamentally alter its service, program, or activity. Kennedy accepts this limitation on a claim proved under his different discrimination rule. Scalia and Thomas do not reach it as an affirmative holding. A bare shortage assertion or the label "existing program" does not prove the defense; a per-person price comparison does not disprove it.

**F. Full programwide corrective rationale.** O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer support considering resources available to the State, the range of services to other people with mental disabilities, and equitable administration of those services. Fixed institutional overhead, transition expense, actual continuing institutional needs, available funding and lawful transfer authority, appropriate placement capacity, and priorities can matter. Immediate relief for particular plaintiffs may be inequitable in light of that responsibility. This concerns the relevant service system, not an unbounded license to invoke every State expenditure or to presume unproved aggregate demand.

Kennedy independently supports the narrower common proposition that the cost inquiry may not be restricted to the two claimants' isolated additional expenditure and must permit the State's institutional/program overhead and relevant administrative responsibilities. He also supports substantial discretion in reasonable systems of cost accounting and appropriate deference to State funding choices. He does not thereby join the whole four-Justice rationale, its sufficient-plan illustration, or its affirmative liability premise. Stevens accepts the ordinary defense and does not forbid relevant effects on others, but declines the corrective rule as a reason to vacate the judgment now reviewed. His position is not a fifth full join in this package.

**G. Sufficient-plan illustration.** O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer support the following illustration of the reasonable-modification standard: a State can meet that standard by demonstrating a comprehensive, effectively working plan for placing qualified persons in less restrictive settings and a waiting list moving at a reasonable pace not controlled by an effort to keep institutions full. In those circumstances the court may not give a litigating person priority by displacing persons properly ahead in the placement queue.

This is more than saying a plan may be relevant evidence. The provisional requirement of a separate, additional disruption demonstration after all those conditions are proved is not retained. The historical four expressly regarded the demonstrated conditions as sufficient in the stated circumstances; neither the neutral record nor their simulated precedents changes that premise. Conversely, a paper plan, an inert list, nominal comprehensiveness, or preservation of institutional census does not meet the conditions. The illustration does not require every State to institute a particular planning procedure, set a universal waiting period, grant immunity to every waiting list, or prove that Georgia has satisfied it. The United States' narrower disruption formulation is a supported argument, not a controlling limitation on what these four may conclude.

Stevens does not join this sufficiency proposition. Kennedy recognizes that plans and priorities may matter but does not join this complete illustration or accept the specified facts as conclusively deciding all claims under his distinct theory. Breyer's acceptance of Kennedy's medical and funding cautions does not withdraw his support for the four-Justice plan proposition. Scalia and Thomas make no affirmative join.

**H. Kennedy's discrimination theory.** He retains the substantive distinction between unjustified discriminatory treatment and a claim to a preferred service arrangement. A viable theory could compare treatment for medical problems of comparable seriousness: whether Georgia generally supplies that treatment in the most integrated appropriate setting but, without adequate justification, imposes a more onerous institutional condition on mentally disabled persons. Regulations and Congress's findings inform that inquiry; they do not make clinical suitability itself sufficient discrimination. The State's existing community program and respondents' suitability therefore do not alone resolve his objection.

His complete simulated *O'Connor* and *Oncale* joins qualify the historical formulation's evidentiary reach: no actual nondisabled recipient, particular outside-group beneficiary, or exclusive comparator route is indispensable. Competent direct or circumstantial proof can establish the prohibited treatment and cause. The comparable-services theory remains a supported substantive route, not the sole permissible form of evidence; animus and unfair stereotypes likewise are possible proof, not universal additional elements. The remand is justified by the lower court's uncompleted discrimination inquiry under his construction, not by the absence of a named outside-group recipient alone. The lower courts must decide whether the existing allegations and materials establish the statutory violation and, if not, whether amendment or further material should be permitted. This limited evidentiary qualification does not make him join the five Justices' rule that the established unjustified isolation itself supplies the sufficient discrimination ground. Breyer does not join Kennedy's distinct theory or reopening of liability.

Kennedy rejects a duty to create a community-treatment program where none exists; he does not treat every expansion of an existing program as necessarily the creation of a new one. Nor does he convert the suggested comparable-services inquiry into a universal numeric cost test, a prescribed new hearing, or a finding that all State allocation choices are beyond review.

**I. Clinical and administrative cautions.** Kennedy and Breyer support the need to avoid poorly supported deinstitutionalization, inadequately staffed community placements, and litigation incentives to discharge people into settings that cannot meet their needs. They support substantial respect for responsible treating professionals and appropriate respect for funding decisions. These cautions preserve genuine clinical and administrative judgment; they do not create an unreviewable medical or fiscal veto or decide the disputed factual justification. Their current *Bragdon* objective-assessment commitments remain: a professional title or sincere belief cannot establish an otherwise unsupported statutory fact. *Youngberg*'s constitutional standard is not silently made the whole ADA rule. No actual dispute over continued contrary treating-professional recommendations is decided here.

**J. Scalia and Thomas's opposing statutory ground.** Both require an actual discrimination predicate and causation under §12132. The fact that disability explains the need for services, combined with eligibility for a preferred delivery setting, does not itself show the required discrimination. Their common ground is that an antidiscrimination command is not a general entitlement to the desired level or mode of medical care; findings and an implementing regulation cannot substitute for the operative prohibition. *Davis*, *Choate*, and *Traynor* supply the access/accommodation/benefit distinction. Congress's express integration direction is the strongest objection, but they read it within the discrimination requirement. Thomas also emphasizes statutory contrast and federalism/notice; Scalia's agreement with the statutory ground does not require an independent Spending Clause holding.

Their exact intraclass limit follows their express prior simulated public commitments: neither the absence of a nondisabled recipient nor membership of all service users in the protected disability population supplies categorical immunity. No actual favored comparator is the only permissible proof. Both joined *O'Connor*'s distinction between the protected population and prohibited cause; Thomas authored, and Scalia fully joined, *Oncale*'s express causal-proof rule without an actual opposite-sex coworker. Their objection here is therefore substantive statutory insufficiency, not categorical incapacity to discriminate within a disability-only program. These employment holdings supply a bounded analogy and Justice-specific consistency constraint, not an already controlling Title II integration answer.

The result does not depend on affirmatively finding that Georgia's allocation was actually neutral, its queue legitimate, or its resources exhausted. The State's submissions and the historical dissent's characterization are not new findings of those facts. The dissenting legal position rejects the plaintiffs' asserted sufficient ground; it does not adjudicate different claims or authorize unlawful confinement.

## 6. No invented majority, subset, or remedy

The five Associate supporters of B and C actually share the operative statutory integration rule. Kennedy's agreement that an integration problem can sometimes be discriminatory is not a sixth join in that sufficient ground. His proposed discriminatory-treatment inquiry asks for a different showing, even with the qualified evidentiary routes in H.
