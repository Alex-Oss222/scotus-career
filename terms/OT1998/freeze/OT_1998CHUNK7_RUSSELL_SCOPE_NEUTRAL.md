## Exact lower-court category

The district court's exception to its disposition sustaining the $100 per-election cap concerned candidates for **Arkansas Supreme Court Justice and Arkansas Court of Appeals Judge**. It was not an exception for every judicial office. The following paragraph is mechanically copied from the authorized extraction of the district judgment, 978 F. Supp. 1211:

```text
B. Arkansas Code section 7-6-203(a)(l) and (b)(1), which imposes a $100 per election contribution limit applicable to all other offices, is upheld, except as to the offices of Supreme Court Justice and Court of Appeals Judge. Application of this section to the offices of Supreme Court Justice and Court of Appeals Judge, is enjoined.
```

The extraction's paragraphs 46-49 confirm that same distinction. The district court sustained the $100 limit for the other covered offices, while enjoining its application to the two named appellate judicial offices. Its Supreme Court example involves statewide elections even though Act I omitted that office from its statutory statewide-office category; its Court of Appeals example involves districts larger than state Senate districts. The geographic meaning of statewide must therefore not be equated with Act I's list of offices subject to the $300 cap.

References to the trial court's judicial exception or its conclusion that the $100 judicial cap was impermissible must name those two offices. Nothing in this extraction establishes a trial holding invalidating the limit for every trial judge or other judicial candidate. Nor does that limit on the source's holding establish that every other judicial application is constitutionally valid in all circumstances. The district's judgment is evidence of the proceedings below, not a controlling holding of this Court. The frozen neutral packet separately identifies the Eighth Circuit's broader invalidation of the candidate limits and its remand for relief; this addition neither erases that appellate judgment nor changes the supplied scope of review.

## Evidence and legal conclusions remain separate

The district court reported seven trial days, including special-election experience under the new caps. Its House and Senate figures and $50-donation calculations concerned those legislative races: approximately $7,000-$16,000 and $23,000-$39,000 in historical per-election costs, requiring 320 and 780 donors at the upper ends in the court's illustration. The calculation was not a finding that all candidates had recruited those donor counts. The court credited legislative testimony and the reported special-election fundraising experience; two professional fundraisers gave contrary testimony. None of those propositions is automatically a finding about every appellate judicial, trial judicial or other office.

For the two named appellate judicial offices, the district court relied on a combination of geographic reach and solicitation constraints. It reported that Canon 5C(2) barred a judicial candidate's personal solicitation or acceptance and limited campaign committees' solicitation to the period beginning 180 days before a primary and ending forty-five days after the last contested election in which the candidate participated. It then concluded that the combined obstacles prevented candidates for those offices from amassing resources for effective advocacy. The reported canon restrictions are distinct from the contribution statute; the district's conclusion about their combined effect is not a statutory exception or a new finding by this Court.

The extraction's paragraph 49 contains the phrase difference in degree while expressly concluding that the limit is unconstitutionally low. The scope conclusion here rests on the unambiguous introductory judgment and the twice-named offices, not on silently correcting or converting that isolated phrase into a different legal test. The extraction's transcription remains intact.

## Competing analytical paths

These possibilities describe legal arguments supported by the supplied evidence and entering law. They are not claims about unprovided filings or commitments.

**Office-specific assessment.** A party defending differentiated treatment can rely on the district's actual evidence: legislative campaigns' reported costs, testimony and special-election fundraising may support a different burden assessment from the larger geographic reach and constrained solicitation of the two named appellate judicial campaigns. A court considering that route must examine the actual contribution-limit burden and the asserted anticorruption justification for each covered category; it may not replace all other offices with the label nonjudicial or assume every judicial candidate shares the named offices' demonstrated combination of burdens.

**Challenge to the differentiation or its sufficiency.** A challenger may contend that successful campaigns or incumbent testimony do not establish adequate opportunities for all affected candidates, that historical averages omit competitive or challenger needs, or that the restrictions impair effective association despite those examples. Conversely, a defender of the cap for the named appellate judicial offices may argue that solicitation constraints and geographic scale alone do not prove prohibitory fundraising effects and that the contribution regime must be assessed in operation, including lawful financing channels. Those are analytical arguments, not new factual findings, evidence of successful alternative fundraising or authority to disregard the lower court's findings without the proper review standard.

**Disposition and remedy.** The Court may reach an office-specific result, a broader result supported by the applicable record and law, or a bounded remand identifying an actual unresolved issue. This correction mandates none of them. Any disposition must identify the exact office category and contribution provision it addresses, reconcile its treatment of the district findings and appellate reasoning, and tie any injunction or severance to the supported legal ground. Trial legislative evidence cannot silently become all-office evidence, and the trial exception cannot silently become a universal judicial exemption. Candidate limits, ordinary-PAC receipts and the small-donor distinction remain independent components; this office-scope repair resolves none of the latter two.
