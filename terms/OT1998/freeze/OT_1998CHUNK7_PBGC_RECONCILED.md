## P1 — the actual statutory connector must be construed

The material scheme is concrete. Section 412(n)(1)(B) addresses unpaid required contributions exceeding $1 million. The pertinent §412(n)(4)(B) rule makes the lien arise on the sixtieth day after the required installment's due date. Section 412(n)(4)(C) directs tax treatment for an amount **with respect to which the specified lien is imposed** and applies rules similar to ERISA §4068(c), (d) and (e). Section 4068(c), 29 U.S.C. §1368(c), expressly addresses a Title 11 or insolvency case and treatment as a tax for Title 11 purposes. The lien trigger and imported scope must be given their own work; the threshold alone does not give every unpaid pension obligation tax priority.

The circuit acknowledged a connection but rejected it because §507 did not itself specifically refer back to §412(n). Its categorical one-direction requirement is not supplied by Reorganized CF&I. Congress's operative tax-treatment instruction does not become legally invisible because it appears outside Title 11 or incorporates a provision there indirectly. Courts must read the enactments together, determine the imported treatment's scope, and then apply the actual conditions. Functional classification cannot substitute for that inquiry.

The earlier CF&I claim was the separate ten-percent §4971(a) assessment. Its payment did not satisfy the contribution deficiency or replace pension/PBGC liability. The seven Associates joining Souter's Part I treated that sanction according to its actual statutory operation and rejected the label alone; they adopted no general Lorber checklist or rule that every pension-related payment is penal. This claim seeks the unpaid contributions themselves and invokes a specific direction expressly linked to Title 11. That is the concrete difference requiring statutory analysis. It does not establish that the condition is met or that ordinary contributions inherently support government. Thomas's previous tax-treatment disagreement remains his own position and is not converted into assent to the seven's functional rationale; the express connector and his Noland statutory-priority reasoning independently support the common result here.

**The alternative ground is preserved in full.** Note 4 reports that the bankruptcy and district courts found the installment due fewer than sixty days before filing, concluded that no lien arose before the petition, and treated the automatic stay as preventing later attachment. The circuit expressly declined to decide that ground because its tax-characterization conclusion made it unnecessary. Those reported determinations and the debtor's legal argument remain available on remand. This Court does not find attachment, declare the lien unnecessary, resolve every stay exception, or assume that postpetition attachment is possible. A lawful resolution of this alternative can still defeat tax priority.

Affirmance on that alternative is the strongest competing disposition; it is not ignored merely because PBGC identifies a connector. The eight return the distinct lien/stay inquiry the circuit left undecided after correcting the categorical statutory error. This remand does not require new evidence or a new lien proceeding, and does not disturb the underlying reported timing fact. The lower courts may resolve the legal issue on the existing record and affirm denial on an adequate alternative. PBGC's broader demand for immediate priority fails because the express linkage retains its conditions and applicable priority requirements. No §510 question or payment order follows.

## P2 — origin of the additional debt, with Reading intact

The additional contribution obligation is compensation attributable to prepetition employee services. A later payment due date, an unterminated plan, continuing funding obligations and later PBGC termination do not by themselves create a new postpetition debt. Denial of additional §503(b)(1)(A) priority rests on that origin. The actual postpetition-service component already allowed administrative status remains untouched, as both paragraph 8 and note 5 establish.

Reading is an independent route requiring a real answer. It accorded administrative treatment to the receiver's postpetition negligence liability without requiring a measurable benefit to the estate. The Court therefore does not affirm on a universal estate-benefit prerequisite. Even assuming that a qualifying statutory liability may receive the same treatment, the additional claim here remains tied to earlier labor rather than a newly incurred postpetition injury or liability. A statutory duty's continued enforceability does not determine the rank or date of origin of every amount owed under it.

The strongest PBGC argument is that a debtor operating its business and retaining its pension plan incurs a postpetition cost or wrong by failing to fund the continuing statutory obligation. That defeats any assertion that filing permits disregard of all funding duties. It does not, without more, convert compensation already earned into postpetition compensation or establish a new Reading injury for the disputed portion. The Court does not categorically classify all pension obligations as merely contractual or exclude all postpetition statutory violations from administrative treatment. It uses the sufficient debt-origin ground identified in the actual lower discussion, rather than depending on the lower court's broader contract characterization.

The Coal Act analogy concerns a distinct trigger tied to continued corporate existence and different asserted tax treatment. Its tax treatment is not the sole distinction supporting this P2 judgment: the additional contribution debt here retains its prepetition-service origin. Thus correction of P1 does not destabilize P2. Section 1114's express treatment of retiree medical payments is a separate enacted category; it supplies no rule that Congress can create a bankruptcy consequence only by locating the provision in Title 11. No new benefit-category exclusion, equitable redistribution or claim disallowance is adopted.

## P3 — identify the liability before applying the allowance rules

Section 1362(b)(1)(A) defines termination liability by the total unfunded benefit liabilities as of termination. Section 1301(a)(18) measures the benefit liabilities using PBGC-prescribed assumptions for §1344 purposes and subtracts current plan assets at the relevant date. This is an actual statutory source and delegation. The fact that a definition serves ERISA does not mean it disappears when the ERISA-created obligation is asserted in bankruptcy.

The lower court's two categorical premises cannot sustain the chosen amount. First, §1301's introductory ERISA-purpose language does not by itself displace the measure of that very liability. Second, §1123(a)(4)'s equal treatment within a class does not require every legally different underlying claim to arise under identical assumptions. Section 1144(d)'s preservation of other federal law requires an actual incompatibility; calling the proceeding bankruptcy is no substitute for identifying one. The Code's distribution policy does not authorize judicial replacement of the liability definition merely because its measure produces a different unsecured-claim amount.

Section 502(b)'s petition-date allowance command remains binding. Its relationship to the termination-date liability must be worked out, not erased in either direction. Annuity-pricing assumptions might serve a different purpose from the proposed prudent-investor discount, and valuation choices affect distributions; those are the estate's serious scope and application arguments. They justify applying the actual statutory commands together. They do not establish that ERISA's incorporated measure is categorically irrelevant. Conversely, incorporating assumptions does not entitle PBGC to immediate allowance of its proposed figure without the Code inquiry.

Haggar supplies authority-before-deference: enforce clear statutory limits and examine any actual conflict before accepting a reasonable agency choice within the genuine delegation. The express assumptions provision supplies authority here; agency identity alone would not. A reasonable lawfully prescribed assumption cannot be replaced merely because the judge prefers another rate. The remand does not hold every rate reasonable, invent a newly preserved challenge, order the agency to promulgate new rules or compel an evidentiary hearing. Any properly presented statutory-fit, reasonableness or application challenge receives ordinary consideration. Recognition of different statutory dates does not itself authorize duplicate discounting.

Rash's statutory retained-collateral measure is not a universal prudent-investor rule. LaSalle's distinction between numerical valuation and the legal allocation of property preserves both inquiries without imposing a market auction on this claim. Stevens's and Ginsburg's Haggar position favors deciding an application when it is supported, but does not supply the distinct petition-date allowance calculation left unresolved here. The remand follows an identified unfinished task, not an assumption that deference always requires remand.
