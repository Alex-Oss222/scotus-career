## A2 — actual Handbook persecution-balancing argument

The Ninth Circuit relied on the quoted portion of Handbook paragraph 156 calling for a balance between the nature of the presumed offense and the degree of persecution feared in applying Convention Article I(F)(b). That is a supported interpretive argument, not merely a hypothetical humanitarian policy position. Its strongest form asks that the domestic exclusion be construed with that guidance and the grave consequence of return in view.

The selected result remains unchanged. Former §1253(h)(2)(C), once its conditions are properly found, makes paragraph (1) inapplicable. The quoted guidance does not supply an enacted additional condition requiring the agency to balance prospective persecution against a crime already found to fall within that exception. The Board could assume the persecution showing for purposes of addressing the independent exception. The Court need not decide the full Handbook's authority in every setting, the enforceability of a treaty, or the contents of unprovided portions of paragraph 156 to resolve that statutory question. It does not declare international guidance categorically irrelevant.

This is distinct from determining whether an offense is serious and nonpolitical in the first place. No balancing reservation permits the agency to ignore any statutory condition, and no discretion to deny otherwise mandatory withholding is created.

## A3 — paragraph 152 and the actual McMullen comparison

The new extract separately establishes the Ninth Circuit's reliance on paragraph 152's language about acts grossly out of proportion to the alleged objective and atrocious acts making political characterization more difficult. It also establishes the court's comparison of Aguirre-Aguirre's conduct with the more severe bombing, murder, torture and maiming it attributed to McMullen. Those are attributed appellate descriptions and arguments, not new Supreme Court findings or verified corrections of the testimony.

**The Ninth Circuit must not be misdescribed as necessarily announcing that atrocity is an indispensable element of every excluded crime.** Its reported comparison can be understood as an objection about consistent characterization and reasoned application. The strongest applicant path is accordingly that the agency failed to explain why this materially different conduct falls on the same side of its political/criminal comparison. A court must examine that objection against the Board's actual reason, rather than dismissing it by a label of deference.

The Board's supplied framework and explanation answer the identified legal objection sufficiently to retain the independent result. Its framework compares political aspect with ordinary criminal character, with gross disproportionality **or** atrociousness as circumstances defeating political predominance. Its actual explanation compares the political dissatisfaction with property destruction and assaults against civilians and finds the latter disproportionate. The existence of a more atrocious earlier example does not make that example a minimum threshold for the alternative disproportionate-conduct route. A lesser quantum of violence does not, by itself, prove a different legal classification or unexplained inconsistency. Conversely, a political motive does not cease to matter simply because the conduct is criminal.

The prior rule that a separate atrocity finding is not mandatory is retained as the boundary of the adopted statutory construction, not as an invented quotation or categorical holding of the Ninth Circuit. No political-necessity or political-success condition is extracted from the new Handbook passages, and no complete paragraph or omitted exception is presumed. The prior rejection of mandatory additional political-necessity/success findings rests on the earlier neutral account and actual statutory framework, not on text absent from these new excerpts.

The judgment corrects the circuit's legal review and does not foreclose a properly preserved, independently supported objection to actual agency consistency or findings. It supplies no new credibility determination and does not make every bus burning, civilian assault or unsuccessful protest a categorical serious nonpolitical crime.

## A4 — actual procedural facts, regulatory trigger and remedy

### Translation and transcription claim

Aguirre-Aguirre testified in Spanish and alleges translation and transcription errors. The claim was first advanced in his Brief in Opposition to Certiorari; he did not present it to the BIA or Ninth Circuit. It is therefore not a defect found below or a ground on which the Ninth Circuit had ordered its remand. The allegation alone supplies no basis to affirm that remand or to declare the testimony unreliable.

He has **already filed a BIA motion for a new hearing based on the alleged errors**. That is an existing procedural avenue, not a filing the Court now commands. Its grant or denial, the alleged errors' accuracy and materiality, the precise motion contents, and any resulting agency action remain unestablished. This judgment neither decides the motion nor prevents its lawful consideration. It orders no hearing, stay, deadline, reopening or immediate execution of removal. Nor does the pending motion automatically defeat review of the presented statutory questions.

The strongest contrary remedial path is that a material translation error could affect both the conduct attributed to the applicant and the serious-nonpolitical-crime inquiry. That is a real concern, but relief on that ground requires a supported procedural basis and an assessment of the allegation in its proper forum. Here its first presentation in this Court and the existing agency motion permit the Court to decide the presented legal issues without resolving that new factual allegation. The Court does not find the allegation false, treat the motion as futile, or announce a universal forfeiture rule for every translation claim.

### Missing BIA brief and the actual evidence-triggered burden

The source affirmatively reports that Aguirre-Aguirre did not file a BIA brief despite receiving an extension. That is supported procedural history. It is **not** a concession of the statutory bar, a default on withholding, proof that he supplied no evidence, a finding that testimony is false, or universal forfeiture of every objection. His actual translation claim has its own established presentation history; the missing brief does not enlarge it.

The quoted 1995 regulation, 8 C.F.R. §208.16(c)(3), operates conditionally: **if the evidence indicates that one or more withholding-denial grounds apply, the applicant bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that the indicated grounds do not apply**. The sequence matters. A decision applying that burden must identify the indicated ground and its evidentiary trigger, then evaluate the applicant's actual showing; a missing appellate brief supplies neither step.

On the existing, uncorrected agency record, the reported repeated gasoline-fueled bus burnings and assaults on civilians, together with the Board's comparison of those acts to the political objective, provide evidence indicating the serious-nonpolitical-crime ground. That is sufficient to make the quoted conditional burden relevant under the adopted legal construction. The conclusion is based on the conduct evidence and agency account, **not** on the failure to file the BIA brief. It neither adjudicates the truth of the newly alleged translation errors nor makes every violent political act satisfy the trigger as a rule.

Once triggered, the applicant's burden is to show by a preponderance that the indicated exclusion does not apply. Political purpose and context already in the record remain relevant to that showing. The Court does not declare the burden unsatisfied merely because no BIA brief was filed and does not replace the Board's actual explanation with a procedural default. The circuit's remaining lawful review must apply the correct construction and burden to the actual administrative record, respecting any properly applicable review constraints. No automatic new evidence or Board reconsideration is ordered.

The regulation's preponderance allocation is distinct from the statute's **serious reasons for considering** the applicant committed the excluded offense. Both differ from the persecution-risk showing, which the Board did not decide, and from the standard for a reviewing court to displace an agency factual finding. None requires a criminal conviction. Elias-Zacarias is not converted into a universal reopening rule or a substitute for the regulatory trigger.

### Refined disposition

Reverse the Ninth Circuit's judgment and remand through that court for review consistent with the actual delegated construction, statutory exclusion, agency explanation and distinct discretionary-asylum decision. The reversal removes the identified erroneous legal requirements. It does not order a new Board hearing or a renewed persecution determination. Any properly preserved residual issue retains its lawful channel; the newly asserted translation claim is not silently reclassified as a preserved issue decided by the Ninth Circuit, and its existing BIA motion remains unresolved.

No asylum or withholding award, new removal command, automatic stay, factual reopening or motion disposition follows. The Board's asylum denial remains a separate exercise of discretion concerning conduct against innocent civilians, not an automatic statutory asylum bar borrowed from withholding.
