## Objective criminal and sentencing record

Jones was convicted of kidnapping with death resulting under 18 U.S.C. §1201(a)(2). The indictment alleged the offense occurred within the United States' special maritime and territorial jurisdiction. The lower opinion reports that Tracie Joy McBride was abducted at gunpoint from Goodfellow Air Force Base on February 18, 1995; another servicemember attempting to help was injured. It recounts Jones's statement admitting abduction and killing, his directing investigators to the body, and autopsy findings of fatal blunt-force trauma and sexual assault. These are the published record recitations relevant to sentencing, not an invitation to relitigate guilt or assume every disputed aggravating inference.

Trial began October 16, 1995, and the guilt verdict was returned October 23. The Government gave notice that it would seek death, and a separate sentencing hearing followed. The death recommendation and special findings are dated November 3, 1995. The district court imposed death; the Fifth Circuit affirmed conviction and sentence.

The special findings establish the following, independently of any later description:

| Component | Actual finding |
|---|---|
| Intent | Unanimous findings that Jones intentionally killed McBride and intentionally inflicted serious bodily injury resulting in her death. |
| Statutory aggravation | Unanimous findings of death during kidnapping and an especially heinous, cruel and depraved offense involving torture or serious physical abuse; no finding of the submitted grave-risk-to-another or substantial-planning/premeditation factors. |
| Nonstatutory aggravation | The jury rejected future dangerousness; it unanimously found 3(B), the victim's young age, slight stature, background and unfamiliarity with San Angelo, and 3(C), personal characteristics and the effect on her family. |
| Mitigation | One or more jurors found ten of eleven listed factors; the general other-background/character line received zero. Counts were: no significant prior record 6; impaired capacity 2; severe mental/emotional disturbance 1; childhood abuse/lack of protection 4; military service 8; likely good prison behavior 3; remorse 4; harm to his daughter 9; unusual internal duress/stress 3; neurological/psychological disorders 1. Seven jurors added a separate entry identifying his former wife. These are factor findings, not votes on a different ultimate sentence. |

The two challenged nonstatutory findings are explicit. Sochor's refusal to infer reliance on an unsupported factor from an undifferentiated advisory recommendation does not permit denying that the jury found these factors. On the other hand, an affirmative factor finding does not itself quantify its influence on the final choice.

## Actual requests, objections, charge and forms

**Written requests.** Requested instruction 4 stated that the Government bore the burden of persuading the jurors that execution was required and that failure to persuade even one juror should result in life without release. Requested instruction 5, titled “Unanimity Required Only for Death Sentence,” included an instruction to report inability to agree and said the court would impose life without release. It also described a unanimous conclusion that punishment should be no less than life without release. These are the requested words, not a determination that every phrase was correct or preserved every later challenge.

**Oral and other written objections.** The November 1 preliminary objections and November 2 charge conference requested that descriptions of deciding death also mention life without release “or a lesser sentence.” At the charge conference counsel specifically referred to requested instruction 5 and asked that the jury be told that if it could not agree on death, the court would impose life without release “or a lesser sentence.” Counsel later renewed requested instruction 28 concerning failure to arrive at a unanimous death decision and the statutory/constitutional objections. The court overruled the objections except for adding a no-adverse-inference instruction about Jones's failure to testify. Thus, the original trial record supplies more than a bare absence of objection, but it also contains affirmative requests using the disputed lesser-sentence wording. The effect must be determined claim by claim.

**Delivered charge.** The judge described a unanimous recommendation among death, life without release or another lesser sentence; said death was never required; required all twelve jurors to agree on death or life without release; and told jurors not to concern themselves with the sentence the court would impose if they did not recommend either death or life without release. It required individual consideration of mitigation and explained that weighing is not mechanical counting. It instructed jurors to deliberate but not surrender an honest belief merely because others disagreed or to finish the case.

**Forms and accompanying directions.** Form B was for unanimous death. The judge directed use of C or D if jurors did not unanimously find the requisite weighing outcome or were otherwise not unanimous in recommending death. Form C expressly required a unanimous recommendation of life without release, with all-juror signatures; Form D recommended some other lesser sentence and contained a foreperson line without an express unanimity phrase. Form A addressed failure to find the intent or statutory-aggravation prerequisite. Form B and the nondiscrimination certification were signed; the latter does not itself resolve whether the sentencing-option instructions were misleading.

These directions create the actual interpretive question. Neither the general unanimity instruction nor the C/D directions may be silently omitted. The final death verdict alone cannot prove that the jury understood every legally available alternative; a possible reading alone likewise does not establish the required degree of unconstitutional confusion or an outcome effect.

## Verified predecision merits positions and concessions

The Government now expressly agrees that this kidnapping conviction permits only death or life without release: §1201 supplies death or life imprisonment, and parole/good-time credits cannot shorten life. It nevertheless argues that this was unsettled at trial, that Jones requested lesser-sentence language and could have gained an advantage from it, and that Jones does not seek reversal merely for the isolated insertion of that option. The concession about lawful punishments does not concede the alleged misleading effect, preservation, prejudice, or the required response to deadlock.

The Government defends the whole charge: the initial unanimity requirement applies to every recommendation; later omission of the word does not undo it; jurors were told not to consider the judge's eventual sentence; and a genuinely divided jury would report disagreement rather than recommend a punishment no juror wanted. It notes that some unanimous special findings also used only a foreperson signature. It distinguishes Mills's blank-mitigation-finding problem from ultimate sentencing disagreement. These arguments must be assessed alongside, rather than in place of, the actual C/D directions.

The Government expressly accepts that refusal of the requested instruction is preserved as an independent claim, but argues that the request did not preserve an objection to the instructions actually delivered. It invokes Rule 30, lack of obvious error, lack of demonstrated prejudice, and Rule 606(b)'s bar on proof by internal juror accounts. Jones distinguishes his combined nonunanimity/lesser-sentence-inference claim from an objection to the isolated unavailable option. He claims preservation through the rejected request, later new-trial/reconsideration motions, the appellate merits consideration and §3595's review provision. Whether those routes satisfy the applicable contemporaneous-objection requirement remains a legal issue; none supplies an invented trial objection. He asserts a reasonable likelihood of the misleading inference even without juror affidavits, while separately seeking to use them.

The Government does **not** concede that deadlock necessarily requires a judicial nondeath sentence. It expressly argues that a new capital-sentencing jury may be convened, invoking traditional manifest necessity, §3593(b)(2)'s good-cause discharge provision and §3593(e)'s unanimity requirement. It reads §3594's otherwise clause to concern a unanimous recommendation of a lesser punishment, not the absence of any recommendation. Jones's contrary argument reads otherwise to require the least severe lawful punishment after deadlock. This is a live statutory dispute. The Government's death-or-life concession does not resolve the availability of another hearing, and the court of appeals' own construction is not controlling entering law.

## Lower judgment, alternative grounds and review posture

The Fifth Circuit rejected the requested deadlock instruction because it read §3593(e) to require unanimity for every jury sentencing recommendation, treated a failure to agree as no verdict, and relied on §3593(b)(2)(C) to allow another sentencing jury. Its conclusion that life was not the default consequence is a statutory holding below, not a factual finding binding the Supreme Court.

The panel rejected the claimed misleading effect of the charge as a whole. It treated the verdict-form-format challenge as unpreserved and found no error in context. It separately found that reference to a lesser sentence was error because §1201 permitted only death or life for kidnapping with death resulting and no meaningful release distinction changed that result. It treated that specific complaint as unpreserved: counsel had requested adding “or a lesser sentence” to other passages, but the court had not adopted that requested addition, so the panel applied plain-error review rather than its invited-error doctrine. It found the error insufficiently clear under its understanding of the previously unreviewed statute. This does not establish intentional waiver under Olano or settle whether the more specific written/oral deadlock requests preserved a related claim.

The panel also rejected use of post-verdict accounts of internal juror misunderstanding under Rule 606(b). It recited that jurors confirmed their votes when polled and no clarifying question was submitted. Those objective procedural recitations may enter; the inadmissible internal accounts are not evidence that confusion or coercion occurred. The effect of §3593(c)'s relaxed sentencing-evidence rule on post-verdict impeachment is a distinct argument, not a basis to disregard Rule 606(b) automatically.

For factors 3(B) and 3(C), the panel found duplication, vagueness and overbreadth. It reasoned that personal characteristics encompassed the listed age/stature/background/unfamiliarity subjects and that undefined background, characteristics and unfamiliarity supplied insufficient guidance. It nevertheless affirmed after removing those factors and purporting to apply harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt to the remaining statutory aggravation and the mitigation. It cited the Government's emphasis on the statutory factors and their eligibility function. Its statement that appellate reweighing and the alternative harmlessness methods lead to the same result must be tested against current law; a new appellate sentencing choice, proof that valid factors support death, and proof that error did not contribute are distinct inquiries. The isolated panel opinion contains no separate dissent.

## Enacted text and rule components

The Government Publishing Office's 1994 Code copies verify §§3593, 3594 and 3595, enacted September 13, 1994, before this offense. No later statutory or rule amendment is imported.

- **Section 3593(a):** Advance notice identifies the proposed aggravators. It expressly permits factors concerning the offense's effect on the victim and family, specified victim-impact information and other relevant information. Good cause may support amendment. This permits a subject; it does not validate every vague formulation or forbidden use.
- **Section 3593(b):** Ordinarily the guilt jury conducts the separate hearing. A newly impaneled jury may serve for a guilty plea, a bench-trial conviction, good-cause discharge of the guilt jury, or necessary reconsideration after initial imposition of sentence; a court-only hearing requires the defendant's motion and Government approval. Whether the good-cause/reconsideration clauses authorize repeated efforts after a sentencing deadlock must be read with §3594 rather than presumed.
- **Section 3593(c):** Aggravators require Government proof beyond a reasonable doubt; mitigation requires the defendant's preponderance showing. Sentencing information is generally admissible without ordinary trial admissibility constraints, but may be excluded where probative value is outweighed by unfair prejudice, confusion or misleading effect. Rebuttal and fair argument opportunity are protected. This does not itself settle post-verdict juror impeachment.
- **Section 3593(d):** Aggravator findings must be unanimous; a mitigator may be found and considered by one or more jurors without others' concurrence. If no statutory aggravator is found, the court must impose a lawful sentence other than death.
- **Section 3593(e):** After the specified statutory-aggravation prerequisite, the sentencer considers whether aggravation sufficiently outweighs mitigation, or absent mitigation is itself sufficient, to justify death. “Based upon this consideration, the jury by unanimous vote, or if there is no jury, the court, shall recommend whether the defendant should be sentenced to death, to life imprisonment without possibility of release or some other lesser sentence.” The statutory words do not by themselves resolve what the court does when the jury makes no unanimous recommendation.
- **Section 3594, complete operative text:** “Upon a recommendation under section 3593(e) that the defendant should be sentenced to death or life imprisonment without possibility of release, the court shall sentence the defendant accordingly. Otherwise, the court shall impose any lesser sentence that is authorized by law. Notwithstanding any other law, if the maximum term of imprisonment for the offense is life imprisonment, the court may impose a sentence of life imprisonment without possibility of release.” The scope of otherwise and the substantive offense's authorized penalty must each be addressed.
- **Section 3595:** The court of appeals reviews the entire trial/sentencing record, procedures and special findings and addresses raised substantive and procedural issues. Section (c)(2)(A) concerns passion, prejudice or another arbitrary factor; (B) insufficient admissible support for the required aggravating finding; and (C) another legal error requiring reversal that was properly preserved under the criminal rules. If a listed condition is found, the statute directs remand for reconsideration under §3593 or nondeath sentencing. The following sentence forbids reversal or vacatur for an error that can be harmless, including an erroneous aggravating finding, “where the Government establishes beyond a reasonable doubt that the error was harmless.” Keep that express Government burden separate from the defendant's ordinary burden under Rule 52(b), and constitutional correction separate from mere factor counting.

Section 1201(a)(2)'s death-resulting penalty is death or life imprisonment, as quoted and construed in the lower opinion; no independent term of years is established as authorized for this offense. Rule 30 requires a distinct, timely objection identifying the matter and grounds before deliberation; a written request, oral refinement, separate form objection and intentional waiver must not be equated. Event-effective Rule 52(b) is governed by Olano and Johnson below. Rule 606(b)'s internal-deliberation restriction and its exceptions for extraneous prejudicial information or outside influence govern any use of juror accounts; an internal misunderstanding of the judge's charge does not become an outside influence by being reported after verdict. No later exception is presumed.

## Supported alternative paths

**Deadlock consequence and duty to explain.** Jones's strongest route reads §3594's otherwise clause and §1201 together to require nondeath sentencing without another attempt to obtain a death recommendation, and argues that a capital jury choosing punishment should understand the legally available consequence. It must answer the unanimity wording and distinguish the jury's decision from the court's later responsibilities, as well as the difference between the written and oral requested formulations. The Government's competing statutory route permits another jury under the good-cause discharge/traditional manifest-necessity reasoning and confines otherwise to a unanimous lesser recommendation; it must explain how that construction fits the unconditional words “Otherwise, the court shall impose any lesser sentence that is authorized by law.” Independently, a route may find no statutory/constitutional requirement to explain a later deadlock consequence where the charge accurately makes death optional and preserves individual judgment. That route must address the actual lesser-sentence language and C/D directions, not rely solely on Shannon's nonsentencing setting. The law's consequence of deadlock and the obligation to tell jurors that consequence are separate holdings.

**Misleading alternatives and preservation.** A supported path finds the written and specific oral requests sufficient to alert the judge to the deadlock/alternative problem, while another distinguishes the preserved requested-instruction claim from an unpreserved objection to any mention of a lesser sentence and from form-format objections. The oral request's own lesser-sentence phrase supplies a serious counterargument to preservation of a no-lesser-sentence claim; it is not automatically intentional waiver of every related constitutional objection. If Rule 52(b) applies, address each condition at its proper burden and scope. If the claim is preserved constitutional trial error, apply the Government's governing harmlessness burden. A conclusion that there is no reasonable likelihood of the asserted misunderstanding must address the whole charge and forms; a conclusion that the directions were misleading must address the general unanimity, individual-conscience, never-required-death and life-without-release directions. Neither route may use barred juror mental-process accounts as proof.

**Factor validity.** The Government's supported alternative defense distinguishes 3(B)'s vulnerability/circumstances from 3(C)'s victim/family impact, relying on the statutory authorization of victim-impact subjects, contextual meaning, and the instruction against counting factors mechanically. Jones's supported objection focuses on the actual undefined wording and overlap, the special-findings format, possible relative-worth assessment, and the risk of adding weight to the same consideration twice. Permissibility of victim-impact evidence is not a complete answer to whether these particular aggravating formulations were lawful; overlap of evidence does not by itself prove constitutionally forbidden double counting. Tuilaepa's selection rule and Stringer's separate weighing-error rule must both receive their actual force.

**Assumed error, harmlessness and federal remedy.** The Court may resolve a properly presented case-specific harmlessness issue while assuming rather than deciding factor invalidity, or may reach the validity issue as an alternative ground supporting the judgment if procedurally available. A harmlessness route must connect the whole charge, evidence, findings and mitigation to noncontribution; eligibility from two statutory aggravators is insufficient by itself. An opposing route finds the lower analysis an inadequate substitute sentencing judgment or insufficient explanation and remands for a lawful inquiry or new sentencing as §3595 and the constitutional rules require. Reweighing and harmlessness cannot be treated as interchangeable merely because both can sometimes sustain a sentence. The statutory Government-burden clause does not automatically displace Rule 52(b) on a distinct forfeited instruction claim, and the preserved-error language does not itself extinguish all plain-error authority. Any categorical construction requires analysis of the actual text and presentation.

These are analytical routes supported by the record and effective law, not a representation that counsel used every formulation or that any Justice accepts one. A whole-FDPA constitutional ruling, a new universal capital-element doctrine, and automatic life for any reversible error exceed the necessary scope unless independently justified by the actual question and law.
