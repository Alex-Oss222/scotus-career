Garcia supplies the validity of generally applicable wage regulation; it does not by itself answer whether this private retrospective remedy is valid. ## Proposition-level compatibility

The eight Associates share the review channel, continued wage controversy, statutory clarity, absence of state consent, preservation of the separate federal proceeding, and separation of immunity from wage merits. They do not share a constitutional amenability rule.

Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support the following common operative proposition: Congress, acting within its valid commerce authority and expressly authorizing this employee action against a public agency in a competent state court, may displace Maine’s asserted nontextual immunity from this FLSA action; Article VI prevents the state-law immunity defense from defeating that valid federal remedy. They support reversal of the immunity judgment and ordinary remand. Their reasons tolerate the distinction between Stevens’s broader published critique of Hans immunity and the three Justices’ common-law/stare-decisis account. Neither group needs a claim that Seminole already decides this action or that every Article I damages action is valid.

O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support the contrary operative proposition: retained constitutional sovereignty bars Congress’s use of its original commerce power to subject an unconsenting State to this private retrospective FLSA damages action in its own courts. They share affirmance, acknowledgment of clear statutory intent, continued substantive federal duty, and preservation of the specialized Hilton result. Their positions create no majority without the Chief, and this document supplies no Chief vote or Court disposition.

The four immunity proponents’ own Seminole position would expressly overrule Union Gas’s five-vote constitutional conclusion if a general original-Article-I immunity holding were announced. Its actual damages judgment, constitutional conclusion and statutory construction cannot be described as never having been precedent. The narrower state-forum question does not require formally overruling a federal-forum judgment, and they can support a formulation confined to this state-forum extension while stating their continuing disagreement with Union Gas. Such a formulation must acknowledge rather than silently erase its adverse constitutional premise. No unanimous or majority precedent treatment is inferred from this four-Justice preference.

## Alternative-path and application conclusions

**Enforcement path.** Statutory clarity, valid substantive regulation, Article VI and the distinct state-forum enforcement authorities support reversal. The strongest objection is that the Constitution itself may retain immunity beyond the Eleventh Amendment’s federal-forum text; if it does, ordinary statutory supremacy cannot overcome it. The four reversal proponents answer that this nontextual defense has no such absolute constitutional status, using their actual published Seminole grounds; they do not answer merely that Congress meant to authorize suit. Hilton is supportive but not dispositive by itself. New York’s state-activity and state-court reservations, and actual Printz Part I, defeat an automatic anti-command inference while leaving immunity to its own analysis.

**Immunity path.** Hans and structural sovereignty, together with the four Justices’ own Seminole positions, support constitutional protection even outside the amendment’s literal forum. The strongest objections are retained Union Gas, Hilton’s state-court enforcement, Article VI’s express judicial duty, and the absence of an existing categorical Seminole prohibition. The four affirmance proponents must supply the state-forum constitutional reasoning and distinguish the retained holdings; symmetry alone and Maine’s lack of consent alone are insufficient. They distinguish jurisdiction to review a state judgment from power to impose the underlying private action. They do not treat Garcia as overruled or convert wage regulation into New York’s compelled regulation of private actors.

**Correction-only remand.** The lower court’s reliance on a displaced categorical Seminole premise warrants correction of its description of governing law, but supplies no satisfactory stopping ground by itself. The distinct federal constitutional question is squarely presented, the state-law waiver issue is resolved, and the neutral record identifies no fact whose development is needed to choose between the two core immunity rules. None of the eight presently selects a remand merely directing the Maine court to decide the same question under a corrected citation. A genuine new threshold or dated legal change would require affected reconsideration, not an invented factual gap.
