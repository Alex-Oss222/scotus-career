1. Respondent pp13–14 n.2 expressly agrees that this kidnapping conviction could receive only death or life without release: section1201 provides death or life imprisonment and parole/good-time credits cannot shorten life. It nevertheless contends this proposition was unsettled at trial and argues Jones expressly requested inclusion of the lesser-sentence option, might have obtained strategic advantage, and does not seek reversal merely for the isolated insertion of that option. This is a governmental concession of available statutory punishments plus distinct preservation/prejudice arguments, not an abandonment of all defense of the charge.
2. Respondent pp15–20 defends the whole charge. It argues the initial express unanimity requirement applied to every recommendation, later omission of the word did not negate it, the instruction against considering the judge's eventual sentence prevented the claimed inference, and a divided jury naturally would report disagreement rather than recommend a punishment no juror favored. Foreperson-only signature did not uniquely signify nonunanimity because other unanimous findings also used the foreperson's signature. It distinguishes Mills's discussion of blank mitigating-circumstance findings from ultimate sentencing deadlock.
3. Respondent pp20–24 argues Rule30 forfeiture and each pertinent Olano requirement: the asserted ambiguity was not clear/obvious; instructions and possible alternative effects defeat demonstrated prejudice; juror affidavits about internal deliberations cannot prove it under Rule606(b) and the earlier common-law rule. It specifically accepts that denial of the requested instruction is preserved as an independent claim but contends that request does not preserve an objection to instructions actually given. It invokes the opportunity to make a distinct contemporaneous objection. No judicial finding about preservation follows from this party argument.
4. Petitioner pp19–25 distinguishes the misleading nonunanimity/lesser-sentence inference from the isolated insertion of an unavailable punishment. He claims preservation through the rejected requested instruction, later timely new-trial/reconsideration motions, and appellate merits consideration; he also invokes the FDPA appellate-review provision. His principal theory is that explicit unanimity for FormsB/C, its omission from FormD and the requirement to choose C or D upon nonunanimity could make jurors think deadlock permitted a sentence below life. He maintains the record shows at least a reasonable likelihood of that reading even without the juror affidavits. He separately argues for considering those affidavits; that assertion does not establish their admissibility.
5. Respondent pp28–32 does NOT concede that deadlock necessarily requires a judicial nondeath sentence. Its express position is that a new capital-sentencing jury may be convened after deadlock. It invokes the traditional manifest-necessity rule, section3593(b)(2)'s good-cause discharge provision, and section3593(e)'s unanimity requirement for every sentencing recommendation. It reads section3594's otherwise sentence as addressing a unanimous recommendation of a lesser punishment rather than the absence of any recommendation. It therefore says the requested mandatory-life instructions misstated law and that Congress did not enact the clear default-nondeath provisions used by several States.
6. Respondent pp30–31 characterizes petitioner's contrary section3594 argument (citing petitioner pp34–35) as treating otherwise to require the least severe lawful punishment upon deadlock. The precise statutory text has separately been supplied to neutral validation; the actual legal meaning remains a live issue, and neither party's interpretation is an admitted Court ruling. Respondent's acceptance that the available punishments for this offense are death/life does not resolve whether a second capital sentencing hearing is authorized.
