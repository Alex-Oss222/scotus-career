## Exact statutory relationships

Section 141(a) directs a decennial population census as of April 1, in the form and content the Secretary determines, **“including the use of sampling procedures and special surveys.”** It separately authorizes other necessary census information. Section 141(b) requires the state population tabulation under subsection (a), needed for congressional apportionment, to be completed and reported to the President within nine months. Section 141(g) defines the census of population to include population, housing and related matters. Section 141(e)(2)'s express bar on using **mid-decade** census information for congressional apportionment or prescribing congressional districts is a different provision; it must not be mistaken for §195's disputed apportionment exception.

Section 195 states: **“Except for the determination of population for purposes of apportionment of Representatives in Congress among the several States, the Secretary shall, if he considers it feasible, authorize the use of the statistical method known as ‘sampling’ in carrying out the provisions of this title.”** Its feasibility condition belongs to the command that follows it. The live question is whether the initial exception forbids sampling for interstate House apportionment or removes that subject from the otherwise mandatory direction while §141(a) leaves discretionary authority. Neither reading may erase the exception or convert the feasibility-qualified command into an unconditional duty.

The special 1997 statute supplies judicial review, not a substantive authorization to sample:

- Section 209(b) permits an aggrieved person to seek declaratory, injunctive and other appropriate relief against statistical methods violating the Constitution or another law **other than that Act**, for congressional apportionment or redistricting.
- Section 209(c)(1) includes dress rehearsals; (c)(2) deems the report ordered by Pub. L. 105-18 and the Census 2000 Operational Plan final agency action regarding statistical methods. This resolves the enacted finality question without itself furnishing Article III injury.
- Section 209(d) includes residents whose representation or district could change, Members, and either House. Subsection (g) authorizes the Speaker or designees to sue for the House. These provisions do not eliminate Article III.
- Section 209(h) defines statistical methods to include representative sampling and statistical adjustment adding or subtracting counts by statistical inference. Subsection (i) says the Act does not authorize such methods for apportionment or congressional redistricting.
- Section 209(j) requires preparations for a census without statistical methods, aiming as close to complete enumeration as possible, and publication of enumerated numbers and additions or subtractions for its listed releases, including apportionment figures. A statutory duty to publish those data does not establish that an unsampled, complete alternative count will actually have been collected under the challenged plan.

Article I's actual-enumeration direction and congressional power to direct its manner operate with the Fourteenth Amendment's whole-number-of-persons rule. Neither the parties' characterization of an “actual head count” nor the lower courts' statutory construction supplies a constitutional decision.

## Material design and evidentiary disputes

The challenged plan combines improved initial enumeration and two statistical operations. In nonresponse follow-up (NRFU), a random sample of nonresponding housing units is visited to obtain direct information for approximately ninety percent of the tract's housing units; remaining nonresponse units are estimated from the follow-up sample. The denominator is **housing units in a tract**, not ten percent of all persons or necessarily ten percent of nonresponders. If the initial response exceeds ninety percent, the plan's minimum follow-up fraction still applies. This is not a plan to solicit forms from only ninety percent of residents.

Integrated Coverage Measurement (ICM) independently interviews a sample of blocks, compares rosters, estimates omissions and erroneous inclusions by poststrata, and applies adjustment factors before aggregation. It can add or subtract estimated persons. The House opinion describes approximately 25,000 sampled blocks and 750,000 housing units. The intended population total, rather than a separately completed full enumeration, supplies apportionment. **Postal Vacancy Check sampling is expressly outside these lawsuits' challenge.** The case does not automatically determine the legality of every census estimate, proxy report, imputation technique or use of sampling for another statutory purpose.

The Bureau argues that differential undercount persists under traditional methods and that the design improves national, state and congressional-district accuracy at feasible cost. The plan's block-error discussion distinguishes random error that tends to diminish with aggregation from systematic bias; it says accuracy should be judged at the geographic levels carrying legal and financial consequences. It also acknowledges the geographic scale of error and that its model comparisons do not contain every possible modeling error. See plan pages 123–128, especially “Error Rates at the Block Level.” These statements are the Bureau's reasoned technical position; they are not a finding that every block, district or state necessarily improves.

The challengers object that estimation and adjustment can change state shares and district representation; they stress model dependence and particular geographic errors, not just total population accuracy. A constitutional merits inquiry must examine the actual comparative explanation and record at the relevant geographic scale. Improved national totals alone do not resolve distributional accuracy. Conversely, a large percentage error in a small block does not alone establish an unconstitutional statewide or congressional-district result.

### Glavin standing evidence

Weber's affidavit, especially paragraphs 7–25 and 39 (JA 56–67, 77–79), uses Census Bureau population projections and 1990 post-enumeration survey undercount estimates as revised in 1992. He predicts that Indiana would receive ten House seats without the planned adjustment and nine with it; he identifies other states at risk and counties with relative population-share losses. **Gary Hofmeister is alleged to be an Indiana voter residing in Marion County.** His particular representation theory should not disappear behind the lower opinion's list of other at-risk states.

The defendants dispute the predictive premises. Wetrogan's declaration, paragraphs 3–14 (JA 93–99), says the preferred population projections use older estimates, do not exactly forecast 2000, and that eight updated Indiana projections yield totals at least 42,000 below the older projection before any sampling effect. Thompson's declaration, paragraphs 3–15 (JA 101–109), disputes transfer of 1990 undercount factors to either redesigned 2000 method, emphasizes sampling error and changes in methodology, and identifies omitted overseas personnel. He reports Indiana's 1990 undercount point estimate and its confidence interval to contest a claim of certainty. He does not concede the alleged seat loss. The May 8 counsel clarification (JA 90) confirms that Weber's county/state share shifts use 1990 data and revised 1992 rates, rather than a separate county projection for 2000.

These are competing evidentiary submissions. The constitutional standing question concerns sufficiently supported threatened personal injury, not mathematical certainty; the record must nevertheless meet the **summary-judgment** stage. The lower court's repeated pleading formulations cannot replace that inquiry. A standing conclusion may accept or reject the predictive sufficiency without declaring the eventual 2000 allocation a proved fact.

Separate theories require separate links: loss of an interstate House seat; intrastate vote dilution from relative county changes and the use of census data in districting; county loss of funds under actual allocation mechanisms; and injury from a feared invalid census and reversion to stale data. A county's funding injury is not automatically each resident's personal injury. A projected failure to complete a lawful census depends on additional steps and must not be treated as inevitable solely because plaintiffs challenge the current plan. If one plaintiff establishes standing for the requested relief, every other asserted plaintiff or injury need not be decided unnecessarily.

### House standing evidence and competing legal characterizations

The House claims entitlement to lawful census information needed for apportionment under 2 U.S.C. §2a(a), and an institutional interest in its lawful composition. The district court accepted those two theories, distinguished a bare demand for executive compliance, and considered the present House capable of protecting a successor House before the census occurred. It did not need to decide the additional political-manipulation and general constitutional-duty theories.

The government's strongest supported response is that receiving a disputed methodology's totals is different from withholding information, that 435 seats remain regardless of distribution, that the asserted injury resembles an abstract institutional disagreement, and that the House sitting when suit began is not the later House allegedly affected. The lower court invoked subpoena cases, Beens and successor/representative analogies in response. Those analogies do not themselves establish the necessary personal or institutional injury, and statutory authorization alone does not decide it. Separation-of-powers and equitable concerns must be assessed on the concrete claim rather than treated as a universal bar to suits by a House.

No same-day result is anticipated.

- **Concrete personal interests and procedural citizen-suit standing**, present operation effective *Lujan*, June 12, 1992: concrete actual/imminent injury, fair traceability and likely redress, supported at the actual litigation stage. A procedural right protects a distinct concrete interest; it does not create that interest or dispense with injury.
- **Individual legislator standing for institutional dilution**, present operation effective *Raines*, June 26, 1997: fully effective legislative votes and abstract dilution do not confer personal standing. The rule expressly leaves an authorized institution's claim satisfying Article III distinct; it does not decide the House's status here.
- **Concrete electoral-information injury**, present operation effective simulated *Akins*, June 25, 1997: continuing denial of particular statutorily required information for the plaintiffs' own electoral use can be concrete despite its wide distribution; a generalized wish for legal enforcement is insufficient. Its FECA setting and actual informational use are material to analogy, not a census holding.
- **Constitutional review of census adjustment methodology**, present operation effective *Wisconsin v. City of New York*, March 20, 1996: a reasonable relationship to enumeration and population-based representation, respect for explained methodological judgment, and attention to geographic accuracy and uncertainty. That decision expressly reserves the constitutional prohibition and §§141/195 questions. It creates no perfect-count presumption or automatic superiority of a head count.
- **Reviewability of the Secretary's completed census determination**, present operation effective *Franklin*, June 26, 1992: the completed determination is final despite presidential transmission; finality and actual-reasons review remain distinct from constitutional reasonableness. Section 209 supplies this plan's express final-action rule. The President's APA status and coercive relief against him remain open.
- *Steel Co.*, June 2, 1997, and *NAACP v. Jones*, November 5, 1998, prohibit assumed disputed Article III jurisdiction to reach contested merits. *Wisconsin Right to Life v. Paradise*, November 24, 1998, requires actual stage-appropriate injury and relief analysis rather than changing the pleaded claim. These rules permit supported nonmerits disposition; they supply no automatic standing or dismissal outcome here.

## Supported paths, remedy and remaining limits

1. Resolve actual Article III and any material ripeness objection before contested merits. A failure of standing requires the appropriate jurisdictional disposition and no statutory or constitutional holding. Section 209's finality and express cause of action do not cure that failure. A sufficiently supported plaintiff can permit review without deciding every alternative injury.
2. On statutory merits, the prohibition reading relies on §195's specific apportionment exception, the earlier statutory structure and the relationship of the 1976 amendments to §141's broader data-collection authority. The permission reading relies on §141(a)'s express sampling authorization, §141(b)'s use of subsection (a)'s tabulation, and the difference between an exception from a mandate and a prohibition. Legislative history and the prior administrative position are relevant arguments, not unqualified factual concessions. The statutes' text and structure must be evaluated before treating administrative discretion as dispositive.
3. If the Act forbids these apportionment methods, relief can rest on that ground without deciding the Enumeration Clause. Any affirmed or revised injunction must retain its stated **apportionment** scope; the proceeding supplies no basis to prohibit all uses of census statistics.
4. If the Act permits the methods, the constitutional challenge remains distinct. The Court may decide a properly presented legal claim on an adequate record or return an undecided constitutional application for lower-court consideration. Permission is not a command to sample, a constitutional approval of every implementation, or an entitlement to the government's requested unrestricted relief. Any constitutional approval must state whether it addresses categorical permissibility or this actual explained design, and preserve review of unauthorized, unsupported or discriminatory implementation.
5. Keep a judgment for each appeal. A resolution providing complete effective relief in Glavin may affect the continuing need for the separate House action; that requires an actual live-relief and proper vacatur analysis, not assumed institutional standing or an automatic merger of the dockets. A House jurisdictional dismissal cannot itself dispose of Glavin plaintiffs' independent claims.
