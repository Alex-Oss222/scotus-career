## Question and posture

The statutory question is whether the jury in a prosecution under 21 U.S.C. section 848 must agree unanimously on the specific violations composing the continuing series, or may disagree about those violations while agreeing that a continuing series exists. This is a faithful issue statement, not a certification of verbatim petition or grant language. The Seventh Circuit sustained the instruction permitting disagreement about which three violations were committed. Richardson seeks reversal and remand for a new trial on Count Two, the CCE count. The Government defends the instruction and alternatively requests initial appellate consideration of harmlessness if the instruction is erroneous.

The express instruction and objection establish a preserved predicate-unanimity issue: the proposed instruction was submitted by Tate, and Richardson objected to its refusal (J.A. 21, 25). The operative charge required unanimity that at least three federal narcotics offenses had occurred, with at least one after March 24, 1989, but expressly disclaimed agreement on the particular three or more offenses (J.A. 34-37). The statute itself does not state the trial instruction's three-offense number or March 24, 1989 date. Those instruction conditions must not be presented as quoted statutory language.

## Positive record facts and their limits

## Independent remedial questions

If the statutory instruction is upheld, this unanimity objection supplies no basis to disturb the CCE judgment. If it is rejected, classification, applicability of harmlessness, any permissible method of application, and remedy remain distinct questions for independent modeling. Richardson argues that express permission to disagree deprived the verdict of the unanimity necessary for harmless review, invoking Sullivan and Carella; alternatively he argues no harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. The Government seeks a remand for initial appellate consideration. Neither position determines the result here.

Roy rejects automatic reversal merely because an element was omitted and permits supported whole-record actual-verdict inferences within its adopted limits; its personal-intent holding does not itself decide the treatment of this express nonunanimity instruction. Sullivan addresses a reasonable-doubt charge vitiating the whole verdict; an analogy to this case needs its own legal basis. Gaudin protects actual elements but reserves universal structural-error treatment. Griffin excludes independent unanimity and elemental-instruction claims from its alternative-factual-ground rule. Johnson's forfeiture framework does not replace this preserved objection. The possible inference from a conspiracy verdict must be examined at its actual scope, without treating the agreement as three substantive transactions or equating ample evidence with the jury's actual unanimous findings.

An appellate remand may preserve consideration of both whether harmless review is available for this defect and, if available, whether the Government can meet the governing standard. Such a remand need not settle either question, and it is not predetermined by this packet. A final harmlessness conclusion, a structural classification, or a new-trial mandate requires its own supported ground and sufficient record. No automatic retrial, acquittal, release, reopening of Count One, sentence recalculation or duplication remedy is supplied. Rutledge's independent dual-conviction law remains current but is not an implied grant of relief beyond the issue and supported posture here.

## Earlier neutral legal framing - mechanically preserved

The following legal discussion is retained as an issue map. Its reference to the requested instruction is clarified above: Tate submitted it and Richardson objected. Its discussion of a supported remand identifies an available path; the independent remedial questions above preserve the open applicability as well as application of harmlessness.

The requested predicate-unanimity instruction and express contrary charge establish preservation of this unanimity objection. The question is the statutory identity of the violations comprising the continuing series, not unanimity about every evidentiary detail, the identity of all supervised persons, an organizational chart, or an additional current-offense sentencing rule.

Two substantive paths require genuine examination. One reads the separately unlawful violations as constituent elements requiring agreement on the same predicates; statutory words, ordinary unanimity, the breadth of potentially disparate drug offenses, and avoidance of serious due-process doubt support that path. The competing path treats the series as one course-of-business element and individual transactions as evidentiary means; it rests on Congress's enterprise-directed design and the settled permission for alternative means. Each path must explain why the particular statutory alternatives are elements or means rather than merely assert the label.

Schad v. Arizona, 501 U.S. 624 (1991), is pre-divergence authority. Souter's plurality, joined by O'Connor and Kennedy among current Justices, considered legislative definition, history, accepted practice, and rational equivalence, while declining one exclusive universal test. Scalia's separate writing protected a traditional crime and traditional mode of submission, but expressly distinguished novel umbrella crimes and different assaults against different victims on different dates. Stevens joined White's dissent emphasizing separately specified conduct and mental-state requirements and the absence of agreement on them. This is Justice-specific evidence, not a predicted result. Schad does not automatically classify this modern federal statute or require unanimity on evidentiary means.

Gaudin, unanimous, allocates actual elements to the jury without classifying every omission as structural. Rutledge's eight-Associate Part I identifies agreement and the distinct CCE series, five-person, managerial and resource requirements; it reserves substantive predicates and does not decide this unanimity question. Griffin preserves independent unanimity and elemental-instruction objections outside its factually unsupported alternative-ground holding. Jones's six-Justice statutory graded-offense holding and the narrower/broader published writings bear on element classification, but adopt no universal maximum-fact rule and decide no CCE predicates. Mitchell's source-of-proof and actual-contribution holdings do not convert every punishment fact into an element.

Remedy is separately modeled. A conclusion that the charge was erroneous does not by itself establish that an automatic new trial is the only permissible mandate. Roy unanimously rejects automatic reversal merely because an element is omitted; its five-Justice controlling actual-verdict inquiry permits supported whole-record inferences, forbids substitution of sufficient evidence for a hypothetical conviction, and requires attention to what the charge led the jury to consider. Sullivan's vitiation of the entire reasonable-doubt verdict remains structural at its actual scope. Johnson concerns forfeiture and cannot replace the preserved-error inquiry here. An appellate remand to consider properly available harmlessness is a supported narrower path if the present record cannot support final application. A structural classification, if proposed, must identify a defect distinct from Roy's bare omission rationale. No unrelated count, prior acquittal or sentence can be reopened by implication.

## Petitioner argument on harmlessness and requested relief

4. The Trial Court's Failure to Properly Instruct the 
Jury was not Harmless Error. 


The trial court's error in improperly instructing the 
jury in this case allowed the jury to return a non-unani- 
mous verdict as to an element of the CCE offense. 
Because of this, the harmless error analysis of Chapman v. 
California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), does not apply. 


In Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275 (1993), this Court 
unanimously held that a constitutionally deficient reason- 
able doubt instruction is not subject to harmless error 
analysis. The Court reasoned that a verdict of guilty 
beyond a reasonable doubt is a necessary predicate of the 
harmless error inquiry. The failure of the trial court to 
give a proper instruction thus rendered the jury verdict 
meaningless, providing no object on which the harmless 
error analysis could be applied. Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 
U.S. at 279-80. 


In this case the jury was erroneously instructed that 
it need not find unanimously as to which three predicate 
narcotics offenses had been committed. Therefore there is 
no actual verdict of guilty upon which the harmless error 
analysis can operate. Furthermore, since there were no 
predicate acts charged substantively, there were no facts 
necessarily found by the jury so closely related to the fact 
tainted by the erroneous instruction as to result in a 
functionally equivalent finding.*? Accordingly the error 
in this case cannot be said to be harmless beyond a 
reasonable doubt and the judgment should be reversed.
