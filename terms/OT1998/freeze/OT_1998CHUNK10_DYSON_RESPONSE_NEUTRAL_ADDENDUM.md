## Legal and institutional significance

Under the dated Rule 15.1 already validated, an opposition is optional in a noncapital case unless the Court orders one. Known nonfiling therefore does not itself establish breach of a filing duty. It also does not create a categorical legal prohibition on summary merits treatment: Rule 16.1 permits an appropriate summary merits disposition after consideration of the documents distributed under Rule 15.

Those permissions do not decide whether the available adversarial presentation is institutionally sufficient. The absence of respondent's Supreme Court opposition, considered alongside counsel's stated status and wish, is now a supported circumstance a Justice may weigh when deciding whether to seek respondent presentation before potentially changing a judgment favorable to a criminal defendant. A Justice may regard the existing record and lower opinion as adequate to resolve a clearly presented legal issue, or regard focused opposition as valuable for identifying preserved jurisdictional, record, scope or remedial concerns. Neither assessment follows automatically from the optional-filing rule, the nonfiling fact, or counsel's status. The addendum invents no argument that an opposition would contain or result it would produce.

Rule 15.2's possible waiver of particular nonjurisdictional objections remains qualified and issue-specific. Nonfiling does not itself identify a particular objection, establish its predicates, concede the merits, eliminate the Court's jurisdictional inquiry or authorize default judgment. No waiver finding is made here.

## Supported procedural alternatives and limits

- **Consider summary merits on the available papers.** A Justice may conclude that the supported record, lower reasoning and controlling law adequately present the actual federal issue, subject to jurisdiction and applicable distribution procedure. That position must rest on actual sufficiency; it cannot treat nonfiling as admission, consent, factual agreement or a duty to rule against respondent.
- **Require opposition before further disposition.** Rule 15.1 permits an order making a response mandatory. A Justice may favor that course because the verified absence of respondent presentation could matter to informed treatment of this criminal judgment. This is an institutional procedural choice, not enforcement of a previously established duty. No such order, response, counsel substitution, assistance arrangement or new deadline has already occurred. Any selected representation arrangement would require its own lawful basis and actual implementation; none is posited here.
- **Defer immediate decision or use ordinary plenary review.** Further consideration may be supported if a Justice considers the present presentation inadequate, while a grant for ordinary proceedings follows the distinct Rule 16.2 route. The new facts do not themselves establish a grant, argument, record request or compulsory entitlement to any of those steps.
- **Deny discretionary review.** This remains a distinct petition-stage choice. Its availability does not affirm the lower federal rule or establish respondent's success on every potential issue. No denial or petition tally is supplied.

The existing neutral packet's separate analysis of finality, actual federal grounds, Labron/Houghton and limited remedy remains unchanged. A procedural preference can be distinct from a view on the federal automobile rule. No non-Stone commitment or coalition is proposed.
