## Identity, participation and questions

The principal merits question is whether a federal court may use its general equitable authority to restrain a debtor's disposition of unrelated assets pending adjudication of an unsecured creditor's action for money damages, where the creditor has no existing judgment or proprietary equitable interest in those assets. The restraint concerns foreign Toll Road Receivables/Notes; personal jurisdiction and the existence of equitable power must not be collapsed into territorial ownership of the property.

The prior question is whether this interlocutory controversy remains live after final judgment and conversion to a permanent injunction, considering the asserted injury during the preliminary period, the $50,000 Rule 65(c) bond, the separate turnover appeal, and the reported abandonment of the challenge to permanent-injunction conversion. The scope of review is the preliminary-injunction appeal; it is not automatically an appeal from every aspect of the later money judgment, turnover order or permanent restraint.

The seated Court consists of Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. ## Objective record and disputes

GMD and its subsidiary guarantors issued unsecured obligations ranking pari passu with other unsecured, unsubordinated debt. Eleven United States investment funds held approximately $75 million of the $250 million issue of 8.25% notes due in 2001. GMD experienced serious financial difficulty, reported liabilities exceeding assets, and defaulted on an August 1997 interest payment; the holders accelerated principal. Mexico's Toll Road Rescue Program promised GMD government-backed notes connected with unpaid construction receivables and expenses. GMD reported expected proceeds of approximately $309 million and assigned rights associated with these assets to Mexican government, employee, bank and other creditors.

The amount and characterization of the assignments were disputed. The lower opinion recites progressively larger disclosed assignments and a projected $5.5 million remainder after Mexican creditors were paid. Its different passages use different assignment totals; no exact total beyond the particular recitation is essential to the categorical power question. The district court found threatened frustration of any eventual judgment given GMD's financial condition and dissipation. The appellate panel characterized the disclosures and preferences adversely and sustained that finding; those judicial characterizations are not additional independent findings that every transfer was fraudulent or that a separate statutory fraudulent-transfer cause had been pleaded and proved.

The investors sued December 12, 1997 for breach-of-contract damages and interim restraint. After hearings, the district court entered the preliminary injunction on December 23 with a $50,000 bond. It found that investors would almost certainly prevail and that the asset disposition and financial condition threatened noncollection. The investors asserted neither a preexisting judgment lien nor an ownership or trust claim in the restrained assets. No independent statutory fraudulent-transfer claim or avoidance remedy is established by this packet.

The panel reported that both sides acknowledged Rule 64/New York law would not supply this injunction: the action sought only money damages and the relevant assets were outside New York. That is the posture and concession reported below, not a national holding that all state attachment law lacks force or that foreign property can never be reached through a valid personal decree.

GMD's predecision merits brief says it did not contest the district court's findings of irreparable harm or likely success in the reviewed appeal. The panel separately recounts its arguments about intent to frustrate judgment and whether the findings sufficiently established irreparable harm. Preserve both: the legal-power question remains contested, and the lower court's treatment of factual/application objections does not negate the brief's stated limitation of the merits presentation.

## Judgment below and subsequent lower proceedings

The Second Circuit affirmed. It treated Rules 64 and 65 as complementary, read De Beers narrowly, and relied on Deckert, First National City Bank, insolvency and threatened noncollection to support general equity power to preserve an adequate final remedy. It acknowledged contrary circuit authority. It also rejected a mandatory additional intent-to-frustrate element, regarding such intent as one way of showing threatened noncollection rather than the only way. It stated an abuse-of-discretion review standard encompassing legal error and clear factual error. These are the lower court's positions, not an existing Supreme Court adoption of its test. No separate panel dissent appears in the isolated opinion body.

On April 17, 1998, the district court entered a $82,444,259 judgment, dismissed GMD's counterclaims with prejudice, directed irrevocable transfer or assignment of enough Toll Road Receivables/Notes to satisfy the judgment, continued the restraint pending transfer, and converted it to a permanent injunction until that obligation was met. GMD did not oppose the money judgment or counterclaim dismissal in the proceedings reflected in the order. The initial turnover obligation was stayed for ten days.

The April 20 notice appealed turnover and permanent-injunction conversion. A May 1 appellate order stayed turnover pending appeal but expressly left the rest of the judgment, including the permanent injunction, effective. Respondents' predecision brief reproduces GMD's July 1, 1998 appellate statement that it had decided not to appeal conversion to a permanent injunction. The separate turnover appeal was argued December 10, 1998 and is described in GMD's merits brief as pending; no later disposition has been supplied.

The investors' April 17 suggestion of mootness identified preliminary-injunction appeal No. 97-9610. A May 4 summary order denied a mootness motion but bore No. 98-7549, the later final-judgment appeal. Respondents dispute whether that order applies to the preliminary appeal or the later appeal and dispute its legal significance either way. The docket-number mismatch is preserved. It does not permit treating mootness as finally settled or silently correcting the order's caption.

## Strongest supported paths and necessary concessions

1. **Threshold dismissal for mootness.** Respondents argue that conversion extinguished the preliminary order, that Camenisch requires the bond dispute to await its proper final-merits posture, and that abandonment of the permanent-injunction challenge eliminates the asserted right to be free of restraint. GMD must answer the distinct appellate-issue point, not merely identify an ongoing contract case. The strongest counterargument is that a valid final restraint after judgment need not establish authority over an unsecured debtor before judgment; a distinct claimed injury during those four months may depend on the challenged preliminary order. The Court must examine whether meaningful relief remains in this reviewed controversy. A threshold disposition adopts no substantive equity-power rule and must specify the treatment of the interlocutory judgment without disposing of the separate turnover appeal by implication.
2. **Live controversy; no power over these unencumbered assets before judgment.** GMD asserts that equity's historically limited jurisdiction does not allow a general creditor, lacking judgment or property interest, to obtain security through an injunction in an action solely for damages. Rule 65 supplies procedure; Rule 64 preserves the legislatively selected attachment routes; merger of law and equity does not automatically enlarge substantive remedies or reorder unsecured-creditor priorities. GMD must distinguish the broad preservation language in Deckert and First National City Bank and account for actual threatened noncollection. Strong likelihood and irreparable injury do not answer whether the proposed remedy lies within judicial power.
3. **Live controversy; bounded preservation power.** The investors argue that courts with personal jurisdiction may prevent imminent insolvency/dissipation from making an otherwise available judgment ineffectual, that general equity can adapt to this commercial setting, and that the demonstrated likelihood of success, actual threat and bond constrain discretion. They read De Beers as barring restraint unrelated to any possible final relief and Deckert/First National City Bank as supporting preservation for eventual monetary recovery. This path must address the absence of a proprietary interest, the historical creditor's-bill limitation, Rule 64's state-law architecture, the distinction between power and discretionary prerequisites, and effects on creditors who are not parties. It may not rely solely on the desirability of collection or treat the pari-passu promise as an adjudicated lien.
4. **Narrower disposition or further proceedings.** If authority exists only for a materially narrower traditional, statutory or property-based route, the packet does not establish its predicates. Any remand to examine such a route must identify its legal source, preservation and factual need; the Court cannot invent a fraudulent-transfer pleading, findings of bad faith, ownership claim or new security interest. If the original injunction exceeded authority, a remand may preserve lawful adjudication of an actual bond claim without awarding damages now. A decision upholding power does not affirm every possible use or automatically decide the separate permanent restraint and turnover order.

The lower court's alternative irreparable-harm reasoning matters only if the merits presentation and review scope permit reaching it. No mandatory intent requirement may be adopted merely because one cited example involved intentional frustration; equally, absence of a new intent element does not prove that every legitimate preference or restructuring effort justifies restraint.
