## Suster v. Marshall — January 28, 1999

### Verified posture, corrections, and review scope

The lower judgment is *Suster v. Marshall*, 149 F.3d 523 (6th Cir. July 30, 1998), Nos. 96-4048, 96-4115, 96-4287, 96-4380 and 97-3174. The archived original petition is captioned *Marshall v. Suster*, No. 98-737. Use the repository's assigned case name while preserving this source alias. The petition invokes 28 U.S.C. §1254(1); the appeal below involved interlocutory preliminary relief under §1292(a)(1) and an asserted collateral immunity appeal.

The Sixth Circuit affirmed preliminary protection from the former expenditure ceiling, affirmed denial of preliminary relief from the distinct nonjudicial-account rule, and remanded the amended expenditure canon and the state-law count. It did not enter a final statewide invalidation of the amended code.

The injunction's scope must be corrected in the working packet. September modification confined the original order to Suster and Cleary; later orders admitted intervenors. The October 22, 1996 order, by consent of all parties, extended expenditure protection to plaintiffs and plaintiff-intervenors **and their respective opponents**. Maloney later received intervention and preliminary relief. Do not reduce the eventual protection to the initial plaintiffs or enlarge it to every Ohio judicial candidate.

The former common-pleas ceiling was $75,000. The $18,750 primary sublimit had been removed before the earlier February 20, 1996 consent decree; that separate decree is not the present judgment. During appeal the expenditure canon changed to population-based amounts. The published opinion explains that it retained review of preliminary relief under the old canon because continuing disciplinary exposure and recurrence mattered, while leaving full review of the amended canon to the District Court. ### Material facts and governing provisions

Suster was appointed in October 1995 after serving in the Ohio House, retained funds raised for his legislative campaign, and wished to use them in a judicial campaign. By the new litigation he had spent $72,710.01. Cleary's term ended in December 2000; she asserted an intention to spend more than $75,000 when running again and a voter's interest in receiving candidate speech. The appellate account states that litigants intended to seek reelection and had received no assurance against enforcement for spending under the old canon. Those facts supply the stated lower-court continuing-interest analysis; mere completion of the 1996 election does not settle mootness.

The old canon's amounts and the transfer prohibition are copied from Appendix A-3 in the source extracts. The transfer restriction governs funds contributed to promote candidacy for a nonjudicial office; it is not a general bar on the candidate spending personal funds or every intercampaign transfer. The lower court described other unchallenged rules concerning personal solicitation, contribution amounts and promises about rulings. Their independent validity is not before this expenditure review.

The petition reproduces amended common-pleas/municipal/county amounts of $125,000 for jurisdictions above 750,000 population; $100,000 above 250,000 through 750,000; $75,000 above 100,000 through 250,000; and $50,000 for 100,000 or less. Its quoted subsection expressly begins with an exception cross-reference to (C)(6)(b), which the petition excerpt does not reproduce. These excerpts establish the revision and the State's argument, not a complete statutory basis for a final ruling on every application of the revised canon. No missing exception is filled by inference. The old preliminary-order review and a remand preserving amended-law consideration do not require adjudicating that unreproduced exception.

The poll, committee reports and lower opinion distinguish public perceptions from demonstrated actual partiality. The Special Committee twice declined to endorse spending caps, including concern that caps favor known incumbents. ## Wright v. Ernst & Young LLP — February 4, 1999

### Verified record and claim boundaries

The Second Circuit's August 6, 1998 judgment, 152 F.3d 169, No. 97-9241, affirmed dismissal of the amended complaint under Rule 12(b)(6). Wright declined the District Court's leave to replead, and final judgment followed September 30, 1997. That is a pleading posture, not a finding that the auditor committed fraud. The actual petition question concerns primary liability for a secondary actor that privately communicates information knowing it will be published without attribution.

The full published opinion verifies the pleaded affirmative conduct: discovery of a BT-Summit accounts-payable under-accrual, a judgment that it was immaterial and probably a prior-year carryover, and oral approval on January 22, 1996 of the figures released January 30. The release was marked unaudited and did not identify Ernst & Young. The March 28 correction changed $1.5 million reported profit to a $200,000 loss; the pleaded price decline exceeded 25%. Later discovery of improper accounting and embezzlement does not itself establish what the auditor knew in January.

The opinion reproduces allegations of recklessness and GAAS failures, the claimed market understanding, and the plaintiff's argument that the auditor's advice would be passed to investors. Paragraphs 12 and 30 expressly address substantial participation. The theory is therefore not invented here. But the opinion's rejection of assistance under historical *Central Bank* is not governing simulated law, and a description of the role as tangential is a legal evaluation in that framework, not an independent finding binding this pleading review.

The 1995 prospectus branch is distinct. The appellate account states that the amended complaint did not plead the later duty-to-correct claim pressed in opposition papers; it also identifies missing allegations concerning 1995 scienter, reliance by the 1996 class, and causation. It concludes that judgment had to be vacated before another amendment could be filed. Those record/preservation propositions must not be erased merely because the private-assistance rule differs. No new complaint, new claim or granted amendment is created by this preflight.

### Entering law and competing grounds

The complete *Central Bank* holding and Standards entry **Culpable affirmative assistance to a securities fraud** are copied in the focused slice, with exact effective authority and limits. The action permits knowing or severely reckless **affirmative substantial assistance** to an underlying securities fraud; extreme departure from ordinary care must present a specific danger of deception known or so obvious that the assistant must appreciate it. The defendant must appreciate the fraudulent activity and assisting role. No independent disclosure duty is needed for affirmative conduct. Professional status, negligence, ordinary services alone, valuation concern alone and mere duty-free silence do not suffice. Reliance on the underlying fraud, causation and recoverable loss remain required; investors need not rely on concealed assistance itself.

*First Jersey*, September 28, 1998, unanimously recognizes responsibility for proved hands-on primary deception, while distinguishing office, ownership and assistance alone. It preserves *Central Bank* and does not transform every accountant into the maker of the issuer's statement. The current-term *Kravitz* and *McClellan v. Cablevision* public copies are retained in the full slice for their bounded received-remedy setting; they do not silently abolish the specific securities action.

Investors may contend that privately supplying/approving the false figures with the required awareness is affirmative participation in a fraud that foreseeably reached the market, even though lack of attribution may defeat their claimed auditor-as-speaker characterization. The auditor may contest both primary attribution and whether the specific pleaded facts establish the demanding mental state, substantial assisting effect, underlying fraud, reliance and loss links. The word unaudited bears on what was represented but is not a blanket license knowingly to assist deceptive figures. A correction and subsequent price decline are not automatic proof of every element.

Supported paths include a confined rejection of primary-misstatement attribution with separate consideration of the preserved assistance route; vacatur/remand to apply *Central Bank* to the actual allegations; or a properly supported independent pleading disposition that does not reintroduce the forbidden categorical no-assistance rule. No liability or damages judgment follows merely from identifying the legal error. Any conclusion that pleading cannot satisfy the simulated elements must address the pleaded approval, identified under-accrual, asserted recklessness and actual theory; it cannot rest on absence of public identification alone. The prospectus branch and any permitted amendment require separate scope treatment.

## Boring v. Buncombe County Board of Education — February 17, 1999

### Verified pleading record and reasons below

The controlling lower judgment is the Fourth Circuit en banc opinion, 136 F.3d 364, No. 95-2593, February 13, 1998, as amended March 18, 1998. The former panel opinion was vacated on rehearing. The simulated grant confines review to the federal First Amendment claim after final Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal. The other constitutional and state-law claims are not revived. Section 1254(1) supplies the review channel.

Boring's amended complaint placed selection and direction of *Independence* within her advanced acting class and school competition work. It also alleged that she followed the usual notice practice, that the principal initially did not object, that she proceeded after he approved performance with edits, and that the controversial-materials policy did not then cover plays and was changed later. The appellate majority **assumed**, rather than found, that she complied with the requested edits. Her compliance and retaliatory-motive allegations must receive the treatment proper to the pleadings; the proceeding contains no trial finding that she defied a curricular order, lied about approval, or was disciplined for a proven legitimate reason.

The complaint alleged a transfer in retaliation for expressing an unpopular viewpoint through the play. The superintendent cited the controversial-materials policy; the principal referred to conflicts from her actions. There was also an auditorium-floor episode involving plywood attached with screws after discussion of stage construction. That episode is described in the complaint account, but its occurrence does not automatically prove an independent same-decision cause. Neither production of a play nor curricular selection establishes that the teacher personally endorses every character's idea.

The en banc majority used two grounds: it characterized the selection/production controversy as an employment/curriculum dispute outside the teacher's protected speech, and alternatively treated curriculum content as a legitimate pedagogical concern. Luttig's concurrence distinguished a teacher's claim to control curriculum from student expression within it, while recognizing that noncurricular classroom speech presents a different question. Wilkinson emphasized local democratic responsibility and the costs of judicial curriculum supervision.

Motz's dissent, joined by Hall, Murnaghan, Ervin, Hamilton and Michael, accepted final school authority but would require a reasonably related legitimate pedagogical basis under *Hazelwood*. It stressed the allegations of regular approval/compliance and objected to deeming every curriculum-related motive legitimate by definition. Alternatively, it regarded the subject matter as public concern under *Connick*, requiring justification rather than pleading-stage dismissal. Hamilton, joined by Murnaghan, additionally stressed that an ordinary evidentiary showing might ultimately vindicate officials but had not been supplied at this procedural stage. Those are competing lower-court legal positions; no appellate faction's characterization becomes a finding of motive.

### Entering law and supported paths

The focused slice copies *Settle*, *Jeffries*, *Waters* and *Brewster*, plus the complete relevant Standards entries and their operative fields. *Settle* unanimously permits genuine academic requirements while rejecting a categorical religious exclusion disguised as pedagogy; it involves a student's research assignment and creates neither a universal curriculum viewpoint-neutrality requirement nor an absolute teacher right to select material. *Jeffries* concerns an unaffiliated off-campus address, expressly not assigned official work; it does not classify every classroom assignment. *Brewster* protects the assumed attendance-error report affecting funding in its particular employee/immunity setting; it supplies no general personal right over curricular content. *Waters*' limited majority propositions do not give every employer a complete investigation code or allow a merely conceivable lawful ground to substitute for the ground actually used.

Pre-divergence *Hazelwood* distinguishes tolerance of personal student expression from control of school-sponsored expressive activity; its legitimate-pedagogical-concern rule governs student speech within that setting. *Pickering*, *Connick* and *Givhan* require the relevant role, content, form and context rather than an automatic rule that anything said at work lacks protection. *Mt. Healthy* separates protected conduct, motivating cause and the employer's preponderance same-decision defense. *Epperson* and *Keyishian* preserve independently applicable constitutional limits on education. None requires deciding a broader future rule for every government employee's assigned speech.

Officials' strongest position is that choosing the school's own instructional material is a delegated institutional function; the First Amendment does not give each instructor a personal entitlement to define it, and content selection is inherent in teaching. Boring's strongest position is narrower than unlimited curricular control: after alleged compliance and approval, officials should not be able to punish her for unpopular expression with no actual pedagogical basis. The actual pleading and permissible protection theory must be resolved before selecting the standard of justification.

Supported paths include a confined determination that this personal curricular-choice claim fails while preserving independently forbidden discrimination and separate citizen speech; recognition of a limited curricular expressive interest governed by genuine pedagogical relation, followed by remand on the allegations; or a supported application of the public-employee framework that addresses its role and public-concern predicates rather than assuming them. A conclusion that there is no personal right here does not establish that all curricular actions are constitutionally immune. A remand does not establish liability, mandate a particular play, supply a final grade, award damages or deny properly presented immunity. If damages immunity is reached, the relevant conduct dates are 1991–1992, not the date of this decision or later simulated cases.
