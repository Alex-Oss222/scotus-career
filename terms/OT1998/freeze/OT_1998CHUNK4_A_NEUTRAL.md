## Citizens for a Constitutional Convention v. Yoshina

**Event:** No. 98-671; supplied merits decision January 21, 1999. The principal federal judgment below is *Bennett v. Yoshina*, 140 F.3d 1218 (9th Cir. 1998), March 27, amended June 23. The [amended lower opinion](../sources/chunk4_a/yoshina_lower_amended.txt) is script-copied from petition Appendix A, pp. 1a–22a. The later state referendum must be included in the January 1999 posture.

**Verified chronology and source status.** The 1996 question received 163,869 yes votes, 160,153 no votes, 45,245 blanks and 90 overvotes. The Hawaii Supreme Court construed Article XVII, §2 to require a majority of ballots cast on the convention question, including blank ballots in the denominator; it distinguished the text governing ratification votes. The state decision is *Hawaii State AFL-CIO v. Yoshina*, 84 Haw. 374, 935 P.2d 89 (1997). The federal plaintiffs were not parties to that state proceeding; participation of two groups as amici did not make them parties. The Ninth Circuit rejected the asserted Rooker-Feldman and Eleventh Amendment obstacles to the federal forward-looking challenge before resolving the merits. These are lower holdings, not assumptions that all possible certification relief is necessarily available.

Before the 1996 vote, the informational sheet posted at polling places described yes and no consequences but did not explain blank ballots. A separate fact sheet available at election offices and on the internet said blanks would not count; the lower opinion characterized its dissemination as limited. Citizens alleged that officials also gave that assurance and that its campaign strategy relied on it. Individual plaintiffs alleged they would have voted differently. The lower court rejected the required degree of likely reliance and significant disenfranchisement; those are judicial evaluations, not proof that no one relied. It did not rest simply on the phrase foreseeable state interpretation. All ballots were counted, and blank ballots were reported as blanks rather than publicly attributed to voters as affirmative no statements. Preserve the difference between legal effect in the denominator and compelled attribution of a political message.

The district court ordered a new referendum; plaintiffs cross-appealed seeking certification that the 1996 proposal passed. The Ninth Circuit reversed, vacated the new-election injunction and related stay, and dismissed the dependent cross-appeal and intervention appeals as moot in light of its merits resolution. It awarded appellate costs to the State. The cross-appeal's dismissal in that procedural setting is not an independent merits rejection of every certification theory.

**November 1998 event.** [Act 131](../sources/chunk4_a/yoshina_act131.txt), approved June 22, 1998, submitted the convention question at the 1998 general election. Its complete three-section text is copied from the state exhibit in the petition appendix; it does not expressly recertify the 1996 result. The [official Hawaii election report](https://files.hawaii.gov/elections/files/results/1998/general/histatewide.pdf), [saved text](../sources/chunk4_a/hawaii_1998_official_results.txt), reports all 334 precincts: **yes 140,688; no 244,753; blanks 26,784; overvotes 295**. The report is dated November 3 and printed November 4, 1998. The proposal failed with or without blanks in the denominator.

**Live-remedy dispute.** The December 3 [supplement](../sources/chunk4_a/yoshina_2._Supplemental_Brief_djvu.txt) and December 24 [reply](../sources/chunk4_a/yoshina_4._Reply_Brief_djvu.txt) expressly continue the request to certify the 1996 vote. Petitioners argue that another election did not restore the asserted entitlement resulting from the earlier vote and that requiring two victories depreciates the original vote. The [State's opposition](../sources/chunk4_a/yoshina_3._Opposition_Brief_djvu.txt) disputes availability and continued preservation of that relief and stresses the authoritative state interpretation. Petitioners reject abandonment. The supplement also reports pending July 7 motions concerning recall, vacatur and fees/costs as of its filing; this source does not prove their status after December 3. Fees and costs must be kept separate from a continuing merits injury. An interest in fees alone does not supply the missing merits controversy.

**Supported analytical paths, neither adopted here.** First determine whether meaningful certification-related relief remains legally available and within the supplied review scope after the 1998 vote. If so, evaluate the federal retroactivity and voting-burden questions on the actual dissemination, reliance, counting and remedial record. The State can argue the state court authoritatively clarified the existing denominator, all votes were counted, official information did not induce widespread disenfranchisement, and the federal court cannot replace state constitutional meaning. Petitioners can argue that the authoritative count applied after voting materially changed the announced practical rule and defeated reliance, while recognizing the distinction between state meaning and federal limits on retroactive application. The First Amendment theory must address the lower court's distinction between ballot effect and forced attribution, and Burdick's rejection of a ballot as a general expressive forum. A court finding no available live relief must separately address vacatur and collateral proceedings; it cannot infer mootness solely from the new vote or revive the district injunction automatically. A court reaching the merits must distinguish affirmance for lack of constitutional injury from rejection of a particular remedy.

## AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Board

**Event:** Supplied January 25, 1999 merits decision across Nos. 97-826, 97-829, 97-830, 97-831, 97-1075, 97-1087, 97-1099 and 97-1141. Two lower judgments require attention: *Iowa Utilities Board v. FCC*, 120 F.3d 753, and *California v. FCC*, 124 F.3d 934. The petition seeks review of both under the applicable multiple-judgment route. The [main lower opinion and rehearing](../sources/chunk4_a/att_lower_and_rehearing.txt), [California opinion](../sources/chunk4_a/att_california_lower.txt), [statutes](../sources/chunk4_a/att_statutes.txt) and [FCC rules](../sources/chunk4_a/att_fcc_rules.txt) are copied from the contemporaneous appendix. Cross-checks: [120 F.3d 753](https://openjurist.org/120/f3d/753) and [124 F.3d 934](https://openjurist.org/124/f3d/934).

**Authority and pricing.** Distinguish power to prescribe a national framework from state commissions' work setting and approving actual rates. The Eighth Circuit treated §152(b) as a jurisdictional fence, read §§201(b), 251(d)(1) and 252 against that background, and vacated the pricing rules for want of power. It expressly did **not** decide their substantive reasonableness. A decision that the FCC has rulemaking authority cannot silently approve TELRIC, settle a confiscation claim, determine a carrier's rate, or erase unadjudicated substantive objections. The original vacatur preserved identified CMRS applications under the distinct §332 authority; the lower opinion's note 21 lists the relevant rules and exception.

The authority dispute also affected the rural-exemption rule, commission treatment of preexisting agreements, the FCC's asserted §208 complaint power and inconsistent state rules. A general federal-rulemaking holding requires a disposition accurately confined to challenged components and the preserved statutory allocation; it does not itself establish every enforcement route asserted by the agency.

**Network-element definition and selection are separate.** Section 153(29) defines a network element to include a facility or equipment used to provide a telecommunications service and features, functions and capabilities provided by means of it, with the listed examples. The lower court sustained coverage of operational-support systems, operator/directory services and vertical-switching functions against a physical-component-only argument. Eligibility as an element is distinct from whether access must be required under §251(d)(2). Do not omit this definitional component merely because the supplied neutral summary emphasizes unbundling.

**Necessary and impair.** Section 251(d)(2)(A) separately addresses proprietary elements and subsection (B) impairment of the requesting carrier's ability to provide its chosen service. The FCC used significant impairment or thwarting for proprietary elements and reduced quality or increased cost for general impairment; it declined to require consideration of alternatives outside the incumbent's network. It retained a proprietary-element alternative involving the incumbent's nonproprietary elements. The lower court upheld these constructions while separately rejecting the general technical-feasibility presumption under Rule 51.317. The strongest agency path treats the standards in their competitive-entry context and invokes its expert judgment; the strongest challenge asks whether the construction leaves an independent limiting function for the enacted words and reasonably attends to available alternatives. Neither path may replace the actual rule with a nonexistent absolute indispensability requirement or an invented cost threshold. Preserve the distinction between interpreting the criteria and the resulting list of required elements under Rule 51.319.

**Existing combinations.** The October 14 rehearing order, amended October 23, 1997, replaced the original combination discussion and extended vacatur from Rule 51.315(c)–(f) to **(b)–(f)**. Rule (b) bars separation of already combined elements except at the requesting carrier's request. It is distinct from affirmative duties to create combinations in (c)–(f). The petition's question expressly challenges invalidation of existing-combination protection. A decision concerning (b) must not silently restore all the other subsections. The competing statutory readings concern whether unbundled means separately available/valued elements or a mandate physically to dismantle existing configurations, read with §251(c)(3)'s combination sentence and the separate resale route in (c)(4).

**Section 252(i).** Rule 51.809 permits a requesting carrier to obtain an individual interconnection, service or network-element arrangement on the same rates, terms and conditions. It does not authorize favorable rates with their pertinent terms discarded. The complete rule includes exceptions where the incumbent proves greater provision costs or technical infeasibility, and a reasonable period of availability after public filing. The lower court rejected it because of expected effects on negotiated tradeoffs. The agency emphasizes the enacted word any, nondiscrimination and its contrary assessment of incentives. Distinguish statutory construction, reasonableness of the agency's assessment, and the practical effect of the actual exceptions.

**California judgment.** The second judgment vacated intraLATA toll-dialing-parity rules on the same lack-of-authority premise. The numbering-cost contention was dismissed as unripe because the allocation remained to be determined. Reversal of the authority premise does not automatically reverse that distinct ripeness disposition or settle a future allocation.

**Source sufficiency and scope.** The case has extensive contemporaneous petitions, cross-petitions, briefs and a very large joint appendix. Defined propositions above rest on the actual rules, statute, petition questions and published lower reasoning, rather than a claim to have reviewed every administrative submission. Several OCR downloads returned HTTP 500; that is a retrieval problem, not evidence a litigant omitted a filing. No whole-record absence claim is made. ## Hughes Aircraft Co. v. Jacobson

**Event:** No. 97-1287, supplied merits decision January 25, 1999. Correct the lower citation to **105 F.3d 1288**, January 23, 1997, with October 23, 1997 amendment and January 23, 1998 clarification. [Lower opinion and corrections](../sources/chunk4_a/hughes_lower_and_corrections.txt); [cross-check](https://openjurist.org/105/f3d/1288). Apply the corrections rather than relying on deleted common-law discussion or the deleted mixed-law-and-fact sentence. The [original complaint](../sources/chunk4_a/hughes_original_complaint.txt), JA pp. 19–32, and [statutory appendix](../sources/chunk4_a/hughes_statutes.txt) are separately copied.

**Posture and facts.** The district court dismissed the original six claims under Rule 12(b)(6), without discovery or leave to amend. The Court reviews legal sufficiency, not trial findings. The plan is a defined-benefit arrangement historically funded by employer and employee contributions; allegations describe an approximately billion-dollar surplus and cessation of employer contributions while employee contributions continued. Precise start-year and contribution-year descriptions vary between narrative and table; no conclusion here depends on choosing one. The 1989 amendment provided an early-retirement incentive to specified active employees. The 1991 amendment added a noncontributory benefit structure, allowed existing active participants a choice, and closed new enrollment in the contributory structure. Whether this creates two legally separate plans, terminates the earlier plan, or merely changes benefit structures within one plan is disputed legal characterization. It is not automatically admitted by calling it a pleaded fact. Conversely, the sponsor label cannot dispose of alleged acts implementing asset distributions without the statutory functional inquiry.

The original complaint seeks equitable distribution of surplus, an injunction against using old-plan assets for the alleged new plan, a neutral trustee, restoration of assets, and fees. Its sixth count invokes both §404(a)(1)(D) and §502(a)(1)(B), as well as the fair-and-equitable administration clause. Do not omit this distinct plan-enforcement theory. The JA also contains a December 1997 amended complaint with later parties and transaction allegations; its presence does not automatically enlarge the judgment under review.

**Components that require separate grounds.**

| Component | Supported competing considerations and limits |
|---|---|
| §403(c)(1), anti-inurement | The statute restricts assets' use independently of whether every act is fiduciary. Plaintiffs claim diversion of employee-attributable surplus to the employer; Hughes argues benefits paid to genuine participants remain plan benefits and employer savings are incidental. A contribution holiday is not itself a distribution of cash to the employer. State whether a legal plan-identity or benefit-payment conclusion resolves the claim; sponsor capacity alone does not. |
| §404 duties | Apply §3(21)(A) function by function: adoption/amendment differs from management or disposition of assets. Spink's governing rule supplies no added discretion requirement for the asset-control branch. Employee contributions are relevant to the statutory protections actually triggered, not an automatic rule that contributors become co-settlors or that amendment becomes fiduciary. |
| §§203 and 204(c), accrued employee benefit | Nonforfeitability of accrued benefits derived from employee contributions is distinct from a present property claim to every dollar of investment surplus. Section 204(c)'s statutory interest and annuity computation must be addressed on its own terms. A defined-benefit label is not authority to disregard that floor; nor does the floor necessarily transfer all actual investment gains to each participant. |
| §§4041 and 4044, termination and distribution | Distinguish an actual statutory termination from the pleaded conclusion that closing contributory enrollment creates a wasting trust. Section 4044's distribution sequence and residual employee-contribution protections operate on their statutory trigger. The sponsor argues continuation of benefit obligations and the statutory termination procedure; plaintiffs argue the alleged closed structure's purposes and liabilities justify distribution. Do not transform the case into a freestanding partial-termination holding without a presented statutory basis. |
| §406 prohibited transaction | Genuine benefit payments are not prohibited merely because the employer benefits incidentally; Spink preserves sham/kickback and independently unlawful-plan questions. The lower majority and Norris dissent disagree whether these allegations describe such a transaction or merely attach a label to lawful plan amendment. State the challenged function, transaction and actual alleged recipient; do not treat every payment as exempt or every employer saving as prohibited. The lower discussion also addresses §406(b)(2); preserve its distinct text if reached. |
| 1989 early-retirement and plan terms | The actual clause requires fair and equitable administration, interpretation and application. Distinguish creating selective eligibility as plan design from violating the enacted plan terms in administration, and address the separately pleaded benefits-enforcement route. No generic rule that retirees must receive every later incentive follows. A dismissal requires a ground responsive to this claim, not merely a statement that the 1991 amendment was lawful. |

**Controlling-law correction.** Spink in this jurisdiction does not categorically dispose of every implementation, anti-inurement or other independent claim. Its sponsor-capacity holding is unanimous; its benefit-payment holding concerns genuine payments under an otherwise lawful plan and preserves independent statutory duties. Breyer and Souter reserved the technical implementation-based prohibited-transaction classification. Curtiss-Wright distinguishes adequate amendment procedure from actual authorized adoption. Varity and John Hancock require asset-and-function precision and separate status from breach and remedy. These operative limits must be applied before considering whether the lower court's contributory/noncontributory distinction survives.

Conversely, no original allegation is declared absent from an unread document.
