### Lower majority, dissent and independent grounds

The Maine majority treated federal-forum immunity and immunity in the State’s own courts as manifestations of an underlying constitutional sovereignty. It reasoned that changing forums could not avoid Congress’s supposed lack of Article I power, drawing substantially on the historical Seminole decision (¶¶4–11). It separately rejected implied state consent: Maine’s overtime statute excluded public employees, and general wage/employment statutes could not supply an implied waiver against that explicit exclusion (¶¶12–13). This is an authoritative state-law no-waiver construction; whether the resulting immunity can defeat the federal claim remains a federal question.

Justice Dana, joined by Justice Rudman, dissented. The Eleventh Amendment addresses federal judicial power, Hilton sustains the distinct state-court enforcement route, Garcia validates the substantive wage requirement, and the express FLSA remedy plus the Supremacy Clause overrides inconsistent state immunity in a competent court. The dissent emphasized the text and statutory purpose rather than symmetry between federal and state forums (¶¶14–23). Its historical Seminole/Union Gas account is also not governing simulated law. Neither side’s lower reasoning is a new Supreme Court holding.

### Governing texts and remedies

Official [29 U.S.C. §216(b), 1994 edition](../sources/chunk11-neutral/alden/29usc216_1994.html), independently checked against the [1998 edition](../sources/chunk11-neutral/alden/29usc216_1998.html), supplies identical operative subsection text apart from line wrapping. The complete subsection is reproduced in the slice. It makes an employer violating §§206 or 207 liable for unpaid minimum wages or overtime, plus an equal amount as liquidated damages, and authorizes an action “against any employer (including a public agency) in any Federal or State court of competent jurisdiction” by affected employees, including similarly situated employees who file written consent. It requires reasonable fees and costs with a plaintiff’s judgment. A Secretary of Labor complaint seeking the specified §217 relief terminates the corresponding private action/joinder right under the subsection’s stated conditions. No such complaint or failed consent is found here.

The separate retaliation sentence permits appropriate legal/equitable relief including employment, reinstatement and promotion for §215(a)(3) violations; it must not be imported as relief for the overtime claim without that distinct predicate. The packet establishes no retaliation claim. Any award still depends on wage coverage, violation, limitations, applicable liquidated-damages conditions and ordinary adjudication; admitting the suit awards nothing.

Official [§203, 1994 edition](../sources/chunk11-neutral/alden/29usc203_1994.html), quoted in the slice, includes public agencies in “employer,” defines “public agency” to include state government and its agencies, and supplies specific public-employee exclusions. Mere probation-officer employment does not resolve every wage exemption or amount. No disputed exemption is newly decided here. Article VI binds state judges to valid supreme federal law; the Eleventh Amendment’s text addresses “The Judicial power of the United States.” Whether an additional structural immunity limits this particular enacted remedy is the central dispute, not an assumption supplied by those words alone.

### Strongest supported alternatives and remedial limits

**Employee enforcement path:** Treat the express public-agency/state-court remedy as valid enforcement of Congress’s valid substantive wage regulation; apply Article VI, Howlett/Testa and Hilton’s state-forum distinction; reverse or vacate the immunity dismissal and remand for ordinary FLSA proceedings. The strongest objection is that sovereign immunity may be a constitutional structural limitation independent of Article III wording and substantive regulatory validity, while Hilton rested on unusually settled statutory construction and reliance. A valid remedy requires its own constitutional basis; clear intent alone is not enough.

**State immunity path:** Recognize a constitutional immunity from this private retrospective damages action in the State’s own courts, distinct from the continuing federal duty and other lawfully authorized enforcement, and affirm on that ground. The strongest objection is that no controlling simulated Seminole rule bars the action, Union Gas survives, Article VI specifically directs state judges, and the remedy text unmistakably names public agencies and state forums. The path must support the constitutional boundary and reconcile Hilton rather than merely repeat the displaced historical Seminole premise. State nonconsent alone cannot decide the federal validity question by definition.

**Narrow correction/remand path:** If the lower judgment depends on the displaced categorical Seminole premise and the reviewing Court leaves the distinct constitutional issue unresolved, identify why a remand is lawful and what remains for decision. Do not order a futile remand solely to avoid deciding the properly presented question, assume state consent contrary to the state-law ruling, or reopen the federal case. If a different threshold or surviving alternative ground becomes material, obtain the specific needed support rather than treating missing research as default.

No path adjudicates overtime owed, awards liquidated damages, authorizes every federal action against States, reopens Mills, or establishes that the United States or an officer has already brought an alternative action.
