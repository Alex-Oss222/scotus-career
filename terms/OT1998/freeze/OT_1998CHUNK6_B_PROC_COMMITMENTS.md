## 1. Murphy Brothers, Inc. v. Michetti Pipe Stringing, Inc., No. 97-1909

### Reach, record and competing constructions

All eight Associates reach the statutory question. The district court denied remand; the Eleventh Circuit reviewed that denial through section 1292(b), reversed and directed remand. This is not an appeal from a district-court remand order barred by section 1447(d). The fax supplied a courtesy copy of the filed complaint before formal service. Removal followed more than thirty days after receipt of the fax but within thirty days after formal service. No earlier summons, waiver or other process event is assumed.

The event-date first paragraph of section 1446(b), reproduced in the fresh preflight, contains two branches: thirty days after receipt, through service or otherwise, of the initial pleading setting forth the claim for relief; or thirty days after service of summons if the initial pleading has then been filed and is not required to be served, whichever period is shorter. Neither the summons branch nor its filed-pleading and no-service-required conditions may be omitted from a general construction. The separate later-removability paragraph is not implicated.

**Service-linked construction, adopted by six Associates.** Informal receipt before formal process does not itself start the original-pleading removal period. Under the first branch, the defendant must have been formally brought under the court's authority and have received the initial pleading; where the complaint arrived first, the later formal-service event starts the thirty days. Where formal process precedes the required receipt, the first branch awaits receipt. The second branch independently starts thirty days upon summons service when the initial pleading is then filed and need not be served, and the statutory shorter-period direction remains effective. This construction does not require actual receipt in every summons-branch case, alter thirty days to another period, or forbid a defendant from removing before service. No unpresented waiver's precise deadline is decided.

The strongest objection is textual: Congress selected receipt, included otherwise, and expressly used service in the alternative branch. The six accept that actual notice, personal jurisdiction and a statutory removal opportunity are distinct. Their construction instead reads the paragraph within the existing process system: otherwise permits receipt of the pleading outside formal delivery once the defendant is subject to process, while the summons branch handles the identified filed-but-not-required-to-be-served pleading. Read together, the branches identify pleading-information events within that formal-process framework rather than an advance-copy deadline. This is statutory construction, not a clear-statement rule or a constitutional holding that Congress could never establish an earlier clock.

**Receipt construction, adopted by two Associates.** Actual receipt of this filed initial pleading triggers the first branch whether or not process has been served; formal service is one means of receipt, not an additional condition. The separate summons branch remains available on its stated conditions, with the earlier expiration controlling. On the supplied intervals, the removal was untimely. The strongest objection is that the clock can expire before an obligation to appear arises. The two conclude that Congress may distinguish a deadline for electing a federal forum from the duty to answer in state court, and the enacted receipt language makes that distinction here. The statute does not authorize default judgment without proper process merely because its removal deadline has elapsed.

## 2. Conn v. Gabbert, No. 97-1802

### Reach, posture and supported paths

All eight Associates reach the granted occupational-liberty and qualified-immunity issue on the supplied summary-judgment record. The district court granted Conn and Najera qualified immunity; the Ninth Circuit reversed in part on Gabbert's Fourteenth Amendment occupational claim. The assumed favorable account includes purposeful interference with consultation during the search. No Justice resolves disputed motive against Gabbert, infers waiver from his failure to request postponement, finds that a search at that time was necessary, or treats the warrant's existence as adjudication of its validity.

The asserted personal injury is the interruption of Gabbert's representation while the special master searched him. No ban, disqualification or broader exclusion from legal practice is supplied. Baker twice sought consultation, asserted her own privilege and the grand jury recessed. Those facts do not establish a new claim on Baker's behalf or decide her rights. Gabbert's separately pleaded Fourth Amendment claim lies outside the granted question and receives no merits disposition here.

**Occupational-merits ground.** The occupational liberty recognized by Roth and Schware does not guarantee freedom from every temporary interruption of professional work during a criminal investigation. This single search-related interruption, even on the favorable account of its intended interference, is not an exclusion from the practice of law or a deprivation of the asserted freedom to pursue the profession. The narrower distinction between an occupational deprivation and an interruption governs; the rule is not that every short deprivation is harmless, that only permanent disbarment can implicate liberty, or that intentional official abuse outside custody is generally lawful.

The strongest contrary argument invokes deliberate interference with the actual exercise of an established occupation: the importance of representation cannot be measured solely by duration, and Graham cannot displace an independently protected injury merely because a search also occurred. The merits supporters accept both qualifications. Nevertheless, the pleaded occupational theory would convert interference with a particular professional act into a general substantive constitutional guarantee against investigative interruption. Roth distinguishes a protected occupational interest from every employment loss; Schware addresses unreasonable exclusion from qualification to practice. Neither supplies that broader guarantee. Collins requires identification of the constitutional injury but does not itself decide this intentional-interference case. Lanier and West Covina preserve genuinely distinct constitutional protections; they do not establish the occupational injury asserted here.

**Qualified-immunity-only ground.** Even assuming a distinct occupational constitutional wrong on the favorable account, the law existing on March 21, 1994 did not make the asserted application sufficiently apparent to defeat these officials' qualified immunity. Elder requires all relevant pre-existing law, not only a factual twin or sources cited below. Roth's general vocational-liberty language and Schware's protection against exclusion do not clearly establish this distinct interruption claim. Deliberate purpose or an appeal to common sense cannot substitute for an identified right whose application was apparent. The current Lanier formulation describes the clarity inquiry but cannot itself provide retrospective notice. This ground resolves damages on the reviewed claim while reserving its constitutional merits; it establishes no absolute prosecutorial immunity.

The strongest objection to immunity is that a sufficiently general rule can obviously cover purposeful interference even without a prior identical search. That principle is accepted. The difficulty is the nature and scope of the personal occupational right, not absence of a duplicate fact pattern. Albright had produced no controlling general answer to overlapping substantive claims before this conduct; its differing published approaches likewise cannot be combined into a clearly established new occupational guarantee.
