[Yoshina](../entering-law/citizens_for_a_constitutional_convention_v_yoshina_merits_1999-01-21.md), January 21, preserves federal relief for fundamentally unfair administration without federalizing every election-law dispute. [Reform Party](../entering-law/reform_party_v_democratic_national_committee_merits_1999-03-17.md), March 17, rejects a parity entitlement while preserving genuinely suppressive burdens and discriminatory administration. **Paths and limits.** The reimbursement condition and compressed campaign require separate examination. Affirmance may follow if the supported facts show public administration, no voter fee or exclusion, unchanged access to ordinary petition routes, and no supported impairment of opponents' opportunity to speak or organize. Relative private resources alone do not establish an actionable classification or suppressive burden. Against that path, public control of counting does not answer every claim that government selectively provided a beneficiary preferential access or an intentionally prohibitive campaign period. A supported severe practical burden requires its own scrutiny; no universal minimum campaign duration or categorical private-financing permission follows. Summary judgment cannot resolve a genuinely supported disputed material fact against the opponent, but an allegation alone does not create one. Any reversal must identify the actual federal injury and a lawful remedy rather than deciding independent Washington constitutional claims or inventing a new statewide election.

The lower judgment, as authenticated in the supplied packet, found no voter exclusion based on inability to pay and no support for the asserted purpose to drown out opponents. Those findings and the summary-judgment posture remain distinct from statutory commands. The fact that ordinary initiatives remain possible is relevant, not automatically dispositive. No later election or disappearance of a concrete claim is presumed; Gable requires an actual continuing-stake issue before a jurisdictional detour.

**Justice-specific material.** O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas joined Burdick's contextual rule; Kennedy and Stevens accepted calibrated scrutiny but dissented on its actual write-in application.
