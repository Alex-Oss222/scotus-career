## Questions and lawful channel

1. Whether the FTC Act's definition of corporation reaches this nonprofit professional association on the supported facts concerning its business activities and members' financial benefits.
2. If the FTC has statutory authority, whether the price-advertising restrictions and the separate quality-advertising restrictions, as actually enforced, can be condemned under an abbreviated rule-of-reason inquiry on this record, or require further competitive analysis.

Coverage and liability remain distinct. The original neutral packet's statement that coverage turns on materially advancing members' commercial operations is the proposed construction supported by the FTC and the court below, not an already controlling Supreme Court resolution supplied by the entering-law materials.

The Ninth Circuit reviewed the Commission's cease-and-desist order under 15 U.S.C. §45(c), affirmed the Commission, and enforced the order. ## Material record and source status

The following facts and descriptions come from the Ninth Circuit's reported account, 128 F.3d 720, 722–725, 728–730. Administrative findings, testimony, and appellate characterizations retain those statuses.

CDA is a nonprofit corporation under California law and federal tax law, comprising 32 local component societies. Roughly 75% of practicing licensed California dentists belong. Membership is not required for a state dental license. A dentist must belong to a component to join CDA and must belong to CDA to join the ADA. CDA provides lobbying, marketing, public relations, management seminars, compliance assistance, continuing education, placement, and patient-complaint procedures. Its for-profit subsidiaries provide access to insurance, equipment financing, telephone discounts, automobile leasing, and mortgages. ADA membership offers additional benefits.

CDA's Code of Ethics prohibits advertising false or misleading in a material respect. Its advisory opinions address partial disclosure, unjustified expectations about results or costs, fee disclosures, relative-price claims, and quality claims. Advisory opinions are not binding by their own force but may be considered persuasive in discipline. CDA and components enforce the rules through requests to alter advertisements, disciplinary proceedings, and membership decisions. CDA can censure, suspend, or expel members. Conditional admissions and counseling arrangements were also used. Components were not respondents to the Commission's complaint; CDA's own standard setting and involvement in individual cases were part of the evidence supporting responsibility.

The challenged practices concern enforcement beyond the facial prohibition on misleading advertising. The ALJ found that CDA barred descriptions of fees as low, reasonable, or affordable; effectively prohibited across-the-board discounts by requiring disclosure of the undiscounted price of all covered services; and restricted quality claims without determining whether individual advertisements were false or misleading. The ALJ also described the treatment of money-back guarantees. The court's note states that CDA had changed its former policy against advertisements intended to allay patients' fears. Do not treat a discontinued policy as a presently enforced ban without further support.

The legal dispute is not resolved by saying that every challenged advertisement was demonstrably deceptive, or that every professional advertisement was demonstrably useful. Neither proposition follows from the reported record. The majority relied on concrete examples of CDA objections to truthful discount and price descriptions and its lack of individualized inquiry into quality claims. CDA disputed the reach and attribution of those practices and asserted the prevention of misleading advertising. Conflicting testimony about the permissible extent of discounts was acknowledged.

The statutory and Code provisions used in the analysis must retain their own conditions. The Code's material-falsity rule, nonbinding advisory guidance, CDA enforcement practices, and the appellate description of state-law requirements are distinct. A CDA guideline's assertion about what California law requires is not itself an independently verified state statutory command. No state-action immunity or legal compulsion follows from that assertion alone.

## Decisions and reasoning below

**ALJ:** Applied the Commission's Massachusetts Board approach, treated the restraints as inherently suspect without a plausible efficiency justification, and found a violation. The ALJ found that CDA lacked market power but considered such proof unnecessary under that approach.

**Commission:** The principal opinion treated price restrictions as unlawful per se, with abbreviated rule-of-reason analysis as an alternative, and treated nonprice restrictions as unlawful under abbreviated analysis. It disagreed with the ALJ on market power. Commissioner Starek concurred in the result under the Massachusetts Board approach; Commissioner Azcuenaga dissented on the sufficiency of the evidence concerning anticompetitive acts and market power. These positions are reported by the appellate opinion; the complete administrative opinions were not independently read in this preflight.

**Ninth Circuit majority, Hall joined by Choy:** Held that substantial commercial activities furnishing tangible pecuniary benefits to members brought CDA within §44, notwithstanding nonprofit form. It distinguished genuinely charitable activity and relied on the business character of the challenged advertising regulation. It rejected per se treatment of the price rules because the facially antideception rules and professional context warranted inquiry into effects. It nevertheless sustained the alternative quick-look analysis for price and quality restrictions separately.

For price advertising, the majority reasoned that suppressing truthful prices and discounts makes consumer comparison and price competition harder. It accepted that disclosures may be procompetitive but found the required quantity of disclosure practically prohibitive of across-the-board discount advertising and found no evidence that this enforcement increased price transparency. For quality advertising, it regarded the suppression of information about services as a restraint on competition, potentially also reducing demand for nonemergency care, and rejected the claim that possible unverifiability justified barring all quality claims without examining truthfulness. Those are the majority's competitive inferences; they are not findings of measured output reduction.

The majority found substantial evidence of agreement through the association's rules, advertising restraint, CDA responsibility, and enforcement reaching truthful advertising. It regarded dentistry as the product market and local areas as the geographic markets. Statewide membership of approximately 75% did not itself establish each local market share; the majority inferred substantial shares in many localities and combined that inference with licensing and education barriers, membership benefits, and evidence of reluctance to lose membership. It conceded the absence of the fuller market-power analysis ordinarily associated with a complete rule-of-reason proceeding. It considered this evidence sufficient in light of the character of the restrictions and its use of abbreviated review.

**Real dissent:** Would find CDA outside FTC jurisdiction because the group-purchasing services do not make the nonprofit association itself a commercial enterprise; benefits arise through members buying from competing outside suppliers. Alternatively, he would reject quick-look condemnation: the rules facially require truthful, adequately disclosed advertising rather than fix prices, and their competitive effects are not apparent enough to dispense with a full inquiry. He regarded the omitted economic and market-power analysis as consequential. His premise conflicts with the majority's characterization of CDA's activities and enforcement; neither characterization should be silently converted into a conceded fact.

## Governing text and entering law

The source files accompanying this supplement contain the full official 1994 text of FTC Act §§4 and 5. Section 4, 15 U.S.C. §44, includes incorporated and unincorporated associations organized to carry on business for their own profit or that of their members, including the stated category without shares and excluding partnerships from that category. Section 5(a), 15 U.S.C. §45(a), prohibits unfair methods of competition and unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce and empowers the Commission over the covered persons, partnerships, and corporations, with the enumerated sector exceptions. No listed sector exception is asserted for CDA in the supplied account. Section 5(c) supplies review and the evidentiary status of Commission findings. The court below uses substantial-evidence review for findings and economic conclusions and independent review of legal questions, with respect for the FTC's informed judgment.

The supplied entering-law slice controls the precise current formulations and coalitions:

- **State Oil Co. v. Khan, November 4, 1997:** Vertical maximum resale-price agreements receive rule-of-reason review rather than Albrecht's per se prohibition. Its discussion of variable competitive effects informs classification; its actual holding does not already decide professional advertising, abolish abbreviated analysis, or replace the distinct rules for horizontal restraints and genuine price fixing.
- **NYNEX Corp. v. Discon, Inc., December 14, 1998:** A vertical supplier substitution does not become a per se group boycott merely because it injures a rival, serves fraud, or lacks an apparent efficiency. The restraint's character and actual competition matter. That result does not transform horizontal association rules into vertical purchasing decisions, immunize such rules, or itself prescribe the depth of the present rule-of-reason inquiry. Its distinctions between fraud, consumer harm, competitor injury, and competitive injury remain relevant at their stated scope.
- Surviving predivergence authorities discussed below include **National Society of Professional Engineers v. United States, 435 U.S. 679 (1978)**, concerning competition-based assessment of professional restrictions and the limits of a defense that competition itself is undesirable; **NCAA v. Board of Regents, 468 U.S. 85 (1984)**, concerning flexible inquiry where competitive effects can be understood without elaborate industry analysis; **FTC v. Indiana Federation of Dentists, 476 U.S. 447 (1986)**, concerning professional information restrictions, evidence of competitive effects, and the scope of market inquiry; **Arizona v. Maricopa County Medical Society, 457 U.S. 332 (1982)**, concerning experience supporting a per se category; and **Bates v. State Bar of Arizona, 433 U.S. 350 (1977)**, concerning the informational contribution of truthful professional price advertising. These references identify relevant earlier authorities without importing every application made by the Ninth Circuit.

The Ninth Circuit also cites postdivergence historical **Morales v. Trans World Airlines, 504 U.S. 374 (1992)** on onerous advertising disclosure. That citation establishes what the lower court relied on; it is not independently admitted controlling law in this timeline. A simulated Morales authority must be supplied before its postdivergence holding is used as binding law. The lower court's own postdivergence circuit authorities likewise retain their proper lower-court status. No earlier equal division is a controlling Supreme Court holding on FTC coverage.

## Supported opposing paths and remedial distinctions

**Coverage:** The FTC can argue that the statutory reference to members' profit deliberately reaches organized collective business assistance, and CDA's substantial commercial services and advertising enforcement satisfy that text without dividend distribution. CDA can argue that indirect benefits from nonprofit professional or group-purchasing services do not themselves show that the association is organized to carry on business for profit; an unlimited benefits test could sweep charitable and educational organizations into the Act. An adopted coverage rule must specify the commercial connection it requires and apply it to these activities. Nonprofit form alone and economic benefit alone are competing asserted shortcuts, not assumed answers.

**Abbreviated analysis:** The FTC can defend the majority's separate price and quality inferences: eliminating truthful comparisons and discounts restricts a basic means of rivalry, and the reported enforcement exceeds prevention of deception. The asserted benefit must be tested against how the restraint works. The existing record and sufficiently clear competitive tendency may permit analysis without a comprehensive definition and measurement of every local market.

CDA can argue that professional services present information and verification problems; a disclosure or substantiation rule can improve reliable comparison even if it excludes some advertisements. The existence of a professional-information problem does not prove this rule reasonable, but may make the direction and magnitude of its competitive effects less apparent than the majority assumed. The ALJ/Commission disagreement, statewide/local mismatch, and absence of full economic analysis matter if abbreviated treatment is inappropriate. Calling restricted advertising an output limitation does not independently prove a reduction in dental services or welfare. Price, quality, and specific enforcement measures may require different levels of inquiry.

The inquiry may be calibrated to the supported restraint and available evidence. Requiring further analysis does not necessarily require every element of the most elaborate possible market study; sustaining abbreviated analysis does not make every professional advertising restriction unlawful per se. Genuine enforcement against materially false or misleading advertising remains analytically distinct from blanket suppression of useful truthful information. The record supports testing those alternatives without fabricating evidence, concessions, new claims, or litigation conduct.

If coverage fails, the FTC order cannot be sustained under the asserted statutory authority and no merits holding on competitive effects is necessary. If coverage exists and the record supports lawful abbreviated analysis of both components, enforcement can be sustained. If the abbreviated inquiry was insufficient for one or both components, vacatur or other appropriately bounded correction and remand would require fuller consideration of the affected restraint; that is not a judgment that CDA's practices are reasonable, an automatic reinstatement of every ALJ premise, or permission for deceptive advertising. Exact remand instructions must match the defect found and the division of agency and appellate functions. No damages or newly imposed sanction is requested or supplied here.
