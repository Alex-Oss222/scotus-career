## McClellan v. Cablevision of Connecticut, Inc.

**Identity and scope.** Second Circuit No. 97-7156; no separate Supreme Court docket supplied. The provided grant concerns an implied private damages action under 47 U.S.C. §531(e). The lower decision concerns a private federal action more generally. Do not collapse the grant's damages question into every possible form of enforcement.

**Verified lower record.** The Second Circuit, 149 F.3d 161, July 17, 1998, reversed the January 6, 1997 Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal. Producer Jerry McClellan and viewers Jonathan Cooper and Notu Bayonne sued after Cablevision indefinitely barred McClellan's future programs and studio access following a program it characterized as sexually explicit. The claims sought damages, temporary and permanent injunctive relief under the Cable Act, and relief under Connecticut's unfair-trade-practices law. These are pleading allegations, not findings of improper exclusion or compensable injury. Cablevision also asserted that McClellan evaded prebroadcast review and refused postbroadcast review. The reviewing court reserved the viewers' separate cognizable-claim issue because neither side had briefed it. No §1983 claim against Cablevision was pleaded.

The district court held that no implied action existed and declined supplemental jurisdiction over CUTPA. The appellate court applied Cort with congressional intent central: it identified programmers as intended beneficiaries; distinguished Touche Ross's reporting provision from a prohibition protecting speakers; treated the 1992 and 1996 amendments against a small body of district-court decisions as evidence of preservation; distinguished statutory enforcement from §531(c)'s franchise enforcement; and read the national diversity-of-information purpose as supporting a federal remedy. Its opinion acknowledges that only three district courts had recognized the action when Congress amended the Act, and one later reversed itself. The lower court did not decide that the disputed program fell outside a lawful exclusion category. Its footnote discusses why a blanket future ban differs from refusing particular prohibited material. That lower discussion is not a Supreme Court merits holding here.

**Operative law.** Section 531(e) is subject to §544(d), prohibits operator editorial control over covered public, educational and governmental uses, and separately permits refusal of a public-access program or portion containing obscenity, indecency or nudity. Permission does not require refusal, and public-access wording does not automatically cover every educational or governmental use. Section 531(c) permits enforcement of franchise requirements by the franchising authority. Section 532(d) expressly provides an action to compel leased capacity; its particular source and relief must not be described as an express general public-access damages action. Section 555a limits relief against governmental franchising defendants; those defendants are not this private operator merely by analogy.

Cort v. Ash, 422 U.S. 66, Touche Ross v. Redington, 442 U.S. 560, Transamerica Mortgage Advisors v. Lewis, 444 U.S. 11, Cannon v. University of Chicago, 441 U.S. 677, and Merrill Lynch v. Curran, 456 U.S. 353, provide the surviving inquiry into enacted rights, intended actions and particular remedies. Suter applies congressional-intent analysis and keeps direct implied actions separate from §1983. Franklin's appropriate-relief rule presupposes a cognizable action. Central Bank and Musick Peeler concern development of an already received securities action; neither establishes that a new statutory action follows from beneficial purpose. Denver Area supplies the actual component judgments and reservations, not a universal public-forum or operator-state-action holding and not a new §531(e) action.

**Paths requiring independent assessment.** One path finds sufficient statutory and legislative context for an action protecting programmers and then determines whether Franklin supports appropriate compensatory relief within it. Its strongest objection is the separate express leased-access remedy, the limited rather than settled judicial practice, and the difference between a statutory prohibition and an intended private remedy. A second path rejects implication of damages because the enacted structure and amendment history do not establish that congressional choice. Its strongest objection is the concrete protected class, absence of a direct statutory enforcement mechanism for that class, and the lower court's distinction between a statutory prohibition and franchise conditions. A narrower path may reject a damages entitlement without deciding every request for prospective relief; it must identify what remains for remand and may not silently treat all equitable relief as authorized or unauthorized. No path awards damages, resolves obscenity, creates an unpleaded §1983 claim, or conclusively disposes of the viewers' reserved claims.

**Justice-specific anchors.** Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg joined the received-action reasoning in Central Bank; Kennedy wrote the contrary statutory-remedy position joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas. Those positions must be tested against the different statutory source, not transferred as a vote. O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter joined Franklin's general remedial ground; Scalia and Thomas accepted the narrower express-enactment ground. Breyer's Gebser concurrence, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, develops attribution within an established action and expressly relies on Cannon and Franklin, making the existence/remedy distinction relevant to him too. Suter supplies the contrary implied-action boundary for O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. ## City of West Covina v. Perkins

**Identity and scope.** No. 97-1230; Ninth Circuit No. 94-56365, 113 F.3d 1004, May 9, 1997. The provided question concerns individualized notice of state procedures for recovering property seized under a warrant, not a new adjudication of the warrant or the officers' qualified immunity.

**Verified lower record.** Police investigated a former boarder and searched the Perkins home while its occupants were away. They took, among other items, cash and a starter pistol. Their receipt named the agency, warrant date, issuing judge and court, search date, officer contacts and attached inventory. It omitted the warrant number. Perkins was told he needed a court order. He reported that the issuing judge was away, staff could locate nothing under his own name, an officer supplied the wrong number, and the warrant was sealed. The officer disputed aspects of that account. A public clerk's index linked the searched address to the warrant number; Perkins was apparently unaware of that method. These distinctions matter: an allegation of a practical obstacle is not proof that a legally necessary identifier was unavailable.

The City obtained return of the property in June 1994, except for the starter pistol reported stolen. Return followed the federal suit and did not itself extinguish a supported claim for the earlier deprivation. The appellate court affirmed individual qualified immunity and held California's available return procedures adequate, but reversed the City's summary judgment on notice and directed judgment for Perkins on that issue. Its requirements included information about the procedure, needed identifying information, and the need for a written request stating why return was due. Judge Leavy dissented from that notice holding, reasoning that lawful seizure followed by adequate state return procedures did not require legal advice from the custodian.

The control supplement additionally identifies a District Court finding that respondents failed to establish that a warrant number was necessary for the return motion. This is accepted as an authenticated objective lower-record recitation, not independently read in the original district order. It must coexist with, rather than be replaced by, the reported obstacles. The core receipt and effort facts are independently corroborated by the full lower opinion.

**Operative law and distinctions.** Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306, concerns notice reasonably calculated to inform affected persons and permit their response; Memphis Light, Gas & Water Division v. Craft, 436 U.S. 1, addresses notice of an available utility-dispute procedure; Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319, supplies context-specific procedural assessment. Whether generally published judicial procedures require the same individualized explanation as an internal procedure is the legal dispute, not a fact established by the packet's earlier characterization of Memphis Light. Good preserves an independent due-process protection but concerns pre-seizure civil forfeiture of real property; movable evidence under a criminal warrant is a different setting. Neither the warrant alone nor the real-property decision automatically resolves the post-seizure-notice issue.

California Penal Code §1536 leaves seized property in official custody subject to the relevant court's orders; People v. Icenogle, 164 Cal. App. 3d 620, recognizes a nonstatutory motion for return even outside a pending prosecution. Section 1540 separately requires restoration if the property differs from that described in the warrant or the warrant lacks the specified probable-cause basis. Aday v. Superior Court, 55 Cal. 2d 789, and the other lower authorities describe additional judicial remedies. These routes and their predicates must remain distinct. The Ninth Circuit regarded the available procedures themselves as constitutionally adequate; the question here is notice, with any properly preserved obstruction issue assessed separately.

**Paths requiring independent assessment.** One path treats notice of the seizure, custodian and identifying circumstances as sufficient where ordinary published judicial remedies are reasonably accessible; it distinguishes a duty to disclose the government's act from a duty to teach generally available law. Its strongest objection is that a nominal remedy cannot afford a meaningful response if the person cannot locate the proceeding or learn how to invoke a genuinely obscure route. A second path applies Mullane, Memphis Light and Mathews to require concise instructions for return after an ex parte seizure, emphasizing the significant property interest and modest cost of changing an existing receipt. Its strongest objection is that adequate state law, identified court and agency contacts supply a lawful route without a universal individualized legal-guide duty. A narrower alternative examines any proved denial of case-specific information necessary to use that route without requiring a general handbook; the District Court's no-necessary-number finding and preservation must be addressed before that alternative can change judgment. No path may treat the officer's disputed statements as findings or automatically reopen the independent qualified-immunity ruling.

**Justice-specific anchors.** Kennedy authored Good's independent process analysis, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg. O'Connor's published Good writing uses Mathews but weighs warrant, record and governmental interests differently; Scalia invokes traditional sovereign procedures without declaring that the Fourth Amendment always displaces due process; Thomas takes a narrower record-specific position. Those three writings are materially different. Breyer joined Richards's nonparty hearing safeguards and its refusal to convert one procedural entitlement into a universal requirement; that is a general procedural anchor, not a result for this notice question. [Good public entry](../../OT1993/output/OT_1993CHUNK1.md) and the copied Richards holdings supply the date-eligible positions. Predivergence Mullane, Memphis Light and Mathews remain the common direct authorities. Modeling must not manufacture a personal West Covina commitment from the real-property result.

**Sources and remaining limits.** [Full appellate opinion](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/113/113.F3d.1004.94-56365.html); its exact receipt and statutory quotations; the bounded [control objective supplement](OT_1998CHUNK3_CONTROL_OBJECTIVE_FACTS.md), West Covina section only. The supplemental finding's original district order, exact grant papers and complete motions were not reviewed. No missing item prevents assessment of the supplied legal question with a remedy respecting unresolved facts; an outcome resting on affirmative obstruction or a particular preserved subsidiary claim requires that source escalation. ## Roberts v. Galen of Virginia, Inc.

**Identity and scope.** No. 97-53; Sixth Circuit No. 96-5298, 111 F.3d 405, April 9, 1997. The preserved question concerns an additional improper-motive element in EMTALA stabilization, not whether the patient ultimately proves every statutory element or a state negligence claim.

**Verified lower record.** Wanda Johnson entered Humana Hospital on May 19, 1992 after grave injuries and was transferred to an Indiana nursing facility on July 24. Her condition deteriorated and she received further expensive care. Use these dates; the control supplement's description of roughly six weeks is inconsistent with them. Her guardian sued the participating hospital under §1395dd and asserted other claims. The district court initially found disputes about stabilization and improper motive; on reconsideration it found insufficient causal connection between any financially motivated employee and the doctor's discharge decision. The appellate majority accepted an additional motive requirement, found no evidence connecting the treating physicians to knowledge of insurance status, and affirmed summary judgment.

The same court expressly recognized a genuine factual dispute about stabilization. The day before transfer the patient developed symptoms of an infection; physicians disagreed about the safety of transfer before response to antibiotics and culture results could be assessed. Whether subsequent deterioration instead resulted from the receiving facility's care was also disputed. A social worker's account supplied evidence of pressure to discharge an unpaid patient. Judge Nelson agreed that the doctor was not shown knowingly influenced by finances but thought the doctor's reliance on the social worker's placement decision allowed a jury to infer a hospital-level causal connection. His dissent accepted the circuit's motive rule and disputed its application; it was not a dissent from that legal element. All three judges affirmed the separate state ostensible-agency ruling.

**Statute in its own terms.** The full official 1994 §1395dd text, including its qualifications, is mechanically copied into SOURCE_EXCERPTS. For the 1992 transfer, §1395dd(b)(1) is triggered when an individual comes to a hospital and the hospital determines an emergency medical condition; it requires further examination and treatment within available staff and facilities to stabilize, or transfer consistent with subsection (c). Subsection (b)(2) separately addresses informed refusal of treatment; (b)(3) addresses informed refusal of transfer. Each requires information about risks and benefits and reasonable steps to secure written informed refusal. None of these refusals is established here.

Under (c)(1), an unstabilized individual may be transferred only upon the specified informed written request or medical certification, together with an appropriate transfer. Physician-absent certification requires consultation and later countersignature. The certification includes a summary of risks and benefits. Appropriate transfer separately requires risk-minimizing treatment within capacity; receiving space, qualified personnel and acceptance; available relevant medical records and the specified certification and on-call information; qualified transport with needed support; and any further regulatory requirement. The definitions distinguish stabilization, transfer, emergency condition and participating hospital. Transfer includes qualifying discharge and excludes movement after declared death or without permission. Subsection (d)(2)(A) expressly allows the person directly harmed by a participating hospital's violation to seek state-law personal-injury damages and appropriate equitable relief against that hospital, subject to the separate two-year limitation. These elements and exceptions are not findings that the transfer met or failed them.

The 1994 compilation's later technical amendment affects the administrative consultation provision, not the stated 1992 stabilization, transfer and civil-action predicates. It must not be used to apply later sanctions changes to the transfer. The 1997 change to the hospital-definition label is likewise immaterial to the supplied ordinary participating hospital. No new hospital-category issue is adjudicated.

**Entering law and paths.** NOW v. Scheidler supplies an analogous unanimous refusal to add an economic-motive condition to another statute while preserving its actual predicates. That analogy does not itself construe EMTALA. Cleland v. Bronson Health Care Group, 917 F.2d 266 (6th Cir. 1990), addresses screening and known-condition stabilization; the lower court extended its motivation discussion from subsection (a) to (b). The alternative is to apply subsection (b)'s own determination-and-stabilization language without that additional condition, leaving statutory knowledge, appropriate transfer, injury and causation for ordinary proceedings. The strongest objection is that Congress addressed dumping rather than federal malpractice; the response requiring evaluation is whether the actual statutory triggers already preserve that distinction. The opposing motive path relies on anti-dumping purpose and the circuit's reading of Cleland; its strongest objection is the distinct language and express civil enforcement, together with the absence of a motive qualification. An institutional-motive factual path within the lower test is separately supported by Nelson's dissent but is not the supplied grant's central legal question.

The control supplement identifies hospital arguments that it lacked actual knowledge of the emergency condition and that proper screening ended its obligations. Those are defense arguments, not findings. A favorable-judgment defense may be considered only within the entering review rules and supported record. The known stabilization dispute cannot become an undisputed absence of knowledge or a finding of full compliance. Rejecting an extra motive element would not decide a subsection (a) screening motive question, create a general negligence action, establish an emergency condition or award damages. A confined remand can leave the genuine alternative predicates and defenses open without deciding them on an incomplete record.

**Justice-specific anchors.** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg joined NOW's rejection of an absent motive limitation and its preservation of all enacted elements. Breyer was not then on the Court; his Gebser concurrence develops the different problem of attributing an established intentional wrong, which must not be mistaken for an EMTALA motive requirement. All eight participating Associates can apply binding NOW while independently assessing this text and statutory context. Franklin and Gebser provide useful action/remedy and attribution distinctions, not a substitute for EMTALA's express action. [NOW public entry](../../OT1993/output/OT_1993CHUNK2.md) and [Gebser](../../OT1997/output/OT_1997CHUNK11.md) are the published anchors.

**Sources and remaining limits.** [Full appellate opinion, including Nelson's dissent](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/111/111.F3d.405.96-5298.html); [official 1994 Title 42 compilation](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title42/html/USCODE-1994-title42.htm), §1395dd and amendment notes; Roberts section only of the control objective supplement. Exact alternative-defense briefing and grant papers were not reviewed. Further factfinding or briefing is required before either defense can support a conclusive alternate merits judgment. No such conclusion is necessary to model the presented motive question and an appropriately bounded remand. Research cutoff: January 13, 1999.

## California Public Employees' Retirement System v. Felzen

**Identity and scope.** No. 97-1732; Seventh Circuit No. 97-2829, Felzen v. Andreas, 134 F.3d 873, January 21, 1998. The question is whether a shareholder objector must intervene to appeal approval of a Rule 23.1 derivative settlement. Do not conflate this with a Rule 23 class member's direct individual cause of action, a shareholder's Article III interest, or an appeal from an actual intervention denial.

**Verified lower record.** CalPERS and the Florida State Board of Administration objected to settlement of derivative litigation arising from alleged misconduct at Archer Daniels Midland. The corporate injury and recovery belonged to the corporation. The objectors claimed the settlement was modest and half would go to counsel; the court did not adjudicate that merits objection because it dismissed their appeal. The lower opinion expressly says they decided not to intervene in reliance on prior circuit decisions. Thus the neutral packet's phrase about not successfully becoming an intervenor must not imply an application and denial that are unestablished. The supplied Supreme Court identity names CalPERS; the lower presence of the Florida board does not establish another Supreme Court petitioner or docket.

The Seventh Circuit applied Marino v. Ortiz, 484 U.S. 301, and Appellate Rule 3(c)'s reference to parties, overruled its prior nonparty-appeal practice, and required intervention. It distinguished injury from party status, emphasized that derivative shareholders assert the corporation's claim, and preserved review of intervention denials. It rejected reliance-based prospective-only treatment under Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Risjord, 449 U.S. 368, Budinich v. Becton Dickinson, 486 U.S. 196, and Christianson v. Colt Industries, 486 U.S. 800. Its claim that Chevron Oil had been wholly overruled must not be imported: this Court's Harper displaces selective prospectivity after application to the first litigants, while reserving other prospective questions. The lower characterization of every party-status restriction as jurisdictional is an issue for analysis, not an independent fact that decides the Supreme Court question.

**Entering law.** Section 1291 finality, §1254 review, Rule 23.1 representative litigation and settlement notice, Rule 24 intervention, and Appellate Rule 3(c) perform distinct jobs. Izumi establishes that financial interest alone and an undisturbed intervention denial do not confer §1254 party status; it does not already decide this shareholder/§1291 question. Richards and Matsushita preserve adequate-representation requirements for binding absent persons, but notice and objections alone neither confer appellate party status nor cure inadequate representation. Marino supplies the predivergence party/intervention rule; United Airlines v. McDonald, 432 U.S. 385, supplies a potential timely postjudgment-intervention comparison, not automatic relief for an unfiled motion. Kamen v. Kemper Financial Services, 500 U.S. 90, concerns the corporation's derivative claim and state-law demand principles. No opinion in this packet establishes that this settlement actually lacked adequate representation.

**Paths requiring independent assessment.** One path requires intervention and treats an appeal from its denial as the available safeguard, emphasizing the separate corporate claimant and statutory party structure. Its strongest objection is that a binding derivative settlement coupled with an expressly invited objection gives the shareholder a concrete procedural role that may be poorly protected if formal intervention is demanded only after circuit practice changes. The opposing path reads the representative settlement and objection procedure as providing sufficient party-equivalent appellate status for a participating objector, while distinguishing Marino's outsiders and Izumi's separate unreviewed intervention denial. Its strongest objection is that the Rules supply notice and intervention separately, injury alone never supplies party status, and shareholder control could displace the corporation and its representative without the intervention safeguard. A narrower remedial path could examine whether a genuine intervention opportunity remains, but must not invent a motion, tolling, reopening, excusable default or mandatory permission. Any treatment of reliance must use Harper's actual present force and address the claimed jurisdictional prerequisite independently.

No path decides settlement fairness, attorney compensation, adequate representation, a direct shareholder claim, or collateral preclusion beyond what is needed to identify the appellate route. Dismissing an appeal is not a merits endorsement of the bargain; permitting the appeal is not rejection of the settlement.

**Justice-specific anchors.** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg joined Izumi's party-status ground. Stevens's Izumi dissent would reach and allow that applicant's intervention; it did not give every interested nonparty an automatic appeal. All eight Associates joined Richards's distinction between adequate representation and shared interests, including Breyer, whose most direct supplied anchor is that later unanimous decision rather than Izumi, decided before his service. Thomas authored Harper's applied-rule portion, joined by Stevens, Scalia and Souter among current Associates; the remedy portion also had Kennedy. O'Connor's distinct temporal position cannot be silently absorbed into the applied-rule coalition. [Izumi public entry](../../OT1993/output/OT_1993CHUNK1.md), [Harper public entry](../../OT1992/output/OT_1992CHUNK9.md), and copied Richards holdings provide the relevant individual joins and limits. Enhanced issue-specific modeling is warranted because representative status, formal intervention, reliance and remedy supply distinct plausible paths.

**Sources and remaining limits.** [Full Seventh Circuit opinion](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/134/134.F3d.873.97-2829.html); [parallel full-opinion text](https://openjurist.org/134/f3d/873), used for the court text only; entering Izumi, Richards and Harper holdings and public writings. Original settlement, objection papers and complete grant were not reviewed. The actual objectors' choice not to intervene is established by the lower opinion; no denied motion is missing from this account. Exact then-effective Rule 23.1/24 and §1291/1254 text should be supplied if the modeling relies on a disputed word or a particular intervention remedy; the generic procedural distinction is sufficiently established for modeling without inventing such a remedy.
