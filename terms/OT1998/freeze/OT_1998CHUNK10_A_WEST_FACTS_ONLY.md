# West v. Gibson: verified predecision source facts

Purpose: factual and date-eligible legal-source handoff for a fresh neutral validator. No non-Stone commitment or proposed disposition is supplied. Research cutoff: June 14, 1999, using the common baseline before that date; June 14 decisions from chunk 9 are excluded.

## Identity and supported record

The assigned merits event is June 14, 1999; docket 98-238; judgment below 137 F.3d 992 (7th Cir. 1998). The assigned question concerns EEOC authority to award compensatory damages against federal agencies under Title VII. The historical Supreme Court's actions are not source facts in this handoff.

Gibson, a VA accountant, was denied a promotion in 1992. His administrative complaint alleged sex discrimination and requested GS-11 backpay and a transfer. The VA rejected the discrimination charge; the EEOC found discrimination and ordered promotion with backpay. Gibson filed his district-court action on January 11, 1996 seeking enforcement and additional relief, including compensatory damages for humiliation, mental anguish, and emotional distress. The agency's subsequent compliance mooted the promotion/backpay enforcement requests. The district court rejected the damages claim for nonexhaustion; the Seventh Circuit reversed that component because it concluded that the EEOC lacked authority to grant this type of relief.

The district court separately rejected frontpay and transfer relief and preserved a fee request associated with the agency's delayed compliance. These are not automatically reopened by the agency-power question. The 1992 discriminatory conduct postdates the 1991 Act; Landgraf's temporal exclusion is therefore not a finding barring this claim.

Source distinction: the Government's merits brief characterizes compensatory damages as not requested administratively. Gibson disputes the dispositive exhaustion inference. His respondent brief reports a request for a monetary cash award in a settlement discussion, says his counselor did not explain the damages remedy, and argues that he completed the applicable procedures. These assertions are not adjudicated proof of exhaustion or estoppel. Do not convert the Government's account into a concession by Gibson, or Gibson's assertions into findings.

## Documented litigation positions

The Government relies on §717(b)'s authority to enforce federal Title VII through appropriate remedies, read with §1981a's later express compensatory-damages authorization. It contends that the jury election operates when a claim reaches district court, that the administrative and judicial provisions form a coordinated system, and that the enactments authorize the same compensatory category at the administrative stage. It contests the need for a separate stage-specific immunity waiver.

Gibson relies on the statutory term action, the any-party jury election, and the fact that a federal agency is bound by an EEOC determination while an employee can seek judicial relief. He contends that Congress conditioned the additional damages waiver on a judicial proceeding and that remedial purpose cannot broaden that consent. Independently, respondent's merits brief at printed pp.31–38 argues actual exhaustion and estoppel. Those alternatives were not the Seventh Circuit's stated basis; their preservation, facts, governing limits, and appropriate treatment remain separate questions.

The lower court's three grounds are judicial-action wording, the jury condition and absence of agency judicial review, and strict construction of sovereign consent. Its opinion is reproduced in the petition appendix at 1a–14a; the district opinion appears at 15a–28a. No determination of the amount of compensable injury is supplied.

## Date-eligible legal sources

- 42 U.S.C. §2000e-16(b)–(d); §1981a(a)(1), (b), (c), and definitions; §2000e-5(g). The Government's petition/appendix and merits brief reproduce the relevant enactments. §1981a excludes punitive damages against government respondents and excludes §706(g) backpay and related relief from its compensatory category; the caps and conditional jury election retain their own terms.
- Canonical Lane v. Peña, June 20, 1996, distinguishes substantive federal coverage from unequivocal consent to the particular Treasury remedy, while identifying the separate federal-employment channel. The prior public Stevens dissent joined by Breyer addresses combined enactments; it is noncontrolling.
- Canonical Landgraf, April 26, 1994, preserves statutory damages categories and the dependent jury condition. It does not decide administrative power here.
- Canonical McCarthy, March 4, 1992, distinguishes congressional exhaustion commands from discretionary judicial exhaustion and confines its damages-only result to its actual compensation-incapable process. It does not by itself decide Title VII exhaustion.
- Current-term Blue Fox, January 20, 1999, distinguishes actual remedial entitlement from labels and Treasury consent; its lien/APA holding does not decide the different Title VII text.
