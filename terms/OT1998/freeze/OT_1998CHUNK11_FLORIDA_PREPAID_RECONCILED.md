## 2. Statutory clarity, patent property and separate §5 positions

**All eight adopt statutory clarity:** 35 U.S.C. §§271(h),296(a) expressly include the State and remove immunity from private patent suit in federal court. Section 296(b) separately supplies the otherwise available patent remedies. The enactment applies to violations on or after October 28, 1992. Congressional intent, constitutional authority, actual State consent, statutory infringement and entitlement to a particular remedy remain distinct. General §1331 jurisdiction or statutory inclusion alone would not supply the required remedy/forum clarity, but these express provisions do.

**All eight recognize patent property and preserve the separate constitutional inquiry:** A patent is an established exclusive property interest; federal creation does not disqualify it from Fourteenth Amendment protection. Recognizing that property and alleging infringement do not by themselves establish deprivation without due process, a compensable injury or an award. Available process and remedies, the conduct alleged, and any applicable culpability requirement require their own treatment. The official reporter at 527 U.S.642 correctly attributes the contrary federal-created-property contention to Florida Prepaid; the Cornell draft's attribution to College Savings is not followed.

**The four affirmative §5 adopters — Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer — join this complete operative proposition:** Section 296(a) validly authorizes this patent action as preventive enforcement of the Fourteenth Amendment. The existing defined property right, the potential loss from State use, proof difficulties, exclusive federal patent jurisdiction and uncertainty about effective compensation provide a supported preventive relationship. A prospective injunction does not compensate completed loss. Extending the ordinary cause to some conduct not itself unconstitutional does not necessarily destroy congruence and proportionality. The Act changes no underlying constitutional property definition and imposes a remedy in a defined field rather than RFRA's universal replacement of constitutional scrutiny.

Uniformity and equal competitive treatment are relevant to the practical remedial setting but are not alone sufficient §5 grounds. The four rely on the relationship to existing property and threatened deprivation without adequate process. They do not find that every State remedy is inadequate or that every allegation of willfulness proves a constitutional wrong. They sustain the action without approving every possible §296(b) award. Fees, enhancement, injunction and compensation retain their own enacted conditions and any properly presented award-specific constitutional limits.

**The four negative §5 adopters — O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas — join this complete operative proposition:** The Act's broad imposition of ordinary private patent liability is not sufficiently fitted, on this record and statutory design, to the constitutional injury of State deprivation without due process. Ordinary patent liability includes innocent infringement and circumstances with adequate remedies; patent property and the usefulness of a federal remedy do not themselves establish the necessary relationship. Congress's national uniformity and equal-treatment aims cannot independently supply §5 authority.

Their objection is substantive fit, not a numerical deficiency or a universal requirement for formal findings, a violation in every jurisdiction, geographical limits, sunsets, or individual constitutional findings for every statutory prohibition. The operative simulated Boerne rule expressly rejects those mandatory conditions. Both sides recognize preventive legislation can reach nonviolations. The negative four do not find Florida's untested claims-bill or takings routes adequate, direct State-remedy exhaustion, or determine whether the alleged infringement occurred.

**Historical precision about culpability:** The historical dissent at 527 U.S.654 questioned whether Daniels's prison-negligence rule necessarily governs patent deprivations and emphasized the actual willful-infringement allegation. All four joined that reservation. The independent freeze's common distinction between statutory infringement and constitutional deprivation is retained, but it cannot be enlarged into an eight-Justice holding that every innocent patent infringement is categorically outside “deprivation.” The four §5 proponents need not decide that constitutional extension to sustain prevention here. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas accept the Daniels objection as part of their negative fit analysis. This preserves the original common proposition at its supported level and corrects any broader reading; it creates no automatic willfulness exception.

**Individual sources:** Stevens and Ginsburg fully joined the actual simulated Boerne rule, which distinguishes prevention from redefining rights; their property-sensitive Sullivan positions do not undermine §5 prevention here. Souter and Breyer's Boerne reservations concerned the constitutional baseline and do not become a general congressional power to redefine rights; their actual Sullivan concurrence preserves individual property and practical loss. O'Connor's conditional Boerne objection depended on Smith, not an unsettled premise that patents are property. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas actually adopted Boerne's fit limitation. Kennedy's own opinion expressly rejects a required evidence inventory. These actual positions explain why the historical substantive split survives the changed articulation of governing law without importing historical Boerne's whole application.

## 3. Prospective commercial consent is independent of abrogation and actual submission

**Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer adopt an independently sufficient ground:** When Congress unmistakably and prospectively subjects specified State commercial conduct to private federal-court enforcement, a State that continues a commercial undertaking it can realistically choose not to operate accepts the condition for violations arising from covered post-enactment activity. The competitive marketing of investment products is an optional means of furthering education; education's public purpose does not make this particular business unavoidable. Congress's substantive Patent Clause authority regulates the underlying patent conduct; the amenability ground here is acceptance of a clear condition, not a general Article I abrogation holding.

The strongest objection is that statutory liability is Congress's command rather than Florida's unequivocal assent. The four answer by the combination of clear prospective terms, continued covered activity and real practical choice. They do not rely on a fictitious federal grant, signed waiver, contract with CSB, quantified profits or exit costs. Neither all commerce, a general regulatory benefit, a heading, nor the mere choice to infringe suffices. Statutory violation remains to be proved.

Their historical full dissent join, including footnote 16, confirms that waiver is an independent ground rather than a consequence of §5. That footnote's analogy to Florida's use of patent-system benefits is not a finding that this defendant owned particular patents or that any State patent application waives every immunity defense. No such broader rule is needed for their actual confined commercial-condition commitment. Their position is derived here from this case's statute, preserved argument and entering law, not a same-day companion ruling.

**O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas reject that proposed rule:** A clear federal declaration of liability is not the State's clear submission to federal jurisdiction. Continuing lawful commerce, even an optional undertaking, differs from accepting a specified federal benefit or affirmatively invoking judicial authority. They reject imputed consent without endorsing an automatic government-purpose exemption or denying Congress's authority to regulate substantive conduct. Actual State waiver, properly conditioned federal grants and interstate compacts remain distinct, with real coercion questions preserved.

**Prior-law boundary:** Welch rejects the general-language consent inference; its four-Justice prospective-condition discussion did not validate every future condition. The actual simulated Hilton preserves specialized FELA state-court coverage and reliance while leaving the old operational federal-waiver theory displaced. The affirmative four distinguish later explicit terms and genuine choice; the negative four say clarity cures intent but not missing State assent. Neither side may silently reverse Hilton or treat mere coverage as consent. Stevens and Souter's actual Hilton joins, Kennedy's actual Hilton opinion, and O'Connor/Scalia's contrary Hilton writing retain their respective scopes. Ginsburg, Breyer and Thomas receive no invented Hilton participation. Hess addresses actual entity status, Lane federal remedy-specific consent, Cedar Rapids accepted funding obligations, and West explicit federal compensation; none already decides this commercial condition.

The historical majority relied on the same-day historical College Savings rejection of constructive waiver. That is unavailable as entering law here. Its four Associate joiners nevertheless independently reject the theory on their established actual-consent grounds and the present record, with the developed arguments above. This is an independent legal conclusion, not a chronological import.
