## Supported record and its limits

**Instruction and law described below.** The penalty jury had death and life without possibility of parole as its alternatives. The charge described the Governor's ability to modify a life-without-parole sentence into one permitting parole. It then directed jurors not to consider the Governor's commutation power in fixing punishment and not to speculate whether or when commutation would occur. The petition also reproduces the concluding direction to consider only the previously listed aggravating and mitigating factors. The corrective language is part of the charge and must accompany any assessment of its possible effect.

Because Coleman had multiple prior felony convictions, the lower courts described California law as requiring consultation with the Board of Prison Terms and the approval of four California Supreme Court Justices before gubernatorial commutation. App. 31 expressly says the Board's recommendation was not binding. Do not turn consultation into a requirement of Board consent merely because the opposition uses broader language. The instruction omitted these limitations; the lower federal courts held the resulting description inaccurate and constitutionally defective. This preparation reports their holding without entering a new constitutional holding.

**Aggravation and mitigation actually presented.** The petition describes convictions for burglary and sale of heroin, false imprisonment and dagger possession, offenses against two girls, and incidents of institutional violence. The District Court's penalty account, App. 100–104, describes twelve prosecution witnesses, including the two victims of sexual offenses, testimony about a woman restrained at knifepoint, an inmate stabbing, assaults on other inmates, and misconduct toward staff. These are descriptions of evidence and lower findings, not new factfinding by this context.

The same District Court account identifies three mitigation witnesses. Dr. Studenski described Coleman's request for psychiatric help, aggressive sexual urges, family losses, his tentative alternative diagnoses, and the isolating conditions and possible effects of the adjustment center. Coleman's mother described domestic violence, his witnessing his father's killing of a neighbor and testifying against him, sibling deaths, and prolonged institutionalization. Minister Charles Jones described favorable attributes as well as temper and lack of control. Counsel linked that evidence to mental and emotional disturbance, background, institutionalization, duress, and emotional age. The record-specific question includes whether the commutation misinformation prevented meaningful consideration of that evidence despite its admission.

## What the lower courts actually decided about effect

The District Court's full commutation analysis is now available at App. 142–151. It discusses the accuracy and mitigating-evidence requirements, compares the instruction with Ramos and Hamilton, and explains why it regarded the corrective direction as insufficient. At App. 149 it expressly considers the prosecution argument, aggravating evidence, and record as a whole. It connects the instruction to difficulty giving mitigating force to the testimony explaining Coleman's temper and institutional history, the jury's prior-conviction request, and the prior convictions' legal effect on commutation. App. 150 links these matters to the prosecution's presentation of danger inside and outside prison. App. 151 concludes that the instruction's inaccuracy and potential diversion from mitigation violated the Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments and grants sentence relief requiring new penalty proceedings.

That is a genuine record-specific analysis. It is incorrect to say that the District Court did nothing beyond identify an inaccurate sentence description or that its reasons are unavailable. At the same time, its commutation discussion does not expressly announce a Chapman noncontribution inquiry, a beyond-reasonable-doubt harmlessness burden, or a substantial-and-injurious-effect determination. It repeatedly uses reasonable-likelihood, likely-preclusion, and potential-diversion reasoning tied to identifying the constitutional defect. The full reproduced District Court order contains no textual citation to Brecht or Chapman. The opposition's assertion that the District Court performed the necessary Brecht inquiry is therefore an argument about the substantive work its analysis accomplished, not a verified separate express finding under that named standard.

The Ninth Circuit expressly receives the State's substantial-and-injurious-effect argument at App. 32. At App. 33 it answers by invoking Boyde, through circuit precedent, and states that undermining the jury's understanding of sentencing options creates a reasonable likelihood of a constitutionally improper application. It then affirms the District Court's sentence ruling. It therefore did not overlook the State's argument. Whether its response, read with the District Court's factual application, performs the legally required causal inquiry is the live question. A mere count of citations cannot settle it, and neither the petition's automatic-reversal description nor the opposition's completed-harmlessness description is a substitute for reading the actual reasons.

The Ninth Circuit expressly did not reach Coleman's other penalty challenges because this ground sustained relief. The District Court had separately addressed those claims, usually adversely to Coleman. Preserve their appellate status without declaring them all unadjudicated below, reviving an abandoned claim, or resolving their merits in this narrow review. The guilt adjudication is outside the State's presented question.

## Supported lawful alternatives for independent consideration

**Existing substantive analysis may suffice.** A court could conclude that the District Court's particular treatment of mitigating evidence, the conviction-document request, the inaccurate sentencing alternative, and the danger argument already establishes the relevant effect under the governing standard, and that the appellate affirmance preserves that adequate determination. This route must identify the actual legal standard and show why the existing reasoning satisfies it. It cannot rely simply on the opposition's assertion that Brecht was applied. The strongest objection is that likely interference with considering evidence identifies constitutional error without establishing its contribution to the sentence, especially in light of the corrective instruction, substantial aggravation, and the prosecutor's sustained institutional-danger argument. The existing lower reasons must answer that objection substantively; absence of required magic words alone does not defeat them.

**A distinct causal inquiry may remain necessary.** A court could conclude that the lower courts' reliance on Boyde and potential diversion did not independently assess the established error's effect under the chosen harmlessness burden. A limited remand could require that assessment on the existing record, without finding actual harmlessness, ordering new evidence, or resolving the underlying constitutional accuracy question. The strongest objection is the District Court's express record-wide application and concrete explanation of how the mistake altered consideration of the sentencing choice. A remand needs an identified unfinished legal task, not the inaccurate premise that no record-specific analysis occurred or an objection only to vocabulary. The receiving court could reach the same result after the proper inquiry.

**The standard is a separate fork.** An extension of State-borne Chapman to this preserved capital-selection trial error is supported by the existing constitutional-error categories and capital-reliability/correction principles. Its strongest counterargument is the expressly reserved reach of the existing habeas holdings and the different function of this error from guilt-phase personal-intent omissions. Alternatively, a court could adopt a substantial-and-injurious-effect framework for this reserved category, grounded in collateral review's distinct remedial role. That path must independently justify the rule, burden, and treatment of genuine uncertainty; historical descriptions of Brecht in the advocates' papers do not supply current law. Under either route, permissible harmlessness review is distinct from automatic relief, and identifying the constitutional defect does not by label alone complete a separate effect inquiry. Nor does this preparation impose a second duplicative prejudice inquiry where the governing definition of some different constitutional claim already incorporates the requisite effect.

**Petition and remedy boundaries.** Denial of review remains available as a petition-stage choice and would add no merits holding. A merits disposition retaining sentence relief needs a supported constitutional violation and the applicable effect determination; a methodological remand need not decide actual prejudice. If sentence relief is ultimately warranted, it remains confined to punishment, with a reasonable state opportunity for lawful sentencing.
