## 5. Minnesota v. Mille Lacs Band of Chippewa Indians — March 24, 1999

### Separately tested extinction grounds

**M1 — 1850 order.** Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer conclude that this combined revocation-and-removal order did not terminate the retained use rights. The supplied lower-court account establishes an unauthorized compelled-removal component under the Removal Act's consent structure and a connected removal purpose. On that supported account, the revocation cannot be severed into an independently chosen executive action without changing the order's operation. They need not decide that the President lacked every power to revoke under the treaty's pleasure clause, that every revocation requires a new substantive good-faith test, or that subsequent nonenforcement independently restores extinguished rights.

Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas take the contrary legal view of severability: the treaty independently authorizes revocation and the revocation command can operate apart from the challenged removal direction. A shared policy purpose does not by itself deprive a legally authorized and independently operative command of effect. Each would reverse on that sufficient ground. They do not need to uphold compelled removal, and do not invent evidence that the President privately would have chosen a different order. Whether an additional treaty or public-land power authorized removal need not be decided to state this independently sufficient dissenting ground.

**M2 — 1855 treaty.** The five affirming Associates conclude that this land cession did not surrender the separately retained off-reservation hunting, fishing and gathering use rights. The agreement must be read in the sense reasonably understood by the tribal parties and against its supported land-purchase negotiations, which did not address surrender of these independent privileges. Dion permits abrogation without an invariable express-word formula, but requires clear evidence that the disputed right was considered and relinquished. Menominee and Fishing Vessel resist casual implication. Klamath's reservation-dependent rights do not make every land cession extinguish independent use rights; Bourland distinguishes loss of exclusion-derived nonmember regulation from retained personal use. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas reject M2 as an independent alternative ground. They read the 1855 cession of all right, title and interest of every nature as encompassing the retained use privilege, an interest in the ceded property. A drafting requirement expressly naming each usufructuary interest is inconsistent with that comprehensive text. The absence of a negotiation discussion is the strongest contrary point, but it does not, for these three, create ambiguity in an otherwise encompassing conveyance. Their view applies to the Mille Lacs Band and the treaty interests within that agreement; it does not invent a cession by a nonsignatory Band or invalidate other treaties.

**M3 — Statehood and Race Horse.** The five affirming Associates conclude that Minnesota's admission did not itself extinguish these federal treaty rights. Reserved use rights can coexist with state authority to impose lawful, necessary nondiscriminatory conservation regulation; state sovereignty does not require their cancellation. Race Horse's treaty-specific temporary-right analysis must be distinguished from the right retained here: termination at the President's pleasure does not itself identify state admission as the terminating event. Its broader suggestion that equal footing alone extinguishes such treaty rights is rejected to that extent; that proposition cannot govern after the compatible state-regulation approach recognized in Winans, Tulee and Fishing Vessel. They do not displace Race Horse's distinct construction of its own unoccupied-lands condition without deciding that separate treaty.

Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would independently reverse under Race Horse. They read the pleasure-qualified privilege as a temporary treaty use right rather than an indefinite right intended to burden the State's ordinary regulatory authority after admission. In their view Race Horse's treatment of such temporary hunting rights remains the controlling analogy; the later cases concerning differently reserved rights do not erase that holding. Their ground does not assert that equal footing nullifies all federal treaties or that all tribal property or land rights disappear at statehood. Each expressly adopts this alternative; their common judgment alone is not used to infer it. The contrary distinction between a presidential-pleasure condition and a statehood condition does not persuade them that the privilege was meant to have permanent operation after admission.

**M4 — Remaining scope and remedy.** The five affirm the recognition of surviving treaty rights against the extinction grounds properly presented. They do not reopen every lower-court ruling about other Bands, allocation, private-land access or detailed conservation administration merely because those subjects appeared in the larger appeal. No supplied later event establishes a distinct termination premise. No unrestricted harvest, numerical allocation, universal right to enter private lands or tribal veto over every state rule follows. Any nonparty preclusion or prospective-officer question retains Richards and the actual simulated Coeur d'Alene rule at its own scope. No title conveyance, retrospective compensation or general immunity from procedure is entered. The three dissenting Associates would reverse the declaration of survival on effective 1850 revocation, and independently on the 1855 cession at its party-specific scope and statehood. None supplies an affirmative disposition of ungranted resource-management questions.
