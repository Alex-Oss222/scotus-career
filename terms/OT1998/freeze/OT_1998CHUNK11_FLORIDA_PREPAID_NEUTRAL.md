## Material facts, allegations, and lower reasoning

CSB, a New Jersey bank, markets CollegeSure CDs; Florida Prepaid markets a competing tuition-prepayment program. CSB owns U.S. Patent No. 4,722,055 concerning apparatus/methods used to administer its plan. CSB sued in New Jersey on November 7, 1994, alleging direct and indirect infringement. The United States' respondent brief, printed pp.6–7, recites a complaint alleging knowing and willful infringement and requesting declaratory relief, injunction and damages, citing C.A. App.100–103. These are allegations, not findings. The United States intervened under §2403(a) to defend the statute. Florida Prepaid's arm-of-State status was agreed in the lower appeal (148 F.3d at 1355 n.1); this packet does not invent a separate contested entity inquiry.

The Federal Circuit treated statutory intent as unmistakable, upheld §5 abrogation because patents are established property and a uniform remedy was sufficiently fitted to protecting them, and did not reach either litigation-conduct or Parden waiver. It regarded claims-bill relief under Fla. Stat. §11.065 as probably sufficient process but concluded that preventive legislation could nevertheless reach States generally. Its footnote separately observed uncertainty about a state takings action, notwithstanding Jacobs Wind, 626 So.2d 1333, given exclusive federal patent jurisdiction. Those are lower-court characterizations and disputed legal premises, not findings that a concrete adequate remedy was available to this claimant.

Congressional material reproduced in the Joint Appendix and described at 148 F.3d 1353–55 includes reported examples of State infringement, testimony acknowledging no evidence of massive/widespread infringement, increasing State-university commercial participation, and concerns that injunctions alone omit past loss and ordinary damages may operate like a compulsory license. The reported incidents do not by themselves prove unconstitutional deprivations without process; their significance to prevention remains disputed. The record's quantity is not a mandatory Boerne checklist. The statute covers more than intentional infringement and does not expressly require a prior showing that State remedies are unavailable.

CSB's filed respondent brief, printed pp.49–50, renews alternative waiver arguments: appearance, a §1404(a) transfer request, and an affirmative counterclaim seeking invalidity, noninfringement and unenforceability; it also invokes continued commercial activity under Parden. These are preserved contentions not lower findings of waiver. The Federal Circuit's choice not to reach them is verified. The Government's respondent brief also offers conditional commercial waiver. Exact litigation actions and their legal effect should be distinguished; a bare appearance alone is not conceded consent.

## Issue snapshots and strongest supported paths

**1. Express enactment and Article I.** Section 296(a), enacted October 28, 1992 and effective for violations on or after that date, expressly removes State immunity for patent suits in federal court. Section 271(h) includes States/instrumentalities/official-capacity actors, and §296(b) makes the otherwise available patent remedies available to the same extent. Constitutional authority remains distinct. The Patent Clause and retained Union Gas support an analytical argument that explicit congressional remedies enforcing a nationally uniform patent system can bind States. The strongest objection is that Union Gas's actual Commerce conclusion supplies no automatic Patent Clause extension, and the Eleventh Amendment/textual plaintiff setting and settled sovereign structure require independent treatment. No majority in Seminole settled that extension; patent-system participation is not statutory waiver by itself.

**2. Fourteenth Amendment protection and preventive fit.** A patent is a recognized exclusionary property interest (35 U.S.C. §261; Hartford-Empire; Consolidated Fruit-Jar). Federal creation does not by itself remove constitutional property protection. A State's infringement is not automatically a due-process violation: fault, authorization and available process/remedy matter under Daniels, Parratt, Hudson and Zinermon. Patent infringement liability alone does not supply every constitutional element. Fitzpatrick establishes §5 power distinct from Article I, while Boerne allows prevention beyond independently unconstitutional applications.

The strongest statute-supporting path emphasizes established exclusive property, difficult proof of intention, jurisdictional remedial gaps, inadequacy of injunctions to compensate completed infringement, and a remedy limited to unlawful use of identified patent property. It argues that broad coverage is a fitted preventive mechanism, not redefinition of property or due process. The strongest opposing path distinguishes ordinary statutory infringement from intentional deprivation without adequate process and asks whether nationwide full patent remedies, including innocent conduct and States providing remedies, exceed the shown constitutional risk. “No widespread infringement” is not equivalent to “no constitutional injury”; nor is the law's commercial subject alone a sufficient §5 justification. Legislative predictions and particular statutory design must be assessed under Boerne's actual standard.

**3. Actual-violation application or narrower remand.** If broad preventive abrogation fails, a legally supported application to conduct that independently violates §1 remains analytically distinct. The knowing/willful allegation makes this path concrete but does not establish lack of due process or an adjudicated constitutional violation. A remand could address preserved allegations and the reality/adequacy of remedies if that ground supports lawful reach and the statutory scope. No automatic willfulness exception, exhaustion command, constitutional damages cause of action, or burden shift is supplied by this preparation. Conversely, blanket dismissal must account for every actually preserved alternative ground that could support the judgment.

**4. Waiver and officer relief.** Litigation waiver and commercial-condition waiver must be analyzed separately from abrogation. Hilton preserves specialized FELA coverage while refusing to revive displaced constructive federal-forum consent; Welch's clear-statement requirement is not proof of actual State consent. A stronger Parden theory would require a justified account of surviving scope and genuine voluntary choice, not merely participation in commerce. The opposed account treats compulsory loss of immunity as abrogation in another form and distinguishes consent to a federal benefit or litigation submission. If waiver is not resolved, the lower court may address the preserved factual/legal grounds on remand. An officer action must identify a connected officer and ongoing conduct and obey Young/Edelman and the actual patent remedy; the present corporate-State action does not automatically become such a suit. Prospective relief cannot pay accrued damages, and no existing officer complaint is invented.

**Remedy distinctions.** Affirmance permits the pending patent action to continue without liability findings. Reversal on all available immunity grounds ends the direct-State action without adjudicating patent merits. Vacatur/remand may preserve a concrete alternative immunity answer or application-specific inquiry. A conclusion that §5 is insufficient alone neither automatically overrules Union Gas nor resolves waiver, Article I scope, or patent validity. Any remand must specify the legal issue retained and avoid inventing a claim or fact.

Seminole's direct-State disposition lacks a controlling amenability rationale. Its majority clear-statement proposition does control. Union Gas's damages judgment, statutory holding and five-vote constitutional conclusion remain effective at their decided scope, without making every plurality sentence law. Hans and Fitzpatrick remain effective. The lower opinions' historical assertions that Seminole overruled Union Gas are descriptions of their historical premise and are displaced here. They cannot be imported as governing law.

The plaintiff is a New Jersey bank suing an admitted arm of Florida. This is an interstate private plaintiff setting within the Eleventh Amendment's words, unlike Seminole's tribal plaintiff. Extending the retained Union Gas constitutional conclusion or adopting a broader common-law account requires attention to that difference. Broad constitutional alternatives in individual Seminole writings are not controlling holdings. Stevens's public writing expressly reserved the textual out-of-state/foreign-plaintiff issue; Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer's constitutional account was conditional because their officer ground succeeded. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas publicly advocated a categorical Article I prohibition and overruling Union Gas; that proposal did not become law. These are source-specific prior positions, not predicted commitments here.

Boerne supplies the operative §5 standard through the full Kennedy opinion joined by Stevens, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg, as well as the Chief Justice. No inference that those three adopted either a universal §5 invalidity rule or deference to every statute follows. Their distinct public grounds are preserved in the exact excerpt.

Current-term boundaries: Haddle (December 14, 1998) distinguishes statutory economic injury from constitutional property entitlement; it does not constitutionalize every business tort.
