## Review and judgment components

All eight non-Stone Justices participate and reach the statutory punitive question under §1254(1). The requested punitive instruction and renewed objection preserve that issue. Employer attribution is a subsidiary remedial issue within the supplied grant; none treats the packet’s paraphrase as a separately verified question. **Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer:** Vacate the en banc judgment sustaining punitive withholding and remand. An independently egregious act is not an additional statutory prerequisite once malice or reckless indifference to federal rights is proved. Their remedy positions differ concerning application and the proposed employer-compliance limit, as specified below.

**Scalia and Thomas:** Affirm withholding punitive damages. They read §1981a(b)(1)’s traditional punitive terminology and its separation from ordinary intentional discrimination to require aggravated punitive culpability, and find the supported promotion evidence insufficient under that construction. They do not disturb the established discrimination verdict or backpay.

No Justice reopens the $52,718 backpay judgment, converts it into a §1981a compensatory award, orders a punitive sum or decides the distinct instatement/fees issues. ## Proposition-level commitments

**M1 — Statutory state of mind, without an independent severity element.** Under §1981a(b)(1), a complaining party must establish malice or reckless indifference to federally protected rights. Intentional differentiation because of sex and consciousness of violating, or consciously disregarding a substantial risk of violating, federal rights are distinct inquiries. Ordinary negligence about legal obligations is insufficient. Actual awareness may be proved circumstantially; obviousness, concealment and knowledgeable implementation can support an inference, but a bare “should have known” negligence formulation cannot replace the statutory mental state. Egregious conduct may prove that state; an additional objectively outrageous act is not invariably required. The same evidence may satisfy different elements without making the elements identical.

**M2 — Judicial sufficiency and jury discretion remain distinct.** The court determines whether a reasonable jury could find the punitive prerequisites on evidence and reasonable inferences favorable to Kolstad. Neither an existing discrimination verdict nor a rejected explanation automatically compels punitive submission in every case. Conversely, the evidence need not be direct or of an entirely new category. Once submission is proper, an award remains discretionary; punitive damages are never owed as of right. Section1981a’s amount caps and ordinary amount review do not replace the threshold showing.

**M3 — Aggravated-culpability alternative.** On Scalia and Thomas’s construction, the separate punitive paragraph incorporates the traditional exceptional character of punitive liability: conduct exhibiting malice, wanton disregard or comparably aggravated indifference, beyond the intentional discrimination necessary for ordinary liability. Familiarity with the prohibition and an intentionally discriminatory act do not invariably supply that aggravation. Circumstantial evidence can establish it, and one act can suffice; no mandatory pattern, personal insult, direct admission or physical injury is required. They do not rest on a categorical prohibition of punitive awards in pretext cases. They regard the present evidence, even with permissible employee-favorable inferences, as failing that heightened punitive characterization. M3 is incompatible with M1 insofar as M1 makes conscious disregard of federal rights sufficient without further aggravated conduct.

**A1 — Punitive attribution requires its own agency inquiry.** Meritor calls for agency principles suited to TitleVII. The ordinary liability finding does not automatically decide all punitive-attribution predicates. Restatement(Second) of Agency§217C and Torts§909 supply four alternatives: authorization of the act and manner; reckless employment of an unfit agent; managerial capacity plus scope of employment; or ratification/approval by the principal or a managerial agent. These are disjunctive alternatives, not a universal demand for personal authorization. For the supported managerial route, actual entrusted discretion and personnel responsibility matter; title alone does not decide the issue. Performing the delegated promotion-selection function may be within scope even when performed discriminatorily or against a prohibition. Neither the absence of board approval nor Allen’s final signature automatically excludes the material role of Wheat. No unfit-hiring, authorization, ratification or alter-ego finding is invented.

**A2 — Proposed limited good-faith adaptation of managerial punitive attribution.** O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would additionally hold that the employer is not vicariously liable for punitive damages solely through A1’s managerial-and-scope branch when the discriminatory decision contravened the employer’s actual good-faith efforts to comply with TitleVII. The employer must establish that limitation by a preponderance: it seeks an exception to otherwise proved attribution and possesses the evidence of its own measures. Efforts must operate as a genuine attempt to implement the legal prohibition, not merely announce it. The rule requires no universal training checklist, prescribed reporting mechanism or guaranteed success. A written policy alone, the label “good faith,” or employee silence alone does not establish the limitation. Nor does it protect an employer’s own authorization or ratification of the punitive wrong or defeat a separately established basis of its own culpability. It limits this punitive imputation; ordinary liability and compensation are not erased.

That is a **new proposed construction**, not a holding imported from Ellerth, Faragher, §1981a(a)(3), or the Restatement. Its basis is the punitive focus on the respondent’s culpability, the common-law concern about personally innocent principals, and Meritor’s statutory adaptation of agency. Section1981a(a)(3) directly bars the section’s damages for specifically described disability-accommodation practices; it does not supply this TitleVII rule’s predicates or burden. The proposed A2 rule does different work, but the express exception is a substantial counterargument against it.

**A3 — No additional categorical compliance immunity for a proved managerial act.** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join A1 but reject A2. Section217C(c) expressly treats a managerial act within scope as a sufficient agency connection, without adding a fifth “personally culpable principal” requirement. Section1981a specifies the mental state and separately supplies an express, carefully delimited good-faith damages exception in(a)(3). An additional categorical TitleVII immunity requires more than a desirable prevention incentive. Actual compliance evidence may bear on whether the relevant actor possessed the required culpability, on the facts of an agency route, and on the jury’s discretionary punitive choice. It does not automatically cancel already proved managerial attribution and culpability. The four do not equate every supervisor with a manager or every attributable hostile environment with punitive liability.

**R1 — Ordinary remand without a present punitive award.** Remove the en banc independent-egregiousness ground and return for application of the correct punitive standard and applicable agency rules. Existing liability and backpay remain. Genuinely preserved independent issues retain their ordinary procedural treatment; the mandate creates no automatic pleading amendment, discovery entitlement or new factual defense. No missing research source proves litigant waiver or evidentiary failure.

**R2 — More specific submission position.** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer would further hold that, considering the supported evidence together rather than demanding an admission or isolating each item, the managerial promotion process, asserted concealment/changing explanations, evidence of biased influence and demonstrated equal-employment policy permit the statutory punitive inference. Wheat’s role cannot be excluded merely because Allen signed the selection. They would return the punitive issue for jury consideration under M1–M2 and A1, subject to ordinary resolution of genuinely preserved independent issues. This is sufficiency for consideration, not a compelled culpability finding or award. The disputed precise titles and evidence do not justify a final punitive sum. They reject remand for a newly recognized A2 immunity.

**R3 — Reconsideration under the additional agency limitation.** O’Connor and Kennedy support R1 and A2 but do not join R2’s jury-direction language. The lower courts must address punitive managerial attribution, the legal/factual availability of the limited compliance rule, and sufficiency under the corrected mental-state standard in the ordinary course. They make no present finding of an effective compliance program, no automatic reopening order and no conclusion that the Association preserved or proved every possible defense. A favorable legal rule is not evidence that its factual conditions exist.

## Adversarial tests and remaining limits

**Mental-state path.** M1’s strongest objection is that knowing sex discrimination will frequently satisfy the punitive state, making the two statutory levels overlap in practice. The six supporters answer that the statute separates the objects of intention; it does not guarantee a prescribed fraction of compensable cases without punitive exposure. Knowledge/recklessness, proper attribution, judicial sufficiency and discretionary award all remain. M3’s strongest objection is that it adds an undefined condition absent from the text and conflicts with Smith’s disjunctive formulation and Hazen Paper’s refusal of an extra outrage condition. Scalia and Thomas maintain that TitleVII’s separately codified traditional punitive standard has a more aggravated substantive character than mere intentional discrimination plus common legal awareness. Their adverse application must stand on that construction, not on an asserted lack of direct evidence or missing research.

**Attribution path.** A2’s best support is that routine compensation and punitive blame have distinct functions,§217C/§909 already limit punishment of principals, and Meritor permits statutory agency adaptation. Its strongest objection is that the Restatement itself makes managerial scope sufficient and Congress wrote an express good-faith damages bar elsewhere. A2 supporters distinguish that accommodation bar from the definition of whose culpability is imputed and confine their addition to the managerial vicarious branch. A3 supporters reject that further adaptation and give genuine compliance its evidentiary/discretionary significance. Neither side assumes the general harassment defense is already law, treats the Ellerth concurrence as controlling, or shifts the underlying TitleVII attribution burden.

**Application and remedy.** The lower majority’s current-decree and final-selector reasoning cannot erase the actual verdict or turn disputed evidence into adverse fact findings. Conversely, neither the verdict nor professional status makes punitive culpability automatic. The expired decree may have a properly assessed evidentiary use, but it proves neither past liability nor a current prohibition of all preselection; admissibility remains for ordinary proceedings. The supplied policy proves text and relevant awareness, not effective implementation. Allen’s final authority and Wheat’s delegated input support a managerial inquiry without inventing an exact title or alter-ego finding. R2 supporters find the cumulative evidence sufficient for jury consideration; R3 supporters require ordinary application of the newly proposed agency limit before commanding submission. Scalia and Thomas would terminate the punitive component on their more demanding statutory standard.
