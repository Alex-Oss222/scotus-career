## Reach, record and separate grounds

The District Court granted summary judgment on qualified immunity; the Ninth Circuit reversed in part on the occupational claim. Review permits the preserved legal constitutional and immunity questions. No supported jurisdictional, standing or vehicle defect calls for an alternative dismissal. The lawyer asserts his own right. The client's privilege and consultation interests cannot be silently substituted as a different claim, and no new third-party-standing ruling is necessary.

The analysis assumes the plaintiff-favorable purposeful interference described below: prosecutors arranged the search during Baker's grand-jury appearance; she twice sought consultation, could not obtain it, invoked her privilege, and the grand jury recessed. The reported absence of evidence of adverse income, reputation, clientele or qualifications and the separate absence of real evidence or allegation of substantial client prejudice remain part of this bounded record. The search's necessity, validity and actual motive are not found. Gabbert's failure to request postponement before the search supplies no waiver.

### M. No deprivation of the asserted occupational liberty on this record

**Rule and application:** Liberty to pursue a vocation is not a general constitutional guarantee that every occasion for performing professional services will remain free from governmental interruption. This search-related interruption of consultation, without a prohibition on practice, impairment of professional qualifications, or the supported consequential interference with legal practice described in the record, does not establish deprivation of Gabbert's liberty to pursue his profession. Even assuming deliberately obstructive timing, the present interruption does not become an occupational deprivation merely because the person searched is a lawyer and his client is then before the grand jury.

The rule turns on the nature and demonstrated effect of this interference, not duration alone. It establishes neither a permanent-exclusion requirement nor a mandatory showing of lost fees, damaged reputation or prejudice to a client in every case. The listed absent effects explain this record's failure to establish the asserted professional deprivation; they are not an exhaustive checklist. Purposeful interference with a professional function can present a materially different claim when supported injury to an independently protected interest is identified. No such different injury is invented here.

Roth's recognition of vocational liberty does not constitutionalize each interrupted service. Collins requires an identified underlying constitutional deprivation, while its voluntary-workplace omission holding does not itself decide affirmative interference. Albright supplies no controlling comprehensive rationale and does not impose a palpable-consequence threshold. Soldal and James Daniel Good, as preserved in the public Albright writings and West Covina, leave genuinely independent protections available. Haddle's statutory injury rule neither creates an occupational constitutional entitlement nor makes the absence of tenure dispositive here. Lewis's rapid-pursuit culpability rule is not transferred to this planned investigation.

**Strongest contrary path and limit:** Deliberately separating a lawyer from a client at the very occasion counsel is needed can burden professional activity without enduring economic loss. That is a substantial objection to a categorical temporary-interference rule. It does not establish that this record deprived Gabbert of the liberty to pursue his profession: the supplied right-to-vocation authorities protect that interest, not a personal constitutional guarantee of every client consultation, and the record supplies no additional professional impairment or substantial client prejudice. M therefore rejects this claim, while declining the proposed universal temporal or financial threshold. Constitutional deprivation and available damages remain different questions; this analysis creates no nominal-damages holding.

### S. Constitutional source selection has a confined, separate function

Graham directs use of the specific protection when it actually covers the complained-of governmental act. Challenges to the search's validity, manner or timing as an unreasonable search belong under the Fourth Amendment. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg would expressly apply that proposition to the search objection here. They would not treat the use of occupational language as a means of dispensing with the appropriate search standard.

That source proposition is not a separate sufficient ground disposing of every possible occupational injury. The Court must still distinguish an independent professional deprivation, which M does on this record. Lanier and the independent-protection authorities forbid turning S into universal Fourth Amendment displacement of due process. The existence of a pleaded Fourth Amendment claim is not proof that it succeeds or fully redresses every conceivable harm. The claim remains outside the grant.

Stevens, Souter and Breyer accept Graham's actual-coverage rule but would resolve this occupational claim through M without joining an additional holding that classifies the entire interference allegation as a Fourth Amendment claim. Their nonjoin leaves the Fourth Amendment claim open; it does not establish a competing constitutional violation. The five affirmative S positions therefore support only the stated source distinction, not an independent entire-claim dismissal or a synthesized Albright rationale.

### Q. Independent qualified-immunity disposition at the conduct date

**Rule and application:** On the plaintiff-favorable conduct reported here, the existing law did not make it apparent on March 21, 1994 that arranging this search-related interruption violated Gabbert's personal occupational-liberty right. Conn and Najera therefore are entitled to qualified immunity on that claim even if a materially broader occupational protection could be recognized. This is an independently sufficient defense to the presented damages claim, not a finding that the conduct or warrant was lawful.

Elder, decided February 23, 1994, requires consideration of all relevant contemporaneous law on the preserved issue. Roth's general vocational protection did not settle this particular application. Collins preserved the distinction between an intentional or unreasonable wrong and an identified substantive deprivation. Albright's January 1994 positions were materially divided about source and independent injury and supplied no controlling general rule against this particular interruption. Anderson's particularized apparent-unlawfulness principle does not require a factual twin, but neither a general occupational-liberty formulation nor the Ninth Circuit's common-sense characterization identifies an established personal constitutional prohibition here. The legal gap concerns the asserted right and its application, not a supposed requirement that a prior case reproduce these facts.

**Strongest contrary path and limit:** A prior rule can plainly cover deliberate obstruction even without a matching precedent, and improper purpose does not become reasonable merely because officials obtained a warrant. Those propositions defeat both a factual-twin demand and any automatic warrant defense. They do not answer the unresolved reach of the lawyer's own occupational guarantee in the absence of the professional impairment shown in the vocation cases. The conduct-time authorities leave that extension insufficiently apparent even on the adverse purpose assumption. No dispute about motive needs resolution to apply Q.

Lanier explains the present clarity method but cannot supply notice retrospectively. Lewis, West Covina and Haddle likewise supply no March 1994 warning. No absolute prosecutorial immunity is adjudicated, no state-remedy ground is invented, and no mandatory merits-first sequence is adopted. Agreement with Q permits its use as an independent alternative; it does not require adding an unnecessary published immunity holding after M disposes of the claim.
