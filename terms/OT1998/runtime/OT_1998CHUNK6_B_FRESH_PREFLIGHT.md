## 2. Murphy Brothers, Inc. v. Michetti Pipe Stringing, Inc.

### Posture and question

The district court **denied** Michetti's motion to remand. Review proceeded through 28 U.S.C. §1292(b); the Eleventh Circuit reversed that denial and directed remand on its receipt-trigger interpretation. The B packet's repeated statement that it "upheld remand" is incorrect. It must not be used to create a §1447(d) jurisdictional defect. The case reaches this Court on review of the appellate judgment arising from the certified denial, not a direct appeal from a covered district-court remand order.

The supported facts distinguish receipt of the courtesy fax of a filed complaint from later formal service. Removal occurred more than thirty days after the former and within thirty days after the latter. No waiver of service, earlier valid summons, defective later service, later-amended pleading or subsequently arising removability is found or assumed. Exact calendar dates are unnecessary to choose between the two proposed triggers because the packet establishes the relevant intervals; a ruling dependent on a different service event would need its actual record.

The statutory question is whether pre-service receipt starts this removal period. The duty to defend, personal jurisdiction, availability of removal and the start of its statutory deadline are distinct propositions. General service principles do not themselves decide what Congress meant by receipt through service or otherwise. Conversely, informative receipt does not by itself establish formal service.

### Entering law and competing paths

The complete first paragraph of event-date §1446(b), including its separate summons/filed-pleading/not-required-to-be-served branch and shorter-period proviso, is incorporated below by mechanical copying. Neither branch may be omitted in a general rule. The question concerns the original pleading; the different later-removability paragraph is not invoked on this record.

The relevant register provisions are **Service timing in Suits in Admiralty Act actions**, present operation Henderson, May 20, 1996; **Diversity cured before trial after erroneous removal**, present operation Caterpillar, December 10, 1996; and **Covered removal grounds and the bar on appellate remand review**, present operation Things Remembered, December 5, 1995, with Quackenbush's June 3, 1996 abstention clarification. Henderson distinguishes service procedure from subject-matter jurisdiction in its actual statute and preserves authorized Rule 4 timing. It does not decide §1446(b). Caterpillar preserves the error in refusing timely remand and supplies only a bounded rule for an actually jurisdictionally sound completed trial judgment. There is no such completed judgment here. The covered-remand-review bar retains its full operation without being extended to this certified denial.

Two principal interpretations remain available for modeling. A service-linked construction treats formal process or an established legal equivalent as necessary before the receipt clock can run; it must explain the words otherwise, the summons alternative and the shorter-period proviso. The receipt construction treats the faxed initial pleading as the statutory information event and must explain why that procedural deadline can run before a duty to appear arises and how its reading fits both branches. These are analytical legal alternatives, not new claims that a particular brief made unquoted arguments. Any narrower construction must identify the actual trigger it adopts and apply the established intervals.

A service-linked outcome would reverse the appellate timeliness ground and return the case for federal proceedings otherwise authorized; it would not adjudicate the contract merits. A receipt-trigger outcome would sustain the appellate remand direction on the stated timing premise. Neither outcome may rely on Caterpillar as permission to disregard the prospective deadline, or establish a universal cure for untimely removal. No equitable extension, service waiver or fees determination is supplied.

## 3. Mitchell v. United States

### Record and separable questions

The objective recitation supplies the material omitted particulars: Mitchell pleaded guilty without a plea agreement to one cocaine-conspiracy count and three school-or-playground distribution counts. She expressly reserved attribution of drug quantity under the conspiracy count. The court told her that quantity would be determined at sentencing and described waiver of the right to remain silent **at trial**. The plea colloquy included sworn responses but did not resolve the disputed sentencing quantity. The conspiracy charge's quantity allegation must not be substituted for an admission contrary to that recorded reservation.

At sentencing, cooperating codefendants adopted trial testimony, one supplied further quantity evidence, and cross-examination addressed the extent of his observations. Mitchell offered no testimony or evidence and challenged reliability through counsel. The district court expressly used her failure to contradict the codefendants as one reason to credit their account, found more than five kilograms and imposed the ten-year statutory minimum, together with the recited supervised-release term and assessment. These are published recitations of the proceeding; they do not authorize new credibility findings.

The Third Circuit's majority treated the plea as waiver notwithstanding the quantity reservation. Judge Michel separately treated any adverse-inference error as harmless because the other evidence supported quantity. That separate rationale must be assessed as such, not attributed to a unanimous lower court.

Model independently: (1) whether the plea or particular colloquy relinquished the privilege as to unresolved punishment facts; (2) if it survived, whether this criminal sentencing court could use silence as affirmative support for quantity; and (3) if the inference was impermissible, its remedial effect. The original packet's statement that preservation of the privilege itself necessarily requires resentencing collapses the second and third questions and is not an adequate neutral path statement.

### Entering law and supported alternatives

Griffin v. California supplies the pre-divergence criminal-trial prohibition on treating the defendant's silence as inculpatory proof, also preserved in the simulated Lucas holding. Estelle v. Smith supplies the sentencing setting for the privilege. Whether those principles extend to this plea and noncapital quantity determination requires analysis, not an assumed outcome. A plea's admission of guilt and waiver of trial are distinct from a claim of testimonial waiver reaching all unresolved sentencing facts. Any Brown-type scope-of-testimonial-waiver argument must address the actual colloquy, the express reservation and the proceeding in which additional testimony is sought; it cannot assume an unrestricted voluntary account absent from the recitation.

Woodard, March 25, 1998, rejects compulsion from the optional clemency interview and, through the eight Associates' additional ground, allows the assumed ordinary civil adverse inference in that distinct setting. Its treatment of Baxter and Minnesota v. Murphy requires the modeling context to confront the civil-inference argument while distinguishing criminal punishment, automatic privilege penalties and compelled criminal use. Neither Woodard nor Balsys answers the present quantity issue. Balsys, June 25, 1998, preserves domestic criminal-use protection while deciding the different independent-foreign-risk question.

Watts/Putra, January 6, 1997, and Edwards, April 28, 1998, permit reliable ordinary relevant-conduct findings within authorized punishment while retaining independent constitutional, evidentiary, Guidelines and Rule 32 limits. Their lower proof threshold does not independently authorize an inference from protected silence. Conversely, the privilege question does not itself allocate every sentencing fact to a jury or set a universal burden. Gaudin retains its actual-element rule. No future sentencing doctrine is imported.

Dunnigan, February 23, 1993, permits a mandatory adjustment after independent findings of willful material trial perjury; it does not equate silence with perjury. The register's **Advance plea-statement waivers for impeachment**, effective January 18, 1995, concerns a knowing voluntary limited agreement and expressly leaves sentencing use and universal waiver of fundamental protections undecided. **Accepted guilty pleas and deferred plea-agreement approval**, effective Hyde, May 27, 1997, and **Notice of statutory elements and collateral challenges to guilty pleas**, effective Bousley, May 18, 1998, govern their distinct plea issues and supply no unexpressed sentencing waiver here.

The October 5, 1998 Wright public holding is material current-term law: a nonfrivolous legal challenge unrelated to admitted factual guilt alone cannot defeat otherwise warranted §3E1.1 credit, and a sentence cannot be saved by an appellate alternative not established independently in the sentencing findings. Wright permits actual acceptance and remorse assessment under lawful grounds. It does **not** decide silence as proof of disputed quantity, constitutional treatment of remorse, or a general right to an adjustment. Those questions must remain separate. Peguero's March 2, 1999 collateral warning-and-causation holding supplies no general direct-appeal harmless-error test.

Lawful paths include a properly supported waiver ruling; retention of the privilege with an independent analysis of the inference; or retention and prohibition followed by a distinct prejudice/remedy inquiry. The harmfulness path must address the district court's express reliance rather than treating sufficient other evidence as automatically establishing that the inference did not contribute. The lower concurrence is the strongest supported remedial alternative. The present recitations permit a bounded legal correction and return for sentencing consideration without a new quantity finding. They do not establish a particular replacement term, plea withdrawal, automatic minimum, acquittal or automatic elimination of every other sentence component.

## 4. Conn v. Gabbert

### Record, channel and review scope

The objective recitation establishes the summary-judgment posture omitted from the short packet. Conn and Najera obtained district-court summary judgment on qualified immunity; the Ninth Circuit reversed in part on Gabbert's Fourteenth Amendment right-to-practice claim. It relied on vocation precedents and its view that the intended interference was unreasonable and clearly prohibited. Its characterizations of purpose and unreasonable conduct are the judgment-below reasoning, not findings this Court must adopt as historical truth.

The reported conduct occurred March 21, 1994. The prosecutors sought correspondence from a grand-jury witness represented by Gabbert, after unsuccessful efforts to obtain it from the witness. A warrant search of Gabbert was conducted by a court-appointed special master; Gabbert requested a private room. His client was called while the search was occurring, twice sought consultation, asserted her own Fifth Amendment privilege and the grand jury recessed. The record says Gabbert did not request postponement before the search and later expected questioning to wait. These facts inform the alleged interference; they do not themselves establish waiver, the lawfulness of the warrant, or an adjudicated motive.

Control separately confirms that a Fourth Amendment claim was pleaded but is outside the granted question. This scope qualification controls: deciding the occupational-liberty and qualified-immunity question neither validates nor adjudicates that other claim. The attorney's asserted personal right remains distinct from the client's self-incrimination or consultation rights. No standing to litigate a new client claim is assumed. The petition's exact wording and full pleadings are not independently recovered; the granted scope is supplied in substance.

### Entering law and alternative paths

Collins's February 26, 1992 voluntary-employment and pleaded-omission holdings do not themselves settle intentional interference with legal representation. Albright's January 24, 1994 judgment has no controlling rationale: the specific-source plurality, Kennedy/Thomas analysis and Souter's additional-injury approach cannot be combined. The original packet's suggestion that a profession claim necessarily requires more than temporary interference is a proposition to assess here, not a holding supplied by those precedents.

Graham remains applicable when a specific constitutional protection actually covers the claim. Lanier, March 31, 1997, preserves that rule while rejecting categorical exclusion of all other due-process injuries. Lewis, May 26, 1998, supplies an emergency-pursuit culpability rule, not a universal intent threshold for planned investigative conduct. Glucksberg's bounded assistance-in-suicide holding creates no comprehensive new methodology for all asserted liberties.

Current-term West Covina, January 13, 1999, preserves a distinct procedural-due-process inquiry in the criminal-warrant setting. It does not establish this substantive occupational right; it does prevent treating every warrant as a dispositive answer to every independent due-process interest. Haddle, December 14, 1998, concerns statutory injury under §1985(2), not a constitutional entitlement to every employment opportunity or a new occupational-liberty rule.

The register's **Relevant precedent in appellate qualified-immunity review**, effective Elder, February 23, 1994, requires examination of all relevant pre-existing law on the preserved clarity issue. That date precedes the March 21 conduct. Lanier later states the governing clarity method but may not itself supply retrospective notice; a factual twin is unnecessary, while an abstract vocational-liberty proposition or common-sense label alone does not resolve a disputed application. Constitutional scope and conduct-time clarity are separate. No mandatory merits-first sequence is established by the entering law.

The register's **Prosecutorial investigation, advocacy and press statements**, reflecting Buckley and Fletcher, requires function-specific immunity rather than immunity from prosecutorial title. It also preserves constitutional injury, causation and qualified immunity as independent matters. No new absolute-immunity disposition should be supplied without establishing its preserved basis and the actual function involved. The current **Qualified-immunity legal appeals and factual sufficiency** rule, with Clanton's December 29, 1997 clarification, reinforces use of proper assumed facts without appellate selection between disputed historical accounts; it does not turn the district court's grant here into a denial or create a new appeal.

Supported paths include rejecting the asserted occupational right as inapplicable to this temporary search-related interruption; identifying an independently protected occupational injury and assessing its actual predicates; or deciding the preserved damages claim through conduct-time qualified immunity without a broader constitutional resolution. Each path must confront the plaintiff-favorable account of purposeful interference, rather than resolve it by inventing search necessity or an undisputed benign motive. A surviving claim would still require causation, personal responsibility, proof and remedy. A rejected occupational claim would not decide the separately pleaded Fourth Amendment claim.

The Albright public writings and West Covina's Scalia/Thomas source qualification give legitimate date-eligible evidence of distinct approaches; none supplies a Conn vote. The independent model must test any actual commitment against these distinctions and the 1994 notice date rather than import an own-case historical result.

# Section 1446(b), first paragraph, at the event date

The following complete two-trigger first paragraph is mechanically copied from the saved authenticated source quotation. Direct govinfo retrieval of the 1994 section was unavailable. This is a statutory-text quotation in a published judicial source under Engine section 14, not a substituted modern statute or a source for the outcome of this case.

“The notice of removal of a civil action or proceeding shall be filed within thirty days after the receipt by the defendant, through service or otherwise, of a copy of the initial pleading setting forth the claim for relief upon which such action or proceeding is based, or within thirty days after the service of summons upon the defendant if such initial pleading has then been filed in court and is not required to be served on the defendant, whichever period is shorter.” (Emphasis added.)
