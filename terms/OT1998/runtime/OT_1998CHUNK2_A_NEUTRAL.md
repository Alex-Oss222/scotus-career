### Issue 1: Privacy interest of invited occupants

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** United States v. Fields / Crawley (February 9, 1998) recognizes Fields’s sustained paid access, key and control and Crawley’s repeated permitted sharing of a closed room as separate personal privacy grounds. It rejects automatic forfeiture through drug activity but establishes neither protection for mere presence nor a rule for every commercial visitor. Carter and Johns’s shorter visit therefore requires its own Olson/Rakas analysis; their host’s permission matters but does not alone settle the issue.
* **Other Governing Law:** Fourth Amendment; *Katz v. United States*; *Rakas v. Illinois*; *Minnesota v. Olson*.
* **Material Other Authority:** A privacy interest is personal and turns on the host-authorized relationship and use of the protected place, not on whether the activity observed was lawful.
* **Open Point:** Whether invited occupants using a deliberately shielded home for a brief private transaction possess a personal privacy interest when they are neither residents nor overnight guests.

### Issue 2: Deliberate observation through closed blinds

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Fields rejects treating limited residential visibility as general publication, but its majority did not decide that the shared yard was curtilage or that the observation was a search. United States v. Pinson (May 8, 1995) protects concealed home-operation information against the particular technological scan there; unaided observation from this window-side position presents an extension requiring analysis of lawful access and actual exposure. Neither precedent supplies a majority finding about this apartment.
* **Other Governing Law:** Fourth Amendment; plain-view and lawful-vantage principles.
* **Material Other Authority:** A residential approach does not become a general observation post merely because it is unfenced or physically accessible; the relevant question is whether the public is ordinarily entitled to occupy that position for the purpose used by police.
* **Open Point:** Whether leaving the ordinary walkway, approaching a ground-floor residential window, and using a narrow gap in closed blinds to observe the interior is ordinary public observation or deliberate acquisition from a restricted residential vantage.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Controlling Reasoning Below:** The Minnesota Supreme Court suppressed the evidence, holding that respondents could challenge the warrantless observation and resulting search.
* **Material Positions and Limits:** The State emphasizes the brief commercial drug activity and contends that the observation required no intrusion into protected space. Carter and Johns rely on invited private use, closed blinds and the officer’s departure from the public approach. Their individual relationship to the home and the legality of the actual vantage require separate analysis.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

No personal privacy interest would defeat each claimant’s own challenge. A protected interest still requires a search and an unavailable justification; an unlawful observation then requires the actual suppression and taint analysis. Fields does not supply an automatic outcome for these shorter-term guests.
