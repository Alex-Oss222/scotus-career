## Supported procedural and legal core

Both matters are properly before the Court under §1254(1). The patent immunity denial is immediately appealable under the operative Puerto Rico Aqueduct rule; the Lanham judgment is a final dismissal. Florida Prepaid's arm-of-State status is undisputed in the supplied record. All nine Justices were seated at argument and remain seated; no authorized source establishes nonparticipation. Nine participants require five votes for a judgment. This analysis supplies no Stone vote and no Court coalition.

The explicit terms of 35 U.S.C. §§271(h), 296 and 15 U.S.C. §§1122, 1125(a)(2) satisfy the congressional-clear-statement requirement. The stated remedies and effective dates matter independently. Congress's intent neither establishes constitutional power nor proves Florida's consent. Statutory inclusion, constitutional abrogation, waiver, a cause of action, and entitlement to a particular remedy are separate questions.

Seminole's six-position statutory holding controls at its actual scope. Its State-removal judgment has no controlling constitutional rationale. Union Gas's damages judgment, statutory holding and five-vote constitutional conclusion survive; Hans and Fitzpatrick survive. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas's proposed general Article I prohibition is a published position, not governing law. Stevens's public Seminole reasoning expressly leaves the textual out-of-State/foreign-plaintiff problem open. Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer's constitutional alternative was conditional and concerned nontextual Hans immunity. Neither reservation can silently become a decided Article I answer in these suits by a New Jersey bank against Florida.

Boerne permits preventive legislation covering some independently constitutional acts, but requires congruence and proportionality to the constitutional injury. No Justice may treat formal findings, a numerical inventory, proof of a violation in every State, individual violation findings for all applications, geographic restrictions, or a sunset as universally required. Conversely, the absence of those formal requirements does not eliminate the substantive relationship that Boerne demands.

## Florida Prepaid — §5 and remedy core

A patent supplies an established exclusionary property interest. Its federal creation does not remove Fourteenth Amendment protection. Infringement, however, does not necessarily establish a due-process deprivation: culpability, authorization, and available process and remedies matter under Daniels, Parratt, Hudson and Zinermon. The knowing/willful complaint allegations are allegations, not findings of a constitutional violation. The lower court's discussion of a claims bill and Jacobs Wind establishes neither an adequate remedy actually available to CSB nor its absence.

The statute-supporting position regards the measure as a bounded remedial protection of an already identified property right: nationally defined exclusive patent rights, difficulty detecting and proving purposeful appropriation, incomplete past-loss relief from an injunction, and genuine uncertainty about state compensation. Ordinary patent remedies directed at the infringing use can have a preventive relation to that constitutional risk even when individual applications need not themselves violate §1. Its strongest objection is that uniform patent enforcement is an Article I objective, not itself a §5 justification, and that strict-liability infringement plus remedies against States providing process can substantially exceed due-process injury. The supporting position must answer with the concrete enforcement relationship, not the desirability of equal commercial competition alone.

The opposing position accepts the protected property and Boerne's preventive authority but finds the statute's substantive design insufficiently fitted: full ordinary patent liability extends to innocent infringement and to States with adequate remedies without a demonstrated connection between that breadth and preventing unconstitutional deprivation. The objection is substantive overbreadth relative to the injury, not a mandatory legislative-evidence tally. Its strongest counterargument is that demanding a prior State-remedy failure for each suit would defeat prevention and impose a condition Boerne expressly rejects. The opposing position may not invent that individual prerequisite; it must rest on why this particular nationwide substitution of ordinary infringement liability lacks the necessary relation.

## College Savings Bank — property and consent core

All eight Associates would reject §5 as a sufficient justification for the particular own-product false-advertising action. CSB's business, goodwill, existing assets and legal claims can receive legal protection, but the alleged competitive loss does not identify a deprivation of one of those assets by Florida. The Lanham Act's prohibition and statutory injury do not themselves establish a Fourteenth Amendment entitlement to prospective customers or competitors' truthful self-advertising. Haddle unanimously distinguishes statutory economic injury from constitutional property; Sullivan and Logan preserve fair adjudication of an actual entitlement without turning every commercial tort into deprivation. This application does not decide trademark infringement, appropriation of trade secrets, palming off, or false statements about CSB's goods. No universal rule that property must always take the form of an exclusionary patent is adopted.

The government's filed merits brief independently supports this distinction: printed pp. 30–33 agree that these own-product allegations do not deprive a competitor of constitutional property and seek reversal through commercial consent. That is the United States' position, not a concession attributed to CSB. The strongest competing §5 argument invokes business property, unlawful interference and preventive protection; it fails here because the connection to an identified constitutional deprivation is missing. Boerne's preventive reach does not authorize Congress to supply that missing substantive right by creating a tort.
