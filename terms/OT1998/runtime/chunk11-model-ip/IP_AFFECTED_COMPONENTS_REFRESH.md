## Earlier law and the specific distinction now resolved

The clean addendum and linked public sources establish that Union Gas involved a private third-party claim against Pennsylvania after the United States sued Union Gas. Its statutory ruling specifically permits private actions; its five-vote Commerce-power conclusion combines Brennan's four-Justice rationale with White's separate constitutional assent. Neither the citizenship of Union Gas nor a United-States-only immunity exception is assumed. The express disclaimer of Parden in the opinion prevents treating Union Gas as an actual commercial-waiver holding. The federal-question/damages posture makes it a substantial authority for the present analytical alternatives without converting every plurality rationale into law.

Welch rejects the inference of federal-forum consent from general statutory coverage. Powell's four-Justice discussion quotes the Parden dissent's distinction for an express prospective condition but does not uphold such a condition. Its actual Texas express-waiver issue is outside the petition and is not reached merely because addressed in the merits brief. Scalia's separate statutory approach does not adopt Powell's full constitutional account. Simulated Hilton then preserves FELA's specialized state-court coverage, including the identified reliance, while leaving displaced the old inference that operating a railroad waives federal-court immunity. That is affirmative current law, not an empty reservation. It does not adjudicate the later Patent/Trademark Acts' explicit federal-forum provisions.

Accordingly, neither a revived general-language Parden rule nor a controlling precedent validating every explicit commercial condition is available. The present judicial choice is whether these 1992 enactments, applied prospectively to Florida's optional investment-marketing enterprise, establish a valid knowing and voluntary condition. A holding accepting that distinction would be a new, confined application and development of consent doctrine, supported by earlier sources but not already dictated by them.

The source of Congress's substantive power to regulate the interstate marketing is its Commerce Clause authority; the consent argument does not purport to obtain that power from §5 or from the existence of an exclusionary property interest. The patent condition is attached to conduct governed by the enacted patent right and Patent Clause authority. In each case the constitutional amenability claim rests on accepted conditions of a genuinely optional commercial undertaking, rather than on a holding that the relevant Article I power unilaterally abrogates immunity. That distinction itself is disputed. A statutory heading cannot decide it.

## Completed independent commercial-consent positions

**Operative formulation accepted by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer:** When Congress unmistakably and prospectively subjects a specified category of State commercial conduct to private federal-court enforcement, a State that continues a commercial enterprise it can realistically choose not to operate accepts that federal-forum condition for violations arising from the covered post-enactment activity. The condition cannot be inferred from general coverage, the breadth of the commerce power, an unavoidable sovereign function, or participation in any regulated activity. The particular investment marketing here is a realistically optional method of furthering education; treating education as a State function does not answer the distinct question whether this competitive investment enterprise must be maintained. The condition concerns undertaking the business under stated terms, not merely a supposed choice to violate the law.

This conclusion rests on the clear statutory text, its prospective operation and the supported nature of the undertaking. It does not find that Florida expressly signed a waiver, received a discretionary federal grant, or entered a contract with CSB. The general Lanham regulatory benefits are context, not a fictional federal-benefit bargain. No new fact concerning profitable operations or an unverified exit cost is necessary to the selected legal classification. Florida's admitted arm status remains intact under Hess.

**Operative objection accepted by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas:** The statutory command proves Congress's intention to impose federal liability; continuing ordinary lawful commerce does not establish the State's consent to that liability. A State's practical option to stop an enterprise does not make a generally imposed federal remedy an accepted federal benefit or actual submission to federal jurisdiction. Calling that regulatory consequence a voluntary condition would substitute statutory abrogation for the required State consent. A clear statement is necessary to the proposed theory but does not supply the missing act of consent. These Justices reject the theory at that point without requiring a categorical rule that all educational activity is essential or immune.

The strongest challenge to the first position is that it treats lawful commercial participation as acceptance of a jurisdictional term the State expressly contests. The strongest answer is the narrow prospective notice/optional-undertaking distinction recognized as an open possibility in Welch, combined with Congress's power to prescribe the terms of the regulated commercial venture. The strongest challenge to the second position is that it would make even an unmistakably stated prospective condition ineffective despite a real commercial choice. Its answer is that regulation of a preexisting lawful activity differs from accepting a specified federal benefit or invoking a court's authority. The disagreement concerns whether that conduct supplies legally effective consent; neither side can dispose of it merely by declaring the wording clear or invoking Parden's name.

## Scope decision after Murphy and the Welch supplement

Murphy's new completed scope holding reinforces the governing distinction: preservation and merits discussion do not enlarge a limited granted legal question into a separate application question. It is an application of the earlier Standard, not a new rule categorically excluding all alternative legal arguments. The IP grants were generic grants of the petitions; they were not Murphy's express grant limited to two of four questions. Their filed questions nevertheless identify the subject matter of review. A generic grant is not a grant of every legal source capable of changing the judgment.

For the patent case, X-Citement Video allows CSB to defend the existing immunity denial on a properly supported alternative without enlarging the judgment. Commercial waiver and litigation waiver were specifically renewed, and their appellate nondecision is verified. The favored §5 or commercial-consent affirmance makes direct Article I decision unnecessary for four Justices. For the other four, remand for the genuinely preserved litigation-submission issue supplies a lawful narrower disposition; it does not depend on finding that a historical filing is missing or that Article I was forfeited. The Federal Circuit should apply the actual retained law to any properly available ground rather than the displaced historical Seminole premise.

For the Lanham case, direct Article I power would supply a distinct source from either presented §5 authority or actual consent. The petitioner's requested reversal cannot invoke X-Citement Video's prevailing-respondent entitlement. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would decline to expand the grant to that distinct question: its potential usefulness, logical priority, or relation to the same Act supplies no sufficient exceptional basis under Izumi/Madsen, and no independent jurisdictional defect requires deciding it. They would affirm the reviewed dismissal on the two presented grounds without announcing that direct Article I authority is categorically unavailable under current law. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer can reverse on the expressly presented consent question and therefore leave the independent scope question undecided.

The contrary fair-inclusion argument remains substantial: both routes concern Congress's authority for the same statutory federal action, and a different legal argument can remain within a preserved claim under Lebron. The four declining extension distinguish an explicitly source-defined §5 question from a broad question whether Congress possessed any authority, and distinguish waiver from unilateral abrogation. The new source does not compel all Justices to treat those legal questions as identical. If a later proposed formulation abandons commercial consent and instead depends on Article I reversal, the four reversal positions must be rechecked for that actual scope proposition; their present constitutional alternatives cannot be aggregated into a new judgment ground without that independent reach commitment.
