**The five-Justice Court §5 portion — Stevens, Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.** The Court independently sustains §296(a) as preventive enforcement of the Fourteenth Amendment. The patent is already a defined exclusive property interest. State use can produce completed loss that a prospective injunction cannot repair; proof of intent and uncertainty about effective compensation, combined with exclusive federal patent jurisdiction, support prevention in this defined field. Boerne permits some reach beyond conduct that would independently violate the Constitution when the means fit the protected right and threatened injury. Congress did not replace the underlying constitutional property definition as RFRA replaced the governing constitutional standard. Uniformity and equal competitive treatment inform the remedial setting but do not themselves establish §5 power. It authorizes this pending action without preapproving every §296(b) fee, enhancement, injunction, or compensation award. Its §5 reasoning is the Court's independent holding in Part II.

**The four-Justice lead commercial-consent portion — Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.** Independently of §5, these four would hold that a State accepts a clear prospective federal-court condition by continuing specified commercial activity it can realistically choose not to operate. Congress has substantive Patent Clause authority over the underlying conduct; the amenability ground is acceptance of the condition, not general Article I abrogation. Florida's competitive investment products are an optional means of furthering education. Clear post-enactment terms and genuine practical choice distinguish this theory from consent inferred from general coverage. Welch did not already uphold every later condition; Hilton preserved specialized FELA state-court coverage without reviving old constructive federal-forum waiver. The four answer the absence-of-assent objection through the combination of actual enacted clarity, continuing covered activity, and genuine choice, not clarity alone. They assume no federal grant, contract, particular State-owned patent, quantified profit, or exit cost. Neither all commerce, abstract regulatory benefits, a statutory heading, nor the mere choice to infringe suffices. Statutory violation and relief remain unproved. This alternative also has four adopters: the dissenters reject it and Stone does not join it. It supplies no controlling consent doctrine.

**The five-Justice Court Article I portion — Stevens, Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.** Congress may authorize private enforcement against States when the underlying obligation and enforcement measure fall within its Article I authority and it unmistakably authorizes State exposure to suit. The Patent Clause and Necessary and Proper Clause support this federal patent remedy. Congress created a national patent system, concentrated infringement adjudication in federal court, and expressly included States in §§271(h),296. Uniform patent rights lose practical content if a State may use an invention while withholding that remedy. The Court extends Union Gas's retained constitutional conclusion into this enforcement principle; it does not treat Seminole as having already decided the extension. Seminole established clear State-action authorization but adopted no categorical Article I prohibition or overruling of Union Gas. The Eleventh Amendment's citizen-State diversity restriction does not bar this federal-question remedy, and Hans does not establish immunity Congress cannot deliberately displace here. CSB's Article I alternative defends the same immunity-denial judgment under X-Citement Video without enlarging relief. The rule remains claim-specific, requiring valid enumerated-power obligations and enforcement measures and a clear congressional choice. It depends on neither consent nor proof of an individual constitutional deprivation. Validity, infringement, causation, defenses, damages entitlement, enhancement and fees remain for ordinary adjudication; no award is made.

**The four-Justice dissent — O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas.** The dissent agrees that the statute clearly addresses States and that patents are property, but rejects the §5 and commercial-consent grounds and does not join the Article I holding. Boerne permits prevention, yet broad ordinary patent liability includes innocent conduct and circumstances with adequate process; national uniformity and a useful federal remedy alone do not establish congruent and proportional enforcement of deprivation without due process. The objection concerns substantive fit, not missing numerical findings, nationwide proof, geographic limits, or sunsets. On consent, congressional clarity establishes Congress's intent, not Florida's unequivocal submission. Continued lawful commerce differs from accepting a specified federal benefit or affirmatively invoking a court. The dissent preserves those distinct possibilities and does not pronounce all public-purpose activity immune from regulation.

The dissent would vacate and remand on limited terms.
