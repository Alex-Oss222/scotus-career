## McClellan Federal Credit Union v. Parker — December 9, 1998

**Validated posture and facts.** The Ninth Circuit opinion, 139 F.3d 668 (March 17, 1998), was read in full from the Caselaw Access Project reporter text, saved at `../runtime/sources_c/parker.html` and `../runtime/sources_c/parker.txt`. Parker filed Chapter 7 on April 28, 1995, without counsel. The car debt was $9,977.56 and the reported car value $9,000–$10,000. His unsecured credit-card debt was $1,986.50. The signed proposed reaffirmation covered the car debt and $1,500 of the unsecured debt; the creditor reduced the car payment from $308.73 to $280 and offered the described membership and future-credit treatment. The bankruptcy court itself convened the approval hearing; the lender attended and urged approval. Refusal of approval was followed by discharge including these debts.

The BAP dismissed for lack of appellate standing. The Ninth Circuit held the credit union pecuniarily aggrieved because denial removed the proposed enforceable personal and unsecured obligations, then independently reviewed and affirmed the bankruptcy court. It held that if continued-payment retention was legally available, refusal of this agreement was within the bankruptcy court's discretion. Standing therefore does not require or establish approval. No default judgment or absence-of-briefing consequence is inferred from Parker's lack of an appellate appearance.

**Complete legal qualifications.** The 1994 Code text of §521(2), retrieved from govinfo and saved as `../runtime/sources_c/section521.html` and `.txt`, covers scheduled consumer debts secured by property of the estate. Filing the intention is due within thirty days of filing or by the creditors' meeting, whichever is earlier, with a court-granted for-cause extension fixed within that period. Performance is due within forty-five days of the notice, with a for-cause extension fixed within those forty-five days. The mandatory duties and those extensions must not disappear from a holding that rejects exclusive substantive options. Subparagraph (C) preserves the debtor's and trustee's rights under the title. It does not itself cancel a creditor's lien.

The separately verified §524(c) conditions include pre-discharge agreement, prescribed rescission and voluntary-agreement notices, filing and applicable attorney certification, no timely rescission, compliance with subsection (d), and, for an unrepresented individual, judicial approval as both not imposing undue hardship and in the debtor's best interest. The §524(c)(6)(B) exception removes that approval requirement to the extent the debt is a consumer debt secured by real property; Parker's automobile and unsecured credit-card debt do not fall within it. Section 524(f) permits voluntary repayment; permission is not restored enforceable personal liability. Reaffirmation is not court approval merely because both parties signed.

**Scope and live alternatives.** One construction treats exemption/redemption/reaffirmation as the exclusive retained-property paths, with subsection (C) preserving rights only within that prescribed procedure. The other reads 'if applicable' and (C) to require disclosure/performance without adding a substantive foreclosure or reaffirmation command. The latter leaves lien enforcement and actual nonbankruptcy defaults intact. Relevant published simulated authorities are Dewsnup (January 15, 1992), Nobelman (June 1, 1993), BFP (May 23, 1994), Patterson (June 15, 1992), Taylor (April 21, 1992), and Wolas (December 11, 1991), each at its particular statutory scope; Ron Pair, Butner and Johnson supply surviving background.

**Source limits.** The opinion does not reproduce the underlying security agreement or decide an independently asserted payment, insurance, impairment or bankruptcy-default clause. The reviewed statutory question can be resolved without inventing such a clause or invalidating all bankruptcy clauses. Continued possession remains subject to an actual lawful default, valid lien, the Code's stay/discharge rules and ordinary remedies. No relief requiring construction of an unseen contract term is ready for adjudication. The merits grant and December 9 calendar are the supplied simulation premise, not imported historical petition treatment.

Primary reprints: [Ninth Circuit opinion](https://static.case.law/f3d/139/html/0668-01.html); [1994 §521](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title11/html/USCODE-1994-title11-chap5-subchapII-sec521.htm); [1994 §524](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title11/html/USCODE-1994-title11-chap5-subchapII-sec524.htm). ## Haddle v. Garrison — December 14, 1998

**Validated issue boundary.** The checked neutral packet supplies the alleged retaliatory discharge, the at-will employment relation, the Rule 12 dismissal on constitutional-property grounds and the Eleventh Circuit affirmance, 132 F.3d 46. Those are sufficient for the admitted statutory injury question; this preflight does not claim to have recovered the unpublished appellate reasoning or complaint. The conspiracy, prohibited federal-witness purpose, acts, actual causation and damages remain allegations or remaining elements.

**Text and authority.** The first portion of §1985(2) protects federal parties and witnesses from specified intimidation or injury connected with attendance or testimony. The civil-remedy language at the end of §1985(3) applies to conspiracies throughout the section and separates injury to person or property from deprivation of a federal right or privilege. It must not be reduced to the Fourteenth Amendment entitlement inquiry. Kush v. Rutledge, 460 U.S. 719, especially 724–727 and footnotes 4, 5 and 9, was read in full as a pre-divergence primary opinion: it distinguishes federal judicial-process protection from the clauses requiring equal-protection purpose and expressly leaves recoverable damages for its own case open. Kush supplies statutory structure, not an already-decided answer to Haddle's injury question. Its appendix and footnote 4 reproduce the enacted language. The current LII Code reprint was used only to cross-check that same language and its R.S. §1980 lineage, not as a source of later law.

Surviving Truax v. Raich, 239 U.S. 33, supplies the distinction between an employer's freedom to end an at-will relation and the employee's protection from wrongful third-party interference. Roth's due-process property test answers a different question. Simulated Darden (March 24, 1992) supplies contextual use of common-law terms; Bray (January 13, 1993) preserves provision-specific private-right requirements without transferring them to a different federal-process clause; Robinson (February 18, 1997) and Inter-Modal (May 12, 1997) distinguish statutory protection from permanent employment or vested-benefit entitlement.

**Alternatives and limits.** Haddle's path treats the lost employment and wages caused by prohibited interference as statutory injury even though the employer could lawfully terminate the relation. Respondents' path imports constitutional entitlement as the property threshold. Resolving that fork need not determine conspiracy among agents of one corporation, independent third-party status, every witness-definition issue, damages amount, reinstatement, punitive damages, or the truth of any allegation. Those issues are not resolved by calling the defendants 'third parties.'

## NYNEX Corp. v. Discon — December 14, 1998

**Lower-court verification and scope correction.** The Second Circuit opinion, 93 F.3d 1055 (August 26, 1996), was read in full using both OpenJurist and the saved CAP primary reporter reprint (`../runtime/sources_c/discon.html` and `.txt`). The appellate court did not order application of a per se rule. It allowed the §1 claim under at least the rule of reason and possibly the per se boycott category, expressly reserving that classification for remand. It also reinstated a separate §2 conspiracy-to-monopolize claim. It affirmed dismissal of the vertical price-fixing claim, §2 monopolization and attempt claims, and all three RICO theories. Those distinct components must not be described as a single surviving per se cause of action or as all antitrust claims dismissed.

MECo acts as purchasing intermediary for affiliated NYTel; it does not perform removal services or compete in their overall market. The lower court rejected the proposed horizontal characterization based solely on MECo's separate incorporation and resale to its affiliate. AT&T Technologies, the competitor of Discon, is alleged as a conspirator but was not named a defendant. At least another telephone company, AT&T Communications, is identified as purchasing removal services in the relevant geographic area. Allegations of inflated purchases, secret rebates, preferential treatment, false information and economic duress remain allegations; a separate FCC settlement included no admission of wrongdoing. No horizontal supplier agreement, closed market or monopoly is found by this Court.

**Law and alternatives.** Klor's, Business Electronics, Northwest Wholesale Stationers and Indiana Federation of Dentists supply the established-category and competitive-effects distinctions; the simultaneous presence of a vertical agreement and an exclusionary purpose does not by itself establish the horizontal coordination of the classic group boycott. Simulated Khan (November 4, 1997), unanimous, applies rule-of-reason treatment to the distinct vertical maximum-price category and retains horizontal and genuine price-agreement distinctions. Kodak (June 8, 1992) rejects unsupported categorical assumptions about competitive constraints; Spectrum Sports (January 25, 1993) requires actual elements for attempt and does not merge attempt with conspiracy. Brunswick and Atlantic Richfield preserve antitrust injury.

The competing paths are (a) per se treatment whenever a two-firm refusal has an asserted anticompetitive purpose and no apparent efficient explanation, and (b) requiring harm to the competitive process under the rule of reason outside an established per se category. Fraudulent regulatory overcharges can injure consumers without that injury resulting from diminished competition in removal services. That distinction neither immunizes fraud nor establishes that no adequately supported competitive-effects claim could exist. A remand rejecting per se treatment must preserve proper examination of the remaining §1 theory and the distinct §2 conspiracy claim within the grant; it does not reinstate the dismissed RICO, vertical-price-fixing, monopolization or attempt claims, or decide their correctness outside the question presented.

Sources: [Second Circuit primary reporter text](https://static.case.law/f3d/93/html/1055-01.html), [independent reprint](https://openjurist.org/93/f3d/1055). The surviving §2 claim is established by Part II.C and the concluding mandate, with footnotes 5 and 6 preserving the price and per se distinctions.

## Calderon v. Coleman — December 14, 1998

**Actual review and instruction.** The Ninth Circuit's July 28, 1998 decision, 150 F.3d 1105, affirms denial of conviction relief and grant of sentence relief. Its penalty Part III is saved in full in `../runtime/sources_c/coleman.html` and `.txt`. The inaccurate description of gubernatorial power is followed by an instruction not to consider commutation or speculate about it. Because Coleman had multiple felony convictions, commutation required consultation with the Board of Prison Terms (whose recommendation was not binding) and approval of four California Supreme Court Justices. The appellate court regarded omission of those limitations as materially misleading. This preflight makes no final constitutional-error holding.

**Harmlessness point established from the actual source.** The State expressly argued lack of a substantial and injurious effect. The Ninth Circuit answered that argument by applying Boyde's reasonable-likelihood test for whether the jury used the instruction to prevent consideration of relevant evidence. It did not separately assess, under the governing harmlessness burden and against the whole penalty record, whether an established error contributed to the actual sentence. It cannot accurately be said that the Ninth Circuit ignored the State's argument altogether; the issue is whether its answer performs the distinct required inquiry.

**State record and counterargument.** People v. Coleman, 46 Cal.3d 749, 761–763 and 780–782 (1988), was read for the penalty evidence, state treatment of this instruction and material qualifications; saved at `../runtime/sources_c/coleman_state.html` and `.txt`. The state court found state constitutional Briggs error but regarded the corrective direction as eliminating prejudice under any standard. The full corrective direction also told jurors to consider only the previously listed aggravating and mitigating factors. Approximately an hour after retiring, jurors requested rereading of the instructions; the reported request was general, not a specific commutation inquiry, and the instructions including the corrective portion were reread. This state assessment supplies the strongest harmlessness argument; it does not make the federal constitutional accuracy issue disappear.

The reported aggravation included prior sexual and violent offenses, prison assaults and a weapon; mitigation included psychiatric evidence of depression with possible schizophrenia (with conscious control and no psychosis in the report), a violent childhood and family losses, and evidence of religious participation, work and respectful conduct. These reported facts make a whole-record assessment possible for the receiving courts. They are not new federal fact findings and do not conclusively establish harmlessness or prejudice. The present source set does not include the full trial transcript or the final district-court harmlessness analysis; no direct present appellate harmlessness finding should be invented from selected facts.
