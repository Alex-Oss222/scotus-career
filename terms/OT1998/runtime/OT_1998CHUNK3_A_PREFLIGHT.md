## City of Cincinnati v. Kruse — No. 98-454

### Event, record and review scope

Merits consideration after the supplied grant, December 16, 1998. That is the approved scheduling premise, not a historical argument date. The question is whether Cincinnati may impose its mandatory candidate-campaign expenditure ceiling on the interests presented, including officeholder fundraising time and public confidence. The City seeks reversal of the Sixth Circuit's April 27, 1998 judgment; rehearing was denied June 18. No Supreme Court argument date or verbatim grant text is established.

The reviewed judgment is *Kruse v. City of Cincinnati*, 142 F.3d 907 (6th Cir. 1998), Nos. 97-3193, 97-3194 and 97-3210. Ordinance 240-1995 caps a council candidate's or candidate committee's election-cycle expenditures at three times the office's annual compensation, then approximately $140,000. The definition covers contribution or candidate/immediate-family funds used for election or defeat advocacy. It expressly excludes independent expenditures, uncompensated volunteer services, and the listed party slate/sample-ballot publications identifying all endorsed candidates. **The Neutral packet's reference to covered independent spending must not be used:** it is candidate-campaign spending that is capped; independent expenditures are expressly excluded.

Separate contribution and disclosure provisions were enacted in November 1995 and retained with minor amendments in 1997. They are not the challenged expenditure ceiling. The lower opinion reports $1,000 individual, $2,500 PAC/committee and $10,000 party contribution ceilings, no limit on personal funds contributed to one's own campaign, and no independent-expenditure ceiling. Those background provisions receive no independent constitutional judgment here.

The challengers include a losing 1995 candidate who spent more than the cap, his committee, contributors and a potential candidate. They brought a facial challenge. Enforcement was preliminarily enjoined by agreement during the litigation. For their summary-judgment motion, challengers accepted the City's asserted facts as true. The district court nevertheless found the ceiling unconstitutional, and the Sixth Circuit affirmed. The Court must distinguish that assumption from a trial finding or a finding that each asserted interest has been proved compelling.

The City's submissions included a study of financing, a public-opinion survey, officeholder affidavits and accounts that meaningful campaigns could be conducted under the ceiling. The reported financing evidence concerned the 1995 campaign, before the new contribution limits operated; the survey ran in 1996. The appellate majority emphasized the absence of experience demonstrating local contribution limits' inadequacy. Judge Cohn concurred in affirmance but would not declare officeholder-time and democratic-confidence interests categorically insufficient on a better record. His separate position is a material lower-court alternative, not controlling Supreme Court law.

### Entering law and material dependency

*Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1, retains the distinction between contributions and expenditures, the demanding review of direct restrictions on political communication, and its rejection of campaign-cost and equalization justifications for the candidate ceilings there. *NCPAC*, 470 U.S. 480, protects independent advocacy. The completed *Colorado Republican* decision protects its genuinely independent party advertisement and rejects party identity as a substitute for a statutory candidate connection. It supplies neither a universal immunity for conduits nor a majority ruling on every coordinated expenditure. *Kentucky Right to Life v. Stengel*, September 14, 1998, continues the actual-transaction distinction and fitted contribution rules; candidate cash is different from an association's expression.

The First and Fourteenth Amendments apply to the municipal ceiling. *Austin*, 494 U.S. 652, remains confined to its distinct corporate-treasury rationale; it does not itself establish a power to cap all candidates' spending. Voluntary public-financing conditions require their own actual terms and remain distinct from this compulsory ceiling.

**Current-term dependency:** The lower opinion's footnote 17 invokes Ninth Circuit *NAACP v. Jones* merits concerning a wealth primary. This Court's November 5, 1998 *NAACP* judgment vacated that merits judgment for an actual standing determination and leaves both financing and optional-statement merits open. The vacated lower merits may not be imported as a controlling or completed simulated holding. *NAACP* preserves ordinary Article III requirements; it does not establish a new defect in these different candidate/contributor claims.

### Supported opposing positions and lawful paths

The City and supporting intervenors seek recognition of the actual local burden of fundraising, public alienation and unequal access; supporting amici below included the Brennan Center and 33 States plus Guam. The challengers invoke the direct quantity restriction, Buckley's candidate-spending rule, the availability of contribution/disclosure enforcement, and the distinction between private resource differences and government barriers. These are source-supported positions; the following refinements are neutral analytical possibilities, not invented filings.

1. Affirm under the continuing candidate-expenditure rule and the inadequate fit of this universal cap to corruption, officeholder duties or public confidence. The strongest contrary argument is that fundraising's displacement of official work is an institutionally different harm, not merely dislike of expensive speech. That argument must receive an answer; the summary-judgment assumption cannot be converted into rejection of the City's evidence as false.
2. Affirm on the narrower record-specific ground reflected in Cohn's concurrence, reserving whether distinct interests could justify a different mandatory system on a developed record. The contrary argument is that such a reservation obscures Buckley's legal rejection of substantially the same cap and interests. A narrow rationale must still identify the governing rule and the defect decisive here.
3. If a Justice regards the distinct officeholder-function or confidence interest as legally cognizable beyond Buckley's rejected justifications, assess whether the accepted facts actually establish necessity and fit; reversal or a bounded remand would require that substantive conclusion or an identified error in the governing inquiry. It cannot rest merely on local experimentation, on proof that $140,000 permits some meaningful campaigning, or on treating candidate spending as a contribution. Any displacement of Buckley's candidate rule needs express justification; neither the packet nor a lower concurrence supplies that conclusion automatically.

The remedy follows the ceiling's validity and actual judgment. No path decides the separate contribution rules, institutes public financing, orders damages or creates a replacement ceiling. ## Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation, Inc. — No. 97-930

### Event, review scope and separable requirements

Merits consideration January 12, 1999; argued October 14, 1998. Colorado seeks reversal of the adverse portions of *American Constitutional Law Foundation v. Meyer*, 120 F.3d 1092 (10th Cir. 1997), Nos. 94-1576 and 94-1581, decided July 28, 1997. The [control objective-facts supplement](OT_1998CHUNK3_CONTROL_OBJECTIVE_FACTS.md) identifies the reviewed components as registration, the personal-name badge and individual paid-circulator identification/payment disclosure. It supplies objective source recitations only.

The original lower litigation also covered age eighteen, six months to circulate, the affidavit, vagueness, the safety clause and a Ninth Amendment argument. The lower court sustained those other challenged requirements. This supplied review does not reopen them. Residency's validity is outside the reviewed questions; the lower court assumed its possible legitimacy without deciding it. Do not convert a hypothetical narrower residency rule into a judicially enacted replacement condition.

- **Registration:** Section 1-40-112(1) required a circulator to be a registered elector and at least eighteen. The lower court invalidated the registration part while sustaining the age part. Colorado acknowledged at least 400,000 qualified but unregistered voters; excluding them reduces the pool of speakers. The State invoked resident traceability, subpoena reach and prosecution. The lower court held registration overinclusive relative to residence because many residents remain unregistered.
- **Personal-name badge:** Section 1-40-112(2)(a) prescribed a volunteer badge with the circulator's name; subsection (b) prescribed a paid badge with the name and the employer's name/telephone. These separate elements must not be merged. The lower court invalidated the badge requirement because of compelled personal identification during advocacy and expressly did not decide whether paid/volunteer status or employer disclosure would be valid standing alone. The reviewed personal-name issue therefore supplies no automatic judgment on those reserved elements.
- **Final individual disclosure:** Section 1-40-121(1) required, upon filing the petition, paid circulators' name, address, county of registration, rate per signature and total paid to each; payment counts as an expenditure under the separate campaign-reform article. The lower court's remedy removed individual-identifying information and described retained amount-per-signature/aggregate-spending disclosure. Individual compensation must be kept distinct from aggregate spending and from identity of proponents.
- **Monthly individual disclosure:** Section 1-40-121(2) required monthly reports ten days after month-end when paid circulation occurred, including proponents, circulators' residential/business addresses, measure identity and each circulator's paid/owed amounts. The Tenth Circuit affirmed invalidation of subsection (2) in its entirety. Control has checked the petition question: the supplied reviewed reporting issue concerns amounts paid and circulator identity, not an independent challenge to sponsor/measure monthly fields. Reserve the independent validity of those other monthly prescriptions; do not infer their conclusive reinstatement. Do not erase the distinction by saying simply that all reporting is invalid.

The complete source-recited provisions and the lower court's express badge reservation are mechanically preserved in [PROVISIONS.md](../sources/chunk3-neutral-a/PROVISIONS.md). The reported affidavit includes the circulator's identity and residential address, sworn circulation/signature assurances and anti-payment assurance. Its continued availability differs in timing and public accessibility from a badge worn during solicitation. Its registration recital cannot retain an independently invalidated registration condition merely because the affidavit as a verification mechanism survived.

### Entering law, facts and competing legal grounds

*Meyer v. Grant*, 486 U.S. 414 (1988), protects petition circulation as interactive political advocacy and treats restriction of the available speakers as a speech burden. It does not remove all neutral petition administration. The *Anderson–Burdick* inquiry measures actual burden against precise interests and necessary fit; severe burdens require narrow tailoring to a compelling interest, whereas reasonable nondiscriminatory administrative burdens can receive less demanding review. The completed *Timmons* ruling concerns a distinct candidate-nomination system, not a rule that every petition-speech restriction is minor.

*McIntyre*'s April 19, 1995 controlling rule requires exacting scrutiny—narrow fit to an overriding interest—for compelled attribution on independent individual political leaflets; electoral subject matter alone does not lower scrutiny. Its reserved candidate-finance and organizational settings remain distinct. The live-solicitation badge requires an application of that rule to a different medium, not an assertion that McIntyre already adjudicated this statute.

*Kentucky Right to Life*, September 14, 1998, upheld the bounded facial payer-identification challenge in paid candidate-finance/organizational advocacy while preserving ordinary issue leaflets, materially different individual applications and demonstrated reprisal claims. That decision is a necessary comparison for identifying the actual financer versus every individual messenger. *Buckley v. Valeo* retains finance-disclosure interests and protections against demonstrated associational harm; it does not automatically equate a referendum circulator with a candidate contributor. *Talley*, *Bellotti*, *Citizens Against Rent Control* and *Riley* supply the distinct anonymity, issue-advocacy, collective-expression and paid-speaker principles at their actual scopes.

The lower trial findings, recited on appeal, include reluctance to circulate while wearing personal identification, especially on unpopular issues, and a resulting chilling burden. The lower court distinguished the greater immediacy and accessibility of a badge from later affidavit disclosure. Those are reported findings, not findings that fraud never occurs or that every disclosure produces harassment. Colorado's own affidavit, warning, criminal-fraud, protest and petition-voiding mechanisms matter to fit. Their existence is not a conclusive finding that they prevent every abuse.

### Lawful component paths and strongest objections

**Registration.** One path treats the exclusion of a large class of otherwise qualified speakers as severe and invalidates registration because address/affidavit and enforcement mechanisms target traceability more closely. Its strongest objection is that registration supplies a usable, administrable identification connection in the formal process of qualifying measures, and enrollment is accessible. The opposing path sustains the requirement as a proportionate eligibility/enforcement condition or seeks an inquiry into actual burden; it must answer the admitted speaker reduction and the fit gap between residence and voter registration. Age and the residence question cannot be decided as substitutes.

**Personal name during advocacy.** One path applies Meyer and McIntyre to invalidate compelled contemporaneous personal attribution while preserving independently lawful later verification and sponsor information. The strongest objection is the circulator's direct role in procuring legally operative signatures, with witnesses needing a reliable way to identify fraud. A sustaining or narrower-remand path must connect that added safeguard to this actual record and explain why affidavit/public-record access and targeted penalties do not adequately address the interest. Neither path decides paid status and employer information standing alone or establishes anonymity from enforcement after fraudulent conduct.

**Individual final/monthly disclosures.** One path finds insufficient fit between identifying every paid messenger and informing voters about financing or grassroots support, particularly without a monetary threshold and with retained sponsor/aggregate information. Monthly publication adds timing-related exposure; a final report raises the distinct later-disclosure question. The strongest opposing path treats compensation and payees as relevant finance/enforcement information despite the absence of a candidate recipient, differentiating public reports from personal badges. It must assess necessity, actual individual burden and the available sponsor/aggregate alternative without presuming that all referendum disclosures are forbidden. A Justice may distinguish final from monthly disclosure or identifying from aggregate information, but each proposed boundary needs the enacted text and actual review scope.

Relief must identify each severable requirement and the corresponding lower judgment. No replacement initiative code, new registration mechanism, blanket immunity from fraud enforcement, destruction of existing public records or damages award follows. An unresolved scope point can be reserved or remanded at its actual bounds; it is not a basis to decide unreviewed age, affidavit, duration or badge-status provisions.
