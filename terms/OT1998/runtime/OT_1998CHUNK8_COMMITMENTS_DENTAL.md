## Statutory coverage: shared operative proposition

Section 4's corporation definition reaches a nonprofit professional association organized to conduct substantial activities that serve its members' commercial profit, even when the association distributes no profits to itself. CDA's substantial business assistance and commercially directed membership activities place it within that definition; a nonprofit or tax designation does not remove the express members'-profit branch.

The business services, commercial subsidiary benefits and member-practice assistance supply the connection here. The proposition is not that every incidental financial benefit to a charitable or educational organization's participants establishes FTC coverage. The association's organization and substantial activities must satisfy the statutory business-and-profit terms. No asserted sector exception removes CDA, and no state-action immunity follows from a guideline's account of California advertising law. Coverage permits the Commission's competition inquiry; it does not establish a restraint's illegality.

**Competing path and strongest objection:** CDA's separate nonprofit identity and the outside suppliers' role could support excluding an association whose own activity is genuinely charitable or educational, with only incidental member benefit. The stronger textual objection to exclusion here is Congress's express inclusion of business for members' profit, which would lose substantial work if only the association's own earnings counted. Conversely, the FTC's broadest economic-benefit theory would sweep too far. The adopted substantial commercial-activity application rejects both shortcuts without attempting a comprehensive rule for every nonprofit.

Each Justice accepts this construction directly from §§44–45 and the validated activities. The shared joins in Khan and NYNEX concern competitive analysis, not an antecedent holding already resolving nonprofit coverage; no such holding is assumed.

## Competing rule-of-reason paths

### Path R: further inquiry before either condemnation

O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter would hold that the abbreviated inquiry used below is inadequate for both challenged categories. Abbreviated analysis is lawful when the restraint and record make its harmful competitive tendency sufficiently clear to resolve the material competitive explanation without fuller market inquiry. Here, the potential for disclosure and substantiation rules to improve the reliability of professional-service information requires closer analysis than the lower court's inference from restricted advertising to reduced competition.

**Price:** The actual prohibitions on fee descriptions and the practically prohibitive disclosure conditions for broad discounts are serious restraints and may be unlawful. But suppressing an advertisement does not invariably reduce useful price information: a discount lacking an intelligible baseline or adequate scope may distort comparison. The record's enforcement findings must be retained and the claimed information benefit tested against those practices. The defect is the sufficiency of the competitive analysis, not a judicial finding that every advertisement was deceptive or every CDA condition useful.

**Quality:** Suppression of quality claims without examining their truth is likewise substantial adverse evidence. Yet information that is difficult for patients to verify can operate differently from a withheld, independently useful service. Whether the challenged restrictions improve reliable comparison, suppress useful rivalry, or do both requires more than equating fewer advertisements with fewer dental services. These four do not assume every quality claim false or valueless and do not excuse a blanket restriction simply because its facial rule invokes ethics.

**Strongest counterargument and answer:** The Commission and court below found actual suppression of truthful price and quality information; Indiana Federation of Dentists permits a direct assessment without an exhaustive market definition, and professional status supplies no exemption. Those propositions remain valid. But the rule of reason still must distinguish a restriction on reliable comparison from a measure plausibly improving it. Unlike a naked refusal to supply a desired service whose usefulness is established, these rules bear on the reliability and conditions of the offered information. The explanation has not been sufficiently examined in relation to these particular practices. Deference to supported findings does not settle the independent legal sufficiency of the abbreviated analysis.

**Remedy:** Vacate the enforcement judgment and return the matter to the Ninth Circuit for further proceedings consistent with the required inquiry, including Commission proceedings as appropriate. Retain supported findings under §45(c). Do not require a maximally elaborate market study in every case, automatically reinstate the ALJ's no-market-power conclusion, set a local share threshold, find CDA reasonable, or prescribe a new administrative evidentiary hearing. The needed analysis must fit each actual restraint. Statutory coverage remains resolved.

### Path A: the existing analysis is sufficient for both categories

Stevens, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer would sustain the existing enforcement judgment under the abbreviated rule of reason. The actual enforcement, not the facial ethical aspiration, establishes collective suppression of useful truthful competition. A sufficiently supported direct showing that competitors suppressed a basic means of comparing their services may establish unreasonable restraint without measuring every local market or proving a separate decline in the number of dental procedures.

**Price:** Preventing dentists from advertising truthful low-price descriptions and making broad discount advertising practically unavailable obstructs customers' comparison and dentists' price rivalry. Truthful discounts can convey useful information even without printing the undiscounted charge for every covered service. These four accept genuine disclosure conditions but find that the supported enforcement goes beyond that justification. This does not approve deception, require all discounts to be advertised or condemn every disclosure obligation.

**Quality:** The relevant restraint suppresses quality information without examining whether the particular claim is false or misleading. Dentistry's verification problems justify attention to false claims and inadequate substantiation; they do not establish a competitive benefit from suppressing the supported class of useful truthful information. The inference concerns competition in communicating services and attracting patients, not an invented measured-output finding. The challenged discontinued fear-allaying policy is not revived as a current restraint.

**Strongest counterargument and answer:** Professional-information defects can make restrictions beneficial; statewide membership does not establish each local share, and the ALJ and Commission differed on market power. These four regard those cautions as insufficient to displace the concrete enforcement findings and the competitive mechanism demonstrated here. The asserted justification must answer the conduct found, and the existing inquiry sufficiently shows that the restraint exceeds prevention of deception. They rely on direct competitive effects rather than turning 75% statewide membership into a finding about every locality. This is an application of the rule of reason, not per se condemnation of professional rules.

**Remedy:** Affirm the reviewed enforcement judgment on the existing alternative rule-of-reason ground. No new order term, sanction, damages award, state-law requirement or ban on legitimate antideception enforcement is supplied. The original administrative order has not been independently quoted; the commitment sustains the enforcement judgment described in the validated packet without drafting additional restrictions.
