## 2. Common legal alternatives and their strongest objections

### Limited status to review the rejected settlement objections

For a shareholder who appears under the Rule 23.1 settlement notice and actually presents objections which the district court hears and rejects when approving disposition of the corporate claim, that recognized participation supplies status for the limited purpose of appealing that disposition. A separate intervention motion is unnecessary for that limited appeal. Financial interest, receipt of notice, or shareholder status without appearance does not suffice. The rule does not make the objector a plaintiff controlling prosecution, authorize new claims, dispense with ordinary preservation and appeal requirements, or guarantee success on the objections.

The legal source is the recognized representative-procedure role, construed with the ordinary appellate framework, rather than a free-standing equitable exception for any interested person. Rule 23.1 is not described as expressly granting an appeal. Section 1291's final-judgment function and Appellate Rule 3(c)'s identification function do not independently answer which participants have that limited status.

Stevens's *Tryforos*, 518 F.2d 1258, Part I and n.22, is direct support: it separates objecting to disposition from undertaking prosecution and expressly includes noticed shareholder objections to settlements. Its actual claim concerned the form of dismissal, but the settlement proposition cannot be dismissed without addressing its stated scope. *Johnson*, 289 U.S. 479, 495–496, supplies an older analogy connecting an invitation, appearance, objections and appeal; its immediate discussion of Equity Rule 37 and its collateral-attack setting prevent treating it as a holding that modern formal intervention is always unnecessary.

The strongest objection is *Marino*, 484 U.S. 301, 303–304: the officers actually objected at the settlement hearing and nevertheless could not appeal without intervention. Thus hearing participation alone cannot carry the argument. The limited-status path distinguishes the shareholders' recognized role in disposition of the represented corporate claim from outsiders asserting separate personal interests in another action. Whether that distinction justifies a limited appellate role is the contested legal conclusion, not a fact resolved by the word "representative." *Izumi* supplies a genuine status boundary but does not decide this claimed Rule 23.1 route.

**Available disposition:** Reverse the appellate dismissal on its status ground and remand for further appellate proceedings within the limited role and ordinary applicable requirements. This orders no invalidation of the settlement, award of fees, factual hearing, discovery, or favorable appellate decision. A genuinely distinct fee challenge remains subject to its own connection to the settlement objections, preservation, and lawful basis; the status holding does not certify those predicates.

### Intervention required to appeal

A shareholder may appear and object under Rule 23.1 without acquiring the party status required to appeal the settlement judgment. A shareholder who is otherwise not a party must properly become one through intervention before taking that appeal. The opportunity to object assists the district court's supervision of the corporate claim; it does not necessarily give every heard objector authority to continue that litigation in the appellate court. An actual denial of intervention is reviewable. Nothing here decides the entitlement, timing, or outcome of an application that was never made.

*Marino* supplies the governing general rule and a concrete instance of heard settlement objectors who nevertheless lacked appellate status. *Izumi* reinforces the distinction between a financial stake, party status, and the route for reviewing exclusion. Rule 24 supplies the intervention procedure; this ground does not claim that Rule 3(c)'s reference to parties alone defines all party status or that §1291 expressly requires formal named-party status in every possible proceeding.

The strongest objection is that Rule 23.1 recognizes a particular objector role concerning the corporate claim itself, and *Tryforos* expressly allowed the appeal without formal party admission. The intervention path answers that objection by treating district-court objection and appellate control as distinct procedural functions, with *Marino* governing the latter. It rejects *Tryforos*'s settlement-appeal proposition to the extent inconsistent with that construction; it cannot honestly dismiss the proposition as absent from *Tryforos* or solely about notice defects. *Johnson* remains consistent with intervention because page 496 discusses that route, but it does not independently establish a mandatory formal-motion rule for modern derivative settlements.

**Available disposition:** Affirm the appellate dismissal on this status ground. The settlement remains undisturbed as a consequence of dismissal; the Court does not approve its fairness or compensation terms. This disposition does not establish that an independent personal claim is precluded or that any collateral attack is available.

### Limits binding both paths

*Richards* and the retained *Matsushita* standard require a constitutionally sufficient representative or other recognized relationship before absent persons' own rights may be bound. Notice and shared interests alone are insufficient; objection opportunities do not cure inadequate representation. Neither that protection nor the corporation's ownership of this derivative claim independently answers appellate status. Requiring intervention to take this appeal is not a general duty to intervene to preserve an otherwise independent claim; allowing this appeal is not a constitutional judgment that this settlement binds or does not bind every shareholder.

The operative standards are **Intervention denial and statutory party status for Supreme Court review** (Izumi, November 30, 1993); **Presented questions, subsidiary arguments and exceptional Supreme Court review** (Izumi's retained independent-question rule, supplemented by X-Citement Video, November 29, 1994, and Lebron, February 21, 1995, for their distinct components); and **Constitutional authority to bind absent litigants** (Richards, June 10, 1996, retaining Matsushita's February 27, 1996 representation component). Their functions remain separate.

Harper, June 18, 1993, bars selective prospectivity within its applied-rule trigger. It does not establish that every conceivable prospective disposition is forbidden or that Chevron Oil was wholly overruled. Conversely, the reservation creates no entitlement to a special transition here. The main status question can be resolved without deciding an independently granted pure-prospectivity question or designing an unsupported intervention remedy. The lower court's broader temporal statements need not be adopted to affirm its status judgment.
