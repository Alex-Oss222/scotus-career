## 1. Verified comparator and its limits

The complete historical Mitchell syllabus, Kennedy opinion, Scalia dissent and Thomas dissent were read from the saved Cornell texts; the entire official report, 526 U.S. 314–343, including the reporter's note and every opinion note, was read as a cross-check. Historically the judgment was reversed and remanded. Kennedy's opinion was joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Scalia's dissent was joined by Rehnquist, O'Connor and Thomas; Thomas also stated his distinct position separately. This is comparator evidence, not a current opinion assignment or an extra Justice in the simulated Court.

The historical decision separates two questions. Its Part II rejects both automatic plea waiver and the asserted waiver through this colloquy. A voluntary witness ordinarily cannot disclose part of a subject and refuse relevant cross-examination, but Brown's rule does not turn a plea's limited factual inquiry into consent to compelled sentencing testimony. The plea's admissibility and the admissibility of actual plea admissions remain distinct from an ongoing waiver of the privilege. A court can still inquire into the factual basis for a plea; refusal to supply necessary information can leave that basis inadequate. Here the plea was accepted, attributable quantity was expressly reserved, and the warning described trial rights. Those qualifications must accompany the retention rule. The privilege remains when unresolved sentencing exposure creates a genuine risk of further incrimination; a sentence fixed and conviction final, with no remaining adverse consequence from further testimony, presents the different no-further-incrimination principle. Finality alone is not a license to disregard another genuine criminal exposure.

The historical Part III prohibits using silence to determine the circumstances and details of the crime at sentencing. It distinguishes civil adverse inferences, prison discipline and executive clemency, and expressly leaves remorse and acceptance of responsibility undecided. It ends with reversal and remand; it does not separately announce a Chapman harmlessness holding or determine a new quantity. The supplied objective record, already exposed before modeling, includes Judge Michel's sufficiency-based harmlessness position and the sentencing judge's express adverse use of silence. The model's distinct prejudice analysis therefore rests on that existing record, Chapman and current Wright, not on an invented historical majority discussion.

The historical dissenters also accept retained sentencing privilege. Their disagreement is whether an ordinary adverse evidentiary inference compels testimony and whether Griffin should extend beyond adjudication of guilt. Scalia criticizes Griffin but expressly declines to propose overruling it, considering its established acceptance sufficient to preserve its existing operation. Thomas would be willing to reconsider Griffin and Carter in an appropriate case; he does not purport to overrule them in Mitchell. These different individual positions must not be collapsed.

## 3. Justice-specific grounds, counterpaths and join limits

### Stevens

Stevens applies Griffin's protection to proof of the crime's unresolved circumstances when punishment is imposed. Estelle supplies the continuing protection against sentencing incrimination; Brown's concern with selective testimonial disclosure does not transform this reserved issue into an admitted one. His prior Witte and Watts positions distinguish legal authority for additional punishment from receipt of information, and his separate Monge position takes the completed punitive adjudication seriously. His Jones minimum-range position reinforces the stakes but is not needed to create a general classification holding here.

The strongest competing path relies on his Woodard join permitting an assumed ordinary civil inference. Initial criminal sentencing is materially different from discretionary mercy. His conclusion also does not make every sentencing fact an element or every consequence of silence unconstitutional. Chapman and his Wright join defeat the contention that sufficient other evidence alone makes the express adverse reliance harmless. He supports the common crime-facts rule and bounded remedy; no distinct minimum-based concurrence or writing is required merely to retain an already published individual view. His prior Baxter participation is not invented: his relevant public civil-inference evidence is Woodard.

### O'Connor

O'Connor's Woodard opinion distinguishes the privilege from ordinary evidentiary consequences of declining to answer. Her Witte opinion, Monge join and Jones dissent preserve particular sentencing allocations without adopting unlimited legislative relabeling. She accepts Estelle's bar on compelled sentencing statements but considers this evidentiary inference, used with other credited testimony, insufficient to establish compelled testimony or an automatic penalty for claiming the privilege.

The strongest objection is that Baxter and Woodard are civil settings and quantity produces a criminal statutory floor. She recognizes that difference without treating it as sufficient to extend Griffin's trial-proof restriction. This is not a finding that the cooperating witnesses are necessarily correct, that silence alone proves quantity, or that a plea admits the threshold. The historical dissent's opening agreement with retained privilege confirms the model's separation of the questions. She affirms on the inference ground, not on waiver or harmlessness. If the contrary error rule controls, her recorded willingness to accept a lawful remand is conditional only; it supplies no vote for constitutional error or reversal.

### Scalia

Scalia distinguishes the actual right to refuse compelled testimony from the evidentiary consequences of exercising it. Griffin and Carter remain operative at their established scope, but their retention does not itself require extending the inference prohibition to all sentencing determinations. His historical Mitchell dissent treats the guilt/sentencing boundary as material, relies expressly on McMillan's different sentencing proof requirements, and disputes both the equivalence of an inference with compulsion and the coherence of separating offense circumstances from other sentencing information. His own Woodard join and Watts concurrence provide current public evidence for those distinctions, subject to their actual-element and independent-constitutional limits.

His personal Jones minimum-range position is preserved; it is not erased by the survival of McMillan or converted into a controlling Court rule. The functional-element alternative is considered and rejected as an adequately established basis for changing his Mitchell vote on the present materials, as section 4 explains. He does not withdraw that earlier personal view, decide a new section 841 classification claim, or announce that every below-maximum fact is constitutionally ordinary. He declines the presented inference extension while leaving a distinct classification application for a case establishing its necessary claim and legal basis.

Scalia agrees that the plea did not waive sentencing privilege and would affirm on the independent inference ground. He does not propose overruling Griffin or Carter here. Harmlessness is unnecessary to that actual disposition. If a contrary controlling error rule governs the remand, his Wright join is consistent with refusing to preserve an affected determination merely because the remaining evidence could support it. That conditional position is not a reversal vote.

### Kennedy

Kennedy's Balsys opinion locates the privilege in protection against domestic criminal use; the unresolved punishment here presents that protected exposure. Dunnigan permits consequences for independently established willful material perjury, not an inference from giving no testimony. Griffin and Estelle together forbid making Mitchell an involuntary source of the Government's disputed crime-fact proof. His Witte join and Jones dissent permit certain judicial sentencing determinations but do not establish that every possible source of proof is lawful.

The strongest counterpath joins sentencing flexibility to Woodard's assumed civil inference. His Woodard position remains consistent with distinguishing an invitation to seek mercy from adjudication of initial criminal punishment. He supports the crime-facts prohibition without adopting a universal element or jury-allocation theory. His Wright opinion and Chapman require evaluation of actual contribution, not merely sufficiency of the remaining testimony. He supports bounded reconsideration without selecting quantity, discrediting a witness as a matter of law or prescribing the replacement sentence.

### Souter

Souter separates the plea's admission from the question expressly left to sentencing. Brown's scope rule, Estelle and Griffin supply the operative distinction. His Gaudin join preserves actual-element safeguards, while Watts preserves independent constitutional protections within lawful sentencing. His Jones concurrence concerns current-offense facts indispensable to a greater statutory maximum and expressly leaves unchanged-maximum minima unresolved. That personal position is not silently enlarged here.

His Woodard and Balsys joins supply the strongest counterpath: not every pressure to speak is prohibited compulsion, and protected criminal use matters. They do not turn the Government's initial criminal quantity determination into civil mercy. He supports the no-inference rule for the crime's circumstances without resolving every sentencing classification. Under Chapman and his Wright join, actual reliance on silence in crediting contested testimony is not harmless merely because that testimony could support the finding. The remand uses lawful evidence and resolves no new jury, plea-withdrawal or fixed-sentence claim.

### Thomas

Thomas's Mezzanatto opinion enforces an actual limited voluntary agreement; it supplies no sentencing waiver absent from this record. His Edwards opinion and Monge opinion preserve particular sentencing factfinding without deciding every constitutional classification. His Jones join is statutory; he did not join either broader constitutional concurrence. Woodard supplies his distinction between an ordinary inference, compulsory testimony and an automatic privilege penalty. He therefore rejects automatic plea waiver but permits this inference in support of otherwise credited sentencing evidence.

Griffin and Estelle present the strongest contrary argument. He accepts their existing operation but declines to extend Griffin's trial-proof rule to the ordinary inference presented here. The statutory-floor consequence does not, without another established legal ground, make Jones's different statutory holding a section 841 element ruling. He grants no permission to compel answers, infer an actual admission, punish invocation automatically or treat silence alone as sufficient proof.

His historical separate position supplies a material distinction from Scalia: he questions Griffin's and Carter's constitutional foundations and would consider reconsidering them in an appropriate case. That individual view is retained as a possible separate-position function, without an overruling vote in this case or attribution to another Justice. His critique concerns the absence of compelled testimony and the conversion of a statutory no-comment policy into a constitutional prohibition. The historical opinion's references to post-divergence decisions are not imported as governing simulated holdings. His actual vote is affirmance on the no-error ground; conditional acceptance of a lawful remand under a contrary controlling rule is not an error or reversal vote.

### Ginsburg

Ginsburg's Balsys statement and join in Breyer's dissent emphasize real penal exposure and protection against official self-condemnation. Here that danger is domestic and immediate. Her Woodard join distinguishes civil process rather than eliminating independent protection in criminal sentencing. Griffin and Estelle prohibit making her silence affirmative proof of reserved crime facts. Brown's concern about a voluntary witness's selective account does not convert this limited plea colloquy into an unrestricted account of quantity.

The strongest inference-permitted path is the ordinary evidentiary distinction accepted in civil matters. She rejects its transfer to this criminal fact determination while leaving ordinary sentencing information and actual-element questions distinct. Her Jones concurrence remains maximum-based and is not broadened into a universal minimum holding. Chapman and Wright prevent sufficiency-only harmlessness in light of express reliance. She supports the common crime-facts rule and bounded relief, without deciding witness credibility anew or guaranteeing a lower sentence.

### Breyer

Breyer's Balsys dissent measures protection against actual criminal exposure. His Watts concurrence distinguishes the lawful relevant-conduct system from a constitutional permission to use every source of information. His Monge join and Jones dissent permit particular sentencing allocations; a rule requiring lawful proof of the crime's circumstances does not contradict them. Estelle preserves the privilege after admitted guilt, and Griffin prevents using the protected silence to complete the Government's proof of disputed criminal acts.

His strongest counterpath is the combination of sentencing flexibility and Woodard's assumed civil inference. He distinguishes criminal punishment from discretionary mercy, and does not equate awareness of possible penalties with a voluntary sentencing waiver. Under Chapman and his Wright join, the express use of silence cannot be dismissed merely because other evidence might support the same quantity. He supports the crime-facts rule and lawful sentencing reconsideration, leaving remorse, acceptance credit, minimum-element classification and independent jury claims unresolved.

## 4. Scalia's proposed departure: exact premise comparison

**Provisional-to-reconciled change:** Scalia's provisional commitment would prohibit the inference specifically because the reserved quantity supplies the ten-year statutory minimum, and would grant sentencing relief on that ground while withholding the broader crime-facts no-inference rule. Reconciliation changes that inference determination and actual disposition to affirmance, while retaining privilege survival and the already published personal Jones range position. The original frozen commitment is preserved rather than rewritten.

The strongest case for the provisional departure is concrete: the public Jones separate position expressly includes minimum-changing facts, the plea reserved this quantity, and the judge used silence to establish it. Scalia could regard Griffin as applying to completion of an element, rather than extending Griffin to ordinary within-range sentencing. His personal constitutional conclusion need not already command a Court majority to support his individual vote. McMillan's continuing precedential force does not, standing alone, refute that possibility.

The historical comparison nevertheless supplies a direct counterexample to the proposed inference about his position. Historical Jones, issued before Mitchell, already states Scalia's considered constitutional objection to removing from the jury facts altering the legislatively prescribed penalty range and expressly invokes his Monge reasoning. The adjacent Stevens concurrence explicitly includes minimum-changing facts. Historical Monge's Scalia dissent already distinguishes constitutionally functional elements from genuine sentencing facts. These ideas therefore were not absent from the historical premise set. Despite them, his subsequent historical Mitchell dissent accepted retained privilege, rejected the adverse-inference claim on this same minimum-quantity record, preserved Griffin rather than proposing its overruling, and relied on McMillan and the guilt/sentencing distinction. [Historical Jones, Scalia concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/97-6203.ZC1.html); [historical Jones, Stevens concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/97-6203.ZC.html); [historical Monge, Scalia dissent and notes](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/97-6146.ZD1.html); historical Mitchell, 526 U.S. at 331–341.

The current public decisions are checked at their exact scope. Monge's common dissent addresses the unproved personal-conduct qualification that permits a greater maximum; it does not supply a newly adopted general minimum rule. Jones's Court holding is statutory, concerns the original carjacking enactment, and decides neither this quantity classification nor a general mandatory-minimum rule. Its separate Stevens position, expressly joined by Scalia in Part I, makes minimum coverage unambiguous but does not decide a mandatory-minimum application, overrule McMillan, or state a new rule about the scope of Griffin. The substantive question is therefore not whether the current join is more explicit. It is whether that explicitness supplies a materially different commitment to applying retained Griffin protection to this reserved floor fact, despite the coexistence of the historical range position and the contrary historical Mitchell application. The supplied sources do not sufficiently establish that further change. Neither generic element-function reasoning nor the form of the new join supplies it by itself.

Claim scope is a limit, not a categorical bar to the alternative reasoning. Historical Monge note 2 expressly accepts an element-classification theory as a way to resolve a preserved double-jeopardy claim even though that theory was not separately presented. That supports the legal availability of functional classification as reasoning within Mitchell's presented inference question. It does not establish an independent jury or indictment claim, a waiver of either, or authority to order a new jury hearing. The rejection of the proposed departure is thus not based merely on the absence of a separate jury claim. It rests on the insufficient Justice-specific bridge from the already existing range position to a different inference result.

The result is qualitative historical reconciliation, not automatic substitution of the historical vote. The explicit current minimum position was given weight and is preserved. The lawful narrow inference alternative was tested and is not declared logically impossible. But there is no changed fact, reservation, sentencing use, claim, controlling element classification, displaced McMillan premise or supported new personal Griffin-scope commitment that adequately explains this departure. The directly evidenced historical inference position remains the more defensible commitment on the authorized record.

No material historical departure remains in the reconciled non-Stone judgment positions. Scalia's provisional departure has been rejected after source review; it must not reappear in assembly as a frozen concurrence or as support for the crime-facts rule. A later material neutral change would require the affected stages to be repeated rather than a silent assembly revision.

## 5. Harmlessness and mandate: actual votes versus conditional positions

For Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, the inference is constitutional error. Chapman places on the Government the burden of establishing beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the affected sentencing determination. The lower concurrence's proposition that other evidence could support quantity does not answer that question. The judge expressly used silence to credit the disputed witnesses and stated that a contrary privilege ruling could warrant closer examination of them. The already approved record supplies no independent conclusive quantity finding unaffected by that use. Current Wright independently rejects preserving an affected sentence through an appellate substitute ground not established in the sentencing findings. Wright's Guidelines holding is not transformed into a general constitutional rule, and the historical Mitchell majority is not falsely credited with a separate Chapman discussion.

Those five support reversal of the Third Circuit's judgment insofar as it sustained the challenged sentence, vacatur of the affected quantity-based sentencing determination, and remand for lawful sentencing without adverse use of Mitchell's silence in determining the crime's facts. They do not hold the witnesses unreliable as a matter of law, substitute an appellate quantity finding, guarantee a lower term, direct a ten-year minimum, command jury factfinding, permit withdrawal of the pleas, or acquit Mitchell. The guilty pleas remain intact. No independent determination automatically eliminates supervised release, the assessment or another sentence component; any consequential adjustment must follow lawful proceedings under the governing law. The historical formal reversal and remand are consistent with this requested sentencing relief, but do not supply new relief by themselves.

O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas find no prohibited inference and would affirm against that claim. They do not reach harmlessness as a basis for their actual judgment. Their recognition that a contrary controlling error rule requires a lawful response, and their opposition to a sufficiency-only appellate substitute under Wright, are conditional remedial positions. They supply no additional vote for error, reversal, vacatur or the five-Justice sufficient ground.

All positions leave remorse, acceptance of responsibility, the general allocation and proof quantum of sentencing facts, independent indictment or jury claims, and any unestablished replacement sentence outside the disposition. Retention of the privilege is not a right to lie, and Griffin's existing trial operation is not overruled by anyone's position here.
