QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


Petitioner was convicted of mail, wire, bank and tax fraud 
in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341, 1343, 1344, and 26 U.S.C. 
§ 7206(1). The trial court, over defense counsel’s timely 
objection, instructed the jury that it “need not consider” 
whether petitioner’s allegedly fraudulent representations and 
omissions were material because materiality “is not a question 
for the jury to decide.” Instead of submitting the materiality 
question to the jury, the trial court entered its own judicial 
finding that the alleged representations and omissions were 
material. On appeal, the Eleventh Circuit held that, as to the tax 
fraud counts, the district court’s removal of materiality from the 
jury’s consideration was constitutional error under United States 
v. Gaudin, 515 U.S. 506 (1995). However, the court concluded 
that the error was “harmless” because “materiality was not in 
dispute with respect to Neder’s tax fraud offense.” As to the 
mail, wire and bank fraud counts, the Eleventh Circuit held that 
the district court’s removal of materiality from the jury’s 
consideration was not erroneous under Gaudin since materiality 
is not an element of any of those statutory offenses. 


The questions presented are: 


1. | Whether the complete removal of an essential 
element of a charged offense from the jury’s consideration is 
subject to harmless-error review. 


2. Whether materiality is an element of the crimes set 
forth in the federal mail fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1341), wire fraud 
(18 U.S.C. § 1343) and bank fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1344) 
statutes.

STATUTORY AND CONSTITUTIONAL 
PROVISIONS INVOLVED 


Article Ill, § 2 of the United States Constitution 
provides that “The Trial of all Crimes, except in Cases of 
Impeachment, shall be by Jury.” The Due Process Clause of the 
Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution provides that 
“No person shall be * * * deprived of !'f, liberty, or property, 
without due process of law.” The %ixth Amendment to the 
United States Constitution provides that “In all criminal 
prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right to a speedy and 
public trial, by an impartial jury of the State and district 
wherein the crime shall have been committed; to be confronted

26 U.S.C. § 7206(1) are reproduced at App., infra, 43a-47a. 


STATEMENT 


This case presents the important and recurring 
constitutional question that this Court left open in United States 
v. Gaudin, 515 U.S. 506 (1995); Johnson v. United States, 117 
S. Ct. 1544 (1997), and, most recently, in Rogers v. United 
States, 118 S. Ct. 673 (1998)—namely, whether the trial court's 
removal of an element of a charged offense is subject to 
harmiess-error review under Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 
18 (1967), notwithstanding the defendant’s constitutional right 
to a jury determination of guilt or innocence as to each element 
of the offense. This case also presents the separate and far- 
reaching question of whether materiality is an element of the 
federal mail, wire and bank fraud statutes. On each of these 
important questions, the courts of appeals are deeply divided 
and in need of Supreme Court review. 


A. In Rogers v. United States, 118 S. Ct. 673 (1998), 
this Court granted certiorari to decide whether the omission of 


3 


an element of a charged offense from the jury instructions is 
subject to harmless-error review. After argument, the Court 
dismissed the petition as improvidently granted on the ground 
that the question was “not fairly presented by the record.” /d. 
at 645. Earlier, in Johnson v. United States, 117 S. Ct. 1544, 
1550 (1997), this Court noted that the answer to the question of 
whether a Gaudin error (the error that occurred here) is subject 
to harmless-error review is “by no means clear.” The Court 
concluded, however, that it “need not decide that question” 
because the defendant in Johnson (unlike petitioner here) did 
not timely object to the error at trial. 

This case provides the ideal opportunity for the Court 
“to address [the] important issue of constitutional law” left open 
in Rogers, Johnson and Gaudin. Rogers, 118 S. Ct. at 677 
(O’Connor, J., concurring). See also id. at 675 (plurality 
opinion) (same); Gaudin, 515 U.S. at 526-27 (Rehnquist, C.J., 
concurring) (noting that the Court left open the question 
whether a Gaudin error is subject to harmless-error review). As 
the Eleventh Circuit noted below (App., infra, 1la n.10), the 
courts of appeals are deeply split over whether a Gaudin error 
is subject to harmless-error review. The Second, Eighth and 
Eleventh Circuits have held that Gaudin errors are subject to 
harmless-error review. See infra at 10 n.4 & cases cited 
therein. The First, Fourth, Fifth, Seventh, Ninth and D.C. 
Circuits, on the other hand, have held that Gaudin errors are 
immune from harmless-error scrutiny. See infra at 10 n.5 & 
cases cited therein. 

This conflict vividly reflects the larger controversy over 
whether elemental omissions like a Gaudin error can ever 
constitutionally be “harmless.” As the Eleventh Circuit 
explained in Rogers v. United States, 94 F.3d 1519, 1524 n.11 
(1996), cert. dismissed, 118 S. Ct. 643 (1998), “[s]ome circuits 
have determined that an instructional omission mandates 
reversal regardless of the circumstances.” (Citations omitted). 


4 


In contrast, “[o}ther circuits have recognized that this type of 
mistake will sometimes be amenable to harmless error scruti- 
ny.” /bid. (citations omitted). Accordingly, the Seventh, Ninth 
and Eleventh Circuits each have openly invited Supreme Court 
review of this issue, and acknowledged “that further guidance 
and clarification would be extremely helpful.” /bid. (citing 
Hennessy v. Goldsmith, 929 F.2d 511, 515 n.2 (9th Cir. 1991); 
Teel v. Tennessee, 498 U.S. 1007 (1990) (White, J., dissenting 
from denial of certiorari)).' This Court should grant the petition 
to resolve the conflict on this important and unanswered 
question of constitutional law. 

B. This case also presents the unanswered and widely 
recurring question of whether materiality is an element of the 
federal mail, wire and bank fraud statutes, each of which 
proscribes a “scheme or artifice to defraud, or for obtaining 
money or property by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, 
representations, or promises.” 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341, 1343, 1344. 
The court of appeals, citing this Court’s decision in United 
States v. Wells, 117 S. Ct. 921 (1997), held that none of these 
statutes contain a materiality requirement since none “contain 
the word ‘material.’” App., infra, 7a. With this holding, the 
Eleventh Circuit has deepened the circuit split over this 
question. The First and Fifth Circuits, for instance, like the 
Eleventh Circuit, have held that materiality is not an element of

Cir. 1994) (“the Supreme Court has yet to decide the question whether

Harmless-Error Review Of Mandatory Presumptions And Elemental 
Misdescriptions, 74 B.U. L. REV. 819, 844 (1994) (noting that the current

5 


the wire, mail or bank fraud statutes. See infra at 21 n. 10 & 
cases cited therein. In contrast, the Second, Fourth, Sixth, 
Ninth and D.C. Circuits have held that materiality is an element 
of these statutes. See infra at 22 n. 11 & cases cited therein. 

Adding further to the confusion, the Seventh, Eighth and 
Tenth Circuits have staked out an indeterminate middle ground. 
The Eighth and Tenth Circuits have held that “[a]lthough 
materiality is not an independent element of a wire fraud 
prosecution, ‘there is a materiality aspect to the determination 
whether the acts of an accused give rise to a scheme to defraud’ 
that ‘is appropriately submitted to the jury as one component of 
the larger factual question as to the existence of fraud and a 
scheme to defraud.’” United States v. Cochran, 109 F.3d 660, 
667 n.3 (10th Cir. 1997) (emphasis added) (citation omitted). 
See also United States v. Slaughter, 128 F.3d 623, 629 (8th 
Cir. 1997) (adopting the Tenth Circuit’s approach). The 
Seventh Circuit, in contrast, has held that while Congress did 
not “want to classify immaterial lies as frauds for purposes of 
the mail- and wire-fraud statutes,” a jury need not be separately 
instructed on materiality because “the term ‘fraud’ embodies the 
concept of materiality,” and thus “[a)n instruction in the 
language of the statute adequately puts the issue before the 
jury.” United States v. Coffman, 94 F.3d 330 (7th Cir. 1996), 
cert. denied, 117 S. Ct. 1426 (1997). 


In short, the courts of appeals are irreconcilably split 
over whether materiality is an element of the mail, wire and 
bank fraud statutes. Such uncertainty over the elements of 
criminal statutes is intolerable. This Court should grant the 
petition to resolve this important and recurring question. 


C. Petitioner was charged with multiple counts of 
federal tax, mail, wire and bank fraud in connection with a 
series of land acquisition and development transactions. The 
indictment alleged, among other things, that petitioner 


6 


concealed from his lenders certain material facts concerning the 
source of certain deposit funds and the structure of those 
transactions, and also that he failed to report certain profits 
from these transactions in his 1985 and 1986 income tax 
returns. At trial, “the district court instructed the jury that if it 
found beyond a reasonable doubt that the alleged statements, 
representations, or promises were false, it need not consider 
whether they were material because materiality was not an issue 
for the jury to decide. The court entered its own findings 
regarding materiality outside the presence of the jury. Petitioner 
timely objected to the court's findings and its failure to submit 
the issue of materiality to the jury.” App., infra, 3a. See also 
App., infra, 28a, 30a, 34a (excerpts of jury instructions). On 
direct appeal, the Eleventh Circuit affirmed petitioner's

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 


I. THE COURT OF APPEALS’ HOLDING THAT GAUDIN 
ERRORS ARE SUBJECT TO HARMLESS-ERROR 
REVIEW CONFLICTS WITH DECISIONS OF OTHER 
CIRCUITS AND STATE COURTS OF LAST RESORT. 


The court of appeals’ decision in this case raises the 
fundamental question of whether a trial court’s removal of an 
element of a criminal offense from the jury’s consideration is 
subject to harmless-error review where the jury made no 
findings concerning the omitted element. This Court granted 
certiorari to decide that question in Rogers v. United States, 118 
S. Ct. 673 (1998), but ultimately determined that the question 
was “not fairly presented by the record.” Jd. at 645. Similarly, 
in Johnson v. United States, 117 S. Ct. 1544, 1550 (1997), this 
Court noted that the answer to the question presented here was 
“not at all clear,” but concluded that it “need not decide that 
question” because there was no timely objection to the 
erroneous instruction, and the case thus was governed by 
entirely different plain error standards. And in United States v. 
Gaudin, 515 U.S. 506 (1995), where this Court held that 
materiality was a question for the jury, not the court, this Court 
left open the question of whether a Gaudin error is subject to 
harmless-error review. See Gaudin, 515 U.S. at 526-27 
(Rehnquist, C.J., concurring). 


The question of whether an elemental omission is subject 
to harmless-error review is vitally significant. The need for 
Supreme Court review of this issue also is immediate and 
compelling, since the federal and state courts have deeply split 
over the answer, and have openly called for Supreme Court 
guidance. This case provides an ideal opportunity to put this 
controversy to rest.

B. The Court of Appeals’ Holding Squarely Conflicts

It is undisputed that materiality is an element of the tax 
fraud offense (26 U.S.C. § 7206(1)) of which petitioner stands 
convicted. It also is uncontested that the district court explicitly 
removed that element from the jury’s consideration by (1) 
deciding that element itself, (2) charging the jury in its tax 
fraud instruction that the jury “need not consider” materiality, 
and (3) instructing that materiality “is not a question for the 
jury to decide.” App., infra, 34a. Thus, as the court of appeals 
held, the trial court clearly violated petitioner’s constitutional 
right to a jury trial under United States v. Gaudin, 515 U.S. 
506 (1995). The court of appeals concluded, however, that this 
constitutional error was subject to harmless-error review, and 
in fact was harmless based on its review of the record evidence. 


App., infra, 13a. 


The court of appeals’ use of the “harmless error” 
doctrine to supply a finding that the jury never made cannot be 
reconciled with this Court’s decisions that consistently have 
safeguarded the defendant’s right to a jury determination on all 
essential elements of the offense charged. See, e.g., Sullivan 
v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275 (1993). Under Sullivan, the trial 
court’s failure to instruct the jury on an element of an offense, 
and the jury’s resultant failure to determine whether that 
element was established beyond a reasonable doubt, cannot 
constitutionally be “harmless” regardless of an appellate court's 
perception of the record or its certainty of guilt.

constitutionally valid determination of guilt on each element of

conviction must be reversed, no matter how inescapable the 
findings to support that verdict might be.” Roy, 117 S. Ct. at 
339 (Scalia, J., concurring). Accord Old Chief v. United States, 
117 S. Ct. 644, 659 ( 1997) (O'Connor, J., dissenting) (same).

B. The Court Of Appeals’ Materiality Holding Is 
Contrary To This Court’s Statutory And Common 
Law Decisions That Consistently Have Found 
Materiality To Be An Essential Element Of Fraud. 


In holding that materiality is not an element of the mail, 
wire and bank fraud offenses, the Eleventh Circuit relied 
entirely on this Court’s recent decision in Wells, 117 S. Ct. at 
926-28, which held that materiality is not an element of 18 
U.S.C. § 1014. The court of appeals concluded that materiality 
is not an element of §§ 1341, 1343 and 1344 for two reasons. 
First, it relied heavily on the fact that these statutes “do not 
contain the word ‘material.’” App., infra, 7a. Second, the court 
noted that § 1341, which served as the model for §§ 1343 and 
1344, “was enacted as part of the recodification of the federal 
criminal code in 1948.” Jbid. As demonstrated below, the 
Eleventh Circuit's materiality holding is plainly wrong, and 
reflects a fundamental misreading of Wells. 


l. Unlike §§ 1341, 1343 and 1344, which proscribe 
a “scheme or artifice to defraud, or for obtaining money or 
property by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, 
representations, or promises,” Wells involved the application of 
18 U.S.C. § 1014, which prohibits certain “false statement{s]” 
to federally insured banks. In holding that the term “false 
statement” does not include a materiality component, Wells first 
reaffirmed the presumption that “Congress incorporates the 
common-law meaning of the terms it uses if those ‘terms * * * 
have accumulated settled meaning under * * * the common 
law.”” 117 S. Ct. at 927 (quoting Nationwide Mut. Ins. Co. v. 
Darden, 503 U.S. 318, 322 (1992)). But finding no evidence 
that the term “false statement” carried any common law 
meaning, or that Congress intended that term to require proof 
of materiality, the Court held that materiality was not an 
element of § 1014. Wells, 117 S. Ct. at 926-28. 


27 


Nothing in Wells, however, even remotely suggests that 
materiality is not an element of §§ 1341, 1343 and 1344. To the 
contrary, Wells’ reaffirmation of the rule that common law 
statutory terms convey their common law meaning inexorably 
leads to the conclusion that materiality is an element of the 
federal mail, wire and bank fraud offenses. 


Unlike the “false statement” statute in Wells, §§ 1341, 
1343 and 1344 each explicitly prohibits “fraud.” In particular, 
they punish a “scheme or artifice” either “to defraud” or to 
“obtain{]” money or property “by means of false or fraudulent 
pretenses, representations, or promises.” 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341, 
1343, 1344. The mail fraud statute, which served as the model 
for the wire and bank fraud statute, was crafted in 1872 as “a 
general proscription against using the mails to initiate 
correspondence in furtherance of ‘any scheme or artifice to 
defraud.’” McNally v. United States, 483 U.S. 350, 356 (1987). 
As this Court explained in Carpenter v. United States, 484 U.S. 
19 (1987), “the words ‘to defraud’ in the mail fraud statute 
have the ‘common understanding’ of ‘wronging one in his 
property rights by dishonest methods or schemes,’ and ‘usually 
signify the deprivation of something of value by trick, deceit, 
chicane or overreaching.’” Jd. at 27 (quoting McNally, 483 
U.S. at 358 (quoting Hammerschmidt v. United States, 265 
U.S. 182, 188 (1924)). See also Durland v. United States, 161 
U.S. 306, 312 (1896). 


The “common understanding” of “fraud” has always 
included materiality as one of its essential elements. For more 
than a century, this Court has emphasized “the fact that 
actionable fraud requires a material misrepresentation or 
omission.” BMW v. Gore, 517 U.S. 559, 579 (1996) (citing 
RESTATEMENT (SECOND) OF TorRTS § 538 (1977); W. Keeton, 
et al., Prosser & Keeton on Law of Torts § 108 (Sth ed. 1984)). 
See also Smith v. Richards, 38 U.S. (13 Pet.) 26, 39 (1839) (a 

“misrepresentation must be of something material, constituting 


28 


an inducement or motive * * * to purchase, and by which [the 
aggrieved party] has been actually misled to his injury”) 
(emphasis added); Stewart v. Wyoming Cattle Ranche Co., 128 
U.S. 383, 388-89 (1888) (same); 1 J. Story, Commentaries on 
Equity Jurisprudence § 271 (1886) (“[t}he materiality of the 
alleged fraudulent statements * * * is one of the necessary 
ingredients of the offence”); id. §§ 260-61, 267-71. It is black- 
letter law that “one of the fundamental principles which 
pervades jurisprudence involving fraud, and the maintenance of 
actions or defenses based thereon, is that to constitute fraud in 
any case, the facts misrepresented or concealed must have been 
material facts which substantially affect the interests of the 
person alleged to have been defrauded.” 37 AM. JUR. 2D Fraud 
and Deceit § 177, at 236-37 (1964) (collecting cases). 


Against this common law backdrop, it is no surprise that 
this Court has held that materiality to be an element of other 
statutes that prohibit fraud but do not mention the word 
“material.” For instance, this Court has held that materiality is 
an element of securities fraud statutes such as § 10(b) of the 
Securities Exchange Act of 1934, which prohibits “any 
manipulative or deceptive device or contrivance” but makes no 
mention of “materiality.” See, e.g., Basic Inc. v. Levinson, 485 
U.S. 224 (1988); Santa Fe Indus., Inc. v. Green, 430 U.S. 
462, 474 (1977). See also 7 L. Loss & J. SELIGMAN, Securities 
Regulation 3421-31 (3d ed. 1991) (noting that materiality is an 
essential element of securities fraud). 


By leaping to the conclusion that §§ 1341, 1343 and 
1344 contain no materiality requirement simply because they do 
not use the word “material,” the Eleventh Circuit misapplied 
this Court’s decision in Wells and overlooked the fact that 
unlike the statute in Wells, the statutes here explicitly prohibit 
“fraud,” a term whose common law meaning always has 
required proof of a material misrepresentation or omission. 


29 


2. The court of appeals also wrongly relied on the 
fact that the mail fraud statute (§ 1341) was recodified as part 
of the Federal Criminal Code in 1948. It is certainly true that 
Wells relied on the fact that § 1014 was recodified in 1948 as 
a conglomeration of 13 earlier statutes, 10 of which had never 
required materiality. 117 S. Ct. at 928. Here, on the other 
hand, § 1341 was merely renumbered in 1948. The mail fraud 
statute, originally enacted in 1872," and amended in 1909,"* has 
always targetted “fraud,” and was recodified in 1948 without 
any substantive change in its language or scope. As the Senate 
Report explained, the purpose of the recodification was simply 
to “correct{] typographical and other minor errors, and clarif[y] 
the language of some sections to conform more closely to the 
original law, or to remove ambiguities which have been 
discovered.” S. Rep. No. 303, 8ist Cong., Ist Sess. (1949), 
reprinted in 1949 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1248, 1248. Thus, the 
Eleventh Circuit's reliance on the fact that “§ 1341 was enacted 
as part of the recodification of the federal criminal code in 
1948” (App., infra, 7a) is wholly misplaced. 


In sum, the Eleventh Circuit's holding that materiality 
is not an element of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341, 1343 and 1344 is 
patently erroneous and should be reversed.

Sections 1341 and 1343 are similar to § 1014 in several

recodification of the federal criminal code in 1948, and § 1343 
was patterned after § 1341. Based on these factors considered 
in Wells, we conclude that materiality is not an element of 
fraud offenses in §§ 1341 and 1343.* As discussed below, we 
reach the same conclusion regarding § 1344.

makes and subscribes any return, statement, or other document, 
which contains or is verified by a written declaration that it is 
made under the penalties of perjury, and which he does not 
believe to be true and correct as to every material matter; or 


(2) Aid or assistance. -- Willfully aids or assists in, or 
procures, counsels, or advises the preparation or presentation 
under, or in connection with any matter arising under, the 
internal revenue laws, of a return, affidavit, claim, or other 
document, which is fraudulent or is false as to any material 
matter, whether or not such falsity or fraud is with the 
knowledge or consent of the person authorized or required to 
present such return, affidavit, claim, or document; or 


(3) Fraudulent bonds, permits, and entries. -- Simulates 
or falsely or fraudulently executes or signs any bond, permit, 
entry, or other document required by the provisions of the 
internal revenue laws, or by any regulation made in pursuance 
thereof, or procures the same to be falsely or fraudulently 
executed, or advises, aids in, or connives at such execution 
thereof; or 


(4) Removal or concealment with intent to defraud. -- 
Removes, deposits, or conceals, or is concerned in removing, 
depositing, or concealing, any goods or commodities for or in 
respect whereof any tax is or shall be imposed, or any property 
upon which levy is authorized by section 6331, with intent to 
evade or defeat the assessment or collection of any tax imposed 
by this title; or 


47a 


(5) Compromises and closing agreements. -- In 
connection with any compromise under section 7122, or offer 
of such compromise, or in connection with any closing 
agreement under section 7121, or offer to enter into any such 


agreement, willfully-- 


(A) Concealment of property. -- Conceals from 
any officer or employee of the United States any 
property belonging to the estate of a taxpayer or other 
person liable in respect of the tax, or 


(B) Withholding, falsifying, and destroying 
records. -- Receives, withholds, destroys, mutilates, or 
falsifies any book, document, or record, or makes any 
false statement, relating to the estate or financial 
condition of the taxpayer or other person liable in 
respect of the tax; 


shall be guilty of a felony and, upon conviction thereof, shall 
be fined not more than $100,000 ($500,000 in the case of a 
corporation), or imprisoned not more than 3 years, or both, 
together with the costs of prosecution.
