STATUTORY AND CONSTITUTIONAL 
PROVISIONS INVOLVED 


Article Ill, § 2 of the United States Constitution provides that 
“The Trial of all Crimes, except in Cases of Impeachment, shall be 
by Jury.” The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment to the 
United States Constitution provides that “No person shall be * * * 
deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law.” 
The Sixth Amendment to the United States Constitution provides that 
“In all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right to a 
speedy and public trial, by an impartial jury of the State and district 
wherein the crime shall have been committed; to be confronted with 
the witnesses against him; to have compulsory process for obtaining 
witnesses in his favor, and to have the Assistance of Counsel for his 
defence.” 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341, 1343, 1344, and 26 U.S.C. § 7206(1) 
are reproduced at Pet. App. 43a-47a. 


STATEMENT 


1. On August 23, 1991, petitioner was indicted on multiple 
counts of federal tax, mail, wire and bank fraud in connection with 
a series of land acquisition and development transactions during the 
1980s. J.A. 33-144. The indictment alleged that petitioner engaged in 
a scheme to defraud lenders by concealing material facts regarding the 
structure of the acquisition transactions and the source of certain 
deposit funds from buyers of petitioner’s real estate developments. 


2 


Ibid. The government also alleged that petitioner’s 1985 and 1986 
income tax returns omitted material facts by failing to report the 
profits from these transactions. 


The government’s land acquisition charges specifically alleged 
that petitioner financed “land-flip” transactions. According to the 
government, petitioner used “shell” corporations that he controlled to 
purchase land from third-party titleholders at bargain prices, and then 
to sell those properties to himself at higher prices using inflated 
appraisals, with petitioner then keeping the excess loan proceeds as 
“profit.” J.A. 42-44. The government further alleged that petitioner 
failed to disclose material facts to his lenders concerning his 
“nominee” relationship with the shell corporations. Ibid. 


The government’s land development charges alleged that 
petitioner fraudulently obtained loans to finance a variety of land 
development projects. J.A. 44-45. Specifically, the government 
alleged that to satisfy pre-sale commitment requirements of his 
lenders, petitioner solicited buyers by agreeing to advance their initial 
deposits, but failed to disclose those advances to the lenders. Jbid. 
The indictment further alleged that petitioner fraudulently obtained 
construction draws in connection with a particular project by 
presenting materially false invoices. J.A. 45-47. 


Finally, the government’s tax fraud charges alleged that 
petitioner's 1985 and 1986 tax returns failed to report $1,372,360 in 
income for 1985, and $4,355,766 in income for 1986, all allegedly 
derived from excess loan proceeds obtained from the real estate 
acquisition transactions. J.A. 122-123. 


Petitioner pleaded not guilty to all counts of the indictment, and 
did not stipulate or admit to any element of the charges. Petitioner 
defended the land acquisition charges on the ground that the land 
parcels purchased by the “shell” corporations were acquired at prices 
well below fair market value, and were sold at the true fair market 
values utilized for the financing. Petitioner also contended that the 
appraisals used to obtain the financing were not inflated, and were 
approved by the lender. J.A. 190-191, 200-205 (closing argument). 
Petitioner also argued that the use of nominee corporations to acquire 
real estate was entirely proper, and that the lenders in question were

unconcerned about, and in some cases were aware of, the structure 
of these transactions. Jd. at 171-188. Petitioner also defended the 
charges on the ground that he relied on the advice of his counsel and 
his accountant when structuring the transactions. /d. at 217-223. 


Petitioner contested the land development fraud charges on the 
ground that, among other things, the loan documents did not prohibit 
him from advancing deposits to secure prospective buyers, and that 
petitioner did not refuse to disclose the source of those advances to 
any lender. J.A. 188-189. Petitioner also argued that his lender was 
aware of how his construction draws were being used, and had given 
its approval. Jd. at 193-194. Finally, petitioner challenged the tax 
fraud charges on the ground that the funds cited by the government 
were not “income” for the 1985 and 1986 tax years, but were merely 
loan proceeds, and that under an allowable interpretaion of the tax 
laws, the loan proceeds were not treated as “income” until the 
properties in question were sold in the future. /d. at 208-211. 


2. Petitioner proceeded to trial in 1992. At the jury 
instruction conference, the government proposed the use of Eleventh 
Circuit pattern jury instructions which specified that the materiality of 
petitioner’s alleged misrepresentations and omissions was for the court 
— not the jury — to decide. Petitioner’s counsel objected on the 
ground that materiality should be decided by the jury. J.A. 165-166. 
The trial court overruled petitioner's objections. J.A. 166-167. While 
the trial court instructed the jury that materiality was an element of 
the offenses charged (J.A. 249), the court also “instructed the jury 
that if it found beyond a reasonable doubt that the alleged statements, 
representations, or promises were false, it need not consider whether 
they were material because materiality was not an issue for the jury 
to decide. The court entered its own findings regarding materiality 
outside the presence of the jury. Petitioner timely objected to the 
court's findings and its failure to submit the issue of materiality to the 
jury.” Pet. App. 3a. See also J.A. 249, 251-252, 252-253, 256. 
During the trial, the trial court dismissed three counts of the 
indictment. J.A. 20. The jury also acquitted petitioner of eight 
charges relating to the submission of inflated appraisals, but convicted 
him on all other counts, including the mail, wire, bank and tax fraud 
counts. J.A. 27. 


4 


3. On June 19, 1995, while petitioner's direct appeal to the 
Eleventh Circuit was pending, this Court decided United States v. 
Gaudin, 515 U.S. 506 (1995), which held that the Constitution 
provides a criminal defendant with the “right to have the jury decide 
materiality.” Jd. at 511. On the basis of Gaudin, petitioner argued to 
the court of appeals that his convictions for mail, wire, bank and tax 
fraud all should be vacated because materiality is an element of each 
of those statutory offenses, and because the trial court's directed 
verdict on the element of materiality deprived him of the 
constitutional right to have the jury decide every element of the 
charged offenses. 


The Eleventh Circuit affirmed petitioner’s convictions. As to the 
tax fraud conviction under 26 U.S.C. § 7206(1), the Eleventh Circuit 
acknowledged that under Gaudin, the district court “erred in not 
submitting the element of materiality to the jury.” Pet. App. 1 la. 
However, following circuit precedent, the court of appeals held that 
“Gaudin errors do not require automatic reversal but are subject to 
harmless error analysis.” Pet. App. lla n.10. Specifically, in United 
States v. Fern, 117 F.3d 1298 (11th Cir. 1997), the Eleventh Circuit 
had held that “a Gaudin error does not fall within that limited class 
of errors known as ‘structural errors,’ ¢.g., errors ‘affecting the 
framework within which the trial proceeds, rather than simply an 
error in the trial process itself.’ To the contrary, a Gaudin error is 
more correctly viewed as analogous to improperly instructing the jury 
on an element of the offense, an error which is subject to harmless 
error analysis.” Jd. at 1307 (quoting Johnson v. United States, 117 
S. Ct. 1544, 1550 (1997)). 

Having found that the trial court's Gaudin error was subject to 
harmless-error review, the court of appeals proceeded to hold that the 
error was harmless “[bjecause materiality was not in dispute with 
respect to Neder’s tax fraud offense, [and thus] the district court's 
Gaudin error ‘did not contribute to the verdict obtained."” Pet. App. 
13a (quoting Yates v. Evatt, 500 U.S. 391, 403 (1991)). 

The Eleventh Circuit also affirmed petitioner’s mail, wire and 
bank fraud convictions, but on different grounds. Citing United States 
v. Wells, 519 U.S. 482 (1997), the court of appeals held that the trial 


5 


court’s directed finding of materiality did not violate petitioner’s 
rights under Gaudin because materiality is not an element of those 
offenses. The court held that materiality is not an element of the mail 
and wire fraud statutes (18 U.S.C. §§ 1341 & 1343) because they “do 
not contain the word ‘material.’ Further, § 1341 was enacted as part 
of the recodification of the federal criminal code in 1948, and § 1343 
was patterned after § 1341. Based on these factors considered in 
Wells, we conclude that materiality is not an element of fraud offenses 
in §§ 1341 and 1343.” Pet. App. 7a. The court reached the same 
conclusion as to the bank fraud statute (18 U.S.C. § 1344), because 
“its language, like § 1343, was patterned after § 1341.” Jd. at 9a. 


The Eleventh Circuit acknowledged that unlike the statute in 
Wells (18 U.S.C. § 1014), which prohibits “false statements,” 
§§ 1341, 1343 and 1344 punish a “scheme or artifice” either “to 
defraud” or to “obtain{]” money or property “by means of false or 
fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises.” However, 
rejecting the Ninth Circuit's contrary holding in United States v. 
Nash, 115 F.3d 1431, 1436 (1997), cert. denied, 118 S. Ct. 1054 
(1998), the court held that the textual differences between § 1014 and 
§§ 1341, 1343 and 1344 were irrelevant under Wells: 

We disagree with the Ninth Circuit’s analysis that since 

the term “representation” included “some notion of 

materiality at common law,” this indicates that Congress 

intended for materiality to be an element of the bank fraud 
statute. * * * This ignores that Congress now has 
employed the terms “material representation” and 

“material misrepresentation” in other statutes. The fact 

that Congress uses the term “material” in conjunction with 

“representation” or “misrepresentation” in some statutes 

but not in others rebuts any presumption that Congress 

now intends to make materiality an element under a statute 
whenever it employs the term “representation” in that 
statute. 
Pet. App. 9a. The court also rejected the view that “there is a 
materiality aspect to the determination whether the acts of an accused 
give rise to a scheme to defraud.” /d. at 7a n.4 (citing United States 


6 


v. Cochran, 109 F.3d 660, 667 n.3 (10th Cir. 1997)). Finally, the 
court rejected the argument that because § 1344 targets “fraud,” it 
necessarily requires proof of materiality. According to the Eleventh 
Circuit, “[t}his approach is inconsistent with Wells, which disfavors 
inferring a materiality requirement where the statutory language does

materiality is not an element of §§ 1341, 1343 and 1344, the court of

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


1. At issue in this case is whether it can ever be harmless 
error for a trial court to direct a verdict in favor of the prosecution on 
an element of a charged offense — that is, for a person to stand 
convicted of a crime even though the jury never found him guilty 
beyond a reasonable doubt as to every element of the crime. The 
Eleventh Circuit answered this question in the affirmative, holding 
that the trial court’s directed verdict on the materiality element of the 
tax fraud charges was subject to harmless-error review because, in the 
view of the court of appeals, “materiality was not in dispute regarding 
Neder’s tax fraud offense,” and therefore “the district court’s Gaudin 
error ‘did not contribute to the verdict obtained.’” Pet. App. 12a 
(quoting Yates v. Evatt, 500 U.S. 391, 403 (1991)). The court thus 
“filled the gaps” in the jury’s verdict and supplied the missing factual 
findings that the jury never made, based on nothing more than its own 
views concerning the record evidence. As we demonstrate below, the 
court of appeals’ decision utterly disregards the right to trial by jury 
and patently misapplies this Court’s harmless-error decisions, which 
confirm that harmless-error review is impermissible where the jury 
has not rendered a verdict on every element of the offense charged. 


Under Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), and its 
progeny, the harmless-error doctrine applies only where the jury 
actually has rendered a complete verdict on every element of the 
offense, and may then be used only to gauge whether the 
constitutional error contributed to the verdict actually entered. In this 
case, the court of appeals unhinged the harmless-error doctrine, using

consideration. Such misuse of the harmless-error doctrine squarely 
violates the Court’s unanimous decision in Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 
U.S. 275, 280 (1993), in which this Court made clear that where a 
jury fails to render a complete verdict, “the entire premise of 
Chapman review is simply absent.” Thus, “the question whether the 
same verdict of guilty-beyond-a-reasonable-doubt would have been 
rendered absent the constitutional error is utterly meaningless. There 
is no object, so to speak, upon which the harmless-error scrutiny can 
operate.” Jbid. (emphasis in original). In this case, the court of 

The court of appeals justified its use of the harmless-error 
doctrine by finding that the materiality element of the tax fraud

court’s own subjective perception of the record evidence. It has firmly 
been established for more than half a century, however, that a court 
may never direct a verdict of guilt regardless of how “overwhelming” 
the evidence may seem. Where, as here, the trial court has directed 
a verdict of guilt on an element of the offense charged, and the jury 
thus has failed to render a complete verdict on every element of the 
crime, the harmless-error doctrine has no application. In such a case, 
regardless of how “overwhelming” the evidence might appear to a

Il. The federal mail fraud, wire fraud, and bank fraud statutes 
each prohibit a “scheme or artifice to defraud.” 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341, 
1343, 1344. This language, which Congress used when it first enacted 
the mail fraud statute in 1872, compels the conclusion that the statutes 
prohibit only schemes involving a material misrepresentation or 

It is a “cardinal rule of statutory construction” (Molzof v. United 
States, 502 U.S. 301, 307 (1992)) that this Court “presume[s] that 


Congress incorporates the common-law meaning of the terms it uses 
if those terms * * * have accumulated settled meaning under * * * the 
common law and the statute [does not] otherwise dictate.” United 
States v. Wells, 519 U.S. 482, 491 (1997) (internal quotation 
omitted). The word “defraud” thus brings with it the common-law 
elements of fraud, which have always included materiality. See, e.g., 
Smith v. Richards, 38 U.S. (13 Pet.) 26, 39 (1839) (in action “to set 
aside a contract for fraud,” “the misrepresentation must be of 
something material”); BMW of N. Am., Inc. v. Gore, 517 U.S. 559, 
579 (1996) (“Actionable fraud requires a material misrepresentation 
Or omission”). 


The presumption that statutory language bears its common-law 
meaning can be overcome, but only if “Congress has unequivocally 
expressed an intent to the contrary.” NLRB v. Amax Coal Co., 453 
U.S. 322, 330 (1981). In this case, every indication leads to the 
conclusion that Congress meant the word “defraud” to bear its 
common-law meaning, including the materiality element. The early 
criminal analog of civil fraud that existed in 1872 (the crime of “false 
pretenses”) required proof of materiality, and the legislative history, 
the early case law, and the surrounding statutory language all confirm 
a similar understanding of the mail fraud statute. 


But even if there were any ambiguity as to the meaning of the 
mail fraud, wire fraud, and bank fraud statutes, they would still have 
to be interpreted to include a materiality element for at least two other 
reasons. First, because the word “defraud” so strongly implies that 
materiality is required, any other reading of the statutes would 
deprive citizens of their right “to be informed as to what the State 
commands or forbids.” Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451, 453 
(1939). Second, because “text, structure, and history fail to establish 
that the Government's position is unambiguously correct,” the rule of 
lenity also requires that this Court “resolve the ambiguity in 
[petitioner's] favor.” United States v. Granderson, 511 U.S. 39, 54 
(1994).

concluded from this that Congress does not “intend[] to make 
materiality an element under a statute whenever it employs the term 
‘representation’ in that statute.” Jbid. It is not the term 
“representation,” however, that is dispositive of the question 
presented. Instead, it is Congress’s use of the word “defraud” that 
obviated the need to use the word “material” in the mail fraud, wire 
fraud and bank fraud statutes because materiality already was an 
element of common-law fraud. There is no such thing as an 
“immaterial” fraud. If the court of appeals’ analysis were accepted, 
no “fraud” statute would ever have a materiality requirement unless 
Congress explicitly used the word “material” in the statute. That is 
not, and cannot be, the law. 


C. Due Process And The Rule Of Lenity Also Require 
That The Mail Fraud, Wire Fraud, And Bank Fraud 
Statutes Be Read To Include A Materiality Element. 


Even if there were any ambiguity as to the meaning of the mail 
fraud, wire fraud and bank fraud statutes (which there is not), 
principles of due process and fair warning and also the rule of lenity 
independently require that the statutes be read to include a materiality 
element. 

1. This Court has “often emphasized the need for clarity in 
the definition of criminal statutes, to provide ‘fair warning * * * in 
language that the common world will understand, of what the law 
intends to do if a certain line is passed.’” Hubbard v. United States, 
514 U.S. 695, 701 n.4 (1995) (quoting McBoyle v. United States, 283 
U.S. 25, 26 (1931) (Holmes, J.)). See also, e.g., Lanzetta v. New 
Jersey, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939) (“All are entitled to be informed as 
to what the State commands or forbids”). A person of ordinary 
intelligence certainly would conclude from the language of the mail 
fraud, wire fraud, and bank fraud statutes that they only criminalize 
“fraud,” which, as explained above, has always required proof of 
materiality. No one would think that he or she could be jailed for 30 
years and fined $1,000,000, as all three statutes provide, for a 


49 


scheme to “defraud” through immaterial misrepresentations or 
omissions. In Williams v. United States, 458 U.S. 279, 286 (1982), 
for instance, this Court refused to read § 1014’s prohibition on “false 
Statement)s]” as covering bad checks because “‘false statement’ is not 
a term that, in common usage, is often applied to characterize ‘bad 
checks.’” The same reasoning counsels against any reading of the 
mail fraud, wire fraud, and bank fraud statutes that would dispense 
with the element of materiality. 

“Due process requires that a criminal statute provide adequate 
notice to a person of ordinary intelligence that his 
conduct is illegal.” Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 77 (1976). Any 
reading of the mail fraud, wire fraud, and bank fraud statutes that 
omits materiality would raise serious constitutional doubts, and must 
therefore be rejected for this reason as well. E.g., United States v. X- 
Citement Video, Inc., 513 U.S. 64, 68 (1994); Edward J. DeBartolo 


Corp. v. Florida Gulf Coast Bldg. & Constr. Trades Council, 485 
U.S. 568, 575 (1988). 


2. The rule of lenity also leads to the same conclusion. It is 
“the familiar rule that, ‘where there is ambiguity in a criminal statute, 
doubts are resolved in favor of the defendant.’” Adamo Wrecking Co. 
v. United States, 434 U.S. 275, 285 (1978) (quoting United States v. 
Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 348 (1971)). As this Court explained in 
McNally, “when there are two rational readings of a criminal statute, 
one harsher than the other, we are to choose the harsher oniy when 
Congress has spoken in clear and definite language.” 483 U.S. at 
359-360. If the use of the word “defraud” — to say nothing of the 
terms “scheme or artifice,” “representation,” and “pretenses” — in 
the mail fraud, wire fraud, and bank fraud statutes does not 
conclusively establish that materiality is an element of these crimes, 
the statutes are at least ambiguous in this respect. Because “text, 
structure, and history fail to establish that the Government's position 
is unambiguously correct,” the rule of lenity requires “resolv{ing] the 
ambiguity in [petitioner's] favor.” United States v. Granderson, 511 
U.S. 39, 54 (1994). See also, e.g., Ratzlaf v. United States, 510 U.S. 
135, 148 (1994); Crandon v. United States, 494 U.S. 152, 158 


50 


(1990); Liparota v. United States, 471 U.S. 419, 427 (1985); Rewis 
v. United States, 401 U.S. 808, 812 (1971).
