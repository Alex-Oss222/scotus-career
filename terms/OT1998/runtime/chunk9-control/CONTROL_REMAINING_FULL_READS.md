## O'Sullivan v. Boerckel — 526 U.S. 838

**Historical topology and decree.** O'Connor's Court opinion had Rehnquist, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas, making six. Souter also concurred. Stevens dissented with Ginsburg and Breyer; Breyer separately dissented with Stevens and Ginsburg. The Court reversed the Seventh Circuit's judgment, 135 F.3d 1194. Do not describe a newly ordered federal evidentiary hearing or a Supreme Court merits adjudication of the three underlying constitutional claims.

**Court's ground and scope.** A state prisoner must afford the state courts one complete opportunity to resolve the federal claims through the State's established ordinary appellate process. Illinois discretionary review was a normal, simple, established component of that process: section 2254(c) concerns the right to raise a claim, not an entitlement to merits review. Rule 315(a)'s criteria neither confined the Illinois Supreme Court's discretion nor made routine claims unavailable. The three claims presented to the intermediate court but omitted from the leave petition concerned a knowing and intelligent Miranda waiver, voluntariness of the confession, and evidentiary sufficiency. Because the time for presenting those claims to the Illinois Supreme Court had expired, their omission produced procedural default.

**Qualifications that must survive a summary.** The Court did not require repetitive collateral presentation of claims already decided on direct review, or every extraordinary procedure alternative to ordinary review. It expressly respected state law making a procedure unavailable. Its narrow formulation was that a discretionary system, without more, does not itself make review unavailable. The Court acknowledged the distinction between technical exhaustion and proper presentation; it did not hold that an expired remedy remained presently available. The District Court had rejected Boerckel's attempted actual-innocence/miscarriage-of-justice showing under Schlup; that lower-court determination is procedural history, not a new Supreme Court resolution of innocence or an abolition of default exceptions.

**Souter.** He joined the Court while emphasizing an open question: a State might expressly identify discretionary review as outside its standard process and state that prisoners need not seek it before federal habeas review, even though the procedure occasionally yields relief. He did not announce a holding resolving that scenario. South Carolina's express rule was his example; Illinois had no equivalent rule established here.

**Stevens, with Ginsburg and Breyer.** He separated exhaustion, which concerns remedies still available when federal relief is requested, from procedural default, which asks whether the State received a fair opportunity to decide the claims. He would find no default after trial and an appeal as of right under Illinois's discretionary system. His grounds included the State's selective-review criteria, the Seventh Circuit's reading that Illinois would apply res judicata rather than waiver, effective appellate issue selection, and comity against imposing unwanted petitions. He accepted cause/prejudice and miscarriage-of-justice exceptions to default. His preference for respecting an express state opt-out rested on the default doctrine rather than allowing a State to define the federal exhaustion statute.

**Breyer, with Stevens and Ginsburg.** State preference should govern whether omission of discretionary review matters. Illinois's rules and low grant rate supported a presumption that it did not require such applications. He would reverse the majority's practical presumption, while stressing that a clear state statement remained an open means of avoiding unwanted petitions under Souter's understanding. This is dissenting reasoning, not a settled opt-out holding.

## Amoco Production Co. v. Southern Ute Indian Tribe — 526 U.S. 865

**Historical topology and decree.** Kennedy wrote for Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, and himself. Ginsburg dissented. Breyer took no part in consideration or decision, as expressly confirmed in the official report at 526 U.S. 880; there were seven votes to reverse and one to affirm. The decree reversed 151 F.3d 1251.

**Court's ground.** The coal reservations in the 1909 and 1910 Coal Lands Acts did not reserve coalbed methane. The relevant question was the ordinary contemporary meaning and practical congressional understanding of coal when the Acts passed. Coal meant the solid fuel; methane was separately understood as gas contained in, and naturally escaping from, coal. The enactments addressed preservation of that solid fuel after coal shortages and fraudulent land acquisition, and chose a narrower reservation than rejected proposals. Later statutes expressly reserving gas or all minerals confirmed the limited structure. Modern coal chemistry was background consistent with the conclusion, not the governing legal definition.

**Qualifications.** The conclusion concerns ownership under these federal reservations. It is not a universal state-property rule about every privately severed coal estate. Producer gas created by destructive distillation, other volatile matter expelled by heating, and solvent-derived coal extracts were distinguished from naturally escaping methane; the Court rejected the Government's suggestion that excluding methane necessarily excluded those coal components. Limited early methane use did not answer whether Congress considered methane part of coal. The Court did not decide competing interpretive canons: after finding the most natural reading excluded methane, it expressly found no need to reach the sovereign-grant canon or petitioners' competing canons.

**Mining and remedy limits.** A right to release gas where necessary and reasonable to mine coal could exist without ownership of the gas. The Court discussed that possibility through ordinary mineral-estate principles; it did not award unrestricted venting rights, decide liability for damaging another valuable estate, or resolve an actual damages dispute. The separate question of injury remained for ordinary negotiation or adjudication if it arose. Respondents' own recognition that migrated gas would belong to the gas estate undercut the claim that their interpretation eliminated split-estate problems.

**Ginsburg.** She would affirm substantially on the Tenth Circuit's and federal respondents' grounds. Methane was a liability when Congress acted; Congress more plausibly placed dominion and attendant responsibility with the coal owner. It was unclear that Congress intended dominion to change when the liability became an asset. She therefore invoked the canon resolving land-grant ambiguity for the sovereign, citing Western Nuclear. Her short opinion supplies this ambiguity/dominion rationale; it should not be expanded into additional grounds that it does not state.

## City of Chicago v. Morales — remaining dissents, 527 U.S. 41

**Historical topology.** Scalia's dissent is his alone. Thomas's dissent is joined by Rehnquist and Scalia. A join in Thomas's opinion must not be treated as Rehnquist's or Thomas's adoption of every argument in Scalia's separate opinion. These dissents opposed the Court's affirmance; neither supplies controlling reasoning. The majority/plurality and concurrence topology remains as stated in the prior verification receipt.

**Scalia.** He would reject the facial challenge under Salerno because constitutional applications existed; he regarded the rule as substantive federal law, not merely third-party standing that a state court could relax. He distinguished the First Amendment overbreadth exception and criticized the plurality's new combination of criminal liability, absent mental-state language, and protected liberty as a basis for facial review. His hypothetical valid application was expressly hypothetical. His accounts of three respondents' arrests rested on cited briefs, not on newly found trial facts.

Scalia denied a historically grounded fundamental liberty to loiter under Glucksberg and distinguished ordinary rational-basis-protected activity from specially protected constitutional rights. In his reading, the punishable act was willful failure to obey a dispersal order; actual purposeless loitering was not a separate required offense element, and a genuine hidden purpose did not prevent the order. Someone who did not hear an order or could not comply would not be guilty. The order supplied notice, and remaining in place without an apparent reason supplied an administrable trigger. His note 12 read apparent purpose in its ordinary legal sense as apparent lawful purpose.

He treated the Illinois Supreme Court's description of absolute discretion as a constitutional assessment rather than a binding construction of particular words. General Order 92-4's probable-cause criteria for gang membership mattered, supported by Ward and Hoffman Estates on enforcement-agency limiting constructions. Ordinary enforcement discretion and possible unlawful implementation did not establish facial vagueness. He challenged Breyer's proposed separation of notice vagueness from standardless discretion, and considered narrower gang-only laws no logical cure for a supposed defect in the same loitering language. Constitutional rights and rational-basis review remained limits on democratic preventive regulation; particular unreasonable orders or interference with protected expression could still be challenged as applied.

**Thomas, with Rehnquist and Scalia.** The long history of vagrancy and loitering laws defeated the asserted historically rooted fundamental right under Glucksberg. Papachristou decided vagueness, not a fundamental right to loiter; the travel decisions' holdings did not establish a right to remain anywhere. Thomas also viewed the offense as refusal to obey a dispersal order. Police have a traditional preventive peacekeeping function, necessarily involving discretion; reasonable gang identification and observed loitering sufficiently limited that discretion, analogously to practical probable-cause and reasonable-suspicion judgments. He did not claim mistakes or discriminatory enforcement were impossible: he preferred addressing them when they arose rather than facially invalidating the law.

For notice, dispersal was ordinarily understandable; even if advance notice of conduct triggering an order was required, the familiar term and its clear core defeated the claim that every application was vague. An apparently harmful-purpose alternative still depended on outward appearance rather than the actor's mental state, and an all-gang-members restriction required additional gang determinations. His democratic-policy and public-safety discussion supported these constitutional grounds, not a separately adjudicated factual finding that every respondent threatened anyone.
