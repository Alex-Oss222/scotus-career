## Threshold disposition comes first

**O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas** would vacate the judgment under review within the two granted components and remand for determination of the supervening continuing-controversy question. The remand asks whether Gable retains a concrete personal exposure to the trigger and own-funds window through supported present deterrence or a reasonable expectation of renewed candidacy, and whether that exposure remains redressable. His 1995 candidacy establishes a real past relation to this law; the unchanged statute and a generalized wish that it change do not themselves prove a continuing injury. The Registry's assertion that he did not file by January 26, 1999 is not a new judicial finding, and nonfiling alone does not defeat a supported deterrence claim. The reply's legal argument likewise supplies no new adjudicated current-candidacy facts.

This is a confined jurisdictional remand, not a merits invalidation or a final finding of mootness. The lower courts may decide the issue on existing supported materials or ordinary properly received jurisdictional submissions; no special evidentiary form, mandatory new trial, or automatically reopened merits record is prescribed. If a live controversy remains, they may proceed to the two claims under effective law. If it does not, they must apply the governing equitable treatment of the respective lower judgments in light of the actual cause and timing of mootness and the cross-petition's position. The Court does not predetermine that equitable issue from an assumed filing or voluntary abandonment. The unreviewed sponsor-identification, slating, and outside-donor merits receive no new Supreme Court disposition.

**Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer** would find the continuing deterrence and recurrence showing sufficient in this election setting and reach the two confined merits questions. They would not find that Gable filed in 1999. They regard the established past candidacy, ongoing challenge to the unchanged financing conditions, and specifically asserted unwillingness to campaign under them as a personal continuing burden distinct from a citizen's abstract objection. Their treatment does not make every old candidate automatically entitled to litigate all future elections.

No merits conclusion below becomes current law if the threshold-remand disposition controls. The merits rows are express alternative commitments, not extra votes that can be counted as a merits majority after a jurisdictional remand. The five threshold-remand Justices would use those merits positions if a Court majority first resolved the present live-controversy issue affirmatively on the supported current record. The strongest objection is that those principles still require this plaintiff's concrete interest; they do not replace proof appropriate to the litigation stage.

Lujan requires personal facts but does not impose a filing certificate as a universal prerequisite. The strongest objection is that a remand can itself prolong review beyond another election and discount a genuinely ongoing chill. The selected remand therefore does not require filing, does not find cessation, and permits supported deterrence or recurrence to establish the controversy.

## Alternative merits: exact statute and shared limits

These alternatives use the Sixth Circuit's reviewed operation. The ordinary participating-slate ceiling is $1.8 million separately for the primary and regular election, ordinarily funded by $300,000-$600,000 of qualifying contributions matched two for one. The statute also requires an opposing slate's $300,000 fundraising threshold for matching eligibility as described below. Following the relevant Registry certification of a nonparticipant's receipt of contributions **or expenditures** above the limit, participating ceilings are lifted and further qualifying fundraising receives the same two-for-one match without the former ceiling. This is not a dollar-for-dollar match of the nonparticipant's expenditures. The public-hearing finding route and written-notification route retain their distinct statutory terms.

The lower court construes trigger activation to lift the late-receipt window for all slates. Gable disputes that construction. These merits alternatives do not silently resolve the dispute against the lower construction. They do not make a factual finding that all slates regain access under unquoted exceptions. The separate runoff threshold and fourteen-day period are not the regular-election rule at issue.

The **own-funds window** bars knowingly placing new personal funds in the campaign during the final twenty-eight days, while permitting spending of funds already there. The grant covers that personal-funds branch, not the independent validity of the outside-donor prohibition. The candidate's new personal outlay is protected candidate expression in substance; labeling the deposit a contribution does not settle scrutiny. No special constitutional right to hide expenditures or avoid lawful reporting follows.

**M1 - responsive-financing challenge.** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas would hold the challenged expenditure-triggered extension of public matching unconstitutional on this operation. They would reject the premise that a subsidy label ends the burden inquiry. The nonparticipant's fundraising or spending is the legal event unlocking unlimited further public matching for opposing qualifying slates; conditioning that adverse public consequence on protected activity creates the burden. The State's voluntary-program and anticorruption interests do not sufficiently justify this response, and leveling the private candidate's advantage does not do so. The participant's own further fundraising condition is important but does not remove the State's selected trigger or unlimited potential increment. These four would reverse the trigger approval and remand for implementing and severability treatment at the scope actually held invalid. They do not automatically abolish ordinary public grants, require that other candidates remain capped, or determine that ceiling-release and late-window-release provisions must fall independently of the subsidy component.

**M2 - responsive-financing challenge.** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer would reject the facial coercion challenge and affirm trigger approval. The nonparticipant retains his funds and freedom to spend; additional funds depend on the participant's own qualifying fundraising rather than an automatic proportional payment keyed to the opponent's speech. Supporting viable voluntary participation and expanding electoral advocacy supply legitimate work for the program. Their Day dissent's reservation of materially coercive or punitive arrangements remains: there is no constitutional numeric safe harbor, and this judgment would not authorize a direct spending restriction in the name of equalization. The lower court's discussion of rational financial choice and competitive pressure does not establish legal compulsion or that this candidate's protected advocacy is actually forbidden. They do not adopt an absolute rule that a public subsidy can never burden another speaker.

**P - personal funds.** If merits are reached, all eight would affirm the invalidation of the twenty-eight-day ban insofar as it bars a nonparticipating candidate's newly supplied personal funds. Buckley's own-speech distinction, strengthened by unanimous Kruse and Suster, applies to the actual ability to finance additional expression at the decisive campaign stage. Advance deposit is materially different from choosing later in response to the campaign; available earlier speech does not justify forbidding additional speech. The program's reporting and implementation interests deserve consideration, but do not establish the necessary fit for a complete personal-funds cutoff. Permitting prompt disclosure is analytically different from guaranteeing opponents time to answer, and the latter cannot justify this speech prohibition merely as equalization. No new reporting regime is judicially enacted.
