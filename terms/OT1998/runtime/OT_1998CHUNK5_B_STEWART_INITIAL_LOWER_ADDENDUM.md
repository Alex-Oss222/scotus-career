## Exact majority gas section

```text
F. Lethal Gas as a Method of Execution
In Arizona, all condemned prisoners will be executed by means of lethal injection unless those who were sentenced before the Arizona legislature adopted lethal injection affirmatively choose lethal gas as the method to be used in their cases. A.R.S. § 13-704(B). The LaGrands come within the group having this option under state law. Thus, they would be executed by lethal gas only if they affirmatively choose to do so.
The court held in Poland v. Stewart, 117 F.3d 1094, 1104 (9th Cir.1997), that Poland’s claim that lethal gas was an unconstitutional method of execution was not ripe. For the same reasons, the- LaGrands’ claim is not ripe and cannot be considered. Id.
```

## Neutral clarification

The majority treated the gas claim as unripe because execution by gas required an affirmative election; it relied on Poland. Section II.F does not contain an express reservation of a later claim or an express statement deciding whether the district default ground survives. Its disposition of this component on prematurity is established. Neither an express preservation term nor an affirmative decision eliminating every default barrier may be attributed to that section. These limits refine the source description without resolving successive classification or the default question.

Pregerson dissented specifically on gas ripeness. He invoked Campbell's treatment of choice and challenged the distinction between an elective and a default method, then argued that the issue was fit for review and that delay would force last-minute litigation. His discussion anticipates a renewed gas claim after the required choice. That is a dissenting forecast and argument, not the majority's express preservation order or a finding about the later emergency filing. His statement that choosing a method cannot automatically insulate an unconstitutional punishment is an identified lower-court position; it is not a current Supreme Court holding.

The majority describes the optional class by reference to sentencing before legislative adoption; the dissent's statutory footnote supplies the more precise offense-date trigger and twenty-day choice deadline. No separate finding about service, exact election timing or compliance with that deadline follows.
