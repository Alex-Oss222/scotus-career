## Dispositions and operative grounds

Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer affirm the reviewed conviction judgment: a present, actual intent to cause death or serious bodily harm if necessary to obtain the vehicle satisfies section 2119. Scalia and Thomas reverse the appellate affirmance of the three carjacking judgments and remand under their narrower construction, with the dependent firearm judgments returned consistently. Neither dissent position orders an acquittal, decides the unadjudicated sufficiency questions or overturns the independent chop-shop convictions. No nonparticipation is established.

The six affirming Associates retain the actual-purpose, timing and proof conditions. The jury must find this defendant's purpose beyond reasonable doubt when he demands or takes the vehicle; an intimidating bluff, mere foresight, recklessness, minor injury, an accomplice's purpose alone, or willingness to decide later does not suffice. A hope that compliance will make the settled violent purpose unnecessary does not negate that purpose. The unlawful vehicle demand is not a condition that makes the prohibited injury lawful. The actual charge and recharge preserved these distinctions.

All six reject an additional statutory requirement that resistance be objectively probable or that the actor hope, believe or know it will occur. Evidence about probability may bear on whether an asserted purpose is real; it is not a new element. This answers the statutory rebuttal within the supplied question, not an unidentified independent trial-instruction request. The frozen preservation limitation remains: neither the existence nor absence of a distinct probability instruction request is established, and missing retrieval is not forfeiture.

## Dissent grounds and permitted accommodations

**Scalia:** The disputed category is a plan to inflict harm upon resistance the actor hopes will not occur. His ordinary-language objection is not that every imaginable condition defeats intent: very remote contingencies may be implicit in ordinary intent, and a reasonably likely condition the actor hopes will occur can be consistent with ordinary usage. His objection is that the unqualified statutory term does not cover the adverse, unwanted resistance contingency merely because the actor has a plan for it. The state decisions are divided and do not establish an accepted federal term of art overriding that ordinary meaning. Congress's deterrent purpose does not erase the means and limits it enacted. Even if the text remains ambiguous after construction, lenity favors the defendant; it applies to degrees of punishment and coverage, not only otherwise innocent conduct.

This clarification confines the frozen rejection of conditional intent to the disputed kind. It is not support for adding a free-standing probability/belief formula to section 2119 or a finding that a particular probability existed here. Scalia does not reach that proposed alternative as an independent instructional ground after choosing the narrower statutory construction. No circumstance of remote inevitability or a hoped-for likely event is silently inserted into the record to obtain a join. A new formulation requiring such a premise would need its own supported compatibility and reconciliation review.

**Thomas:** Preserve his distinct narrower ground. The statute does not define intent to include conditional purpose, and the cited decisions and secondary material do not establish a sufficiently settled historical convention to presume Congress adopted that specialized meaning. He need not adopt Scalia's entire linguistic taxonomy, empirical concern about prosecutorial use, or every example from other crimes. The text/tradition objection supports the same result; residual criminal ambiguity reinforces the frozen narrower construction. Model-code provisions and selected state statutes are interpretive evidence, not enacted federal law. A substantive separate explanation may carry this difference; no historical publication pattern is mechanically required.

For the six affirmers, the strongest contrary objection remains Congress's deliberate addition of a mental element. Their actual-purpose requirement answers it without guessing that the amendment was a drafting error. They do not require commission or attempt of a separate injury offense to prove the mental element. Neither group relies on a presumed drafting mistake, private dialogue, generalized violence, an automatic gun inference or future law.
