## 2. Source-supported record and its limits

### Notice and invitation

The May 30, 1997 notice was addressed to current holders of ADM common stock and issued pursuant to Rule 23.1 and a district-court order. It warned that approval would bar challenges to the fairness, reasonableness or adequacy of the settlement and pursuit of settled derivative claims. It expressly disclaimed any view about the truth of allegations or merits of claims and defenses. The described settlement included payment of $8 million to ADM, specified governance measures, dismissal with prejudice, and releases of defined settled claims, while reserving specified company claims. Those descriptions establish what notice communicated, not that every described release was legally effective or that the settlement was fair. The errata at J.A. 142 clarified that only Illinois-action claims were being settled in that court; Delaware counsel would later apply for dismissal there. No later Delaware action is inferred.

J.A. 140 permitted any current shareholder to appear at the July 7 hearing and show cause why settlement or judgment should or should not be approved. Being heard required written objections and their basis, the supporting papers and briefs, filing by July 3, and proof of service on the specified counsel. Unless the court otherwise ordered, failure to object in that manner would waive objections to fairness, reasonableness and adequacy. The preliminary order, J.A. 148, likewise permitted shareholders of record as of May 29 to appear and show cause and specified the July 3 delivery, service and receipt requirements. The difference between current-holder language in the notice and record-holder language in the order is preserved; no eligibility dispute is established here.

This was an express invitation with conditions for objection and consequences for failing to object. It was not an unconditional command that every shareholder personally appear. The notice did not state an express intervention requirement for being heard. Neither that omission nor the invitation itself establishes appellate party status. No conclusion about the legal effect of these forms is supplied by labeling them compulsory process or mere advertising.

### Appearance, objections and district-court action

The printed hearing excerpts identify Mark Hansen as counsel for both CalPERS and the Florida board as objectors; the judge admitted counsel pro hac vice. The judge heard argument on the objectors’ request to postpone or vacate the hearing and hold it later. Counsel questioned settlement terms, investigation and available time. The two sides disputed the timing and sufficiency of actual awareness: objector counsel said his clients became aware of the settlement paper about two weeks before the hearing and he had been retained about ten days earlier; proponents described earlier notice and contacts. These statements are competing advocacy, not findings of defective service or forfeiture. The judge’s acceptance of an appearance and hearing of objections is established; it is not an order granting intervention.

The printed hearing selection jumps from transcript page 20 to page 79. It therefore does not supply a complete account of intervening argument or a separately stated disposition of every preliminary request. At J.A. 345–347 the judge announced approval as fair, reasonable and adequate after hearing proponents and opponents, discussed litigation strengths, discovery, collection, delay and opposition, and found no basis for collusion. These are the district court’s findings and reasons, not findings of this Court. The appellate-status question does not require this Court to decide whether they were correct.

The notice of appeal is dated July 14, 1997, names both institutions, and identifies the July 7 final judgment and dismissal and July 7 attorney-fee/expense order. The plaintiffs’ August 15 motion to dismiss the appeal asserted that neither institution was a party or had sought intervention. The lower court applied Marino and Appellate Rule 3(c), rejected the circuit’s earlier nonparty-appeal practice, distinguished shareholder injury from party status, and preserved appeals from intervention denials. ## 3. Legal sources that bear on the competing routes

**Izumi.** The controlling 1993 decision distinguishes Article III interest, statutory party status under §1254(1), and proper question presentation. An undisturbed denial of intervention and financial interest alone did not allow review of the underlying vacatur order. Its intervention question was separate and not properly presented. Felzen instead presents the existence of a representative-objector appellate route itself, with no intervention denial to reverse. Izumi supplies an important party-status boundary; it does not expressly decide whether Rule 23.1 appearance and objection provide limited status under §1291. Section 1254, §1291 finality, Rule 23.1, Rule 24 and Appellate Rule 3(c) must retain their separate functions.

**Richards and Matsushita.** Valid representative adjudication may bind absent persons, but shared interests and notice alone do not create adequate representation; inadequacy is not independently cured by objection or opt-out opportunities. Notice of another person’s suit does not impose a general intervention duty to preserve an otherwise independent claim. That preclusion protection is distinct from appellate status. Here the underlying derivative claim is corporate, unlike Richards’s direct personal tax claims. Neither the derivative label nor the ability to object establishes adequacy on this record, and no fresh inadequacy finding is necessary simply to identify the route for appeal.

**Tryforos, 518 F.2d 1258 (1975).** Circuit Judge Stevens wrote the opinion. Spanos responded to a Rule 23.1 notice and attempted to object to dismissal with prejudice because the corporation had not been served. The district judge imposed additional appearance conditions and rejected his participation; Stevens held that requirements appropriate to assuming further prosecution were not necessarily appropriate to this limited objection. Footnote 22 expressly recognizes appeal by a nonparty shareholder who appears under Rule 23.1 notice to object to proposed dismissal or settlement, and excludes a shareholder who fails to appear. This is direct own-writing evidence, not unidentified circuit custom.

The actual merits objection concerned the form of dismissal and a defect apparent in the record, while Felzen concerns settlement approval and associated fees. The footnote’s stated rule includes settlements; the body’s distinction between limited objection and taking over prosecution must also be confronted. The holding does not give every shareholder control of the corporation’s litigation. Its separate rulings about counsel’s defective appeal notice, service on the corporation, and attorney fees must not be collapsed into the objector-status proposition. Tryforos predates Marino; its continuing force must be assessed under later Supreme Court authority rather than assumed from authorship or discarded without addressing its precise premise. Cohen, Ackert and Pianta are cited in the footnote; this packet does not independently certify their holdings or intervention histories.

**Johnson, 289 U.S. 479 (1933), especially 495–496.** Johnson and Boehm chose a separate receivership suit instead of appearing in the original proceeding. At page 495 the Court explained that, had they accepted that proceeding’s invitation, they could have objected to the judge’s authority or the receivers’ fitness and appealed if those objections were rejected. The original orders allowed other creditors and interested persons to be heard; the opinion separately recounts formal intervention by Manhattan and creditor committees. Footnote 4 invokes the then-applicable receivership appeal provision and earlier cases.

Page 496 immediately addresses Equity Rule 37: intervention was subordinate to and recognized the propriety of the main proceeding, but that restriction would not have obstructed these litigants because they agreed a receivership was appropriate and disputed only the judge and receivers. This is the strongest textual obstacle to treating page 495 as a decision that formal intervention is unnecessary. Conversely, the page 495 account describes invitation, appearance, objection and appeal without expressly prescribing a separate formal motion. Johnson distinguishes a direct attack in the original proceeding from a collateral attack; it did not decide modern Rule 23.1 or Rule 24, nor adjudicate an actual rejected direct appeal by these petitioners. Both passages and the receivership setting must travel together. Neither an unlimited nonparty appeal rule nor an unequivocal formal-motion requirement follows by quotation alone.

**Marino, 484 U.S. 301 (1988).** The full official report confirms that white officers objected at a Title VII settlement hearing but did not intervene, initially or for appeal; the Court rejected their direct appeal. It states the general party-or-properly-become-party rule and identifies intervention and review of a denial as the appropriate route. Hearing participation alone was insufficient there. Its separate collateral-action judgment was affirmed by an equally divided Court and supplies no general preclusion rule. The report does not expressly resolve the distinct Rule 23.1 derivative-shareholder route. Extending its rule or distinguishing that route requires reasoning; neither construction is a recovered fact about Felzen.

**Harper and remedy.** Once this Court applies a federal rule to its litigants, it governs still-open direct-review cases within that rule’s substantive scope. Selective prospectivity is barred; the decision does not dispose of every conceivable prospective ruling or reopen final judgments. Genuine procedural and remedial questions remain distinct and cannot disguise selective prospectivity. Calling party status jurisdictional is a legal premise to justify, not a license to ignore the actual scope of Harper or to invent equitable jurisdiction. United Airlines v. McDonald remains a comparison concerning timely postjudgment intervention, as the original preflight identifies; it does not supply a missing motion or an automatic entitlement in this record. Kamen’s corporate-claim/demand distinction likewise supplies no automatic resolution of appellate status.

## 4. Strongest supported lawful paths

**Limited representative-objector review.** A participating shareholder may argue that Rule 23.1’s recognized objection role, followed here by appearance and actual hearing, provides limited status to obtain review of the rejected settlement objections. The claimed source is that procedural role in adjudicating the corporate claim, not financial interest or notice alone. Tryforos directly states the route; Johnson supplies a related equity analogy; Izumi involved an undisturbed denial and a different presented question. The route can preserve the corporation’s control of prosecution and distinguish Marino’s adverse-interest outsiders without allowing appeals by shareholders who did not appear.

Its strongest opposing case is that the Rules deliberately distinguish objection from becoming a party, and Marino denied an appeal despite actual hearing participation. Johnson’s next-page intervention discussion and special receivership setting may undercut the asserted independent route. Tryforos’s limited form-of-dismissal holding and its intervening legal history must be addressed. A proponent must define the role, conditions and scope sufficient for appellate status; notice and corporate ownership alone cannot do that work. Allowing review would ordinarily require reversal of the appellate dismissal and further appellate proceedings, not invalidation of the settlement or a fee award.

**Intervention required for appeal.** A shareholder may object under Rule 23.1 while remaining outside the parties entitled to appeal; Rule 24 supplies the route to party status, and review of an actual denial protects against improper exclusion. Marino’s hearing-objector facts support that separation, and Izumi prevents economic interest from becoming appellate status by itself. Johnson can be read to assume proper intervention, with Rule 37 posing no obstacle. On that construction, Tryforos’s broader statement does not survive the governing rule or is confined to its limited posture.

Its strongest opposing case is the precise representative procedure at issue: invited shareholders appeared to protect a corporate claim about to be disposed of, and the governing circuit precedent expressly recognized their limited appeal. Merely repeating “nonparty” begs the question whether this recognized role is sufficient for this purpose. The path must explain why the Rule 23.1 role does not qualify, confront Stevens’s Tryforos distinction, and avoid turning Richards’s absence of a general intervention duty for preclusion into the opposite rule. Requiring intervention can sustain dismissal without endorsing settlement fairness. It cannot rest on a fictional denied motion or create an automatic late-intervention opportunity.

**Narrower procedural or reliance disposition.** A supported remedy could address a legally available intervention opportunity or transition only if its source and actual procedural predicates are established. This transfer establishes neither a pending motion nor a right to reopening. A pure-prospectivity question must first satisfy the grant and presentation rules; its appearance in the original petition does not establish that it was granted. Harper’s reservation does not itself authorize prospective relief, while its applied-rule holding cannot be expanded into a universal answer before identifying the governing precedent and reach. Do not stop the bounded party-status decision merely because these optional remedies need more record; do not adopt one on invented facts.

No path here determines settlement fairness, attorney compensation, collusion, adequacy of representation, a direct individual shareholder claim, or the full collateral-preclusion boundary. Those issues remain distinct unless a further authorized question and supporting record makes one necessary.
