## Holdings

The following two holdings are supported by the opinion of the Court and all nine Justices. They resolve the knowing-deception theory and the summary-judgment record. They do not establish a general primary-fraud recklessness rule.

### A knowingly false execution undertaking can be primary securities fraud

**Operative rule.** A broker-dealer that accepts a customer order without price instructions while knowingly intending to disregard the best-execution undertaking it thereby represents can commit primary deception under § 10(b) and Rule 10b-5 when the representation is materially false and the other requirements of a private securities-fraud action are proved. Breach of care or fiduciary duty, inferior execution or the broker's status alone does not establish such fraud; the customer must establish legally sufficient reliance, causation and actual loss as well as the required deception and culpability.

**Controlling explanation.** The parties agreed below that accepting an order without price instructions implies an undertaking consistent with best execution and that acceptance with an intention to breach that undertaking would be a material misrepresentation. Their agreement does not establish what the undertaking required in each transaction or concede any defendant's liability.

First Jersey recognizes a broker's own knowing deception through implied transaction representations. That principle reaches a knowingly false execution undertaking, but First Jersey's proved facts and SEC-enforcement posture do not establish these private customers' reliance, injury or damages. Santa Fe Industries v. Green excludes fiduciary unfairness without deception, and Ernst & Ernst v. Hochfelder excludes negligence. The present theory respects both limits because it requires a false undertaking made with knowledge of its falsity, rather than a later failure to exercise due care.

The securities firms contend that customary execution at the national best bid and offer defined their undertaking and defeated culpability. Such custom is relevant to the actual representation and state of mind. It cannot conclusively immunize an undertaking that the firms understood to be false when made. The customers must prove the representation actually made, including material instructions or disclosed limits, and every other private-action element. A less favorable price creates no presumption of reliance or automatic damage award.

The evidence permits the knowing-deception theory to proceed. The Court therefore does not decide a general primary-liability recklessness standard or transfer Central Bank's separate affirmative-assistance standard to this direct claim.

### The disputed contemporary execution evidence precludes the categorical summary judgment

**Operative rule.** Execution at the national best bid and offer, or through the Small Order Execution System, does not by itself defeat a primary-fraud claim where evidence supports a genuine dispute that better terms were reasonably available and the firm knowingly accepted the order on a false undertaking. Reasonable availability is assessed under contemporary conditions, including order size, the security's trading characteristics, speed, clearing costs and the expense and difficulty of market access, as well as quoted price; later rules or technology do not establish earlier duties, access or culpability.

**Controlling explanation.** The claims concern transactions between November 4, 1992, and November 4, 1994. Customers identify three possible sources of better terms: SelectNet or Instinet quotations, crossing opposing customer market orders, and matching customer orders with in-house limit orders. Expert and industry declarations, evidence of the firms' own use of alternative systems and evidence of particular trades with contemporaneous better quotations support a factual dispute. The firms' evidence of expense, delay and ordinary industry practice supports their defense, but does not conclusively resolve that dispute in their favor.

The relevant question is the best terms reasonably available to the customer. A nominally better quotation need not improve the customer’s execution after practical costs and timing are considered. Conversely, a customary published benchmark is not conclusive when a firm knowingly ignores a feasible improvement inconsistent with its undertaking. Small-order execution through the Small Order Execution System is part of that practical inquiry; the record establishes no universal safe harbor, and not all orders used that system.

This reasoning applies separately to the asserted electronic quotations, crossed market orders and in-house limit orders. It mandates no particular venue, guarantees no best possible price and imposes no universal search of every quotation for every order. Later execution regulations cannot be imposed retroactively on these transactions. Particular-trade evidence supports a real damages controversy, but does not prove injury in every trade, determine class certification or establish reliance, causation or recoverable amounts. The ordinary rules for resolving the remaining issues continue to apply.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Securities and Exchange Commission v. First Jersey Securities, Inc.:** Applied for knowing primary deception through a broker's implied transaction representations. Its established findings and SEC-enforcement posture are distinguished from the disputed facts and private-action elements here.
- **Central Bank of Denver v. First Interstate Bank of Denver:** Its separate culpable-affirmative-assistance route remains intact. Its severe-recklessness standard for that route is not made a general primary-fraud standard, and assistance liability is unnecessary to this disposition.
- **United States v. O'Hagan:** Its requirement of actual deception connected to a securities transaction remains intact. The Court does not extend its distinct source-deception theory to supply the representation here.
- **Santa Fe Industries v. Green, 430 U.S. 462:** Followed. Fiduciary unfairness or breach alone does not constitute federal securities fraud.
- **Ernst & Ernst v. Hochfelder, 425 U.S. 185:** Followed. Negligence is insufficient; the Court does not treat Hochfelder as resolving every primary-liability recklessness question.
- **Basic Inc. v. Levinson:** The requirement of legally sufficient reliance is preserved. A worse execution price supplies no automatic reliance presumption in this case.

## Law After Decision

A customer's claim may proceed on evidence of a broker's own knowingly false best-execution undertaking. Neither a published quotation benchmark nor industry custom categorically forecloses that claim. Practical execution circumstances and the full requirements of private fraud remain essential. The Court leaves the broader primary-recklessness question unresolved, and no new execution regulation or automatic price guarantee follows.

## Separate Writings

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring; no joins.** The Chief Justice joins the Court's knowing-deception holding and affirmance. He would also hold that reckless, materially misleading acceptance of customer orders under the existing best-execution undertaking can constitute primary § 10(b) and Rule 10b-5 fraud when every ordinary private-action requirement is proved. Acceptance communicates an intention to honor that undertaking; an actionable false representation need not be confined to a defendant's express statement. His broader culpability position goes beyond the sufficient ground the Court adopts and does not become the Court's rule.

He agrees that best execution depends on terms reasonably available under contemporary market conditions, that industry custom informs but does not conclusively resolve culpability, and that later SEC rules cannot create retroactive liability. He would leave knowing or reckless falsity, reliance and actual damage for case-specific proof. Neither breach of fiduciary duty alone, execution at the national best bid and offer nor the existence of a better price automatically establishes fraud, and the concurrence recognizes no reliance presumption from price disparity.
