STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. Introduction. 

Respondent American Dental Association ("ADA") is 
a Chicago-based professional organization whose purpose is 
to promote the art and science of dentistry and the oral 
health of the public. It maintains an office of approximately 
17 employees in Washington, D.C. who advocate the 
interests of the ADA and its members’ patients before 

Congress and Executive Branch agencies (Pet App. 43a; 
Oct. 23, 1995 Tr. 102-03). 

Plaintiff was a high-level, highly compensated 
employee who worked in the ADA’s Washington office. 
From the time of her hire in 1988, she received regular 
raises in salary. There is no evidence that she was ever 
harassed or belittled, and she testified at trial that she 
generally was pleased about the way she was treated 
(Oct. 19, 1995 Tr. 53, 55, 59). In 1991, her position was 
raised in grade, with an increase in pay. After her first and 
only request for promotion was denied in 1992, she did not 
tell anyone she believed the decision was based on her 
gender. Instead, she testified that she was willing to "be 
gracious about it" and accept the outcome if she could have 
a Salary increase equivalent to what she would have earned 
in the promotion (id. Tr. 20, 57-58, 107-08). While she 
received in increase in pay thereafter, this lawsuit ensued 
(id. Tr. 19-20).

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 

The Civil Rights Act of 1991, § 102, 105 Stat. 1072, 
provides for the award of punitive damages under Title VII 
of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 in cases of intentional 

discrimination against nongovernmental entities, in addition 
to remedies already available under Title VII, and in addition 

to compensatory damages (for future pecuniary loss, pain 
and suffering, and for other nonpecuniary losses) "if the 
complaining party demonstrates that the respondent engaged 
in a discriminatory practice or discriminatory practices with 
malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected 
rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). 

Plaintiff Carole Kolstad’s argument that all cases of 
intentional discrimination are eligible for jury consideration 
of punitive damages is inconsistent with the two-tiered 
structure of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, the common law 
nature of punitive damages, and the traditional roles of the 
court and the jury. Actions undertaken with malice or with 

13 

reckless indifference to federally protected rights uecessarily 
involve egregious conduct, and it is the court’s role to make 
the threshold determination of whether sufficient evidence 
exists for the jury to consider the award of punitive damages 
under the statutory standard. 

The Act provides for two tiers of liability, one for 
basic liability and for compensatory damages, and the other 
for punitive damages available only if the plaintiff makes the 
additional showing under 42 U.S.C. § 198la(b)(1). If the 
only check on the award of punitive damages were the jury’s 
"discretionary moral judgment,” as urged by plaintiff, that 
would "flatten" the two-tiered scheme of the statute in a 
manner unintended by Congress (Pet. App. 6a). 

Plaintiff incorrectly argues that punitive damages 
depends only on the isolated mental state of the defendant. 
Evil motive (in the case of malice) or other requisite intent 
is a necessary but not sufficient ingredient for the award of 
punitive damages under Title VII, because "[t}he focus is on 
the character of the tortfeasor’s conduct in imposing punitive 
damages." Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 54 (1983). The 
type of conduct required for punitive damages is a 
“discriminatory practice" committed with "malice" or with 
"reckless indifference to the federally protected rights." 
Mental state cannot be separated from the conduct at issue 
under the statute. The conduct necessary to impose punitive 
damages must be egregious, as the very purpose of punitive 
damages awards is to punish a defendant "for his outrageous 
conduct and to deter him and others from similar conduct in 
the future." Smith v. Wade, id., quoting Restatement 
(Second) of Torts § 908(1) (1979).

A. A Comparison Between the Compensatory 
and Punitive Damages Provisions in the 

1991 Civil Rights Act Confirms the Two- 
Tiered Structure of the Act. 

In the 1991 Civil Rights Act, Congress allowed for 
compensatory and punitive damages for violations of Title 
VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a. 
The structure of the Act provides for two tiers of culpability 
in cases of intentional discrimination, one for basic liability 
and compensatory damages, and the other for punitive 
damages. 

The Act authorizes Title VII plaintiffs to recover 
punitive damages against an employer "who engaged in 
unlawful intentional discrimination (not an employment 
practice that is unlawful because of disparate impact)" where 
the plaintiff: 

demonstrates that [the employer] engaged in 
a discriminatory practice or discriminatory 
practices with malice or reckless indifference 

to the federally protected rights of an 
aggrieved individual. 

42 U.S.C. § 198la(a)(1) and (b)(1). By this language, the 
Act makes clear that the availability of punitive damages is 
limited to a specific subclass of cases: ones involving 
intentional discrimination but, more narrowly, ones in which 

15 

the plaintiff also demonstrates that the employer engaged in 
purposeful discrimination with "malice" or with “reckless 
indifference" to federally protected rights. 

Whereas the predicate for both compensatory and 
punitive damages is "intentional discrimination," 42 U.S.C. 
§ 198la(a)(1), compensatory damages are available 
irrespective of any additional showing concerning the nature 
of the employer’s actions or the mental state associated with 
those actions. The Act simply states that "the complaining 
party may recover compensatory damages" in Title VII cases 
of intentional disCrimination "as allowed in subsection (b)." 
Id. 

Subsection (b) is headed "Compensatory and punitive 
damages." No separate evidentiary standard of proof for 
compensatory damages is set forth. Rather, subsection (b) 
lists the types of compensatory damages recoverable ("future 
pecuniary losses, emotional pain, suffering, inconvenience, 
mental anguish, loss of enjoyment of life, and other 
nonpecuniary losses"), § 198la(b)(3), states that they are 
exclusive of economic damages otherwise recoverable under 
Title VII, § 1981a(b)(2), and places a cap on the total award 

for compensatory and punitive damages, § 1981a(b)(3). 

By contrast, subsection (b) sets forth a separate 
evidentiary burden for punitive damages. Subsection (b)(1) 
is entitled "Determination of punitive damages." Under that 
provision, a complaining party may recover punitive 
damages if she "demonstrates" that the employer "engaged 
in a discriminatory practice or discriminatory practices" with 
"malice or with reckless indifference to the federally 
protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 

The very structure of this provision shows that 
something more than proof of basic liability or the award of 

16 

compensatory damages is required. If no additional showing 
of culpability were required, the provision would not set 
forth separately what the plaintiff needed to "demonstrate[]}" 
with regard to malicious or recklessly indifferent 
"discriminatory practices." Instead, the provision for 
punitive damages would be parallel to the provision for 
compensatory damages (which requires no additional 
showing), and would simply recite that punitive damages 
could be awarded in addition to compensatory damages, 
without a higher standard of proof. The statute requires 
more, and that is the most basic reason why plaintiff's 
argument that all cases involving intentional discrimination 
should go to the jury on punitive damages is inconsistent 
with the language of § 1981a. 

The conclusion reached by the court of appeals flows 
from this structure of the Act (Pet. App. 5a): 

We think that by enacting a separate 
provision setting out a special standard for the 
imposition of punitive damages, Congress 
showed that it did not intend to make punitive 
damages automatically available in the 
standard case of intentional discrimination 
under Title VII. The structure of the statute - 
- one standard for basic liability, another for 
the exceptional remedy of punitive liabili 
strongly suggests that, before the question of 
punitive damages can go to the jury, the 
evidence of the defendant’s culpability must 
exceed what is needed to show intentional 
discrimination.

B. The Award of Punitive Damages Under 
Title VII Depends on the Nature of the 
Discriminatory Conduct, Not Simply the 
Mental State of the Defendant. 

Plaintiff incorrectly argues that the isolated mental 
state of the defendant is solely determinative on the issue of 
punitive damages under § 198la, and that this result is 
required by a "literal" reading of the statute. In reality, 
plaintiff's position is that the words "reckless indifference” 
should be read in isolation from the rest of the words in the 
punitive damages provision. Indeed, plaintiff argues that the 
punitive damages standard is even further removed from the 
whole text, by parsing "reckless" and "indifference" apart 
from the rest of the words in the law. 

ADA agrees that this case should be resolved on the 
basis of the language of the statute. But the entire provision, 
not just a piece of it, must be read in the context of the 
statute as a whole. Robinson v. Shell Oil Co., 519 U.S. 
337, 117 S. Ct. 843, 846 (1997); Conroy v. Aniskoff, 507 
U.S. 511, 515 (1993); King v. St. Vincent Hosp., 502 U.S. 
215, 221 (1991). While the employer’s mental state is an 
obvious essential ingredient in a violation committed with 
malice or with reckless indifference to federally protected 
rights, it is the discriminatory conduct associated with the 
mental state that is determinative. The Act requires the 
plaintiff to prove that the defendant (1) acted ("engaged in 
a discriminatory practice or discriminatory practices") (2) 
with malice or with reckless indifference to federally 
protected rights. The two cannot be separated. Otherwise, 
as plaintiff would have it, defendants would be punished 
unfairly for thoughts isolated from discriminatory actions. 
Cf. Williams v. Cerberonics, Inc., 871 F.2d 452, 457 (4th 
Cir. 1989) (personal animus as opposed to racial animus not 

18 

a basis for Title VII liability). That is not what the statute 
says. 

Plaintiff barely mentions the "malice" prong of 
§ 1981a in her brief, but the difference between malice and 
reckless indifference in the present context reinforces the 
Statutory requirement not to separate the discriminatory 
conduct from the employee’s mental state. 

Malice means with evil motive or an actual intent to 
injure the plaintiff. See, e.g., Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 
48, 56 (1983). Reckless indifference entails more than 
negligence and less than malicious behavior with respect to 
the discriminatory conduct, a point not in dispute (see Pet. 
Br. 17). See Farmer v. Brennan, 511 U.S. 825, 835-37 
(1994) (recklessness has been equated with deliberate 
indifference; reckless disregard of substantial risk of serious 
harm is same as deliberate indifference to that risk; 
deliberate indifference is more than mere negligence, but less 
than acting for the very purpose of causing harm or with 
knowledge it will result). Malicious and reckless conduct 
are by definition egregious (see § II.A., infra). 

If anything, the malice inquiry focuses more on 
mental state apart from conduct than does recklessness. 
Because evil motive or actual intent to harm indicating 
malice is by its nature egregious, there is no need to gauge 
the seriousness of the conduct separately from the mental 
state where malice is involved. For reckless indifference, 
another layer of analysis enters the picture, because the focus 
is more-often than not on whether the defendant should have 
known (rather than actually knew) that it was acting in 
wanton disregard of federally protected rights. For 
recklessness the statute requires a sufficiently heightened 
state of conduct to demonstrate objective awareness of the 
wrongdoing (the “should have known” standard), one always 

19 

greater than is sufficient for basic liability. The more 
serious the conduct, the more likely it will cross the 
threshold from negligence to recklessness. The EEOC 
recognizes this principle: "The more blatant and outrageous 
the discrimination, the stronger the inference that the 
employer knew or should have been aware of the substantial 
risk that its behavior had crossed the line of legality." (Brief 
for the United States and the Equal Opportunity Commission 
as Amici Curiae {"EEOC Br."] at 11). Thus, the 
determination of recklessness, more than malice, depends 
upon the relationship between the mental state of the 
defendant and the degree of seriousness of the discriminatory 
conduct. 

Plaintiff's amici suggest that punitive damages make 
up for small compensatory damages awards in some cases. 
The size of the compensatory damage award is irrelevant to 
the availability of punitive damages, save for the aggregate 
cap on compensatory and punitive damages under § 1981a. 
Small compensatory damages awards may serve to limit size 
of punitive damages awards under the “ratio” principle 
discussed in BMW of North America, Inc.y. Gore, 517 U.S. 
559, 580-83 (1996). By the same token, higher punitive 
damages may be awarded for a "particularly egregious act" 
resulting in small economic damages, or for "particularly 
reprehensible conduct." /d. at 576, 582. In either case, it 
is the degree of the conduct that governs, not the isolated 
mental state of the defendant or the small size of the 

compensatory award.

culpability that results from acting with 
malice or reckless disregard of another’s

the word “outrageous” in precisely this

The EEOC concludes that the “better reading of those

Construing the statute in this manner, by equating 
recklessness and egregiousness and by doing so with 
reference to defendant’s conduct instead of an isolated 
mental state, is in harmony with the decision of the court of

additions to the statute. It also explains, contrary to

B. Congress Intended for Egregious Conduct 
To Be a Necessary Element of Punitive 

Damages, As Derived From Common Law. 

This Court has issued caution concerning the extent 
of the utility of the legislative history of the 1991 Civil 
Rights Act because of its ample dosage of "frankly partisan 
statements." Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 
262 (1994). Legislative history and the principles of 
statutory construction, however, confirm the appropriateness 
of equating the statutory standard of maliciousness and 
recklessness with egregiousness under common law 
principles, confirm the two-tiered framework of the statute, 
and necessarily reject plaintiff's argument that every case of 
intentional discrimination can result in an award of punitive 
damages. 

First, plaintiff ignores the fundamental principle of 
statutory construction that when Congress uses a term that 
has been applied at common law, the term has the same 
meaning as in common law. As this Court stated in Molzof 
v. United States, 502 U.S. 301, 307 (1992), quoting 
Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952): 

[W]here Congress borrows terms of art in 
which are accurrulated the legal tradition and 
meaning of centuries of practice, it 
presumably knows and adopts the cluster of 
ideas that were attached to each borrowed 
word in the body of learning from which it 
was taken and the meaning its use will convey 
to the judicial mind unless otherwise 
instructed. In such case, absence of contrary 
direction may be taken as satisfaction with 
widely accepted definitions, not as a departure 
from them. 

See United States v. Shabani, 513 U.S. 10, 13 (1994) 
("settled principle of statutory construction that, absent 
contrary indications, Congress intends to adopt the common 
law definition of statutory terms"). 

In Molzof, this Court construed 28 U.S.C. § 2674, 
the Federal Tort Claims Act provision excluding the award 
of punitive damages against the United States. This Court 
recognized that punitive damages is a "legal term of art that 
has a widely accepted common-law meaning," and is a 
concept "deeply rooted in the common law." 502 U.S. at 
306, 311. The Court stated that the common law definition 
of punitive damages "focuses on the nature of the 
defendant’s con..uct," and agreed with the characterization 
that punitive damages are those "intended to act as 
punishment for egregious conduct." /d. at 307, 305-06. 

The Restatement (Second) of Torts, quoted in Smith 
v. Wade, 461 U.S. at 46-48, recaps the common law 
standard in a manner that parallels the language of the Act: 
"Punitive damages may be awarded for conduct that is 
outrageous, because of the defendant’s evil motive or his 
reckless indifference to the rights of others." Jd. at 
§ 908(2). Comment b to § 908 similarly states: "since the 
purpose of punitive damages is not compensation of the 
plaintiff but punishment of the defendant and deterrence, 
these damages can be awarded only for conduct for which 
this remedy is appropriate -- which is to say, conduct 
involving some element of outrage similar to that usually 
found in crime." 

The EEOC (EEOC Br. 20 n. 15) argues alternatively 
that the punitive damages standard in § 1981a represents a 
departure from the common law. The necessary implication 
of this argument is that Congress called the remedy punitive 
damages but really meant to establish a lesser form of 

31 

remedy. As concurring Judge Randolph wrote, such a result 
could only be “the product of a very clever draftsman, 
someone who wanted to convey the appearance of limiting 
punitive damages to exceptional cases, while hoping that 
courts would draw upon other legal sources to find the 
limitation an illusion" (Pet. App. 23a). Judge Randolph 
correctly concluded otherwise, stating that "[t]hose who 
voted for this legislation surely must have believed they were 
voting for a two-tiered damages system,” intending "punitive 
damages to be reserved for only the worst cases" (id.). 

Second, as noted by the court of appeals (Pet. App. 
6a-7a), the legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 
supports the two-tiered nature of the statute. The House 
Report states: 

Plaintiffs must first prove intentional discrimination, 
then must prove actual injury or loss arising 
therefrom to recover compensatory damages, and 
must meet an even higher standard (establishing that 
the employer acted with malice or reckless or callous 
indifference to their rights) to recover punitive 
damages. 

H.R. Rep. No. 40(1), 102d Cong., 1st Sess. at 72. 

Third, the caselaw supports this reading of punitive 
damages, borne of the common law. In addition to the cases 
cited by the court of appeals recognizing that egregious 
conduct is necessary for the imposition of punitive damages 
under 42 U.S.C. § 198la (Pet. App. 19a-20a, quoted in 
§ I1.D., supra), the court of appeals also referred to cases 
which read Smith v. Wade as requiring the legal standard of 
egregiousness to be met in cases under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 
(Pet. App. 14a-15a). See also Barber v. Nabors Drilling 
U.S.A. Inc., 130 F.3d 702, 710 (Sth Cir. 1997) (plain error

to submit punitive damages to jury under § 198la where

A. The Traditional Role of the Court Must be 
Preserved in Cases Involving Claims for 

Punitive Damages. 

By arguing that all Title VII cases of intentional 
discrimination with sufficient evidence to go to the jury on 
basic liability should also be submitted to the jury on 
punitive damages, plaintiff crafts a special rule warranted 
neither by the statute nor by precedent. The court of appeals 
correctly concluded that there is no basis for plaintiff's 
argument “to deny the court’s traditional role in deciding 
whether a reasonable juror could find the defendant's 
conduct sufficiently egregious for the punitive damages issue 
to be submitted to the jury in the first instance” (Pet. App. 
14a). 

The general principle announced by the Court in 
Brady v. Southern Ry. Co., 320 U.S. 476, 479-80 (1943) 
(citations omitted) still pertains: 

When the evidence is such that without 
weighing the credibility of the witnesses there 
can be but one reasonable conclusion as to the 
verdict, the court should determine the 
proceeding by non-suit, directed verdict or 
otherwise in accordance with the applicable 
practice without submission to the jury, or by 

33 

judgment notwithstanding the verdict. By 
such direction of the trial the result is saved 
from the mischance of speculation over 
legally unfounded claims. 

The preservation of the traditional role of the trial 
court in determining whether there is sufficient evidence to 
go to the jury applies in employment discrimination cases 
involving punitive damages, the same as in other areas of the 
law. See Wallace v. SMC Pneumatics, Inc., 103 F.3d 1394, 
1396 (7th Cir. 1997); Fisher v. Vassar College, 114 F.3d 
1332, 1346-47 (2d Cir. 1997) (en banc); see also Jackson v. 
Bunge Corp., 40 F.3d 239, 246-47 (7th Cir. 1994) ("while 
the measurement of punitive damages is a jury question, the 
preliminary question of whether the facts of a particular case 
justify the imposition of a punitive damages award is 
properly one of law"). As stated by the Fourth Circuit in 
Cline v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. , 144 F.3d 294, 306 (4th Cir. 
1998), quoting Stephens v. South Atlantic Canners, Inc. , 848 
F.2d 484, 489-90 (4th Cir. 1988), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 
996 (1988), “[a]Jthough any form of discrimination 
constitutes reprehensible and Mee conduct, not every 
lawsuit...calls for submission of this extraordinary remedy 

to a jury.” 

Plaintiff argues that the jury serves as a check upon 
itself, retaining “discretionary moral judgment" over whether 
or not to render an award of punitive damages in any given 
case. The court of appeals correctly disposed of this 
argument by noting that the reference to “discretionary moral 
judgment" in Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. at 52, "simply 
restates the commonplace that the jury can choose not to 
award them even when the evidence is sufficient to give it 
the choice” (Pet. App. 13a).
