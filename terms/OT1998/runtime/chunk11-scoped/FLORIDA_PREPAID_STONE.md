# Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v. College Savings Bank

# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Approval Status:** Approved. The revised position stated below was user-approved 2026-10-30 and 2026-10-31.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** September 28, 1998; no prepared OT1998 position is a completed Court decision.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the Federal Circuit’s judgment sustaining the denial of sovereign immunity on the Article I ground, and remand for ordinary adjudication of the patent claims. Section 5 does not sustain the Act at the scope presented.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** Remove the sovereign-immunity obstacle to this expressly authorized action. The patent action proceeds to ordinary merits adjudication; patent validity, infringement, causation, defenses and entitlement to particular remedies remain for the lower court. Affirming jurisdiction to proceed grants no damages, enhanced award, fees, injunction, compulsory license or other relief.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Patent Clause enforcement

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Decide the preserved question within this proceeding; the proposed result is a position, not an entered Court judgment.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** May Congress authorize this action against a State?
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Article I sustains the action and suffices for the judgment. Congress may authorize private enforcement against States when the underlying obligation and enforcement measure fall within its Article I authority and Congress unmistakably authorizes the State’s exposure to suit. Congress possesses power under the Patent Clause and the Necessary and Proper Clause to impose the federal patent obligation on covered States and to authorize ordinary private federal enforcement when the authorization is unmistakably clear; sections 271(h) and 296 satisfy the clarity requirement. The action may proceed without actual State consent. The availability of suit decides no validity, infringement, remedies, defenses or amounts.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Article I sustains the action and suffices for the judgment. Congress may authorize private enforcement against States when the underlying obligation and enforcement measure fall within its Article I authority and Congress unmistakably authorizes the State’s exposure to suit. Congress possesses power under the Patent Clause and the Necessary and Proper Clause to impose the federal patent obligation on covered States and to authorize ordinary private federal enforcement when the authorization is unmistakably clear; sections 271(h) and 296 satisfy the clarity requirement. The action may proceed without actual State consent. The availability of suit decides no validity, infringement, remedies, defenses or amounts.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Uniform patent rights lose an important part of their content if a State may exploit a patented invention while withholding the ordinary federal remedy. Congress created a national patent system, concentrated adjudication in federal court, and unmistakably included States in §296. Union Gas supplies a retained Article I foundation; extending its principle to the Patent Clause is an express doctrinal extension, not a claim that Seminole already decided it. Sections 271(h) and 296 expressly establish state coverage, federal suit and otherwise available remedies. Extend the retained constitutional conclusion of Union Gas into a general Article I enforcement principle; the simulated Seminole Tribe supplies no contrary categorical prohibition. Adopt the federal-question reading of the Eleventh Amendment and clarify that Hans does not establish an immunity Congress cannot displace in this setting. Reach Article I as a preserved alternative ground for affirmance: because Section 5 does not sustain the Act at the scope presented, the Patent Clause argument is the source of congressional authority supporting the same immunity-denial judgment, without enlarging the respondent’s requested relief. Enumerated-power and supremacy reasoning, together with unmistakable statutory authorization, makes this ground sufficient for the judgment by itself. The remedial power remains tied to enforcement of a valid enumerated-power obligation and a clear congressional choice.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** No finding of infringement, patent validity or damages entitlement.

### Issue 2: Section 5

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Decide the preserved question within this proceeding; the proposed result is a position, not an entered Court judgment.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Does Section 5 independently sustain the authorized action?
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Section 5 does not independently sustain the Act at the scope presented. Section 5 authorizes Congress to prevent and remedy unconstitutional state deprivation of patent property through legislation congruent and proportional to that constitutional injury. Congress need not establish a fixed numerical count of prior violations, produce formal findings in a prescribed format, restrict every preventive measure to proven violators, or require each plaintiff to prove a constitutional tort. Nevertheless, a statute enforcing ordinary federal patent liability against States without differentiating infringement from unconstitutional deprivation must have an adequately supported preventive relationship to the constitutional wrong. For this enactment, patent-property status, federal patent jurisdiction and possible limitations on state remedies do not sufficiently establish that the statute’s full infringement liability and remedial exposure are a congruent and proportional response to unconstitutional state deprivations. The Patent Remedy Act does not independently qualify as Section 5 legislation at the scope presented. Congress is not forever barred: a differently supported or fitted measure remains possible.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Section 5 does not independently sustain the Act at the scope presented. Section 5 authorizes Congress to prevent and remedy unconstitutional state deprivation of patent property through legislation congruent and proportional to that constitutional injury. Congress need not establish a fixed numerical count of prior violations, produce formal findings in a prescribed format, restrict every preventive measure to proven violators, or require each plaintiff to prove a constitutional tort. Nevertheless, a statute enforcing ordinary federal patent liability against States without differentiating infringement from unconstitutional deprivation must have an adequately supported preventive relationship to the constitutional wrong. For this enactment, patent-property status, federal patent jurisdiction and possible limitations on state remedies do not sufficiently establish that the statute’s full infringement liability and remedial exposure are a congruent and proportional response to unconstitutional state deprivations. The Patent Remedy Act does not independently qualify as Section 5 legislation at the scope presented. Congress is not forever barred: a differently supported or fitted measure remains possible.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Structural interpretation implemented through Boerne’s congruence-and-proportionality inquiry, with no mechanical evidentiary quota. Fitzpatrick recognizes Congress’s Section 5 power to subject States to suit when enforcing the Fourteenth Amendment, and patents constitute protected property. The simulated Boerne requires congruence and proportionality between the constitutional injury and the means adopted; prevention may reach some conduct that is not independently unconstitutional, and no findings quota or prescribed format is required. Infringement is not itself deprivation without due process: Daniels, Parratt, Hudson and Zinermon preserve the distinction between injury to property, the State’s conduct, available process and unconstitutional deprivation. The Act imposes ordinary infringement liability and the full patent remedies on every State without differentiating infringement from unconstitutional deprivation. Patent-property status, exclusive federal infringement jurisdiction and possible limitations on state remedies do not establish an adequately supported preventive relationship for that full liability and remedial exposure, and national uniformity and equal competition are Article I objectives that do not themselves supply Section 5 power. The Article I ground on which the judgment rests does not depend on commercial participation constituting consent.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Fitzpatrick and Boerne retain their independent force. Consent inferred from a State's continued commercial participation is rejected; actual consent through a distinct State choice remains available; whether Florida's litigation conduct waived immunity need not be decided because the Article I ground sustains the judgment. Reserved: the adequacy of any Florida state-law remedy (the record lacks the facts); any federal constitutional damages cause, punitive or enhanced awards, and Section 5 for a different statute with a more closely demonstrated predicate (each outside the question presented).
