## 2. Supported facts and procedural qualifications

The lower opinion is the principal published account. Its legal conclusions remain lower-court conclusions under review, not already controlling Supreme Court law. Affidavits, pleadings, party summaries, and agency publications retain their own evidentiary status.

1. **Institution and claim.** L.C. filed in May 1995; E.W. intervened in January 1996. Both had mental disabilities and were receiving services at Georgia Regional Hospital/Atlanta. Their case concerns continued provision of needed services in an institutional setting after community treatment became appropriate, not the legality of their initial admissions. The parties describe both intellectual disability and mental illness, with distinct diagnoses and treatment histories; no categorical judgment about all persons with either condition follows.
2. **L.C.'s chronology.** The sources report extended institutional treatment, efforts to secure community care, a failed trial stay with her mother, and a subsequent institutional interval before a supported community placement in February 1996. The lower opinion recognizes her placement before judgment and orders continued appropriate services. Petitioners describe funding and support needs; respondents describe administrative resistance, poor knowledge of alternatives, and harmful institutional effects. Those competing causal accounts are not resolved by calling the placement medically possible.
3. **E.W.'s changing condition.** The appellate opinion records that some experts advised against immediate community placement at particular times. Its summary-judgment conclusion rests on the later agreement that appropriate community treatment could meet her actual care and supervision needs. Failed earlier placements lacked suitable monitoring. Late-1996 kidney disease required hospital treatment and surgery; the lower opinion reports the surgeon's view after surgery that the physical condition did not require continued state-hospital residence. The opinion expressly permits return to institutional care when a changed medical condition requires it. Do not recast this record as uninterrupted professional agreement that any community setting was adequate.
4. **Actual placements.** By the appeal both respondents were receiving community services. The lower opinion reports February 1996 for L.C. and a postjudgment placement for E.W. The provider affidavit at JA 160–164 supplies July 1997 for E.W., her September 2, 1998 move to a more independent supervised apartment, and the provider's assessment that both were progressing despite challenging needs. This is the provider's account, not a new Supreme Court finding or a guarantee against later readmission.
5. **Choice and voluntary status.** The approved packet supports that these respondents sought and did not oppose community services. Petitioners distinguish involuntary admission from later voluntary treatment; respondents dispute the implication that they could practically leave a locked facility without suitable support. The Title II regulation preserves an individual's choice to decline an accommodation. Neither side's description establishes that every voluntary admission is legally compelled or that nominally voluntary status defeats an integration claim.
6. **Judgments below.** The district court declared a Title II violation, enjoined further violation, required an appropriate community placement for E.W., and required services necessary to maintain L.C.'s community placement. It rejected the State's funding defense based on existing community programs and lower per-person costs. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed the statutory determination and remanded for actual consideration of additional costs, fixed institutional overhead, Medicaid options, transfer authority, and demands on the mental-health budget. It did not command universal deinstitutionalization or decide the harder classwide case. Lower opinion ¶¶1–3, 32–49 and nn.8–10.
7. **Constitutional claims below remain distinct.** The district court did not decide respondents' Fourteenth Amendment claims; the circuit declined the State's request to adjudicate them in the first instance. The statutory claim's success or failure does not silently adjudicate those claims. Lower opinion n.3.

**Mootness requires an actual account.** Placement alone does not establish mootness. The lower court invoked capable-of-repetition/yet-evading-review principles for L.C., citing eighteen admissions and instability associated with services/funding; it identified E.W.'s court-ordered placement and repeated unstable admissions as additional reasons the controversy persisted. The continuing injunction and the officials' request to undo it also matter. The later provider account is evidence of progress, not a promise of permanent provision. These sources permit evaluation of an ongoing dispute without inventing a new return to hospital. The lower court's conclusion is reviewable; the operator's lack of a later medical update proves neither ongoing injury nor mootness. See lower opinion n.2; *Honig v. Doe*, 484 U.S. 305, 318–323 (1988); *Vitek v. Jones*, 445 U.S. 480, 486–487 (1980).

**Later district proceedings, not the judgment now reviewed.** JA 176–177 reproduces an order signed October 19 and filed October 20, 1998. It grants a motion in limine, limiting the remand hearing to additional expenditures for these two respondents and excluding the possible wider impact on others. It schedules an October 29 hearing but does not itself establish what occurred there. Both respondents' brief pp.13,15 and petitioners' reply pp.14–16 report subsequent rejection of the defense; the reply identifies a January 29, 1999 order. Respondents report a February 26, 1999 notice of appeal. The exact January order and notice are not separately supplied in the safe files. The later ruling and pending appeal can be described as convergent party accounts with these limits, not treated as a second judgment before this Court or a basis for automatic consolidation.

## 3. Resource record and disputed inferences

The relevant inquiry cannot be reduced to comparing a hospital's average per-person charge with one group home's payment. The lower opinion recognizes evidence of fixed costs that persist unless an entire wing or hospital closes. Petitioners also invoke continuing institutional availability for patients whose needs change, services to people outside institutions, and the effect of individual priority on a wider program. These are substantive defenses; whether their evidentiary support satisfies the proper standard remains open.

Respondents invoke Georgia's statutory authority to transfer funds, existing community programs, the lower per-person expense of community services, available federal matching support, and agency materials endorsing community services. The lower opinion reports more than 2,100 approved Medicaid slots but only 700 used in 1996. The reply and Benson affidavit report expansion from 283 funded placements in 1992 to 1,938 in 1997, closures and shifting funds. Those different dates must remain attached to the figures; the 700 figure is not a finding about all periods or the present. Approval of a federal slot does not itself prove availability of the State's matching funds, an appropriate placement, or staff capacity.

JA 159 quotes the State's rehearing submission that the incremental expenditures for L.C. and E.W. “are, by definition, not unreasonable, nor could they ‘fundamentally alter’ the services provided by the State.” That bounded legal concession concerns the two respondents viewed alone. It is not a concession that the asserted programwide consequences are immaterial or affordable. The October in-limine order shows why that distinction mattered to the later defense. Conversely, the State's wider-cost assertion is not an adjudicated demonstration that either individual's placement would disrupt lawful treatment of others.

JA 160–164 gives the provider's approximate annual State-match calculations (about $18,500 for L.C. and $20,548 for E.W.), separately describes payments for room and board and other services, and identifies uncertainty about some expenses. It supplies neither a complete total-cost accounting nor proof of marginal State savings. JA 195–202 contains Benson's policy and funding affidavit, including the asserted approximately 1,900-person waiting list and estimated $100 million cost, medical and consumer-choice concerns, and reform history. Those are sworn assertions and estimates, not appellate findings of the precise program impact.

The United States proposes consideration of an orderly, systematic and prompt placement plan, including priorities and disruption caused by a particular request and similar requests; it recognizes overhead, transition costs and potential additional demand. No safe source establishes that Georgia actually possessed a legally sufficient plan, that any list moved at a particular pace, or that the two respondents were lawfully assigned their places on such a list. A possible plan defense is an argument, not entering law or a found fact.

## 4. Operative text and law entering the event

The [generated entering-law slice](../../entering-law/OT_1998CHUNK11_OLMSTEAD_NEUTRAL.md) was created with `tools/build_entering_law.py`, then supplemented with selected verbatim opening Holdings and authorized public excerpts. The controlling baseline is the opening trackers plus public law effective through **June 21, 1999**. The original packet's statement that no current-term decision was supplied is stale. No June 22 Sutton outcome governs this uncoordinated same-day matter.

**Title II text.** Section 12132 states: “Subject to the provisions of this subchapter, no qualified individual with a disability shall, by reason of such disability, be excluded from participation in or be denied the benefits of the services, programs, or activities of a public entity, or be subjected to discrimination by any such entity.” Section 12131(1)(A)–(B) includes any State or local government and its departments, agencies, districts, and instrumentalities. Section 12131(2) defines a qualified person through the essential eligibility requirements, with or without reasonable modifications to rules, policies, or practices, removal of architectural, communication, or transportation barriers, or auxiliary aids and services. Section 12133 incorporates the remedies, procedures, and rights in 29 U.S.C. §794a for alleged §12132 discrimination.

**Delegation.** Section 12134(a) directs the Attorney General to promulgate implementing regulations, excluding matters within the specified Transportation authority. Section 12134(b), except for “program accessibility, existing facilities” and “communications,” requires consistency with the ADA and the specified January 13, 1978 Part 41 coordination regulations; the two excepted subjects use Part 39 instead. The Part 41 integration language and §12134's direction are central to respondents' statutory argument. They must be read with the discrimination text, not in place of it.

**Regulations.** Under then-operative 28 C.F.R. §35.130(d), “A public entity shall administer services, programs, and activities in the most integrated setting appropriate to the needs of qualified individuals with disabilities.” Section 35.130(b)(7) states: “A public entity shall make reasonable modifications in policies, practices, or procedures when the modifications are necessary to avoid discrimination on the basis of disability, unless the public entity can demonstrate that making the modifications would fundamentally alter the nature of the service, program, or activity.” The regulation places the fundamental-alteration demonstration on the public entity; it does not specify a numerical budget threshold or universally require a hearing.

Section 35.130(b)(1)(iv) forbids different or separate services on the basis of disability unless necessary to provide services as effective as those provided others; paragraph (b)(2) preserves access to nonseparate programs despite permissibly separate programs. Paragraph (c) permits benefits beyond those required, including for a particular disability class. Section 35.130(e)(1) states: “Nothing in this part shall be construed to require an individual with a disability to accept an accommodation, aid, service, opportunity, or benefit provided under the ADA or this part which such individual chooses not to accept.” Paragraph (e)(2) separately provides that neither the Act nor the Part authorizes a representative or guardian to decline food, water, medical treatment, or medical services. The choice qualification cannot be used to manufacture authority for a guardian to refuse those necessities.

The statute's §12101(a)(2), (3), (5), and (8) findings identify isolation/segregation, institutionalization, overprotective rules, failures to modify practices, and equal participation/independent living. They inform interpretation; they do not by themselves establish the elements, preferred placement, or cost burden in this case. The safe statutory/regulatory reproductions are the United States appendix pp.1a–11a and petitioners' appendix pp.7a–14a, corroborated on the central provisions by the lower opinion. OCR splits columns; quoted core provisions were checked across these copies. No later ADA amendment or current regulation is used.

**Relevant surviving pre-divergence law.** *Southeastern Community College v. Davis*, 442 U.S. 397 (1979), distinguishes reasonable accommodation from fundamental alteration. *Alexander v. ## 5. Strongest supported alternatives and their limits

**Integration as statutory discrimination.** Respondents and the United States read §12132 together with the explicit integration findings, §12134's regulatory direction, §35.130(d), and the qualified-person/modification structure. On that view, conditioning necessary existing services on avoidable isolation can discriminate by reason of disability without a nondisabled person needing the identical disability service. The case can be confined to professionally appropriate community care that the individuals seek, while preserving fundamental alteration and actual institutional needs. The strongest counterargument is that identifying disability as the reason services are needed is not the same as identifying disability discrimination: the State invokes scarce capacity, no nondisabled comparator, lawful institutional care, and no general benefit-expansion mandate. Any affirmative rule must explain causation and service scope rather than equating all institutionalization with discrimination.

**No asserted statutory discrimination under this allocation.** Petitioners read the statute as protecting access and evenhanded treatment, not compelling a preferred delivery setting whenever another setting can be appropriate. They invoke *Choate*, *Traynor*, the specific Medicaid scheme, the absence of express least-restrictive language, professional administration, and *Gregory/Pennhurst*. The reply expressly accepts that Title II can cover programs serving only disabled people—for example, denial of mental-health services because of an additional disability—and targets the asserted equal allocation here. The strongest counterargument is that this reading may deprive the integration regulation and Congress's findings of independent work, and cannot dismiss §12134's express instruction as merely an agency preference. Validity and lawful interpretation of the regulation require actual analysis under *Auer* and *Haggar*, not refusal based solely on litigation presentation.

**Integration rule with more complete resource protection.** If the integration principle is sustained, a court could distinguish per-person affordability from the State's obligations to the whole served population, including temporary overhead, appropriate priorities, existing services for others, and a supported implementing plan. The United States' pp.19–22 discussion supplies this alternative. The strongest objection is that respondents frame the defense as unchallenged and separately appealed, while the original judgment already ordered a resource remand. Before changing the rule or vacating a component, explain why the particular defense question is fairly included in question 1 and necessary to resolve the reviewed judgment. Do not adjudicate the January 1999 order as though independently before the Court.

**Affirm the limited statutory proposition while reserving finer defense questions.** Respondents argue that the petition presents the prima facie integration question, leaving precise defense application to the separate appeal. This preserves the existing resource remand and avoids unnecessary decree design. Its strongest objection is that the meaning of the prima facie duty may depend on a meaningful limiting defense, and the actual October evidence restriction can reveal whether the rule under review is too narrow. This is a genuine scope/remedy fork, not a license to ignore the defense or to declare it finally satisfied.

**Individual suitability and summary judgment.** The appellate opinion found no genuine material dispute about appropriately supported placement at relevant times; petitioners dispute its treatment of changing medical/behavioral assessments and administrators' responsibilities. If the authorized question permits revisiting that application, preserve ordinary Rule 56 treatment of evidence and supported inferences. Medical appropriateness is not capacity to serve any person anywhere given unlimited money; resource allocation is not a substitute for an actual medical determination. Reopening the entire medical record, finding a present risk, or ordering a new examination is not automatic. A legal disposition that leaves supported suitability findings intact can avoid deciding an ungranted fact-review question.

**Remedy consequences.** Statutory affirmance would sustain the integration principle within its stated limits, with lawful treatment of the remand and live injunction. A changed fundamental-alteration standard may support a confined vacatur/remand, without commanding discharge, a particular appropriation, closure, provider, deadline, hearing, or new agency proceeding. Reversal of the statutory ground would not establish the meritlessness of unadjudicated constitutional claims or authorize otherwise unlawful confinement. Actual community placements and continuing service obligations matter to the form of prospective relief. No possible disposition independently proves Georgia has a sufficient plan or that its resource defense fails.
