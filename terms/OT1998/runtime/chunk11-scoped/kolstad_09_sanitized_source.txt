Section la authorizes an award of punitive damages on _ 
either of two distinct showings, proof of reckless indifference 
to federally protected rights or a demonstration of malice. 

Because petitioner would on remand seek an award of punitive 

damages on alternative grounds, we urge the Court to address 

the legal standard applicable to each, and we discuss those 
issues separately below. 

We do not contend that the existing finding of intentional 
discrimination in this case entitles petitioner as a matter of law 
to an award of punitive damages; whether such damages should 
be awarded remains a question for the jury, which would be 
free to deny that remedy despite the proven discrimination. We 
urge only that petitioner is entitled to have a jury consider her 
claim for punitive damages under both of the two grounds set 
out in the statute.

(3) A General Rule Denying Punitive Damages in “Garden 
Variety” Cases Would Be Inconsistent With The Express 
Purpose of the 1991 Civil Rights Act 
The central holding of the court of appeals majority was 

that there are certain types of intentionally discriminatory 

practices for which punitive damages should never be awarded. 

The court below apparently envisaged that most discrimination 

claims would fall within this prohibition, describing the types 

of discriminatory practices it wished to immunize from 
punitive damage awards as “garden variety” (Pet. App. 18a) or 

“standard” (Pet. App. 5a) discrimination. Respondent 

contemplates that rules could be adopted by the courts 

9 

10 

deciding in most cases to forbid such awards, and permitting 
jury involvement in the decision only in extreme and atypical 
situations. The argument for this system is built on wisps of 
inference assertedly emanating from the interstices of the 
structure of the statute. The fatal flaw in this proposal can be 
found in the text: both the actual standard articulated in section 
1981a(b)(1) and the terms of section 198la(c)(1), which 
provides that where punitive damages are sought “any party 
may demand a trial by jury.” 42 U.S.C. §1981a(c)(1). 
Questions regarding the respective roles of juries and 
judges under the 1991 Civil Rights Act must be addressed in 
light of the special importance which the Act, and those who 
supported it, attached to assuring that where damages were 
sought either party would have a statutory right to a jury trial. 
Although Congress has provided in many hundreds of statutes 
for awards of damages, section 1981a(c)(1) is one of the few 
laws which expressly confers a statutory right to trial by jury.” 
The inclusion in the 1991 Act of that statutory right was of 
practical importance; absent that provision there would have 
been no jury trial right in Title VII cases against federal 
agencies or in Title VII cases tried in state courts. More 
importantly, supporters and opponents alike understood that the 
1991 Act deliberately worked a sea change in the Title VII 
enforcement process by according to the victims of intentional 
discrimination the right to have their claims decided by juries. 
In the highly partisan legislative history of the 1991 Act, 
the proposal to provide for jury trials in intentional 
discrimination cases was one of the most bitterly fought over 
issues. The inclusion in the legis!ation of a provision for 
compensatory and punitive damages was prompted in particular

Respondent first advances a factual argument. We stressed

however, is the quintessential function of the trier of fact. 
Although not required to do so, a reasonable jury could 
assuredly conclude that such remarks revealed an animus or 
demeaning attitude towards women. While, for example, the 
term “bitch” has a variety of meanings, one of them is an 
expression of animus toward women who are ambitious or who 
use their authority in a manner contrary to the wishes of the 
man speaking; that particular usage of “bitch” fits the 
circumstances in which the disputed remarks occurred in this 
case. The lower courts have repeatedly recognized that use of 
the term “bitch” can be sufficiently gendered and offensive to 
constitute a significant element of a sexual harassment claim.”

are legally irrelevant unless those statements are “related to the

precisely the issue which the trier of fact must decide.

Cases such as this involving the interaction of several 
decisionmakers are controlled by this Court’s decision in Price 
Waterhouse v. Hopkins, 490 U.S. 228 (1989). Once a plaintiff

employment decision, the defendant bears the burden of proof
