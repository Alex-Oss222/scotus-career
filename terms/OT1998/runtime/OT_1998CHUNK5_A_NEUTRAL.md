## Minns v. United States — No. 98-903

**Scope/posture.** §1254(1) review of Fourth Circuit, 155 F.3d 445, September 2, 1998, affirming Maryland dismissal of August 8, 1997, 974 F. Supp. 500. Authorized question concerns children's FTCA claims and Feres. Wives' claims and Military Claims Act review below are not automatically included.

**Record.** Wives and children of three servicemen allege congenital injuries after Gulf War inoculation/pesticide exposures. Complaints attack substance selection, storage, testing, administration and failure to warn; they allege direct civilian contact as well as transmission through the servicemen, semen or clothing. These are allegations before discovery, not causation findings. They do not concede that a manifested injury to the fathers is indispensable. Appellate opinion describes all children as having Goldenhar syndrome; petition says two. Avoid turning that nonessential discrepancy into a medical finding.

Fourth Circuit adopted a genesis rule covering civilian injury dependent on military acts incident to service, despite no pleaded manifested paternal injury. It independently applied §2680(a): substance selection/administration and warning decisions were treated as policy choices; operational versus planning labels do not control; no binding statute/regulation eliminating discretion was identified. Plaintiffs say pre-discovery dismissal assumed the decisionmaker, policy character and compliance with fixed safety/manufacturer directions. The appellate court did not reach combatant-activities or foreign-country exceptions. District court refused a universal foreign-country bar because alleged domestic decisions/warning omissions had to be credited. Walsh venue issue remained unaddressed. Separate MCA finality ruling is outside this children's FTCA grant.

**Lawful forks.** Feres, Stencel, Shearer, Johnson and Brown survive at their own scopes. Aktepe (March 10, 1998) rejects military subject matter as sufficient for nonjusticiability; its actual result rests on policy selection without an independently manageable duty and expressly reserves Feres and immunity. Blue Fox (January 20, 1999) requires consent for the actual remedy; its APA holding supplies no new FTCA exception.

Compare extending incident-to-service protection to these military-preparation-dependent claims against distinguishing civilian personal injury from derivative loss and assessing each actual duty and statutory exception. Rejecting genesis treatment would establish no negligence, state-law cause, causation or entitlement. The first path's strongest objection is the FTCA's inclusion of ordinary civilian claims arising from military activity and its distinct express exceptions; the second's strongest objection is judicial scrutiny of the same preparations for war.

Berkovitz/Gaubert discretionary function is independently sufficient only if its judgment and protected policy predicates are established. Binding directives and ordinary nonpolicy negligence remain distinct. A claim-specific remand for actual directives/policy responsibility is an available analytical path if the lower court's categorical premise fails; hypothetical directives in the petition do not establish their existence. Do not silently decide §2680(j), §2680(k), state duty, causation or venue.

**Justice sources, no commitments.** Ginsburg's Lombard dissent, 690 F.2d 215, 227–234 (D.C. Cir. 1982), directly rejects reflexive genesis treatment of children's own injuries while reserving other exceptions and proof obstacles; its 35-year interval/post-service information differs from Minns. Scalia's Johnson dissent, joined Stevens, opposes nontextual expansion and points to enacted exceptions. His Gaubert concurrence ties policy discretion to an officer's responsibilities and explains significance of an undeveloped record. O'Connor and Souter joined Gaubert's majority. Kennedy's simulated Aktepe opinion, joined O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Breyer, supplies the actual claim-specific constraint. Enhanced issue-specific analysis is required.
