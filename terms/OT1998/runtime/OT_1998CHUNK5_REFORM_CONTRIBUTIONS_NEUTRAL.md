## The actual claim requiring separate treatment

Paragraph 20 reports that the Reform plaintiffs challenge the $1,000 individual candidate-contribution limit as applied because it prevents them from raising and spending the difference between their smaller public subsidy and the major-party subsidy. This is an actual reported theory within the combined financing challenge. It must not disappear into an analysis of the subsidy formula alone. It also is not a new freestanding challenge to every FECA contribution provision or an independent party-expenditure claim.

Keep three propositions distinct. First, the Fund Act does not itself limit minor-party qualified expenses to their smaller subsidy: section 9003(c) permits qualified expenses up to the major entitlement and private supplementation within its conditions. Second, lawful permission to seek private contributions does not establish that a particular contribution limit leaves meaningful practical association. Third, unequal fundraising totals alone do not establish that the contribution rule has become unconstitutional or that the plaintiff has a right to equal public money.

The appellate court describes the pleaded injury as inability to close the funding gap and says plaintiffs did not allege the different suppression of voting strength or ability to field candidates identified in its reading of Buckley's reservation. That description controls the bounded source account. It is not a factual finding that a minor-party candidate can never suffer a prohibitory contribution burden, nor a requirement that a party actually disappear before a claim can be substantial. No unreported donor offer, exhausted donor pool, price adjustment, unavailable campaign service or additional allegation is supplied.

## Controlling contribution law and its limits

**Direct candidate contributions.** Buckley's contribution/expenditure distinction survives. Kentucky Right to Life, Part V, unanimously rejects the preserved facial challenges to the two direct $1,000 candidate/slate limits under that distinction. A direct payment supplies candidate resources and presents a corruption or circumvention concern different from financing one's own expression; prevention does not require proof of a completed corrupt bargain. But Kentucky's particular preserved facial record, including its reported concession, does not adjudicate Reform's different as-applied claim. It creates neither a permanent $1,000 minimum or safe harbor nor authority to equalize political influence. Its reserved cumulative and prohibitory burdens remain available on a sufficient actual showing. Kentucky's state-law knowledge terms and independent-expenditure exception do not migrate into FECA.

**Intermediary association.** Day's unanimous Part III requires meaningful collective political association and fit to an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism for the reviewed broadly restrictive intermediary ceiling. Its recipient class expressly excluded candidates' principal campaign committees and political-party units. Its $100 annual holding therefore does not itself invalidate an individual's direct candidate-contribution limit. Kentucky Part VI applies Day to a $1,500 annual aggregate across all covered intermediaries, requiring examination of that aggregate's breadth and actual functions without announcing invalidity, a dollar floor or a new evidentiary burden. Neither case makes every contribution an expenditure. The exact register is **Intermediary contribution limits and meaningful association**, present operation effective May 15, 1995; Kentucky's later component holdings apply and clarify its operation at their own scope.

**Independent expression and actual transactions.** Colorado Republican's six-Justice Part I protects a genuinely independent party advertisement and rejects party identity alone as a substitute for the candidate connection required by the alternative contribution theory. It preserves distinct direct candidate cash, actual transfers, earmarks and genuine conduits. The three-Justice proposal to protect even coordinated party-authored expression does not control and does not exempt ordinary candidate contributions. Reform's paragraph 20 concerns the latter channel, not a supplied independent advertisement falsely labeled a contribution.

**Source-specific receipts and public funding.** Kentucky Part VII sustains its two candidate-receipt branches on their actual facial record, while preserving later concrete fundraising disability, selective foreclosure or prohibitory cumulative effects. It does not rest on consent to unknown public-funding conditions. Florida's demonstrated-support subsidy holding separately concerns allocation of public benefits. Neither decision allows voluntary participation or a subsidy label to answer an independently sufficient contribution-burden claim automatically.

The slice supplies the exact prior writings and joins for individual analysis. Prior agreement with direct-contribution doctrine does not fix any Justice's application here, and a prior intermediary or party-expression disagreement does not by itself decide this direct-contribution claim.

## Supported paths and the required legal answer

One supported path rejects the presently reported as-applied theory separately from the facial funding challenge. It explains why an inability to match major-party totals, without the different supported suppressive or prohibitory burden needed to distinguish Buckley's treatment, does not defeat the direct-contribution rule or its fitted anticorruption justification. Buckley expressly considered minor-party competition, and a minor candidate's possibility of winning prevents a categorical premise that the candidate can pose no contribution-related concern. This path must not rely on Florida alone, treat $1,000 as eternally constitutional, find actual corruption by these plaintiffs, or infer an unsupported waiver from Kentucky's separate record.

The competing supported legal path asks whether the actual pleaded combined operation sufficiently alleges a distinct practical impairment of meaningful candidate association or competition that Buckley, Day and Kentucky leave open. The strongest argument is that formal supplementation is not practical supplementation when the contribution constraint independently disables effective advocacy, and the passage of time cannot immunize a fixed limit from an adequately supported as-applied challenge. The opposing answer is that the reported allegation remains disparity rather than that distinct burden. A conclusion that this claim is substantial must identify its sufficient existing allegation and distinguish the settled equalization demand; it may not supply new facts or simply borrow Day's intermediary holding. A conclusion that it is insufficient must explain the contribution claim on its own terms without declaring every future cumulative claim foreclosed.

A substantial challenge to the FECA candidate-contribution limit implicates section 437h's circuit en banc certification for an authorized plaintiff, subject to Article III and the established threshold exception.
