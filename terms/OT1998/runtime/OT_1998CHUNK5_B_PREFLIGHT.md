## American Manufacturers Mutual Insurance Co. v. Sullivan

**Clerical correction:** The petition and merits covers identify **No. 97-2000**. The supplied No. 97-1620 does not match those recovered papers. The sources are Archive item `micro_IA40385014_0186`, petition and petitioners' merits brief; the item was found by searching the collection listing by both docket and name. The lower judgment is *Sullivan v. Barnett*, 139 F.3d 158 (3d Cir. March 13, 1998), No. 96-2140. The supplied March 3, 1999 event and January 19 argument date are retained as approved scheduling/header premises, not independently checked by opening a Supreme Court opinion.

The actual petition asks both whether private compensation insurers become state actors when electing to use utilization review and whether due process requires payment of disputed bills before reasonable-and-necessary treatment is determined. Petitioners' merits brief, printed p. 30, expressly limits its second issue to withholding pending utilization review. It says it does not seek review of the other features of the Third Circuit's procedural ruling. Exact language appears in the copied passages.

The Third Circuit's opinion describes ten individual employees and two employee organizations; defendants include public administering officers, the director of the State Workmen's Insurance Fund, the School District of Philadelphia and private carriers. The private-carrier dismissal was entered January 24, 1996. The lower opinion identifies the subsequent public-defendant dispositions as November 7 and December 13, 1996. The plaintiffs sought certification, but the district court did not certify a class. The Joint Appendix reproduces the amended complaint's requests for declaratory and injunctive relief, restoration, compensatory damages and punitive damages. Those are requests, not awards.

The lower opinion describes a compulsory compensation system, with employers using private insurance, the state fund or self-insurance. It quotes section 531(5)'s thirty-day payment rule and its disputed-treatment qualification, and section 531(6)'s prospective, concurrent and retrospective review. A same-profession provider with the same or similar specialty reviews reasonableness or necessity. The employer or insurer pays the initial-review cost. An aggrieved provider, employer, employee or insurer has thirty days after the report to request the department's review; the compensation judge considers the report as evidence but is not bound by it.

The Bureau examines initial requests for completeness, not their substantive legitimacy, and assigns a review organization. The lower opinion characterizes suspension as permitted, not required. It says review assumes a work-related condition and does not decide continuing disability, maximum medical improvement or fee reasonableness. Medical providers may continue treatment, subject to payment risk. These reported administrative features should remain distinct from the appellate court's legal conclusions about attribution and deprivation.

Act 57, effective August 23, 1996 according to the lower opinion, eliminated the intermediate reconsideration step. A worker-favorable initial determination lifts suspension while compensation-judge review proceeds; an adverse determination leaves it in place pending that review. The lower court's descriptions of the original and revised procedures must not be merged.

The Third Circuit reversed the private-actor and procedural dismissals, rejected the separate challenge to the adequacy of review standards, and remanded the former reconsideration-fee claim because the record did not establish whether named plaintiffs actually paid those fees. It also left official/individual capacity and immunity for further treatment, with class certification unresolved. Its footnote 24 reports that neither party disputed the district court's property-interest determination below. Petitioners' merits brief separately argues no deprivation of the workers' own entitlement, as distinct from third-party providers' bills. These are the actual presentation facts for the validator's reachability inquiry; no conclusion about waiver or a required scope of decision is supplied here.

The Joint Appendix, pp. 50–54, reproduces revised notice forms. The employee notice warns that an adverse reasonableness/necessity determination may result in nonpayment, permits a personal written statement within twenty days and describes an appeal with the determination form. The respondents' merits brief, pp. 3–4, attributes that notice change to Bureau action after the Third Circuit decision and argues that prospective due-process review is unnecessary/advisory; petitioners, pp. 30–33, maintain their withholding issue remains and describe effects on public insurers. These are competing party presentations. No historical Supreme Court treatment of other petitions is imported.

## NFFE Local 1309 / FLRA v. Department of the Interior

The recovered union petition identifies **No. 97-1184**; the companion Archive item identifies **97-1243**. The Fourth Circuit judgment is 132 F.3d 157, October 31, 1997, Nos. 96-2855 and 97-1135. The two matters remain individually identified. The supplied March 3, 1999 date is a scheduling/header premise.

The union petition's two questions are distinct: a statutory duty to bargain over union-initiated proposals on matters not covered by an agreement and not waived; and, in any event, a duty to bargain over a proposed contract clause creating such a midterm obligation. The actual proposed clause is reproduced verbatim in the exact passages. It speaks of negotiable matters not covered by the agreement. It is not an unrestricted obligation to reopen settled terms.

The Fourth Circuit describes the facts as undisputed. During regular negotiations, the union proposed the clause; the Survey declared it nonnegotiable. An unfair-labor-practice complaint followed. The parties waived an ALJ hearing and stipulated facts. FLRA ordered bargaining about the clause, distinguishing a general statutory midterm duty from negotiability of a contractual clause and citing its treatment of a substantially identical proposal. The court granted review and denied enforcement under its *SSA* and *Energy* circuit decisions. It expressly allowed voluntary midterm bargaining while rejecting compelled bargaining.

The lower opinion records the opposing positions on the Impasses Panel: the agency and court relied on possible imposition of a midterm clause; the Authority disputed inevitability, cited an instance of refusal to impose one, and emphasized that bargaining can produce changes or concessions. The court treated its prior statutory interpretation as binding circuit law. These are lower-court reasons and party arguments, not Supreme Court holdings.

Official 1998 Code text is saved and copied for 5 U.S.C. sections 7101, 7102, 7103, 7105, 7106, 7114, 7116, 7117 and 7119. The definitions, management reservations, negotiability restrictions, bargaining duties, unfair-labor-practice routes and impasse procedures retain their separate terms. The lower opinion's quotation of 5 U.S.C. section 7703(c) is not substituted for the actual judicial-review provision in section 7123(c).

**Entering-law reading:** *MCI*, *NationsBank*, the relevant administrative-law register and the current *AT&T v. Iowa Utilities Board* and *Your Home* public copies are supplied. This handoff makes no inference that any one of those decisions resolves this statute.

## Federal Republic of Germany v. United States

The recovered original filing identifies **No. 127, Original (A-736)** and names Germany, Ambassador Jürgen Chrobog and Consul General Wolfgang Rudolph as plaintiffs, and the United States and Governor Jane Dee Hull as defendants. The diplomats sue in their official capacities and on Germany's behalf. The complaint invokes Article III's ambassador/consul head; the motion invokes 28 U.S.C. section 1251(b)(1). The exact 1998 statute is copied separately. No lower judgment is under review.

The motion seeks original leave and a temporary restraining order or preliminary injunction against Walter's execution pending the ICJ case, with an express alternative short restraint pending ordinary consideration of leave. The complaint asserts imminent violation of the U.N. Charter and ICJ ruling, and a second count based on international comity. It seeks declarations and prospective restraint against Hull. The complaint's initial text addresses the March 2 acting-President communication; the filed motion and memorandum expressly add the full ICJ order issued March 3. These two international acts must remain distinct.

The complaint alleges German consular officers learned of the case in 1992; Arizona disclosed its earlier knowledge of nationality only in late February 1999; Germany then brought the ICJ proceeding March 2. The complaint uses February 24 for disclosure; the ICJ order reports an asserted February 23 clemency-proceeding admission and Germany's statement that it became fully aware February 24. Those source descriptions do not establish a judicial finding of excusable or inexcusable delay.

The complaint alleges the clemency panel recommended a stay and Hull declined one. The ICJ order, paragraph 12, reports the German representative's same account. This supplies a concrete alleged gubernatorial act alongside the complaint's general law-execution language, without deciding the legal sufficiency of the officer connection.

The authenticated March 3 ICJ order is recovered in full. It identifies the parties' Convention/Optional Protocol status, its provisional prima-facie jurisdiction conclusion and the requested measures. The operative paragraph directs the United States to take all measures at its disposal to prevent execution pending decision, inform the ICJ of measures taken and transmit the order to Arizona's Governor. It reserves the merits and retains the matter. Paragraph 12 records the United States representative's objection to a procedure without a hearing; the order is not evidence that no diplomatic or litigating position was expressed at all.

Germany's March 3 reply reports four opposing points from the Solicitor General's letter: the asserted absence of a Convention cause for conviction vacatur; doubt about the ambassador/consul jurisdictional head; the suggested nonbinding character of provisional measures; and an Eleventh Amendment objection. The reply contests those points and requests full consideration. The separate letter itself was not recovered. Do not attribute additional argument, concessions or exact wording to it beyond the reply's attributed account or a controlled objective extract.
