QUESTIONS PRESENTED 
1. Whether a claim under the Emergency Medical 
Treatment and Active Labor Act, 42 U.S.C. § 1395dd requires 
proof 
of an improper motive on the part of the hospital, its 
staff or physicians as a pre-requisite for recovery? 
2. Whether, if proof of an improper motive is a pre- 
requisite 
for recovery under the Emergency Medical 
Treatment and Active Labor Act, 42 U.S.C. § 1395dd, proof 
of improper motive on the part of hospital administration and 
social services staff involved in a patient’s treatment and 
discharge 
is sufficient 
without 
additional 
proof that 
independent contractor treating physician who executed a 
patient’s discharge order was also improperly motivated? 
3. Whether, under Kentucky law, surgical residents 
not employed directly by a hospital, are the hospital’s 
ostensible agents whose negligence can be attributed to the 
hospital?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
This action was filed in the United States District 
Court for the Western District of Kentucky on August 30, 
1993 alleging, inter alia, that the Respondent, Galen of 
Virginia, Inc., formerly d/b/a Humana Hospital-University of 
Louisville, d/b/a University of Louisville Hospital (hereinafter 
“Humana”) had violated the terms of 42 U.S.C. § 1395dd, 
The Emergency Medical Treatment and Active Labor Act 
(hereinafter “EMTALA”) 
by transferring the Petitioner, 
Wanda Y. Johnson (hereinafter “Johnson”), to a nursing home 
in Indianapolis, Indiana before Humana had stabilized 
Johnson's condition. Johnson, a 36 year old mother of one 
child, was run over by a truck while walking home from her 
job as a cashier for Hardees Restaurant in Bardstown, 
Kentucky, late on the evening of May 19, 1992. She was 
flown from Bardstown to Humana where she was diagnosed 
with a left pelvic fracture, right fibular head fracture, T-4 and 
L3-LS5 fractures and a closed head injury. Johnson underwent 
exploratory surgery upon admission to Humana during which 
her spleen 
was removed. She was placed on a mechanical 
ventilator and tracheostomy. During her hospitalization, she 
developed multiple pneumonias and diffuse gastritis. Her 
hospital course was further complicated 
by fever 
and sepsis 
with an elevated temperature to 108 degrees, 
the origin of 
which was never accurately determined. 
Following the 
temperature spike, Johnson's neurologic status deteriorated to 
a state of flaccid quadriparesis. Despite Humana’s treatment, 
3 
Johnson's temperature spikes could not be controlled and 
episodes of pneumonia and urinary tract infection continued 
through the last week of her hospitalization. 
In early July, 1992, Johnson's physician requested that 
the social services department at Humana begin making 
contacts 
with rehabilitation facilities 
and nursing homes to 
explore potential placement for Johnson if and when her 
condition was stabilized and she was ready to leave an acute 
care setting. 
Humana’s social workers immediately experienced 
difficulty in their efforts to find potential placement facilities 
for Johnson. 
The social workers learned that Johnson's 
husband had abandoned Johnson and their five year old son 
and had begun cohabitation with another woman 
in 
Springfield, Kentucky. No other close family member existed 
to provide assistance to the Humana social workers in 
developing a discharge plan for Johnson. 
Jane Roberts, 
Johnson’s aunt, occasionally visited the hospital and 
attempted to locate Johnson's husband for the social workers. 
However, Roberts had not been appointed as Johnson's 
guardian at that time and did not have the legal authority, nor 
had she been requested, to act as Johnson's surrogate decision 
maker. 
Another significant complication in locating a facility 
which would accept Johnson was her inability to pay. By 
mid-July, 1992, it became evident that Johnson's application 
for Kentucky Medicaid benefits would not be approved. 
Johnson had no private health insurance and obviously did not 
4 

have the resources to compensate any facility for her care. 
Johnson’s 
medical 
expenses 
at Humana 
had already 
approached $400,000.00 and it was obvious to Humana that 
it would never be compensated for those charges. 
In the midst of Humana’s placement dilemma, Joanna 
Nolte, 
a 
marketer 
for 
Hometown 
Living 
Centers 
(“Hometown”) in Indianapolis, Indiana, visited Louisville in 
an effort to initiate a relationship with the Humana hospitals 
whereby Humana would place its patients in Hometown’s 
Indianapolis nursing homes. 
On the evening of July 22, 1992, Nolte met with 
members of Humana’s Social Services Department. Among 
those who attended Nolte’s presentation was Nancy Fred, 
who had taken charge of Johnson’s discharge plan. At the 
conclusion of Nolte’s presentation, she was approached by 
Nancy Fred. At that time, Fred requested that Nolte look at 
Johnson and consider accepting Johnson in one of the 
Hometown facilities. According to Nolte, Fred appeared to 
be in a real placement dilemma with Johnson. Nolte testified 
that Fred advised her that she was receiving pressure from the 
administration at Humana to find a placement for Johnson 
because of Johnson’s inability to pay for her care. 
Nolte 
explained to Fred that Johnson’s level of acuity exceeded 
Nolte’s ability to grant approval and indicated that she would 
have to refer Fred’s request to the Administrator and Director 
of Nursing at Mayfair Manor for evaluation. 
All the while, Johnson’s medical condition continued 
to be volatile. On July 23, 1992, her temperature spiked to 
5 
101 degrees. A sputum culture revealed positive cocci and 
her doctor suspected that she had yet another episode of 
urinary tract infection. On the evening of July 23, 1992, 
Johnson’s right lung collapsed and treatment was required 
every two hours. On July 24, 1992, the date of Johnson’s 
discharge, her white blood count was over twice the normal 
level, her temperature had been rising for two days, and the 
nurses noticed cloudy urine, thick yellow sputum and an 
elevated heart rate. Karen Martin, the nurse on duty during 
Johnson’s last day of hospitalization at Humana, testified that 
all of these symptoms indicated the presence of infection. 
Meanwhile, Mayfair Manor declined Humana’s 
referral of Johnson because her care required too many skilled 
_ nursing hours. Yet, somehow another Hometown facility, 
Crestview Nursing Home (“Crestview”), which had even 
fewer skilled nurses than Mayfair, picked up the Johnson 
referral and agreed to accept her. The first notation that 
Crestview had even been considered by the social workers at 
Humana appears in a social services note on July 24, 1992, 
wherein Nancy Fred indicated that Johnson would be 
transferred to Crestview on that date at 2:30 p.m. 
In its haste to expedite Johnson’s 
transfer 
to 
Crestview, Humana ignored its standard procedures and 
protocols for discharge and transfer. No authorization for the 
transfer was obtained from Johnson’s husband or mother. 
Nancy Fred simply called Jane Roberts, Johnson’s aunt and 
occasional visitor, and fold her that Johnson would be 
transferred to Crestview Nursing Home in Indianapolis that 
6 

afternoon. Fred gave Roberts Crestview’s telephone number. 
She did not ask Roberts to check with Johnson’s family to 
determine whether they would like to visit the facility before 
the transfer was arranged or whether they would even approve 
of a transfer which would place Johnson some three hours 
driving distance from their home. 
Fred gave Roberts no 
information about Crestview or about any benefits it could 
provide to Johnson. In fact, neither Fred nor anyone else at 
Humana could then provide any such information because no 
one knew much about Crestview. 
In addition to Humana’s violation of its own discharge 
policy and protocols which mandate advising the patient or 
her family of discharge plans in order to provide the patient or 
family at least an opportunity to decline the discharge and 
make other arrangements, Humana also violated its policy and 
protocol by failing to gain approval for the transfer from the 
accepting physician at Crestview. 
The physicians for 
Crestview were unaware of Johnson’s transfer until she had 
already arrived. 
There can be little question that Crestview was not an 
appropriate placement for Johnson. 
Even the Crestview 
physicians testified that its facility was inappropriate. 
Johnson’s condition deteriorated significantly as a 
result of her transfer from Humana. The nurses at Crestview 
were so alarmed by Johnson’s condition upon her arrival in 
Indianapolis that they thought she was going to die. Within 
hours of her arrival at Crestview, Johnson’s temperature had 
spiked to 105 degrees and she was rushed to Midwest 
7 
Medical Center where she remained hospitalized for almost 
six months. 
Dr. John W. Stuy was Johnson’s principal 
treating physician during her hospitalization in Indianapolis 
and he testified that the complications experienced by 
Johnson and which caused her deterioration were present 
while she was hospitalized in Louisville but were exacerbated 
because she was transferred from Humana to a facility which 
was unable to properly care for her. Dr. Stuy testified 
that the 
antibiotic that was prescribed for Johnson when she left 
Humana was not sensitive to the cultures which were grown 
at Midwest Medical Center and was not treating her infection. 
He commented that if she had remained on the antibiotic 
much longer, she may have died. 
Dr. Stuy testified that 
Johnson should never have been transferred from Humana. 
As a final insult, Humana’s transfer of Johnson to 
Indianapolis placed her in a jurisdiction where she was 
ineligible for public medical assistance. Had she remained 
hospitalized at Humana, Johnson would never have incurred 
personal liability for the payment of medical expenses 
because of Humana’s receipt of millions of dollars from 
Kentucky governmental entities to provide care to indigent 
patients. 
However, once Johnson was transferred from 
Humana to Indianapolis she was no longer entitled to that 
benefit. In fact, Johnson’s application for Medicaid benefits 
in Indiana was denied because she was not a resident of 
Indiana. As a direct result of Humana’s unilateral decision to 
ship Johnson 
to Indiana, Johnson incurred medical expenses 
of over $300,000.00 for which she is now personally 

obligated. 
Although the District Court had initially determined 
that genuine issues of material fact existed which precluded 
Summary Judgment in favor of Humana on the EMTALA 
claim, upon reconsideration it determined that proof of 
improper motive was a prerequisite for recovery under 
EMTALA. 
It then found that even though Johnson had 
presented proof that could indicate that Humana’s staff and 
administration 
was improperly motivated 
in arranging 
Johnson’s transfer, because Johnson had not provided proof 
that the independent contractor physicians who signed the 
order to discharge her had also been improperly motivated, no 
genuine issue of material fact existed as to “causation” and 
therefore granted Humana Summary Judgment on Johnson's 
EMTALA claim and dismissed her case. 
On appeal, Johnson argued that every other Circuit 
Court of Appeals in the country which had considered the 
issue had expressly rejected the notion that proof of improper 
motive was required to establish a claim under EMTALA. 
However, in a two to one decision, the Court of Appeals for 
the Sixth Circuit split with the interpretation of EMTALA by 
the other Circuit Courts of Appeals and held that EMTALA 
required proof of improper motive. It further held that proof 
of improper motive by hospital administration is insufficient 
and required proof that the discharging physician was 
improperly motivated for a claimant to succeed under 
EMTALA. 
REASON FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 
Certiorari Should Be Granted To Resolve Conflicts 
Among the Lower Courts in the Interpretation of a 
Federal Statute. 
The principal issue in this case is whether a claim 
under EMTALA requires proof of an improper motive on the 
part of the hospital, its staff or physicians as a prerequisite for 
recovery. The issue is a substantial one to every potential 
claimant under EMTALA. In fact, if a claimant must prove 
the inner thoughts and prejudices of attending hospital 
personnel in order to recover under EMTALA, the successful 
pursuit of a civil EMTALA claim is virtually impossible. 
If certiorari were granted, this would be the first case 
before this Court interpreting the prerequisites to recovery 
under EMTALA. This Court could then provide direction to 
the lower courts in an area where there has developed a clear 
split in interpretation of the relevant federal statute. 
The various Circuit Courts of Appeals have struggled 
to interpret EMTALA as providing a substantial federal 
remedy while attempting to provide some limits on the 
application of the statute so that EMTALA is not utilized as 
a federal medical malpractice statute. The Circuit Courts of 
Appeals have uniformly held that, “EMTALA is not a 
substitute for state law malpractice actions, and was not 
intended to guarantee proper diagnosis or to provide a federal 
remedy for misdiagnosis or medical negligence.” Power v. 
10 

Arlington Hosp. Ass'n, 42 F.3d 851, 856 (4th Cir. 1994). See 
also Roberts v. Galen of Virginia, Inc., 111 F.3d 405 (6th Cir. 
1997); Summers v. Baptist Medical Ctr. Arkadelphia, 9\ F 3d 
1132, 1137 (8th Cir. 1996); Correa 
v. Hospital San 
Francisco, 69 F.3d 1184, 1192 (1st Cir. 1995), cert. denied, 
__ US. __, 116 S. Ct. 1423, 134 L.Ed.2d 547 (1996), 
Eberhardt v. City of Los Angeles, 62 F.3d 1253, 1258 (9th 
Cir. 1995); Repp v. Anadarko Mun. Hosp., 43 ¥ 3d 519, 522 
(10th Cir. 1994); Holcomb 
v. Monahan, 30 F.3d 116, 117 
(11th Cir. 1994). 
The Courts have labored to define exactly what a 
claimant must prove if the statute does not create a federal 
remedy for medical malpractice. The Court of Appeals for 
the Sixth Circuit determined that the way to limit the 
potentially sweeping scope of the statute's language is to 
require that a claimant prove improper motive in order to 
recover under EMTALA. Roberts v. Galen of Virginia, Inc., 
supra.; Cleland v. Bronson Health Care Group, Inc., 917 
F.2d 266 (6th Cir. 1990). 
While the Sixth Circuit's 
interpretation clearly prevents EMTALA from being utilized 
as a substitute for a state law malpractice action, in the 
opinion of the Fourth Circuit such an interpretation also 
makes proving a civil EMTALA claim virtually impossible. 
Power v. Arlington Hosp. Ass'n, supra. The Fourth Circuit 
explained its rejection of the Sixth Circuit's reasoning as 
follows: 
11 
First, there is nothing in the statute 
itself that requires indigence, inability to pay 
or any other improper motive on the part of a 
hospital as a prerequisite 
to recovery. The 
language of § 139Sdd(a) simply refers to “any 
individual” 
who presents to the emergency 
room. Second, it seems to us that the expanse 
of motives suggested by the Sixth Circuit... 
is so broad as to be no limit at all, and as a 
practical matter amounts to not having a 
motive requirement. Anyone 
who alleges that 
she did not receive an appropriate 
medical 
screening examination can simply find an 
improper motive that fits, whether it is sex, 
nationality, income, or occupation, and simply 
allege 
it. Which leads, thirdly, to the most 
fundamental 
problem 
with 
the 
motive 
requirement: 
the proof predicament. We agree 
... that having to prove the existence of an 
improper motive on the part of a hospital, its 
employees or its physicians, 
would make a 
civil EMTALA claim virtually impossible. 
We 
do not believe that proving 
the inner 
thoughts and prejudices of attending hospital 
personne! is required in order to recover under 
EMTALA. 
Power v. Arlington Hosp. Ass'n, 42 F 3d, at 857-858. 
12 

Based on this or similar reasoning, the Courts of 
Appeal for the District of Columbia Circuit, the First Circuit, 
the Fourth Circuit and the Eighth Circuit expressly disagree 
that proof of an improper motive is a prerequisite to recovery 
under EMTALA. 
Gatewood v. Washington Health Care 
Corp., 933 F.2d 1037 (D.C. Cir. 1991); Correa v. Hospital 
San Francisco, supra., Power v. Arlington Hosp. Ass'n, 
supra., Summers v. Baptist Medical Ctr. Arkadelphia, supra. 
Rather than adopting the limitation espoused by the Sixth 
Circuit, each of th: «her Circuit Courts of Appeal have 
determined that the proof required of a claimant in order to 
succeed under EMTALA consists of evidence to show that 
the claimant was not treated as other similarly situated 
patients are treated, within the hospital's capabilities. 
According to each of the Circuit Courts of Appeal that have 
considered the issue, other than the Sixth, it is up to the 
hospital itself to establish its own policies and procedures. 
Having done so, the Hospital must apply them alike to all! 
patients. 
The significance 
to a claimant of a uniform 
interpretation of the prerequisites to recovery under EMTALA 
could not be more clearly evident than it is with the facts of 
the case 
at bar. Both the District Court and the Court of 
Appeals in this case held that a genuine issue of fact existed 
as to whether or not Humana had stabilized Johnson's 
emergency medical condition prior to her discharge and 
transfer. 
Furthermore, Johnson was able to produce 
substantial evidence that she was not treated as other similarly 
13 
situated patients are treated at Humana. Specifically, Johnson 
produced Humana’s own policies and procedures which 
require that when a patient is transferred from the hospital's 
critical care complex to another facility, Humana’s social 
services staff obtain approval for the transfer from the 
patient's family and further require the attending physician at 
Humana to obtain the prior approval of the physician at the 
receiving facility before discharging and transferring a patient. 
These requirements were not simply ministerial in 
nature but went to the very heart of the discharge and transfer 
procedure 
at Humana. In fact, Johnson’s 
most fundamental 
argument in this case is that had anyone acting with Johnson's 
best interest at heart been given an opportunity to investigate 
Crestview as a potential placement they would have readily 
recognized that Crestview could not handle Johnson’s care, 
would have refused transfer to Crestview and would have 
insisted that other options be pursued. It makes sense that 
after a transfer decision has been made for a patient coming 
out of the critical care complex one of the most important 
functions of the physician is the arrangement of physician 
acceptance of the patient at the receiving facility. It is 
certainly probable that patient's coming from a critical care 
consultation between the physician arranging the transfer and 
Where, 
as in Johnson's case, the transferring physician 
knows nothing about the receiving facility, one-to-one 
consultation with the physician at the receiving facility is 
14 

imperative so that the transferrnng physician can determine 
that the receiving facility is capable of handling the patient's 
care needs. Had Humana physicians or social workers simply 
followed their own policies and procedures, this whole 
litigation 
would have been avoided. The litigation would 
have been avoided because the physicians responsible for 
accepting patients at Crestview would have declined 
acceptance 
of Johnson. 
This proof of disparate treatment is sufficient in every 
Circuit in the United States, other than the Sixth, to get an 
EMTALA 
claim to a jury. However, 
in the Sixth Circuit no 
matter how compelling the other evidence, if the discharging 
physician cannot be implicated with a showing of improper 
motive in the execution of his or her discharge of a patient, a 
claim under EMTALA will fail. 
The prerequisites for recovery under EMTALA should 
be uniform throughout 
the United States. Claimants 
in the 
Sixth Circuit should not be required to prove more, and 
cannot expect that they can prove less, than claimants in other 
areas of the country. Potential claimants in Circuits which 
have not directly addressed the issue should not be left to 
guess whether their Circuit will adopt the interpretation of the 
Sixth Circuit or the interpretation of the other Circuits. This 
clear divergence in the interpretation of this important federal 
statute among the Circuit Courts of Appeal is a compelling 
reason for this Court to issue a Writ of Certiorari in this case.

KENNEDY, Circuit Judge. Plaintiff, Jane Roberts, 
appeals the District Court’s order granting summary judgment 
for the defendant in this action under the Emergency Medical 
Treatment and Active Labor Act, 42 U.S.C. § 1395dd (Supp. 
1996). 
Plaintiff alleges that her critically-ill niece, Wanda 
Johnson, was transferred from the defendant hospital to a 
nursing care facility in an unstable condition in violation of 
the Emergency Medical Treatment and Active Labor Act. 
For the reasons set forth below, we AFFIRM the judgment of 
the District Court. 
I. 
Wanda Johnson was hospitalized at Humana Hospital- 
University of Louisville (“Humana”) on May 19, 1992 after 
sustaining massive severe injuries caused by a collision with 
a truck. 
A few weeks before July 22, 1992, the surgical 
resident treating Johnson requested a Humana social worker 
to search for a long term care facility for Johnson. While the 
social worker, Nancy Fred, was aware Johnson had no 
insurance, the resident who authorized her discharge had no 
such knowledge. On July 22, 1992, after two nursing homes 
turned Johnson down following on-site evaluations of her 
condition by their directors of nursing, a represeatative of 
Crestview Health Care Facility in the State of Indiana agreed 
A2 
to take Johnson. She was transferred 
to Crestview on July 24, 
1992. 
The day after Johnson’s arrival at Crestview, 
Johnson’s condition deteriorated significantly. She was taken 
to Midwest Medical Center, also in Indiana, where she 
remained for many months incurring $388,679.93 in medical 
bills. The State of Indiana rejected Johnson's application for 
medical assistance under the Indiana Medicaid Program 
because 
she 
failed 
to meet 
the 
Indiana 
residency 
requirements. 
On August 30, 1993, plaintiff, as guardian for Wanda 
Johnson, filed this action under the Emergency Medical

DAVID A. NELSON, Circuit Judge, concurring in 
part and dissenting in part. 
I! agree with the court's 
disposition of Mrs. Johnson's negligence claim. As explained 
in Part IV of Judge Kennedy’s opinion, the surgical residents 
could not properly be found to have been ostensible agents of 
defendant Humana - and absent an agency relationship, the 
residents’ alleged negligence could not properly be imputed 
to Humana. 
I disagree with the court’s disposition of Mrs. 
Johnson’s claim under the Emergency Medical Treatment and 
Active Labor Act. A covered hospital that is acting from an 
improper motive is prohibited by the Act from discharging a 
patient such as Mrs. Johnson for transfer to another facility 
without 
first having stabilized 
her medical condition. 
Stabilization has not been achieved if the transfer is likely to 
result in material deterioration of the patient’s condition. 
Like my colleagues on the panel, I believe that there is a 
A23 

genuine issue of fact as to whether Mrs. Johnson was 
stabilized at the time of her transfer from Humana to the 
Crestview facility. Unlike my colleagues, 
| believe that there 
is also a genuine issue of fact as to whether Humana acted 
from an improper motive. The issue is a material one, under 
our case law, so it seems to me that summary judgment ought 
not to have been granted on the statutory claim. 
As far as the record discloses, to be sure, Dr. Abou- 
Jaoude -- the physician who issued Mrs. Johnson’s discharge 
order -- was not knowingly influenced by improper financial 
considerations when he decided that it was appropriate for 
Mrs. Johnson to be discharged. But as Dr. Abou-Jaoude 
made clear in his deposition testimony, the discharge decision 
was not made in a vacuum. Mrs. Johnson was a “high-risk” 
patient who needed “skilled nursing care,” Dr. Abou-Jaoude 
testified. He clearly would not have discharged her had he 
thought she was going to a boarding house, for example. The 
doctor talked to social worker Nancy Fred “multiple times” 
about Mrs. Johnson’s discharge plan, and he engaged in 
“frequent discussions” with Ms. Fred about where Mrs. 
Johnson was going to go and who was going to take care of 
her. He specified placement in a skilled nursing care facility, 
according to his testimony, and he assumed that the social 
workers would locate a facility with nurses who could take 
care of the patient. As far as selection of a specific placement 
was concerned, Dr. Abou-Jaoude testified, “we just left it in 
the hands of the social workers. . . .” 
A24 
The social workers’ hands may not have been as clean 
as the doctor’s apparently 
were. There is evidence 
from 
which a jury could infer that Ms. Fred was receiving pressure 
from Humana’s 
administration 
to get Mrs. 
Johnson 
discharged, there being no prospect that Humana would ever 
be paid for taking care of this patient. There is evidence 
from 
which a jury could infer that the Crestview facility was not an 
appropriate placement for Mrs. Johnson. And given the 
extent to which Dr. Abou-Jaoude relied on Ms. Fred to locate 
a facility to which Mrs. Johnson could be transferred without 
any material deterioration in her condition, it seems to me that 
it would be permissible for a jury to find that rather than 
letting Mrs. Johnson stay put until an appropriate placement 
could be found elsewhere, Humana sent Mrs. Johnson to an 
inappropriate facility because Humana was not being paid. 
Such a finding would make Humana liable under the Act. 
Accordingly, I respectfully dissent from affirmance of the 
summary judgment for Humana on Mrs. Johnson’s statutory 
claim.

A party moving for summary judgment has the burden 
of showing that there are no genuine issues of fact and that 
the movant is entitled to summary judgment as a matter of 
law. Adickes v. S.H. Kress & Co., 398 U.S. 144, 151-60, 
90 
S. Ct. 1598, 16 L. Ed. 2d 142 (1970); Felix v. Young, 536 
F.2d 1126, 1134 (6th Cir. 1976). Not every factual dispute 
A27 

between the parties will prevent summary judgment. The 
disputed facts must be material. They must be facts which, 
under the substantive law governing the issue, might affect 
the outcome of the suit. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 106 
S.Ct. 2505, 2510 (1986). The dispute must also be genuine. 
The facts must be such that if they were proven at trial, a 
reasonable jury could return a verdict for the non-moving 
party. /d. at 2510. The disputed issue does not have to be 
resolved conclusively in favor of the non-moving party, but 
that party is required to present some significant probative 
evidence which makes it necessary to resolve the parties’ 
differing versions of the dispute at trial. First National Bank 
of Arizona vy. Cities Service Co., 391 U.S. 253, 288-89 (1968). 
The evidence must be construed in a light most favorable to 
the party opposing the motion. Bohn Aluminum & Brass 
Corp. v. Storm King Corp., 303 F.2d 425 (6th Cir. 1962). 
The Hospital has moved for summary judgment, 
addressing Counts 1, 2, and 3 of the complaint. We will 
consider each of these arguments.

Section 1395dd is designed to keep hospitals from

A28 
to determine whether an emergency medical condition exists.

acted with an improper motive. 
Therefore, the Hospital

The plaintiff does not allege that the screening given 
by the

in May,

Count 2 of the complaint alleges that the Hospital 
acted under color of state law when it transferred Johnson to 
required to comply with the constitutional requirements of 
equal protection 
and due process. 
In order to proceed with these claims, the plaintiff 
must satisfy the state action requirement of the Fourteenth 
Amendment. Blum v. Yaretsky, 457 U.S. 991 (1982). “That 
amendment erects no shield against merely private conduct, 
however discriminatory 
or wrongful.” /d. at 1002. 
The Supreme Court has set forth the following three 
tests to determine whether challenged conduct amounts to 
State action: (1) the public function test; (2) the state 
compulsion 
test; and (3) the symbiotic relationship 
or nexus 
test. Wolotsky v. Huhn, 
960 F.2d 1331, 1335 (6th Cir. 1992) 
(citations omitted). The plaintiff argues that under either the 
public function or nexus test, the Hospital’s conduct was state 
action. 
The plaintiff first attempts to establish that the 
A39 

Hospital 
is fulfilling a public function. The test requires 
that 
“the private entity exercise powers which are traditionally 
exclusively 
reserved to the state... .” Wolotsky v. Huhn, 960 
F.2d 1331, 1335 (6th Cir. 1992). However, 
the provision of 
hospital services is not a power which has traditionally been 
exclusively reserved to the state. Moreover, the plaintiff has 
not established that in the absence of the Hospital, the state is 
required to provide these hospital services. See, Blum, 457 
U.S. at 1011. Therefore, the Hospital is not fulfilling a public 
function. 
Next, the plaintiff asserts that “there is a sufficiently 
close nexus between the State and the challenged action of the 
regulated entity so that the action of the latter may be fairly 
treated 
as that of the State itself.” Plaintiff's Memorandum at 
30 (citing Jackson v. Metropolitan Edison Co., 419 U.S. 345, 
351 (1974)). The plaintiff contends that since (1) the hospital 
was previously operated by the University, (2) the University 
expressed certain desires in its agreements with the Hospital, 
(3) the Hospital received appropriations from the state, and 
(4) the Hospital participated in Medicaid, there was a 
sufficient nexus between the state and the Hospital to amount 
to state action. 
The ultimate issue is whether the alleged infringement 
of rights is “fairly attributable to the state.” Crowder v. 
Conlan, 740 F 24 447, 449 (6th Cir. 1984). In Crowder, a 
physician sued a private hospital for civil rights violations 
alleged to have occurred in the restriction of his staff 
A40 
privileges. /d. The Crowder court found no state action in 
the 
hospital’s 
decision 
where 
the hospital 
received 
considerable revenues from the government, the hospital was 
subject to extensive regulation, and the hospital’s physical 
plant was owned by the county and leased to the defendant. 
The court noted that the county was not involved in the day- 
to-day operations of the hospital nor was it involved 
in the 
challenged action. /d. 
In Blum, the issue before the Court was whether state 
action attaches to a nursing facility’s summary discharge or 
transfer 
of a patient. Blum, 457 U.S. 991. The nursing homes 
in question were receiving federal funds for at least 90% of 
their patients. /d. Although the plaintiff in Blum actually 
sued state officials for the conduct of private parties, the 
reasoning applies in the instant case as well. Crowder, 740 
F.2d at 450. The Court in Blum noted, “[t}hat programs 
undertaken by the State result in substantial funding 
of the 
activities of a private entity is no more persuasive than the 
fact of regulation of such an entity in demonstrating that the 
State is responsible for decisions 
made by the entity in the 
course of business.” Blum, 457 U.S. at 1011 (holding that 
neither substantial regulation nor funding of a private 
enterprise 
amounted to state action). The Court found that the 
plaintiffs had failed to establish state action, because the 
decisions were independently made by private individuals. 
Id. at 1008. The state had not exercised such coercion or 
given encouragement that the choice must be deemed that of 
A4l 

the state. /d. at 1004. 
The United States Supreme Court and the Sixth 
Circuit Court of Appeals have found state action lacking in 
similar circumstances. We find state action lacking here as 
well. As in Blum and Crowder the decision in this case to 
transfer Johnson was not that of the state but that of the 
privately owned and operated hospital. 
Therefore, the defendant’s motion with respect to this 
count will be granted. 
IV 
Count 3 of the complaint alleges negligence on the 
part of the Hospital through its corporate acts, the acts of its 
employees, and the acts of the doctors as its “ostensible 
agents.” 
The Hospital challenges the allegations in this count 
on the ground that the medical residents were not the 
ostensible agents of the Hospital. 
The uncontroverted 
evidence is that the Hospital required conscious patients to 
sign a form acknowledging the independent status of 
physicians. The Hospital asserts that it did not hold itself out 
as ostensible employer of the medical residents and should 
not be liable for their alleged negligence. 
It is apparent from a review of the Kentucky cases 
concerning ostensible agency that the manner in which the 
principal (the Hospital) holds itself out to the public is 
controlling. 
In Williams v. St. Clare Medical Center, 657 
S.W.2d 590 (Ky. App. 1983), the court of appeals adopted 
A42 
ostensible 
agency to hold a hospital liable for the negligence 
of an independent contractor nurse. 
The Williams court 
noted: 
Apparent authority is not actual authority, but 
rather “is that which, by reason of prevailing 
usage or other circumstance, 
the agent is in 
effect held out by the principal as possessing. 
It is a matter of appearance, fairly chargeable 
to the principal and by which persons dealt 
with are deceived, and on which they rely. 
Id. at 595 (quoting Estell v. Barrickman, 571 S.W.2d 650, 652 
(Ky. App. 1978). By taking no action to give notice to the 
contrary, the defendant in Williams held out the nurse as its 
employee and was held liable for his negligence. 
This theory was applied to emergency room doctors 
by the Supreme Court of Kentucky in Paintsville Hosp. Co. 
v. Rose, 683 S.W.2d 255 (Ky. 1985). In that case, the court 
held that a “general representation to the public [was] implied 
from the circumstances.” 
/d. at 256. Although proof of 
reliance on the part of the unconscious patient was lacking, 
the court found the emergency room doctor to be the 
ostensible 
agent of the hospital, focussing on the appearance, 
the representations, and the manifestations of the principal 
and its apparent agent. /d. at 257. 
The Kentucky courts recognize that the determining 
factor in a question of ostensible agency is whether the 
A43 

principal holds out the third party as its agent. 
The 
undisputed evidence in this case is that the Hospital did not 
hold out these doctors as its agents. In fact, it required each 
conscious patient to sign an admission form acknowledging 
that the doctors were in fact independent contractors.' 
According to the Rose court, where the hospital holds 
out the 
doctors as its agents, the plaintiff need not prove actual 
reliance to establish apparent agency. Conversely, when the 
evidence demonstrates that the hospital as a matter of routine 
practice disclaims an agency relationship with its resident 
doctors, apparent agency is not shown even if the patient is 
unconscious and does not specifically receive notice of the 
disclaimer. Just as reliance is not necessary to prove apparent 
agency if the hospital does not routinely disclaim it, reliance 
is not necessary to disprove apparent agency where the 
hospital’s routine is to disclaim it. 
The test is whether 
the Hospital held out these doctors 
as its agents. 
The undisputed evidence is that it did not. 
Therefore, the defendant’s motion for summary judgment 
with regard to this portion of Count 3 will be granted. 
‘See, e.g. Floyd v. Humana of Virginia, Inc., 787 S.W.2d 267, 270 
(Ky. App. 1989), diser. rev. denied (holding that where the plaintiff 
had signed 
a form acknowledging the independent status of the doctors and there was no 
representation or other action to induce one to believe that the doctors were the 
agents of the hospital. there can be no valid argument that the ostensible 
agency doctrine should apply.)

CONCLUSION 
The Hospital 
has moved 
for partial summary 
judgment. As to Count 1 of the complaint, there are material 
issues of fact with regard to the issues as presented to the 
court. Therefore, the Hospital’s motion will be denied for 
that count. 
However, the plaintiff did not establish state 
action, and accordingly the motion will be granted as to 
Count 2. As to Count 3, the doctrine of ostensible agency 
does not apply to the Hospital in relation to the independent 
contractor doctors involved in treating Johnson at the 
Hospital. Therefore, the Hospital’s motion will be granted 
with regard to that portion of Count 3 which asserts a claim 
under the theory of ostensible agency.

OF OPINION 
This matter is before the court on the motion of the 
defendant for reconsideration of the court’s order dated 
November 13, 1995. In that order we denied the defendant’s 
motion for summary judgment with respect to count | of the 
complaint and further denied the defendant’s motion for 
remand. 
We discussed the relevant facts in our previous 
memorandum opinion and see no need to repeat them here. 
I 
In its previous memoranda, the defendant argued that 
since it did not act with an improper motive, the plaintiff's 
claim must fail. 
We did not consider the defendant's 
A48 
argument concerning causation, as it was not squarely before 
us. 
However, the defendant’s motion for reconsideration 
raises this argument and we will now address it. 
A hospital may be liable under the Emergency 
Medical Treatment and Active Labor Act (EMTALA) for 
“failing to stabilize” a patient’s condition prior to transferring 
the patient. Cleland v. Bronson Health Care Group, Inc., 917 
F.2d 266, 271 (6th Cir. 1990) (quoting DeBerry v. Sherman 
Hosp. Assoc., 741 F. Supp. 1302 (N.D. III. 1990). However, 
the plaintiff must establish that discriminatory motive caused 
the failure to stabilize. Cleland, 917 F.2d at 271. EMTALA 
provides protection not from medical malpractice but from 
discriminatory treatment or transfer. /d. at 272 (noting that 
EMTALA does not apply to hospital which acts in the same 
manner as it would for the usual paying patient). 
We determined in our prior opinion in this matter that 
there was an issue of fact with regard to whetlier Johnson was 
stable when she was transferred. 
We also noted that the 
plaintiff presented some evidence as to an improper motive on 
the part of the defendant hospital. However, we expressly 
confined our decision to those issues and did not address the 
issue of causation. 
The defendant contends that the decision to transfer 
Wanda Y. Johnson was based entirely on her medical 
condition, as determined by her physicians who were unaware 
of her financial status. It presents the affidavits of the doctors 
responsible 
for 
Johnson’s 
discharge. 
Defendant’s 
A49 

Memorandum, 
Ex. B (affidavits of Drs. Richardson, 
Harbrecht, and Abou-Jaoude). Each of these doctors states 
that Johnson was stable when she was transferred. They also 
state that her financial condition did not affect their medical 
decision to authorize her transfer. 
If “[iJn the hospital’s opinion, the patient was stable, 
and they would have believed that a patient with any differing 
characteristics would have been stable[,] “then” within the 
meaning of the Act [EMTALA] the hospital did its duty to 
stabilize” Johnson. Cleland, 917 F.2d at 271. This is true 
even if it turns out the “patient was in fact not stabilized{.]” 
Hines, 827 F. Supp. at 431 (explaining Cleland, which held 
that hospital fulfilled its obligation although the patient was 
later determined not to have been stable). 
The defendant has the burden of showing that there 
were no genuine issues of fact and that it is entitled to 
summary judgment as a matter of law. Adickes v S.H. Kress 
& Co., 398 U.S. 144, 151-60 (1970); Felix v. Young, 536 F.2d 
1126, 1134 (6th Cir. 1976). 
In this case the defendant 
presented evidence tending to negate the causative effect of 
any improper motive held by the defendant. 
Thus, the 
plaintiff was required to present some significant probative 
evidence making it necessary to resolve the parties’ differing 
versions of the dispute at trial. 
First National Bank of 
Arizona v. Cities Service Co., 391 U.S. 253, 288-89 (1968). 
Yet, the plaintiff presented no evidence that either the medical 
opinion that Johnson was stable or the decision to authorize 
ASO 
her transfer was caused by an improper motive. There is no 
issue as to this material fact and summary judgment is 
warranted. 
For the foregoing reasons, the defendant’s motion for 
reconsideration will be granted. 
The EMTALA claim in 
count | of the complaint 
will be dismissed. 
The defendant, in its original motion for summary 
judgment, 
asked the court to remand. Remand 
in this case is 
improper, inasmuch as the suit was Originally filed with this 
court. Yet, the plaintiff filed this suit invoking our federal 
question jurisdiction, and all that remains of the plaintiffs 
complaint is a claim for medical negligence under state law. 
“Needless decision of state law should be avoided both asa 
matter of comity and to promote justice between the parties, 
by procuring for them a surer-footed reading of applicable 
law.” United Mine Workers of America v. Gibbs, 383 U.S. 
715, 726 (1966). The Gibbs court noted that where State 
claims are before the court on pendent jurisdiction, “if the 
federal claims are dismissed before trial, even though not 
insubstantial in a jurisdictional sense, the state claims should 
be dismissed as well.” /d. 
For the above reasons, the federal EMTALA claim in 
count | of the complaint will be dismissed with prejudice and 
the pendent state law claim in what remains of count 3 will be 
dismissed for lack of jurisdiction in this court. 
An order in accordance with this opinion will be 
entered this date.
