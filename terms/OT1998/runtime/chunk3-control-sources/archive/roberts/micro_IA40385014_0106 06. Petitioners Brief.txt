STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
The Petitioner, Wanda Y. Johnson (hereinafter “John- 
son”), is a 40 year old mother of one child who was run 
over by a truck while walking home from her job as a 
cashier for Hardees Restaurant in Bardstown, Kentucky, 
late on the evening of May 19, 1992. (Deposition of 
Wanda Johnson, hereinafter “Johnson Depo., R. 50” at 
17-18)'. She was flown from Bardstown to the Respon- 
dent’s facility, Humana Hospital 
- University of 
Louisville (hereinafter “Humana”) where she was diag- 
nosed with serious fractures to her pelvis, right leg and 
four vertebrae of her spine as well as a brain injury. 
(Deposition of Walid Abou-Jaoude, M.D., hereinafter 
“Abou-Jaoude Depo., R. 67” at Exhibit 2). Johnson under- 
went exploratory surgery upon admission to Humana 
during which her spleen was removed. (/d.). A tracheos- 
tomy was surgically inserted into her neck and she was 
hooked to a ventilator to support her breathing. (/d.). 
Johnson's medical condition remained volatile through- 
out her hospitalization at Humana as she experienced 
numerous complications, including recurrent urinary 
tract infections and multiple pneumonias.

116-117, id. at 14-15). Her hospital course was further 
complicated by fever and overwhelming infection with 
an elevated temperature to as high as 108 degrees. (Abou- 
Jaoude Depo. at Exhibit 2). During her hospitalization, 
Johnson's neurologic status deteriorated to a state of flac- 
cid quadriparesis. (J.A. at 116, id. at 15). Despite 
Humana’s treatment, Johnson’s temperature spikes could 
not be controlled and the episodes of pneumonia and 
urinary tract infection continued through the last hours 
of her hospitalization. (J.A. at 126-128, id. at 49-50; Exhibit 
2). 
Despite the volatility of her condition, in early July, 
1992, Johnson’s physicians requested that the social ser- 
vices department at Humana begin making contacts with 
rehabilitation facilities and nursing homes to explore 
potential placement for Johnson if and when her condi- 
tion was stabilized and she was ready to leave an acute 
care setting. (J.A. at 118-120, Abou-Jaoude Depo., R. 67 at 
18-20; Deposition of Michele Weaver, R. 45 at 8). 
The social workers who work at Humana are 
employed directly by Humana. Their job includes making 
placement at nursing homes and rehabilitation facilities 
for Humana patients who will not be able to return home 
following their hospitalization. (Deposition of Nancy 
Fred, hereinafter “Fred Depo., R. 10” at 4-5). The social 
workers are also called upon to seek legal intervention 
for patients who are incapable of independent decision 
making. (Id. at 5). 

Humana’s social workers immediately experienced 
difficulty in their efforts to find potential placement facil- 
ities for Johnson. The social workers learned that John- 
son’s husband had abandoned their five year old son and 
was cohabitating with another woman in Springfield, 
Kentucky. (Id. at 14-15, 23). Although the husband occa- 
sionally called the hospital, he would not leave a tele- 
phone number or contact location which was reliable 
enough for the social workers to maintain a consistent 
dialogue in developing a discharge plan. (Id. at 23-24). 
Furthermore, Johnson’s mother was elderly and was her- 
self ill and unable to function independently. (Jd. at 23). 
No other close family member existed to provide assis- 
tance to the Humana social workers in developing a 
discharge plan for Johnson. Jane Roberts, Johnson’s aunt, 
occasionally visited the hospital and attempted to locate 
Johnson’s husband for the social workers. (J.A. at 19; id. 
at 24-25). However, Roberts had not been appointed as 
Johnson’s guardian at that time and did not have the 
legal authority, nor had she been requested, to act as 
Johnson’s surrogate decision maker. (Deposition of Jane 
Roberts, hereinafter “Roberts Depo., R. 51” at 21). 
When Humana’s social workers began their search 
for a placement for Johnson, she still could not breathe 
without assistance of a mechanical ventilator. (Fred 
Depo., R. 10 at 11-12). The ventilator dependency limited 
placement options. (Id.). However, days before her dis- 
charge, Humana unhooked Johnson from the ventilator 
and Humana’s social workers contacted Chandler Nurs- 
ing Home near Evansville, Indiana on July 20, 1992 as a 
possible placement facility. (J.A. at 19-23; id. at 25-28). 
Based on clinical information provided by Humana, 
Chandler Nursing Home accepted Johnson conditioned 
upon on-site evaluation and approval by its Director of 
Nursing. (Id.). However, on July 22, 1992, only hours 
before the planned transfer, Chandler’s Director of Nurs- 
ing rescinded the nursing home’s acceptance of Johnson 
following her personal evaluation. (J.A. at 23; id. at 28). 
Although Chandler was licensed as a skilled facility, its 
Director of Nursing determined that Johnson’s condition 
was too serious for the facility to provide adequate care. 
(J.A. at 23-24; id. at 29). Chandler’s decision to deny 
Johnson’s placement was an obvious setback for 
Humana’s social workers. However, they still expected 
within days to receive approval from a physical reha- 
bilitation facility which had been previously contacted by 
Humana social worker Nancy Fred. On July 20, 1992, 
Fred “predicted” in her progress notes that approval by 
that facility, New Medco Rehabilitation Center, would 
take only another one to two weeks. (J.A. at 19; id. at 25). 
New Medco’s expected acceptance of Johnson must have 
been encouraging to Fred since it was her opinion that a 
rehabilitation facility was more appropriate to treat John- 
son’s condition than was a nursing home. (Id. at 53). 
Another significant complication in locating a facility 
which would accept Johnson was her inability to pay. (Id. 
at 16-18). By mid-July, 1992, Johnson’s application for 
Kentucky Medicaid benefits had still not been approved. 
Johnson had no private health insurance and obviously 
did not have the resources to compensate any facility for 
her care. (Roberts Depo., R. 51 at 29; J.A. at 38-39; Fred 
Depo., R. 10 at 55). Johnson’s medical expenses at 
Humana had already approached $400,000.00 and it was 
obvious to Humana that Johnson would never be able to 

compensate it for those charges. (Deposition of John 
Nord, hereinafter “Nord Depo., R. 47” at 18-23). tiow- 
ever, regardless of Johnson's inability to pay, Humana 
was obligated to provide treatment to her as part of a 
contractual relationship between Humana, the Common- 
wealth of Kentucky, the City of Louisville, Kentucky and 
Jefferson County, Kentucky whereby Humana received 
millions of dollars in subsidies in exchange for its agree- 
ment to provide services at no charge to Kentucky 
patients who did not qualify for Medicaid, who had no 
other resources and who were without private health 
insurance. (Id.). 
In the midst of Humana’s placement dilemma and 
shortly after Chandler had denied acceptance of Johnson, 
Joanna Nolte, a marketer for Hometown Living Centers 
(“Hometown”) in Indianapolis, Indiana, visited Louisville 
in an effort to initiate a relationship with the Humana 
hospitals whereby Humana would place its patients in 
Hometown’s Indianapolis nursing homes. (J.A. at 26-27, 
41-42; Fred Depo., R. 10 at 31; Deposition of Joanna Nolte, 
hereinafter “Nolte Depo., R. 18” at 22). 
On the evening of July 22, 1992, Nolte met with 
members of Humana’s Social Services Department. (Id.). 
Among those who attended Nolte’s presentation was 
Nancy Fred, who had taken charge of Johnson's dis- 
charge plan. (Id.). Nolte was neither a doctor nor a nurse. 
(Nolte Depo., R. 18 at 4). Her job was to locate potential 
residents for her employer’s nursing homes and to refer 
those potential residents to administrators and directors 
of nursing at the respective facilities. (Jd. at 16-17). Nolte 
explained that Hometown operated several nursing 
homes in Indianapolis, including Mayfair Manor (“May- 
fair”) and Crestview Nursing Home (“Crestview”). (J.A. 
at 27-28; Fred Depo., R. 10 at 32). At the conclusion of 
Nolte’s presentation, she was approached by Nancy Fred 
who requested that Nolte look at Johnson and consider 
accepting Johnson in one of the Hometown facilities. (J.A. 
at 42-43; Nolte Depo., R. 18 at 23-24). According to Nolte, 
Fred appeared to be in a real placement dilemma with 
Johnson. (J.A. at 48; id. at 31). Nolte testified that Fred 
complained that she was receiving pressure from the 
administration at Humana to find a placement for John- 
son because of Johnson’s inability to pay for her care. 
(J.A. 44-46; id. at 26-27). Nolte explained to Fred that 
Johnson's level of acuity exceeded her own unilateral 
authority to grant approval. However, Nolte agreed to 
refer Fred’s request to the Administrator and Director of 
Nursing at Mayfair for evaluation. (J.A. 46-47; id. at 
29-30). 
All the while, Johnson’s medical condition continued 
to be volatile. On July 23, 1992, her temperature rose to 
101 degrees. (J.A. at 126-128; Abou-Jaoude Depo., R. 67 at 
49-51; Exhibit “A”). A sputum culture was abnormal and 
her doctor suspected that she had yet another episode of 
urinary tract infection. (Id.). On the evening before her 
discharge, Johnson’s right lung collapsed and treatment 
was- required every two hours. (Deposition of Donna 
Kaelin, R. 48 at 16). The morning of Johnson’s discharge, 
her white blood count was over twice the normal level, 
her temperature had been rising for two days, and the 
nurses noticed cloudy urine, thick yellow sputum and an 
elevated heart rate, all indicating the presence of infec- 
tion. (Deposition of Karen Martin, hereinafter “Martin 

Depo., R. 9” at 14-19; Deposition of Suzanne Griffith, R. 
49 at 9-10). The nurse on duty during Johnson's last day 
at Humana felt strongly enough about Johnson's condi- 
tion to specifically document in her nursing notes that 
she had called the elevated white blood count and tem- 
prature as well as the cloudy urine to the attention of the 
attending physician. (Martin Depo., R. 9 at 21-23). 
Meanwhile, Mayfair declined to accept Johnson 
because her care would require too many skilled nursing 
hours. (J.A. at 50; Nolte Depo., R. 18 at 33-34). However, 
on that same day Crestview, which had fewer skilled 
nurses than Mayfair, agreed to accept her. (Id.). The on- 
duty nurse at Humana learned at about midday on July 
24, 1992 that Johnson would be transferred to Crestview. 
(Martin Depo., R. 9 at 7). In fact, the first notation that 
Crestview had even been considered by the social 
workers at Humana appeared in a social services note on 
July 24, 1992, wherein Nancy Fred indicated that Johnson 
would be transferred to Crestview on that date at 2:30 
p.m. (Patient Progress Notes dated July 24, 1992 attached 
to Fred Depo., R. 10 at Exhibit “L”). 
In its haste to expedite Johnson’s transfer to Crest- 
view, Humana ignored its standard procedures and pro- 
tocols for discharge and transfer. (J.A. at 11-18, 33-34; 
Fred Depo., R. 10 at 41-42; Policy Procedure 768-901 and 
768-912). No authorization for the transfer was obtained 
from either Johnson’s husband or mother. (/d.). Further- 
more, although Johnson's condition had clearly deterio- 
rated during the previous two days and Johnson wasn’t 
even competent to understand or participate in her own 
discharge plan, Humana’s social workers made no effort 
to obtain court intervention and have a guardian or sur- 
rogate decision maker appointed for Johnson.? (J.A. at 22, 
116; Fred Depo., R. 10 at 27-28; Abou-Jaoude Depo., R. 67 
at 15). Instead, Humana unilaterally decided to ship John- 
son to Indianapolis and Nancy Fred simply called Jane 
Roberts, Johnson’s aunt and occasional visitor, told her 
that Johnson was being transferred to Crestview Nursing 
Home that afternoon and provided Roberts Crestview’s 
telephone number. (Roberts Depo., R. 51 at 22-23, 42-43). 
Fred did not ask Roberts to check with Johnson’s family 
to determine whether they would like to visit the facility 
before the transfer was arranged or whether they would 
even approve of a transfer which would place Johnson in 
a different state and some three hours driving distance 
from their home. (J.A. at 33-36; Fred Depo., R. 10 at 
41-43). Fred gave Roberts no information about Crestview 
or about any benefits it could provide to Johnson. 
(Roberts Depo., R. 51 at 23-24). In fact, neither Fred nor 
anyone else at Humana could then provide any such 
information because they knew almost nothing about 
Crestview. (J.A. at 27-29; Fred Depo., R. 10 at 32-33, 
48-49). 
Fred didn’t know and had failed to ask Nolte if 
Crestview had ever handled a patient with Johnson’s 
level of acuity. (Id.). Fred also didn’t know and again 
failed to ask whether or not Crestview had ever been

Despite the fact that Humana had never previously 
placed a patient at Crestview and despite the fact that it 
made no inquiries to gather even basic information, it 
decided to ship Johnson to Crestview and thereby rid 
itself of a patient who placed a serious financial drain on 
the hospital. 
Even more significant than Humana’s violation of its 
own discharge policy mandating that social workers 
advise patients or family of discharge plans in order to 
provide them at least an opportunity to decline the dis- 
charge and explore other arrangements, was Humana’s 
violation of its discharge policy by failing to gain 
approval for the transfer from the accepting physician at 
Crestview. (Roberts Depo., R. 51 at 43; Abou-Jaoude 
Depo., R. 67 at 35-36; J.A. 6-10; Policy Procedure 0420). 
Crestview’s doctors were unaware of Johnson's impend-

her medical condition until she had already arrived. (J.A. 
at 80-81; Deposition of Kambiz T. Karimi, M.D., hereinaf- 
ter “Karimi Depo., R. 89” at 12; Deposition of Taiseer

10-12). 
No reasonable person could argue that Crestview 
was an appropriate placement for Johnson. Even Crest- 
view’s own physicians admit that Crestview was inap- 
propriate and indicated that had they known all the 
relevant information about Johnson's condition they 
would not have accepted her at Crestview. (J.A. at 81-82; 
Karimi Depo., R. 89 at 13; J.A. at 88-91; Shatara Depo., R. 
90 at 23-26).

Johnson's condition deteriorated significantly as a 
result of her transfer from Humana. Upon Johnson’s 
arrival, the nurses at Crestview were alarmed by John- 
son’s condition and they believed she was going to die. 
(J.A. at 58-59; Deposition of Charna Y. Treser, hereinafter 
“Treser Depo., R. 92” at 66-67). Within hours, Johnson’s 
temperature spiked to 105 degrees and she was rushed to 
nearby Midwest Medical Center where she remained hos- 
pitalized for almost six months. (J.A. at 62; Deposition of 
John W. Stuy, M.D., hereinafter “Stuy Depo., R. 88” at 7, 
71). Dr. John W. Stuy, Johnson's principal treating physi- 
cian during her hospitalization in Indianapolis, testified 
that the complications experienced by Johnson in Indi- 
anapolis predated her transfer and were exacerbated 
because of her transfer to a facility which was unable to 
properly care for her. (J.A. at 62-69, 76-79; id. at 7-14, 
15-17, 27-31). Humana had transferred Johnson before it 
had received the full lab reports relative to the infection 
suffered by her during the last day of her hospitalization 
in Louisville and simply had prescribed a broad spectrum 
antibiotic hoping it would fight off the infection. (P.A. at 
A13; J.A. at 128; Abou-Jaoude Depo., R. 67 at 51). Accord- 
ing to Dr. Stuy, the antibiotic prescribed for Johnson 
when she left Humana was not sensitive to the lab cul- 
tures grown at Midwest Medical Center, indicating that 
the antibiotic was not treating her infection. (J.A. at 62-69, 
76-79; Stuy Depo. at 7-14, 15-17, 27-31). His opinion was 
that if she had remained on the wrong antibiotic much 
longer, she may well have died. (/d.). Dr. Stuy emphat- 
ically stated that Johnson should never have been trans- 
ferred from Humana. (Id.).

In addition to imperiling Johnson's life and health, 
Humana’s hurried transfer of Johnson to Indianapolis 
placed her in fiscal peril because she was ineligible for 
public medical assistance in Indiana. (Stipulations dated 
April 20, 1995, R. 62). Had she remained hospitalized at 
Humana, Johnson would not have incurred personal lia- 
bility for the payment of hundreds upon hundreds of 
thousands of dollars in medical expenses because of 
Humana’s contract with Kentucky governmental entities 
which paid it millions of dollars annually to provide care 
to indigent patients. (Nord Depo., R. 47 at 18-23). Unfor- 
tunately for Johnson, once she was transferred from 
Humana to Indianapolis she was no longer entitled to 
free medical care. Adding insult to injury, Johnson’s 
application for Medicaid benefits in Indiana was denied 
because she was not a resident of Indiana. (Stipulation 
dated April 20, 1995, R. 62). As a direct result of 
Humana’s unilateral decision to ship Johnson to Indiana, 
Johnson incurred and is now personally obligated to pay 
medical expenses in excess of $388,000.00. (Id.). 
In October, 1992, Roberts was appointed as Johnson's 
Guardian and shortly thereafter Roberts initiated this 
action against Humana alleging that Humana had vio- 
lated the provisions of the Emergency Medical Treatment 
and Active Labor Act, 42 U.S.C. § 1395dd, et seq. (here- 
inafter “EMTALA”) by transferring Johnson to Crestview 
before stabilizing her condition. After voluminous dis- 
covery, Humana moved the District Court for Summary 
Judgment on the EMTALA claim. (R. 63). In response to 
Humana’s Motion for Summary Judgment, the District 
Court determined that the relevant issues were, “whether 
Johnson was stabilized when she was transferred to 
13

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 
A. 
Although EMTALA was enacted in response to 
the widespread perception that hospitals were “dump- 
ing” indigent or uninsured patients for purely economic 
reasons, the statute as ultimately passed by Congress 
provides protections to “any individual” who comes to 
an emergency department or hospital seeking emergency 
medical treatment and is denied needed care. 42 U.S.C. 
§ 1395dd(a), (b)(1). This expansive language has been 
unanimously recognized by the Circuit Courts of Appeal, 
including the Sixth Circuit. However, the Sixth Circuit 
has substantially limited EMTALA’s practical application 
by requiring not only proof of violation of the statute but 
also further proof that the cause of the statutory violation 
was an improper motive by the hospital. 
The Sixth Circuit’s injection of an improper motive 
requirement is not based upon any express language in 
EMTALA. In fact, the plain reading of EMTALA compels 
a conclusion clearly contrary to that reached by the Sixth 
Circuit but in accord with the holdings of every other 
15 
circuit court of appeals in the country which has consid- 
ered the issue. EMTALA contains no motive requirement. 
The clear Congressional purpose of EMTALA was to 
deter patient dumping and to compensate victims of 
dumping. An interpretation of EMTALA which places the 
significant burden upon claimants to establish improper 
motive would undermine EMTALA’s purpose. 
Furthermore, Johnson's interpretation of EMTALA 
that no proof of improper motive is required is buttressed 
by the absence of a fault requirement in the civil enforce- 
ment subsection of Section 1395dd(d). While a neighbor- 
ing subparagraph requires the Secretary of Health and 
Human Services to prove that hospitals and doctors have 
negligently violated EMTALA before civil monetary pei- 
alties can be imposed, no such fault requirement is con- 
tained in the subparagraph permitting civil enforcement 
of EMTALA by harmed individuals. This conspicuous 
absence of a fault requirement in the private cause of 
action subsection of EMTALA demonstrates that Con- 
gress did not intend to saddle an individual with having 
to prove fault in order to recover under EMTALA. The 
Secretary of Health and Human Services, who adminis- 
ters the civil monetary fine program created by EMTALA, 
also interprets EMTALA as containing no requirement 
that a claimant prove some “impermissible motive” on 
the part of the hospital. In short, there is no statutory 
basis for imposing a requirement on claimants under 
EMTALA to prove that a hospital’s violation of the stat- 
ute was the result of some improper motive, and impos- 
ing such a burden would thwart the attainment of the 
Act’s goals. 

16 
B. The Sixth Circuit stands alone in its injection of a 
requirement that a claimant under EMTALA prove that 
the offending hospital acted with an improper motive. 
The Fifth Circuit expressly rejected the argument that 
proof of an improper motive was required under 
EMTALA in Burditt v. U.S. Dept. Of Health and Human 
Services, 934 F.2d 1362 (5th Cir. 1991). Burditt, like the case 
at bar, involved an EMTALA claim under Section 
1395dd(b) that a patient was improperly transferred from 
a hospital before the patient’s condition had been stabi- 
lized. 
Three other Circuits have rejected the notion that a 
claimant is required to prove an improper motive on the 
part of the hospital in cases dealing with the “appropriate 
medical screening examination” section of EMTALA. The 
District of Columbia Circuit, the Fourth Circuit and the 
First Circuit have specifically considered the Sixth Cir- 
cuit’s reasoning and expressly rejected it, holding that 
proof of an improper motive is not required under 
EMTALA. Additionally, while not specifically citing the 
Sixth Circuit’s position or addressing a claim that 
improper motive must be shown, four other circuits have 
reached conclusions that are inconsistent with the Sixth 
Circuit’s construction of EMTALA. These courts have 
recognized that it is impermissible to unilaterally change 
the plain reading of EMTALA to impose proof require- 
ments which have no statutory basis.

While not specifically citing the Sixth Circuit’s posi- 
tion or addressing a claim that improper motive must be 
shown, four other circuits have considered “appropriate 
medical screening” cases and have reached conclusions 
that are inconsistent with the Sixth Circuit’s construction 
of EMTALA. The conclusion of those courts has been that 
the appropriateness of a medical screening examination 
under EMTALA depends not on the motivation with 
which the hospital acted in failing to follow standard 
screening procedures but, instead, on whether the screen- 
ing examination is even-handedly administered to all 
patients whose medical conditions are in relevant 
respects similar. See Eberhardt v. City of Los Angeles, 62 
F.3d 1253, 1258 (9th Cir. 1995) (“[T]he test is whether the 
challenged procedure was identical to that provided sim- 
ilarly situated patients.”); Repp v. Anadarko Mun. Hosp., 43 
F.3d 519 (10th Cir. 1994) (“[A] hospital satisfies the 
requirements of § 1395dd(a) if its standard screening 
procedure is applied uniformly to ail patients in similar 
medical circumstances.”); Holcomb v. Monahan, 30 F.3d 
116, 117 (11th Cir. 1994) (“As long as a hospital applies 
the same screening procedures to indigent patients which 
it applies to paying patients, the hospital does not violate 
[Section 1395dd(a)].”); See also Marshall v. East Carroll 
Parish Hosp. Serv. Dist., 134 F.3d 319, 322 (5th Cir. 1998) 
(“[AJn EMTALA ‘appropriate medical screening examina- 
tion’ is not judged by its proficiency in accurately diag- 
nosing the patient’s illness, but rather by whether it was

performed equitably in comparison to other patients with 
similar symptoms.”).!2 
The universal rejection of the Sixth Circuit’s creation 
of an improper motive requirement under EMTALA makes 
it abundantly clear that other courts find no such limiting 
language in EMTALA and recognize that they cannot uni- 
laterally change the plain reading of the statute.
