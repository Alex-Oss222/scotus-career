STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
Statutory Background 
Congress enacted EMTALA to prevent hospitals from 
“dumping patients,” that is, from transferring patients 
upon physician’s orders to other institutions for economic 
or other non-medical reasons. 
The problem of patient 
dumping became acute in the 1980s for two reasons. 
First, the number of Americans who lacked health in- 
surance grew rapidly from the late 1970’s through the 
mid-1980’s. 
H.R. Rep. No. 100-531, at 7 (1988). 
Sec- 
ond, by reforming the Medicare payment system in 1983, 
Congress eliminated the ability of hospitals to pass on to 
the government the costs of caring for indigent patients. 
Id. Given these systemic incentives for hospitals not to 
admit indigent or uninsured patients, Congress concluded 
that federal legislation was necessary because “28 States 
[had] no” laws that would “ensure that no emergency 
patient is denied emergency care because of inability to 
pay.” 131 Cong. Rec. S 13,904 (daily ed. Oct. 23, 1985) 
(statement of Sen. Kennedy). 
The Act responds to the patient-dumping problem by 
requiring hospitals to provide patients for whom treat- 
ment is requested in an emergency room with (a) an 
appropriate medical screening examination, and with either 

(b) stabilizing treatment of the emergency medical con- 
dition or (c) an appropriate transfer to another medical 
facility for such treatment. 
42 U.S.C. §§ 1395dd(a), 
(b), (c). The Act provides for multiple methods of 
enforcement, and thus Congress did not look to private 
individuals as the primary enforcers of its ban on patient 
dumping. First, the Secretary of Health and Human Serv- 
ices can impose civil money penalties on participating 
hospitals and physicians who negligently violate any re- 
quirement of the Act. 42 U.S.C. § 1395dd(d)(1). Any 
physician who commits a violation that is “gross and 
flagrant or is repeated” also can be excluded by the Sec- 
retary from participation in Medicare and state health 
care programs. 42 U.S.C. § 1395dd(d)(1)(B). 
Second, 
any individual who suffers harm as a direct result of a 
violation by a participating hospital can bring a private 
civil action to recover damages for personal injury that 
are available under state law. 
42 U.S.C. § 1395dd(d) 
(2)(A). Third, a medical facility, such as a transferee 
hospital, that suffers a financial loss as a direct result of 
a violation by a participating hospital also can bring a 
civil action to recover damages that are available under 
state law. 42 U.S.C. § 1395dd(d)(2)(B). The relevant 
provisions of EMTALA are reprinted in the appendix to 
the petition for certiorari. 
Pet. App. A55-A66. 
Factual Background

She was

Pur-

In this case, however, it is important to

This Court has long followed the “ordinary rule of 
statutory construction that if Congress intends to alter the

incumbent upon the federal courts to be certain of Con-

The Sixth Circuit reached the same ultimate judgment, 
but by a path that cannot be located within the language 
of the statute. Respondent urges the Court to do more 
than simply inform the court of appeals of the error of its 
way, and instead, take advantage of this case as an Oppor- 
tunity to make clear that EMTALA is not a radical sta- 
tute that effectively supersedes state malpractice law for 
all patients who arrive at a hospital’s emergency room. 
The Court should hold that EMTALA fills the gap in 
state law by creating a physician-patient relationship that 
imposes upon hospitals limited duties to respond to the 
known needs of a patient’s immediate emergency condi- 
tion and then looks to state law exclusively to govern the 
physician-patient relationship thereafter. 
By taking this 
approach, the Court will accomplish much more than 
merely eliminating an aberrational rule of law that ap- 
plies only in four States, which is what petitioner urges the 
Court to do. Instead, the Court can provide critical guid- 
ance that will spare both hospitals (and physicians) and pa- 
tients the need to expend scarce resources litigating the 
metes and bounds of EMTALA and spare federal courts 
the burden they now assume of being inundated with 
routine medical malpractice cases masquerading as fed- 
eral statutory causes of action. 
The argument of the United States as amicus curiae 
nudges the Court to go beyond the limited objectives of 
petitioner. Respondent thus urges the Court to recognize 
the provocative nature of the government’s submission 
and to reject it by entertaining and adopting respondent’s 
alternative theories in support of the judgment below.
