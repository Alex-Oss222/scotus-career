STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. 
Procedural History Of The Litigation In The 
District Court And Summary Of The Ninth 
Circuit’s Opinion 
Plaintiffs and Respondents Lawrence, Clara and April 
Perkins (hereinafter “plaintiffs” or “Perkins”) sued the City 
of West Covina (hereinafter "City") and various detectives 
and officers of the City, initially alleging various Fourth 
Amendment violations arising out of an execution of a 
search warrant in connection with a murder investigation 
involving a former boarder. 
(J.A. 7-11.)* Several items, 
including a “starter pistol" and $2,469.00 in cash, were 
seized from plaintiffs’ residence during the execution of the 
warrant on May 21, 1993. 
(J.A. 84.) 
a 
Specifically, plaintiffs’ complaint alleged that 
officers of the West Covina Police Department searched 
plaintiffs’ home without probable cause, caused damage 
to plaintiffs’ property during the search, seized money 
from plaintiffs home and conducted the search in a 
manner that far exceeded the scope authorized under the 
warrant. 
(J.A. 7-9.) 

4 
On May 10, 1994, the district court granted summary 
adjudication in favor of the individual officers with respect 
to several of plaintiffs’ Fourth Amendment claims and 
granted summary adjudication in favor of the City insofar 
as the claims against the City were based upon the officers’ 
search of plaintiffs’ residence. 
(Pet.App. B 4.) However, 
the district 
court postponed 
ruling on 
an unalleged 
procedural due process claim, i.e., that the postdeprivation 
judicial remedy for return of the seized property was 
inadequate, and ordered further briefing from the parties. 
(Pet.App. B 6-7.) 
Plaintiffs never 
filed an amended 
complaint adding this unalleged claim, and the district court 
did not rule on this claim prior to entry of the final 
judgment. 
(Pet.App. D 2.) 
Judgment was entered in favor of all defendants on 
August 4, 1994, and plaintiffs appealed on August 31, 
1994. 
(J.A. 65; Pet.App. C.) 
However, on April 30, 
1996, the Ninth Circuit remanded the case to the district 
court to clarify and resolve the procedural due process 
issue, i.e., plaintiffs’ claim “that the City violated due 
process by failing to provide adequate postdeprivation 
procedure for the recovery of seized property. 
(Pet.App. 
D 2.) 
The District Court’s Findings and Conclusions on 
Limited Remand. On the limited remand, the district court 
issued an order stating that the postdeprivation due process 
claim was not resolved during the prior proceedings and 
invited the parties to file a motion for summary judgment 
to attempt 
to resolve 
the claim. 
(Pet.App. 
E 2.) 
Accordingly, the City filed a motion for summary judgment 
and 
the plaintiffs 
filed 
a cross-motion 
for summary 
judgment. 
(Pet.App. E 2.) After a hearing and having 
considered the arguments made by the parties, the district 
court granted summary judgment in favor of the City, 
holding that plaintiffs waived any claim concerning the 
adequacy of the postdeprivation judicial remedy provided 
5 
by California Penal Code section 1536. The district court 
reasoned that the waiver rule applied because: 
‘[t]he evidence shows at most that the plaintiff 
[Lawrence Perkins] made one trip to the Municipal 
Court, was advised that there was no file in his _ 
name and ceased any efforts to obtain an order 
returning his property. He filed nothing, he made 
no inquiry about the procedures... .’ 
(Pet.App. E 7.) 
The district court also refused to excuse plaintiffs’ 
failure to avail themselves of this judicial/statutory remedy 
on the grounds that the City failed to give “notice” of the 
remedy, finding that plaintiff Lawrence Perkins "was told 
that he had to get a court order to get his property back, he 
was told what court and what judge to go to, and 
he was 
given the date of the warrant and of the search." (Pet. App. 
E 7.) 
Therefore, "[t}he City’s failure to give Perkins 
formal 
notice of the procedure 
did not render 
that 
procedure ‘inadequate to the point that it [wa]s meaningless 
or nonexistent.’" 
(Id.) 
The Ninth Circuit’s Opinion. On appeal, a two-judge 
majority of the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals reversed 
summary judgment in favor of the City, and directed the 
district court to enter summary judgment in favor of the 
plaintiffs. 
The Court of Appeals determined that under 
well established legal precedent, there was "no requirement 
of a prior hearing before the seizure of possessions under 
the search warrant." 
(Pet.App. A 14-15.) The court also 
found that California Penal Code sections 1536, 1540, and 
State 
mandamus 
procedures, 
provided 
“adequate” 
postdeprivation judicial remedies to recover the property. 
(Pet.App. A 17.) The Court of Appeals acknowledged that 
plaintiffs were told by a City police officer that they had to 
obtain a court order for the return of the property, and 

6 
acknowledged that the officers executing the warrant left a 
note at the search site which stated: 
To Whom it May Concern: 
l. 
Thess premises have been searched by peace 
officers of the West Covina Police Department 
pursuant to a search warrant issued on 5-20-93, 
by the Honorable Dar Oki, judge of the 
Municipal Court, Citrus Judicial District. 
2. 
The search was conducted on 5-21-93. A list of 
the property 
seized pursuant 
to the search 
warrant is attached. 
3. 
If you wish further information, 
you may 
contact Detective Ferrafi or Det. Melnyk at 
[telephone number]. 
(J.A. 76-77; Pet.App. A 4-5.) The form also listed “Lt. 
Schimanski"’s name and his telephone number. 
(J.A. 77; 
Pet.App. A 5.) 
The Court of Appeals also acknowledged that “the 
search warrant was listed in a public document with the 
court clerk by the address where she search occurred, along 
with the number of the search warrant." (Pet.App. A 6.) 
Nonetheless, the court held that the City violated plaintiffs’ 
procedural due process rights by failing to provide adequate 
postdeprivation "notice" of this judicial remedy which, 
according to the court, was required under Memphis Light, 
Gas, & Water Div. v. Craft, 436 U.S. 1, 98 S. Ct. 1554, 
56 L. Ed. 2d 30 (1978), and the balancing test set forth in 
Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319, 96S. Ct. 893, 47 L. 
Ed. 2d 18 (1976). (Pet.App. A 17-20.) Specifically, the 
couft below concluded that the City violated plaintiffs’ 
rights by failing to include in the note left at the search site 
"thé availability of a judicial remedy should [they] wish to 
claim [their] property," or to “provide some guidance for 
invoking that remedy." 
(Pet.App. A 18.). The court held 
that, at minimum, the “note” left on the premises must not 
7 
only include the information which was provided in this 
particular case, but also the following: 
the notice 
must 
inform 
the recipient of the 
procedure for contesting the seizure or retention of 
the property taken, along with any additional 
information required for initiating that procedure in 
the appropriate court. 
In circumstances such as 
those presented by this record, the notice must 
include the search warrant number or, if it is not 
available or the record is sealed, the means of 
identifying the court file. 
It also must explain the 
need for a written motion or request to the court 
Stating why the property should be returned. 
(Pet.App. A 22.) 
At the same time, however, the Court of Appeals 
correctly acknowledged that the trial court, not police 
officers, has exclusive authority over the procedures for 
obtaining the release of seized property: 
[o}fficers seizing property do so on behalf of the 
court for use in a judicial proceeding, and they 
must 
respond 
to court -orders 
to release 
the 
property. (Citation). The officers have no right in 
the property or in its possession, and have no 
authority over it except as the court may order. 
(Citation [emphasis added]. ) 
(Pet.App. A 16.) 
B. 
Summary Of The Evidence 
When the warrant authorizing the search of plaintiffs’ 
residence was issued by the municipal court, it was sealed 
because 
it was 
connected 
to 
an 
ongoing 
murder 
investigation. 
(J.A. 45.) 
Pursuant to California Penal 
Code section 1535, the City police left at the plaintiffs’ 

home an inventory sheet which listed 17 items seized 
pursuant to the warrant, including a starter pistol and cash 
in the sum of $2,469. 
(J.A. 84.) 
Attached 
to the 
inventory sheet, the City police also left a form called 
“Search Warrant: Notice of Service.” (J.A. 76-77.) This 
form stated: 
“If you wish further information, you may 
contact Det. Ferrari” or another detective, and gave their 
telephone numbers. 
It also identified the searching agency 
(West Covina Police), gave the date that the warrant was 
issued (May 20, 1993) as well as the date of the search 
(May 21, 1993), and identified the issuing court and judge 
(Dan Oki of the Citrus Municipal Court). 
(J.A. 76-77.) 
Following the search of plaintiffs’ residence, Detective 
Ferrari filed 
the search warrant and the return of search 
warrant with the criminal clerk of the Citrus Municipal 
Court. 
(J.A. 45.) Even though the search warrant was 
sealed, it was listed in a public document with the criminal 
court clerk by the address where the search occurred, along 
with the number of the search warrant. 
(J.A. 45-46; 
Pet.App. A 6.) 
Shortly after the execution of the search warrant, Mr. 
Perkins contacted the police department regarding the 
property seized from his residence. 
Mr. Perkins was 
advised that a court order was required for the release of 
his property from the police department. 
(J.A. 122.) He 
was further advised to go see Judge Oki who had signed 
the original search warrant. 
(J.A. 40, 124.) Mr. Perkins 
contends that about one month after the execution of the 
search, he attempted to get a court order for the release of 
his property by going to the Citrus Municipal Court. (J.A. 
122-123.) 
He claims that he was advised by court 
personnel that Judge Oki was on vacation and that he 
would be gone a week or two but that 
any judge could 
release the property. 
(J.A. 40, 124.) 
Mr. 
Perkins 
attempted to obtain an order for the release of the property 
by providing his name only. 
(J.A. 124.) When he was 
purportedly advised that there was nothing in the court 
9 
under his name, he ceased any efforts to obtain an order 
for the return of his property. (J.A. 123-124.) He did not 
submit any kind of written request, fill out any form, go 
back to the court on any other occasion, or make any other 
inquires 
in any manner 
to the court. 
(J.A. 
124; 
Pet.App. E 7.) 
Rather, he merely contacted the police 
department to advise that he had been unable to obtain a 
court order for the release of his property, at which point 
the suggestion was made by Detective Ferrari that Mr. 
Perkins may want to retain an attorney to assist him in 
getting a court order. 
(J.A. 40.) 
Even after plaintiffs retained counsel prior to or in 
December of 1993 in connection with their instant lawsuit, 
plaintiffs and their counsel did nothing to obtain a court 
order for the release of the seized property. 
Plaintiffs’ 
counsel was aware of the search warrant number as early 
as January 26, 1994 and at the latest, March 15, 1994. 
(J.A. 70.) 
However, even having the search warrant 
number, plaintiffs’ counsel did nothing to obtain an order 
for the release of the property seized. 
(J.A. 70.) In fact, 
it was only in response to the City’s motion for summary 
judgment 
that the office of plaintiffs’ counsel 
even 
contacted the Citrus Municipal Court on May 2, 1994 and 
inquired about obtaining the number 
for the subject 
warrant. 
(J.A. 77.) 
- 
When it became apparent to the City’s counsel that 
plaintiffs were going to attempt to rely on their own lack of 
effort to support their contention that the West Covina 
Police Department was wrongfully maintaining custody of 
their seized property, the City’s counsel prepared and filed 
on May 17, 1994, a request and proposed order releasing 
the seized property to the plaintiffs or their counsel which 
was signed by the judge on June 17, 1994 (J.A. 69.) On 
June 24, 1994, upon receipt of the June 17, 1994 court 
order, the police department released all items referenced

The analysis set forth in Mathews v. Eldridge, supra, 
and applied in Memphis Light, supra, provides 
no support 
for the Ninth Circuit’s expansion of procedural rights in the 
context of post-deprivation remedies. 
In Memphis Light, 
this Court held that a municipal utility agency subject to 
control by the municipality had a constitutional duty to 
provide 
“notice” of available internal administrative 
procedures for disputing utility bills prior to discontinuance 
of gas and electricity. 436 U.S. at 4, 14-15, 98 S. Ct. at 
1557, 1563. 
Three critical facts, absent in the present 
case, were essential to this Court's determination. 
First, 
because the utilities were deemed to be “essential” to the 
health and safety of citizens, and even a brief cessation 
could result in serious health and safety concerns, the 
Mathews v. Eldridge factors required a municipal utility

4, 19-20, 98 S. Ct. at 1537, 1559, 1565. 
Second, the municipal utility company had authority 
over the “res” in question, namely, the services deemed to 
be “essential” to its customers. 
Third, the municipal utility company had developed 
dispute resolution procedures as required by state law. /d. 
at 15, n. 16, 98 S. Ct. at 1563. Pursuant to that authority, 
the utility developed detailed internal procedures for the

Id. at 5, n. 4, 13, 98S. Ct. at 1559, 1562 ("*The billing of 
customers, the determination as to when a final notice is

prior to a termination of life-essential utility 
In the present case, the City’s police officers were not

Fuentes v. Shevin, supra, 407

Moreover, the procedures at issue are not procedures

It makes sense to require the

But when the procedures are provided by state law, all

plaintiffs have to do is look to the statutes. See, Texaco,

Furthermore, 
unlike Memphis Light, a California

Nonetheless, the Court of Appeals, on pain of section

The Ninth Circuit’s use of the Mathews v. Eldridge,

this Court’s continued efforts to prevent federal courts from

Understandably concerned with principles of comity and

Efforts

rebuked by this Court in Graham v. Connor, 490 U.S.

In these decisions, 
this Court 
expressed concern over the constitutionalization of state law 
by the creation of additional “substantive” due process

at 394-395, 109 S. Ct. at 1870-1871; 510 U.S. at 273-74, 
114 S. Ct. at 813.

procedural rights in cases such as Parratt v. Taylor, supra, 
and Hudson v. Palmer, supra, where a state has eliminated

deprivation judicial or statutory remedies. 
In Parratt v.

the Fourteenth Amendment would necessarily result 
in turning every alleged injury which may have 
been inflicted by a state official . 
.

superimposed upon whatever systems may already 
be administered by the States. 
(Citation)." 
Id. at 544, 101 S. Ct. at 1917.

B. 
The Effects Of The Decision Below Are Far 
Reaching And Require Overly Burdensome 
Changes To Existing State Property Seizure 
Procedures 
Which 
Have 
Long’ 
Been

A municipality has no authority over court procedures 
governing the return of lawfully seized property, anymore 
than a municipality has control over the means through 
which a tort action can be prosecuted. 
Yet, in this 
particular case, the Ninth Circuit has imposed a burden on 
police departments to research court procedures--which 
may vary from time to time and from court to -court-- 
concerning the precise manner in which property obtained 
under a valid search warrant may be recovered. To avoid 
section 1983 liability, municipalities must fashion some sort 
of “notice” 
in the form of legal advice, continuously 
monitor court processes 
for any relevant changes in 
procedure, update the required legal advice to reflect any 
changes in the relevant court processes, and provide the 
necessary information after a deprivation has occurred but 
before an aggrieved party pursues the judicial remedy. 
The Ninth Circuit's rationale for the Perkins’ notice 
promises to pave the way for further federal intrusion into 
areas which traditionally have been relegated to the states. 
Indeed, it takes little imagination to predict the Ninth 
Circuit's next step, namely, to require public agencies--who 
have no authority or control over existing state law judicial 
and statutory remedies recognized in cases such as Parratt 
v. Hudson, supra, Hudson v. Palmer, supra, and numerous 
other circuit court decisions--to provide legal advice and 
guidance on invoking such remedies. 
In doing so, the 
newly created Perkins notices would also render several 
long established state statutory provisions regulating routine 
State 
property 
seizures 
constitutionally 
inadequate, 
necessitating their revision by the state legislature. 

36 
For example, it has long been recognized that when 
persons are arrested and incarcerated, their personal effects 
are “seized”, inventoried and placed in official custody. 
Hudson v. Palmer, supra, 468 U.S. at 541, 104 S. Ct. at 
3207 (Justice O’Conner concurring); see also, Illinois v. 
LaFayette, 462 U.S. 646, 103 S. Ct. 2609, 77 L. Ed. 2d 
65 
(1983). 
Moreover, 
law

94 S. Ct. 1234, 1239, 39 L. Ed. 2d 771 (1974). 
In 
California, law enforcement personnel are authorized to 
seize and retain property from prisoners under Government 
Code section 26640 while Penal Code section 4403 requires

(Id.).

the
