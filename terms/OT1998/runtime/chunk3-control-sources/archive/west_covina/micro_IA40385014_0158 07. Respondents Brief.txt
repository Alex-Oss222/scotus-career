STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. 
Introduction. 
At issue in these proceedings is the constitutional 
adequacy of notice given to Respondents after the seizure 
of property from their home during the execution of a 
search warrant. Pet.App. Al7. Pursuant to a policy of the 
City of West Covina, their police officers, after having 
served a search warrant on Respondents’ unoccupied 
residence, left a form “Search Warrant: Notice of Service” 
document which provided only limited information 
regarding the seizure of the property. J.A. 76-77. The 
officers left the notice pursuant to a standard practice, 
custom, or policy of the City. Pet.App. Al3. The docu- 
ment gave notice that, on May 21, 1993, the residence had 
been searched by the West Covina Police Department, 
pursuant to a warrant signed by Judge Oki on May 20, 
1993. The notice also identified the name and phone 
number of the lead detectives. Pet.App. A17-18. The offi- 
cers also left an inventory of the property taken. Penal 
Code 1535. The officers did not, however, leave a copy of 
the search warrant (which includes the identifying search 
warrant number), as most other states and federal law 
require. Neither did the officers leave any other informa- 
tion which would allow the Respondents to identify their 
property. As a consequence, Respondents were denied an 
opportunity for a hearing to seek return of their property.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 
Notice is a fundamental component of due process, 
regardless of the pre or postdeprivation character of a 
case. The United States concedes that notice is required 
under the circumstances presented in the record. The 
Petitioner does not. The case law amply supports the 
mandate of notice in a postdeprivation context. Where 
there is no opportunity for a predeprivation hearing or 
notice, the necessity of adequate postdeprivation notice is 
compelling. The notice must include the fact of the sei- 
zure, the key information about the seizure to access the 
procedure for securing return of property, and the means 
for securing the return of property. The need for notice of 
the seizure itself is fundamental, as the United States 
concedes. The need for the basic information to identify 
the property is also clear, for without it, the postdepriva- 
tion procedure is meaningless. Finally, the need for notice 
sacrifices formality for efficiency. See Martel v. County of Los 
Angeles, 56 F.3d 993 (9th Cir. 1995). 
of the means of securing return of property is as compel- 
ling as the need for predeprivation notice, in the circum- 
stances such as those presented in this record. The 
imposition of a duty to provide this fundamental notice is 
consistent with the relevant line of Court precedent and 
merely defines notice in a postdeprivation context, with- 
out expanding or creating any new federal due process 
rights. 
The Parratt-Hudson line of cases requires that, in 
cases where there is no need for predepvivation notice 
and a hearing, due process is satisfied orly if a meaning- 
ful postdeprivation remedy is available 
Notice, most 
often, will affect the meaningfulness of that postdepriva- 
tion remedy. By providing notice of only the seizure of 
property itself, the Petitioner deprived Respondents of 
the information necessary to implement the procedure for 
return of property and, therefore, rendered the process 
meaningless. 
Moreover, a fundamental flaw of the Petitioner’s and 
Amici'’s position is their assumption that there was no 
impending hearing affecting the disposition of the 
Respondent's property. This erroneous assumption led to 
inadequate notice being given to Respondents. The gov- 
ernment, once it retained, as opposed to seized, the prop- 
erty, was engaging in nonrandom and authorized conduct 
which renders the Petitioner’s position against notice 
unsupportable. 
The traditional notice test set out in Mullane v. Central 
Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306 (1950), and 
expounded upon in Mathews 
v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319 
(1976), involves the balancing of the governmental and

private interests. This balance establishes that the notice 
provided to Respondents was insufficient and favors 
requiring the Petitioner to correct its form of notice. The 
burden involved in correcting the notice, which would 
have avoided the increased risk of erroneous deprivation 
of property, was minimal. The Petitioner already pro- 
vided a document entitled “Search Warrant: Notice of 
Service” at the residence. A simple modification would 
satisfy due process. Additionally, there were other feas- 
ible and customary alternatives available to correct the 
form of notice chosen. 
Reliance on Memphis Light, Gas & Water Div. v. Craft, 
436 U.S. 1 (1978), is appropriate in that the Court 
addressed the necessity for notice in that case. Notice is a 
broad due process concept, the discussion of which is 
relevant in any context. Additionally, since there was an 
impending hearing looming, and any emergency 
in 
retaining the property no longer exist-', the Mathews 
balancing test, applied in Memphis 
 <ht, was appro- 
priately utilized. This court has ex, aged the Mathews 
balancing test in many types of due process cases, and it 
remains a useful guide. 
None of the California statutes or cases cited by the 
Petitioner or Amici provide adequate notice to Respon- 
dents on the record of this case. Respondents were unable 
to access the procedure for return of property because 
they were not given the information needed to identify 
the property before the state court. No statute or case 
could have provided this factual information. Therefore, 
the maxim that one is charged with the knowledge of the 
law, is inapplicable to this case. With regard to the notice 
of procedures for the return of property, the cases cited in

support of Petitioner's position are irrelevant and distin- 
guishable form the facts as presented in the record. 
The notice supplied by the Petitioner is qualitatively 
inferior to that provided by the federal government and 
the majority of other states. Fed.R.Crim.P. 41, upon which 
most state statutes are modeled, requires that the law 
enforcement authority is to leave a copy of the search 
warrant at the residence searched. Had Petitioner com- 
plied with a similar mandate, it is likely that Respondents 
would have been able to retrieve their property under the 
appropriate procedures. The federal statute and similar 
state statutes betray Petitioner's position. Those agencies 
have adopted laws which satisfy due process; the notice 
provided here falls below that standard. 
Finally, since due process is flexible and subject to 
the dictates of each particular situation, Respondents rec- 
ognize that reasonable minds can differ in balancing the 
competing interests. However, respectfully, all reasonable 
minds would conclude that due process requires notice 
which includes the search warrant number, case name, or 
case number, the lack of which prevented the Perkins 
family from obtaining not only a meaningful hearing on 
their request, but also the actual return of their property. 
This principled holding should be affirmed by this Court. 
The requirement that the Petitioner give notice of the 
identification of the procedure is also supported by the 
record and Court cases. Without compromising that posi- 
tion, Respondents assert, alternatively, that the court 
should affirm the holding that the notice was less than

A. 
This Court Has Previously Recognized the 
Need for Notice of Remedies in Postdepriva- 
tion Contexts. 
In Schroeder v. City of New York, 371 U.S. 208 (1962), 
the City of New York, through its water board, instituted 
a condemnation proceeding whereby water was to be 
diverted from a river some 25 miles upstream from the 
plaintiff's property. The notice of condemnation proceed- 
ing was instituted not for a hearing on the right to object 
to the diversion, but on the City’s “intention to make 
application to such court for appointment of commission- 
ers of appraisal.” Id. at 209. Under the statute, regardless 
of the plaintiff's right to appear at the hearing on the 
appointment of appraisers, 
a landowner had three years 
within which to file a claim for damages with the city.
