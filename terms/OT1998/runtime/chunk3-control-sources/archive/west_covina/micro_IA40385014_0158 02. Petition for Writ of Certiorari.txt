REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 
This Court should grant the Petition for Certiorari. The 
court of appeals creation of a new procedural right to 
“notice” in the form of legal advice concerning post- 
deprivation judicial remedies is not only erroneous, but also 
inconsistent with decisions of this Court and decisions in 
numerous circuit courts of appeals. 
Moreover, 
by 
imposing section 1983 liability under this new procedural 
right over and above that provided by admittedly adequate 
State law remedies, the published decision raises serious 
concerns relating to principles of comity and federalism. 
I. THE COURT OF APPEALS ERRONEOUSLY 
CREATED A NEW NOTICE REQUIREMENT 
IN POST-DEPRIVATION PROCEDURAL DUE 
PROCESS CASES IN A MANNER THAT 
CONFLICTS 
WITH 
THIS 
COURT’S 
DECISIONS AND DECISIONS IN OTHER 
CIRCUITS.

This Court’s decisions, as well as the decisions in other 
circuits, 
have recognized 
two different 
Categories of 
sa 
procedural due process claims, and have articulated a 
framework 
for 
determining 
whether 
the 
particular 
procedures 
at issue provide due process. 
In "pre- 
deprivation” 
cases, 
a 
public 
entity 
(usually, 
an 
administrative agency) has authorized certain procedures to 
be followed before a deprivation can occur. 
In this 
category of cases, the Court employs the balancing test set 
forth in Mathews 
v. Eldridge, supra, to determine whether 
the "notice" given and the procedures made available 
provide an meaningful opportunity to be heard prior to the 
deprivation of a property or liberty interest. Mathews 
v. 
Eldridge, 424 U.S. at 341-349, 96 S. Ct. at 905-910 
(weighing the disability claimant’s interest in continued 
disability benefits, the fairness and reliability of existing 
pre-termination procedure, the probable value of additional 
procedural safeguards, and the burden of such additional 
safeguards, due process does not require an evidentiary 
hearing prior to the discontinuance’ 
of disability benefits); 
is 
Li 
W 
iv. V 
, 436 U.S. 1, 
16-19, 98 S. Ct. 1554, 1564-65 (Mathews factors require, 
municipal utility company to provide notice of internal 
procedures for protesting the proposed termination of utility 
services before termination); Dixon 
v. Love, 431 U.S. 105, 
112-115, 97 S. Ct. 1723, 1727-29, 52 L. Ed. 2d 172 
(1977) (no evidentiary hearing required under Mathews 
factors prior to revocation of license for outstanding traffic 
tickets because respondent has opportunity for full judicial 
hearing in the underlying traffic convictions). 
However, this Court, as well as other circuit courts, 
also recognize a second category of cases which involve 
"post-deprivation" judicial or statutory remedies to correct 
a deprivation that has already occurred. In these cases the 
analysis set forth in Mathews 
v. Eldridge, supra, has been 
applied 
in determining 
whether 
some 
form of pre- 
deprivation notice and hearing is required, and the court 
has determined that "the value of predeprivation safeguards 

[e.g., notice and an opportunity to be heard before the 
deprivation] 
-- is negligible in preventing the kind of 
deprivation at issue." Zinermon 
v. Burch, 494 U.S. 113, 
129, 110 
S. Ct. 975, 985, 108 
L. Ed. 2d 100 (1990); 
see 
also Decker v. Hillsborough County Attorney's Office, 845 
F.2d 17 (1st Cir. 1988) (state’s failure to return a criminal 
defendant's property to him immediately upon his acquittal 
did not constitute deprivation of property without due 
process 
because 
the 
state 
statute 
provided 
judicial 
procedures through which to assert his property right); 
Murphy 
v. Collins, 26 F.3d 541, 543 (Sth Cir. 1994) (no 
procedural du~ process violation where prisoner's property 
confiscated in violation of the prison’s procedures because 
prisoner could pursue a tort action for conversion); Ramsey 
vy. Board of Education of Whitley County, 844 F.2d 1268 
(6th Cir. 1988) (no procedural due process violation where 
school district unilaterally reduced teacher's compensation 
for sick leave days because teacher could pursue breach of 
contract action); Barnett 
v. Centoni, 31 F.3d 813, 816 (9th 
Cir. 1994) (no procedural due process violation where a 
state inmate’s prison status was downgraded in a 
disciplinary action because California law provided an 
adequate 
post-deprivation 
remedy 
for 
any 
property 
deprivation); New 
Alaska 
Dev. Corp. v. 
Guetschow, 869 
F.2d 1298, 1305 (9th Cir. 1989) (the availability of "post- 
deprivation state iaw remedies provides all the process due” 
for a court-appointed receiver's alleged misappropriation of 
funds in a divorce proceeding). 
In post-deprivation cases, the available judicial or 
Statutory remedies are not inadequate simply because 
recovery might be difficult, time-consuming, speculative, 
or uncertain). Parratt 
v. Taylor, 451 U.S. at 543-44, 101 
S. Ct. at 1917 (since the state tort remedy was available at 
the time of the plaintiff's loss and he failed to use it, he 
failed to state a procedural due process claim); United 
States v. An Article of Device "Theramatic", 715 F.2d 
1339, 1342-43 (9th Cir. 1983) (rejecting plaintiff's claim 
that a post-seizure judicial hearing was inadequate because 
it put the “burden on defendant to request a hearing"); 
Easter 
House y. Felder, 910 F.2d 1387, 1405-06 (7th Cir. 
1990); Vicory 
vy. Walton, 721 F.2d 1062, 1065-66 (6th 
Cir. 1983), cert. denied, 469 U.S. 834 (1984) ("in section 
1983 damage suits for deprivation of property without 
procedural due process the plaintiff must plead and prove 
that 
state 
remedies 
for 
redressing 
the 
wrong 
are 
inadequate"). 
All of these decisions hold that the availability of 
adequate post-deprivation judicial or statutory remedies 
satisfies the procedural component of the Due Process 
Clause if the remedies provide the aggrieved party with an 
adequate mechanism to correct a deprivation that has 
already occurred. None of these cases even remotely 
suggest that due process requires a municipality, who has 
no authority over the property in question or the procedures 
necessary to invoke these remedies, to provide advance 
legal advice or information concerning how to pursue the 
remedies after a deprivation has occurred but before an 
aggrieved party invokes the judicial remedy. 
In Parratt 
v. Taylor, 451 U.S. 527, 101 S. Ct. 1908, 
1917, prison officials lost a prisoner's personal property. 
Auer balancing the relevant interests, this Court determined 
that the normal “predeprivation notice and opportunity to 
be heard" was "pretermitted," and that the state tort claims 
act provided an adequate post-deprivation remedy. (Id. at 
538-539, 541, 543-44, 101 S. Ct. 1914, 1916-17.) 
This 
Court rejected plaintiff's claim that the state tort claims 
remedy for unlawful destruction of property was inadequate 
because "it provides only for an action against the State as 
opposed 
to 
its individual employees, 
it contains 
no 
provisions for punitive damages, and there is no right to a 
trial by jury." 
451 U.S. at 543-44, 101 S. Ct. at 1917. 
The court reasoned thus: 

- 10 - 
Although the state remedies may not provide the 
respondent with all the relief which may have been 
available if he could have proceeded under § 1983, 
that does not mean that the state remedies are not 
adequate 
to satfy the requirements 
of due process. 
Id. at 543, 101 S. Ct. at 1915. Since the state tort remedy 
was available at the time of the plaintiff's loss and he failed 
to use it, he failed to state a procedural due process claim. 
Id. 
Similarly, in Hudson v. Palmer, 468 U.S. 517, 104 S. 
Ct. 3194, 82 L. Ed. 2d 393 (1984) a prisoner alleged that 
during the search of his cell, a prison guard deliberately 
and 
maliciously 
destroyed 
some 
of the 
prisoner's 
possessions. 
This Court determined that correctional 
officers were not in a position to provide a predeprivation 
procedure, and that under Virginia law, the prisoner’s right 
to sue the correctional officer under state law for 
conversion or detinue provided all the process that was 
due. 
Id. at 534-35, 104 S. Ct. at 3204. 
The Court 
rejected plaintiff's argument that state law remedies should 
be deemed inadequate because they were "far from certain 
and complete,” observing that the fact that an injured party 
"might not be able to recover under [state law] remedies 
the full amount which he might receive in a section 1983 
action is not . 
. 
. determinative of the adequacy of the state 
remedies. (Citation)" 468 U.S. 
at 535, 104 S. Ct. at 3204- 
05. 
Similarly, in Easter 
House v. Felder, 910 F.2d 1387, 
1405-06 (7th Cir. 1990), plaintiff, a daycare provider, sued 
a state licensing agency and its employees for allegedly 
depriving them of a property interest because of the 
agency's delay in renewing the provider's daycare license. 
The court held that various post-deprivation 
tort remedies 
provided all the process that was "due" 
even though "the 
state road to recovery" might be a "lengthy and speculative 
process": 
-ll- 
We note that almost all litigation, whether 
conducted in a state or federal forum, may be 
Id. at 1406; see also, Economic 
Dev. Corp. v. Stierheim, 
782 F.2d 952, 955-56 (llth Cir. 1986) (state law tort 
remedies provide all the process that is due even if relief 
under section 1983 would be greater). 
As the majority acknowledges, under California law the 
authority over these judicial remedies is vested exclusively 
in the court system. (App. 
A 16.) As such, the judicial 
remedies are indistinguishable from the category of judicial 
or statutory remedies available to civil rights plaintiffs in 
cases such as Parratt v. Taylor, supra, Hudson v. Palmer, 
supra, and numerous decisions in the circuit courts of 
Despite these decisions, the court of appeals essentially 
concluded that a municipality's failure to provide adequate 
advice at the search site concerning how to pursue the 
judicial remedies available under California Penal Code 
sections 1536 and 1540 rendered the judicial remedy too 
burdensome. 
Clearly, the decision is based on the same 
argument repeatedly rejected by this Court and other circuit 
courts, 
namely, 
that 
a post-deprivation 
remedy 
is 

-12- 
inadequate

A law enforcement officer is 
entitled to qualified immunity in a § 1983 
action if the district court determines that, 
in light of clearly established principles 
governing the conduct in question at the 
time of the challenged conduct, the officer 
could reasonably have believed that the

governing the official’s conduct was 
clearly established at the time the 
challenged conduct occurred. 
The second 
step then asks whether, under that clearly 
established law, a reasonable officer could 
have believed the conduct was lawful. 
{1} 
Mendoza v. Block, 27 F.3d 1357, 1360 (9th Cir. 
1994) (as amended) (citations omitted). 
The plaintiff

violated was clearly established at the time of the alleged 
misconduct. Romero v. Kitsap County, 931 F.2d 624, 
627 (9th Cir. 1991).

The cash 
The officers found the cash in the bedroom closet

[5] A large amount of cash may be evidence of

See, e.g., United

United States v. Soyland, 3 F.3d 1312, 
-A 1l- 
1315 (9th Cir. 1993)($4,600), cert. dismissed, 115 S. Ct.

clearly established that large amounts of cash may be 
evidence of drug activity. We now consider whether the 
officers acted reasonably in the light of that rule. 
[6] In each of the cases cited above drugs were 
also discovered. 
In this case the officers found no drugs 
in Perkins’ home. 
Nevertheless, they had other reasons

large amount of cash relative to the Perkins family’s 
apparent financial situation. Although--as was the case 
here--a large bundle of cash may turn out to be 
innocently earned, that does not mean that the detectives 
could not reasonably believe otherwise. 
We hold that, in light of the law clearly 
established at the time of the search, the detectives could 
reasonably believe their seizure of a large amount of cash 
was lawful.

Ill. 
Perkins’ right to trial 
Perkins argues that summary judgment violates 
his right to trial. This issue is frivolous. Summary 
adjudication of a case with no material issues of fact does 
not deprive a party of the right to trial. 
See, e.g., Pease 
-A 23- 
v. Rathbun-Jones Engineering Co., 243 U.S. 273, 278 
(1917). Summary Judgment is an integral part of the 
federal rules, which are designed to ensure the just, 
speedy, and inexpensive determination of every issue. 
Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 327 (1986) 
(quotations omitted). Because it determines only 
questions of law, summary judgment does not deprive 
the nonmoving party of its right to a jury trial. See /tel 
Capital Corp. v. Cups Coal Co., 707 F.2d 1253, 1261 
(11th Cir. 1983).

LEAVY, Circuit Judge, concurring in part and dissenting 
in part: 
I concur in all of Judge Boochever’s opinion 
except section II.B. I dissent from that section because I 
do not believe that due process requires "that the police 
notify Perkins of the availability of a judicial remedy 
should he wish to claim his property; and provide some 
guidance for invoking that remedy." By imposing a duty 
on the police officers to advise Perkins on matters of 

-A 24- 
state law, the opinion adds a due process requirement 
that has support neither in the case law nor in the Due 
Process Clause. 
I would agree that in circumstances requiring a 
predeprivation hearing, notice of such a hearing or 
process is necessary. The Supreme Court’s standard of 
"notice reasonably calculated, under all the 
circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the 
pendency of the action," Mullane v. Central Hanover 
Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950), has been 
developed and applied in the context of predeprivation 
procedures. See e.g., Mullane, (publication notice of 
proceeding to settle trust funds accounts found to be 
inadequate); Memphis Light, Gas & Water Div. v. Craft, 
436 U.S. 1 (1978) (notice of dispute resolution 
procedures prior to terminating utility service held to be 
inadequate); Greene v. Lindsey, 456 U.S. 444 (1982) 
(posted notice of eviction found to be constitutionally 
inadequate); Schneider v. County of San Diego, 28 F.3d 
89 (9th Cir. 1994), cert. denied, 115 S.Ct. 1112 (1995) 
(due process violation where seizure notice did not state 
that abandoned vehicles would be destroyed). These 
requirements are inapplicable where, as here, Perkins’ 
property was rightfully seized pursuant to a search 
warrant without need for a predeprivation proceeding. 
Because Perkins’ property was lawfully seized, 
due process requires "the availability of some meaningful 
means by which to assess the propriety of the State’s 
action at some time after the initial taking." Parratt v. 
Taylor, 451 U.S. 527, 539 (1981). The majority 
concede that the postdeprivation procedures provided 
under California law "satisfy the requirements of due 
process 
. 
. 
. and conform with the requirements laid 
-A 25- 
down by the Supreme Court of the United States." 
Perkins’ property was rightfully seized and held by the 
police, and California law provides a means for him to 
obtain return of his property. 
I cannot agree that due 
process requires the City of West Covina to advise 
Perkins of his remedies. 
As the majority points out, Perkins brought this 
action in November 1993. In June 1994, Perkins’ 
property was returned upon motion of the city. Surely 
the majority does not intend to allow recovery for 
deprivation of Perkins’ property while he and his lawyer 
failed to seek its return. The Due Process Clause does 
not require the City of West Covina to provide legal 
advice to Perkins or any other person with a claim 
against the city.

As a general rule, a municipality is not liable 
under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 on the basis of respondeat 
superior. Instead, the municipality will only be held 
liable if plaintiff shows that the violation resulted from 
an unconstitutional policy, practice or custom. See

In order to prevail, plaintiff need not

constitutional deprivations, or, plaintiff may prove that

the unconstitutional action was taken by an individual

engaging in unconstitutional conduct. McRorie

Perkins was told he needed a court order. He 
was told which court and judge to go to. It is not 
unreasonable to require him to submit some kind of 
written request in an attempt to test the adequacy of the 
procedure. Plaintiffs’ counsel says "Lawrence Perkins 
tried desperately to get his property returned." Oppos. 
at 2. But that is an egregious mischaracterization 
of the 
evidence. As defendants 
say in their reply, "[t}he 
evidence shows at most that the plaintiff made one trip to 
the Municipal Court, was advised that there was no file 
in his name and ceased any efforts to obtain an order 
returning 
his property. He filed nothing, 
he made no 
inquiry about the procedures... ." Reply 
at 1. 
Plaintiffs argue that Perkins’ failure to invoke that 
§ 1536 procedure should be excused because he was not 
given notice of it. But he was told that he had to get a 
court order to get back his property, he was told what 
court and what judge to go to, and he was given the date 
of the warrant and of the search. The Court believes 
that Perkins was given all of the information he needed 
in order to submit an informal written request to the 
court having jurisdiction over his property. The City’s 
failure to give Perkins formal notice of the procedure did 
not render that procedure "inadequate to the point that it 
[wa]s mncaningieee or non-existent." Easter House, 
Supra. 
* Defendants also argue that state law provided two 
additional adequate remedies: a writ of mandate 
and an 
action for conversion. 
In view of the Court’s holding 
that plaintiff 
has failed to show that the § 1536 procedure 
was constitutionally inadequate, the Court expresses no 
opinion as to these other two alleged procedures. 

-E 8- 
Ill. 
Conclusion 
| 
Even assuming arguendo that Perkins had no 
means of obtaining the warrant number, the procedure 
derived from § 1536 for release of property by court 
order was not such a clearly inadequate remedy as to 
excuse Perkins’ obligation to attempt it. Since he did not 
do so, thus depriving the Court of the ability to evaluate 
its adequacy, the Court holds that plaintiffs have failed to 
prove that state law did not provide a constitutionally 
adequate remedy for the return of seized property. The 
Court therefore GRANTS defendants’ motion for 
summary judgment dismissing plaintiffs’ procedural due 
process claim, and DENIES plaintiffs’ cross-motion for 
summary judgment on such claim. 
In accordance with 
the limited remand order, the Court will serve this Order 
upon the Ninth Circuit panel hearing the appeal in this 
action in order to advise it of the disposition of plaintiffs’ 
procedural due process claim. 
IT IS SO ORDERED.
