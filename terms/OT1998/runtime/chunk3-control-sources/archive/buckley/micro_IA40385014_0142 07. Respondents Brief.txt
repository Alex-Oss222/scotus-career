B. 
THE "FRAUD" FACADE. 
Petitioner, and her state amici colleagues, rely heavily 
on fraud prevention to justify the petition circulation 
restrictions at issue here. They suggest that fraud is, or will 
become, rampant if non-registered voters are permitted to 
collect signatures without identification badges and absent the 
filing of written reports on circulator activity. They assert that 
fraud is "very flagrant" (Petitioner's Brief, p. 5) and/or “large 
scale." Amicus Brief of State of Washington, p. 3. They 
argue that the elimination of fraud is paramount. 
The 
protection of political expression and Petition Clause are a 
distant second on their priority list, even though these rights 
are "inspired by the same ideals of liberty and democracy that 
gave us the freedoms to speak, publish, and assemble.” 
McDonald v. Smith, 472 U.S. 479, 485, 105 S.Ct. 2787, 
2791 (1985); and see also Thomas v. Collins, 323 U.S. 516, 
531, 65 S.Ct. 315, 323 (1945) ("The grievances for redress 
of which the right of petition was insured, and with it the right 
of assembly, are not solely religious or political ones."). 
Neither the evidence at trial, nor the briefs of the 
Petitioner and the amici, demonstrate that the allegation of 
“flagrant” or “large scale" fraud is other than an imaginary 
construct. 
There is no empirical or anecdotal evidence to 
show that the registration, reporting, or badge requirements 
will reduce this already truncated problem. 
At trial Petitioner admitted to finding nine instances of 
alleged fraud where individuals were referred for prosecution 
in 1992. Petitioner's Brief, p. 5. This alleged fraud affected 
between several hundred and several thousand signatures. Tr. 
D. 2, p. 146, In. 14. 
In this same year over 1,200,834 
petition signatures were submitted to the Petitioner. Exhibit A 
attached hereto. Thus, assuming arguendo 2,000 fraudulent 
Signatures, a mere .17% occurrence of fraud was perpetrated. 
Notably, this fraud was not discovered by Petitioner. 
At trial Petitioner's assistant testified: “We do not actually 
review for fraud up front. 
That is done by the opponents 
normally ...." 
Tr. D. 2, p. 141, Ins. 5-6. This alleged 
fraud was committed by registered circulators, because only 

registered voters are permitted to circulate petitions in 
Colorado.’ There were no badge or monthly paid circulator 
reporting requirements in 1992 when the alleged fraud was 
perpetrated. 
Assuming arguendo that Petitioner's fear of fraud is 
motivated by a higher calling than restricting the petition 
process and First Amendment rights, safeguards against fraud 
are already in place in Colorado which more than adequately 
safeguard the electorate.

B. THE FIRST AMENDMENT IS NOT 
GOVERNED BY "BALLOT ACCESS"

Petitioner has characterized its encroachment on the

It relies upon

See e.g. Burdick v.

In so arguing, Petitioner urges this Court to use a more

Candidate petitions are not constitutional safety valves 
to redress grievances. They may be tempered because "the 
right to vote is the right to participate in an electoral process 
that is necessarily structured to maintain the integrity of the 
democratic system." Burdick, 504 U.S. at 441, 112 S.Ct. at

A candidate’s petition is not calculated to redress a

88, 103 S.Ct. 1564, 1569-70 (1983).
