QUESTIONS PRESENTED FOR REVIEW 
Whether the State of Colorado may constitutionally 
regulate the process of circulating initiative and referen-

identification badges; and (3) proponents of an initiative

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
The American Constitutional Law Foundation, Inc., 
and the individual Respondents listed above (henceforth, 
collectively, “ACLF”), as plaintiffs in the District Court, 
challenged several of Colorado’s statutory provisions reg- 
ulating the initiative and referendum process in Colo- 
rado, relying primarily (but not exclusively) on the 
principles established by this Court in Meyer v. Grant, 486 
U.S. 414 (1988). Premising its challenge on the principles 
that initiative and referendum petitions involve core 
political speech, that promoting and circulating petitions 
involve core political speech and action, and that the 
entire process, from conception of the proposal for politi- 
cal change, to drafting the proposal and presenting it in 
petition form to Colorado citizens to first sign, and later 
vote on, invokes First Amendment protections of the 
right to petition for redress of grievances, ACLF pre- 
sented evidence and arguments showing that each of 
Colorado’s challenged regulations violated the First 
Amendment rights ot respondents. 
ACLF challenged Colorado’s prohibition against per- 
sons who are not registered voters from circulating initia- 
tive or referendum petitions in Colorado. This prohibition

“under-age” circulators was a legitimate state restriction.

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 
I. COLORADO’S REGULATIONS OF THE INITIA- 
TIVE PROCESS TRENCH UPON CORE POLITICAL 
ACTIVITY, INCLUDING SPEECH, ASSOCIATION, 
ASSEMBLY, AND PETITIONING, WHERE FIRST 
AMENDMENT PROTECTIONS ARE AT THEIR 
ZENITH. 
Colorado tries to, but cannot avoid the implications 
of this Court’s unanimous decision in Meyer v. Grant, 486 
U.S. 414 (1988). Meyer teaches that the initiative process 
involves core political activity, including speech, associa- 
tion, assembly, and petitioning for redress of grievances, 
areas where First Amendment protections are at their 
zenith. Any state burdens on such fundamental rights are 
unjustified without a showing of a compelling state inter- 
est and a showing that the state’s restrictions are nar- 
rowly tailored to burden no more First Amendment 
activity than necessary. Isolated instances of problems 
and speculative concerns are not sufficient to justify 
intrusions on these fundamental rights of political action. 
Abuses of a fundamental right can be dealt with without 
curtailing exercise of the right. 
898 P.2d 
1076 (Colo. 
1995) (multiple subjects because 
established public trust doctrine in water rights and changed 
water district election procedures to insure doctrine properly 
implemented by election officials). 
Colorado argues now - as it attempted in Meyer - 
that it has a broad latitude to regulate the initiative 
process, and that its regulations are reasonable, not bur- 
dening First Amendment activity. Colorado would per- 
suade this Court that it should treat the regulations at 
issue in this case, as ballot access regulations, and that 
the Court should then borrow standards of review devel- 
oped in candidate and political party ballot access cases, 
rather than subject Colorado’s challenged regulations to 
the review typically utilized in ballot issue cases, the 
strict scrutiny review appropriate for restrictions on core 
political speech, petitioning, and political action. 
ACLF will show that the regulations at issue chill 
speech, reduce the number of voices which can legally be 
raised to present the initiative petitions, interfere with 
petition proponents’ rights of assembly and association, 
and intrude upon and suppress ACLF’s right to petition 
for redress of grievances. The challenged regulations not 
only procedurally interfere with and stifle exercise of 
First Amendment rights. They also carry criminal sanc- 
tions for willful violations. 
Colorado’s “justifications” for the challenged regula- 
tions will be shown to result from a hostility against 
citizen initiatives, from a desire to “police” protected 
political speech and activity, and from a disdain for 
“direct democracy” (i.e., popular sovereignty). Colorado’s 
supposed “justifications” 
for its restrictions, will be 
shown to be non-compelling and not narrowly tailored.

II. COLORADO’S PROHIBITION OF PETITION CIR- 
CULATION BY ANYONE OTHER THAN R6&GIS- 
TERED VOTERS IS A RESTRAINT ON SPEECH 
AND THE RIGHT OF PETITION. 
Qualified electors (eligible to register as voters, but 
not registered) may not circulate petitions, non-residents 
may not circulate petitions, and citizens under 18 may not 
circulate petitions, seeking signatures from Colorado’s 
citizens to place a ballot measure before Colorado’s 
voters. As this Court held previously in Meyer v. Grant, 
486 U.S. 414 (1988), petition circulation involves the advo- 
cacy of what the initiative proposes, 
it involves core 
political speech. It is also a part of ACLF’s actions in 
petitioning for redress of grievances. The First Amend- 
ment-protected rights of free speech, assembly, and peti- 
tioning are “cognate” rights, deserving of similar 
protection. Any unqualified person (under 18 years of 
age, a non-resident, an unregistered elector) is guilty of a 
criminal offense for circulating a petition, if their action is 
willful. See C.R.S. §§ 1-40-112(1), 1-40-130(1)(h). 
Colorado’s concern for the “integrity” of elections 
cannot justify its restrictions, prohibitions, and criminal 
sanctions on petition circulation by persons who are not 
registered voters. There is no voter registration, age or 
residency requirement in the First Amendment.

Il. COLORADO’S REQUIREMENT THAT PETITION 
CIRCULATORS WEAR IDENTIFICATION 
BADGES 
IS OFFENSIVE, AND 
IT IS A 
RESTRAINT ON POLITICAL SPEECH AND 
ACTION. 
As the 10th Circuit ruled below, in American Constitu- 
tional Law Foundation v. Meyer, 120 F.3d 1092 (10th Cir. 
1997), requring each [initiative] petition circulator to 
wear an identification badge violates the circulator’s 
right to engage in anonymous speech. This has a chilling 
effect on political speech, resulting in less political 
speech, thus less advocacy for the petition, especially for 
controversial initiative proposals. Colorado (and its sup- 
porting amici from Washington and other states) argues 
that it wishes to - in effect — police the advocacy of 
[initiative] petition circulators, and that identification 
badges will further that state purpose. Pet. Brief at 37-38; 
States Am. Brief at 23. That might be true, and, if so, 
would offer a clinching argument why the regulation is 
inconsistent with the First Amendment. 
IV. COLORADO’S REPORTING REQUIREMENTS 
REQUIRING REPORTS OF AMOUNTS PAID TO 
CIRCULATORS AND THE IDENTITY OF PAID 
CIRCULATORS BURDEN FIRST AMENDMENT- 
PROTECTED ACTIVITIES. 
The Tenth Circuit below also found that Colorado’s 
reporting requirements for amounts paid to circulators, 
and of the identity of paid circulators, failed to survive 
exacting scrutiny, because the requirements were not nar- 
rowly tailored to serve legitimate state interests. 

12 
Colorado again argues these regulations are merely 
reasonable ballot access regulations and that, essentially, 
Colorado can do as it pleases in this area. 
ACLF will show that the Tenth Circuit was correct in 
finding these regulations burden expressive rights, and 
that this Court, in two previous cases (Buckley v. Valeo, 
424 U.S. 1 (1976), First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, 
435 U.S. 765 (1978)), laid out the requirements to justify 
such restrictions, and that Colorado failed to justify its 
restrictions. 
ARGUMENT 
I. COLORADO’S REGULATIONS OF THE INITIA- 
TIVE PROCESS TRENCH UPON CORE POLITICAL 
ACTIVITY, INCLUDING SPEECH, ASSOCIATION, 
ASSEMBLY, AND PETITIONING, WHERE FIRST 
AMENDMENT PROTECTIONS ARE AT THEIR 
ZENITH. 
In reviewing a First Amendment challenge to election 
laws, the court must first determine the appropriate stan- 
dard of review. Colorado suggests that this is simply a 
ballot access case, where reasonable regulations are per- 
mitted as long as they are rationally related to a legiti- 
mate state purpose. 
Colorado argued to the 10th Circuit, that its regula- 
tions were reasonable “ballot access” regulations, and 
that ballot access cases should be looked to in order to 
determine the appropriate standard of review of the regu- 
lations challenged in this case. The 10th Circuit discussed 
13 
Colorado's arguments, and then applied different stan- 
dards of review to different challenged regulations. 
The 10th Circuit struck down three challenged 
restrictions, each time applying exacting scrutiny, and 
finding each restriction unconstitutional. The 10th Circuit 
struck down the requirement that circulators be regis- 
tered voters, relying on Meyer v. Grant. Pet. App. A, at 
29-30. The 10th Circuit struck down the requirement peti- 
tion circulators wear identification badges, relying on 
McIntyre v. Ohio Election Commission, 514 U.S. 334, 115 
S.Ct. 1511 (1995), and on Meyer v. Grant. Pet. App. A, at 
36-38. They then struck down the reporting requirements, 
relying on Buckley v. Valeo. Pet. App. A, at 38-41. ACLF 
will point out that each of these restrictions was invali- 
dated by the 10th Circuit upon that Court finding that the 
subject restriction did indeed restrict expressive activity. 
ACLF respectfully suggests that the “variable scru- 
tiny” test of Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party, 117 
S.Ct. 1364 (1997), and of other candidate or political party 
ballot access cases (see Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428 
(1992), Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983)), has no 
applicability to review of the regulations challenged in 
this case. Candidate and political party ballot access cases 
traditionally evaluate the burden that election laws place 
on associational and voting rights, rights not expressly 
stated, but found to exist within the First Amendment

III. COLORADO’S REQUIREMENT THAT PETITION 
CIRCULATORS WEAR IDENTIFICATION 
BADGES 
IS OFFENSIVE, AND 
IT IS A 
RESTRAINT ON POLITICAL SPEECH AND 
ACTION. 
Colorado attempts to justify its requirement that ini- 
tiative petition circulators wear identification badges, 
evading the First Amendment protection this court has 
found for anonymous political speech, in McIntyre v. Ohio 
Election Commission, 514 U.S. 334, 115 S.Ct. 1511 (1995). 
Colorado again attempts what it first tried in Meyer v. 
Grant, to persuade this Court that petition circulators are 
somehow administrative agents of the state. Pet. Brief at 

36 
39-40. If Colorado can accomplish this, then petition pro- 
ponents will have no First Amendment protections. Colo- 
rado would then be free to proceed with a full-fledged 
circulator licensing scheme. 
The fact that Colorado places adminstrative burdens 
on petition circulators may itself be problematic from a 
First Amendment perspective. If Colorado can control, 
and license, anyone involved in initiative circulation, the 
right to petition for redress would be meaningless, in this 
context. The chilling effects of forced identification are 
not speculative. These effects are known by common 
sense and historical and personal experience. Colorado’s 
badge requirement reaches not only fraudulent initiative 
circulators, but all legitimate circulators, as well — but it 
reaches no candidate petition circulators. It cannot rea- 
sonably be denied, and has been established in the 
record, that there are many people who will avoid peti- 
tion circulation if they are required to wear identification 
badges. To paraphrase this Court in McIntyre, “anony- 
mous petitioning is not a pernicious, fraudulent practice, 
but an honorable tradition of advocacy and dissent.” 
McIntyre, supra, 514 U.S. at 356. As Justice Thomas 
recounted in McIntyre, Federalist efforts to force dis- 
closure of authorship in the press led to strong Anti- 
Federalist reactions on behalf of freedom of the press, 
followed by a quick retreat of the Federalists. 
Id. (J. 
Thomas, concurring), at 367. Protection of anonymous 
speech has important historical roots. 
Colorado has different concerns. Again, they wish to 
police the activities of petition circulators. As Donetta 
Davidson, elections officer, explained the purpose of 
badges: “One, it shows the basis of who they are, and so 
37 
somebody can recognize the name and report that to the 
Secretary of State if there is a problem with the circula- 
tor.” Tr. II, at 101. What kind of circulator problems is 
Colorado concerned with? Ms. Davidson: “The com- 
plaints was [sic] that they were being —- not giving it the 
right information, miscommunicating the issues, misleading 
the electors, plus actually - actually trying to force him to 
sign petitions.” Id., at 102 [emphasis added]. 
And what was the result of these complaints before 
circulator badges were required? Ms. Davidson: “There 
was nothing we could do about the complaints. 
. 
. 
. We 
didn’t have any idea of what the circulators’ names were. 
They could not be identified. 
. 
. 
. [E]ven the ones that 
were brought up in hearings, there was no identification, 
even when they had pictures of the circulators.” Id. 
This testimony is a chilling example of Colorado’s 
disregard for freedom of speech and for the right to 
petition for redress of grievances. What is even more 
disturbing, is that Colorado deliberately elicited this testi- 
mony, thinking it would prove useful in persuading this 
Court that its regulations were simply reasonable regula- 
tions of ballot access, where the Court should apply a 
“flexible standard of review.” See Pet. Brief at 26. 
The prospect of facilitating Colorado’s desire to call 
petition circulators to account for misleading arguments, 
to parade circulators in front of Colorado’s Secretary of 
State, under subpoena, so that they can attempt to satis- 
factorily explain — under threat of possible criminal pun- 
ishment —- why they misrepresented an initiative, or why 
they didn’t use the state’s preferred explanation of the 
effects of an initiative, should give this Court pause when 

38 
considering Colorado’s arguments concerning the “rea- 
sonableness” of Colorado’s regulations. 
This Court has previously addressed the issue of 
forced identification, or registration, prior to engaging in 
public speech: “We think a requirement that one must 
register before he undertakes to make a public speech to 
enlist support for a lawful movement is quite incompat- 
ible with the requirements of the First Amendment.” 
Thomas v. Collins, 323 U.S. at 540 (1945). “If the exercise of 
the rights of free speech and free assembly cannot be 
made a crime, we do not think this can be accomplished 
by a device of requiring previous registration as a condi- 
tion for exercising them.” Id. 
Similarly, Colorado can’t make petition circulation by 
persons not wearing badges, or by persons not registered 
to vote, illegal, invalid, and criminal, by the device of 
requiring the circulator to sign an affidavit. Colorado 
would ignore the advocacy role of the circulator, having 
given the circulators token state responsibility or author- 
ity (through the circulator affidavit), in order to license 
their political speech and activities: “They [circulators] 
are not simply advocates for a measure and their critically 
important role in the initiative process more than obviates any 
advocacy interest circulators may have...” Pet. Brief at 40 
[emphasis added]. If the choice is between First Amend- 
ment protections for circulators and requiring circulators 
to execute an affidavit, as Colorado implies, then this 
Court should separate speech and state, thus also 
invalidating the affidavit requirement, something the 
10th Circuit refused to do. Pet. App. A, at 42-44. Political 
speech tolerates no license.

IV. COLORADO’S REPORTING REQUIREMENTS 
REQUIRING REPORTS OF AMOUNTS PAID TO 
CIRCULATORS AND THE IDENTITY OF PAID 
CIRCULATORS, BURDEN FIRST AMENDMENT- 
PROTECTED ACTIVITIES. 
Colorado has still not accepted its loss in Meyer v. 
Grant, in which this Court invalidated Colorado's ban on 
paid petition circulators. Now it attempts to expose and 
shame and intimidate paid circulators, through its badge 
requirements (paid petitioners’ badges must say “PAID 
CIRCULATOR”), and through its reporting requirements 
requiring the disclosure of the name and the business and 
residence address of all paid circulators and the amounts 
paid to them. It is clear that Colorado wants to expose 
paid petitioners to retribution or retaliation or harass- 
ment and to brand these petitioners with a public brand 
of infamy, as PAID CIRCULATORS. Colorado doesn’t like 
paid circulators. 
Colorado argues that petition circulators have no 
right to object to identification badges, since they will 
ultimately be identified anyway when they execute the 
circulator’s affidavit at the end of a petition. Pet. Brief at 
43. (As discussed above, Colorado can’t accomplish indi- 
rec. , — extinguishing First Amendment protections for 
circulators — by means of the affidavit requirement, what 
it cannot accomplish directly.) Colorado cites to this 
Court's opinion in Uphaus v. Wyman, 360 U.S. 72 (1958), a 
case in which New Hampshire sought compelled corpo- 
rate disclosure of the list of all attendees at a camp run by 
World Fellowship, Inc., a corporation suspected of sub- 
versive activities. Since World Fellowship was already 
required to disclose, as a hotel keeper, the names of all of 
its guests, the Court 

40 
apparently [drew] some comfort from the New 
Hampshire lodging house registration statute 
for its conclusions about the lack of substan- 
tiality of the guest’s interests in nondisclosure. 
Since the statute adm:tte ily would not cover 
what the Attorney General desired to obtain and 
since the New Hampshire courts themselves did 
not rest on it, it is difficult to find any basis for 
this reliance. 
Uphaus v. Wyman, 360 U.S. 72, 97 (1958) (J. Brennan, 
dissenting). 
Likewise, it is difficult to find any basis for Colorado 
relying on dicta from Uphaus, where subversive activity 
was being investigated and the security of the state was 
at stake, leading the Court to find that the state’s interest 
in survival was superior to the associational privacy 
interests of persons who had already made their identi- 
ties public by signing a guest register. See Id., at 81. 
ACLP’s interests are not simply privacy interests and 
they are not as insubstantial as those of the guests of 
World Fellowship, World Fellowship’s guests didn’t have 
to wear identification badges or sign a guest register 
under oath, and Colorado’s survival as a state is not 
threatened by the activities of unidentified initiative peti- 
tion circulators. 
Colorado’s arguments justifying the requirement of 
identifying paid [initiative] petitioners in monthly reports 
are disingenuous. Colorado argues that “the essential 
purpose of the monthly reports is the identification of the 
circulators.” Pet. Brief at 44. If that were true, then Colo- 
rado would also require monthly reports showing the 
activities of unpaid [initiative] circulators. The reporting 
41 
requirement struck down by the 10th Circuit only applies 
to petitions circulated by paid petitioners, and the only 
circulators who must be identified are paid [initiative] 
circulators. See C.R.S. 1-40-121. No similar reports are 
required for candidate petition circulators, whether paid 
or unpaid. Recall the testimony of David Aitken. Tr. I, at 
115. 
Colorado demonstrates its lack of a compelling inter- 
est and the under-inclusiveness of its regulations requir- 
ing these initiative reporting requirements, by not 
imposing similar requirements on paid and unpaid candi- 
date petition circulators. Colorado did not attempt to 
show at trial that paid petitioners tended to be corrupt 
and that unpaid circulators were all honorable. Finally, 
the showing of isolated and insignificant examples of 
petitioning fraud, made without a showing of any harm 
resulting (except to an initiative proponent who loses 
signatures), cannot justify jettisoning the First Amend- 
ment. 
CONCLUSION 
The Court should affirm the Tenth Circuit’s holding 
that Colorado's registration, badge, and reporting 
requirements are unconstitutional, violating First Amend- 
ment rights of free speech, assembly, and petitioning for 
redress of grievances. The Court should clarify that hold- 
ing by further finding that Colorado’s ban on underage 
circulators is unconstitutional (for the same reasons that 
banning any non-registered circulators is invalid), as is 

42 
the circulator affidavit requirement found in C.R.S. 
1-40-111(2).
