STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
Colorado became a State in 1876. 
Thirty-four years 
later, 
in 1910, the citizens of Colorado amended her 
Constitution to allow for statutory and constitutional changes 
through the use of citizen initiatives. 1910 Colo. Sess. Laws 
ll. 
As originally adopted, the law required that eight 
percent of eligible voters sign a petition to propose a 
measure; that each signer must be a qualified elector; and 
that each person’s residence and the date of signing be 
included on the petition. 
Jd. 
A qualified elector was a 
person who was over the age of 21, a citizen of the United 
States, and "resided in the state twelve months immediately 
preceding the election at which he offers to vote and in the 
county, city, town, ward or precinct, such time as may be 
prescribed by law." Colo. Const. art. VII, § 1 (1902). 
Colorado courts quickly determined that the Colorado 
General Assembly could enact laws to implement these 
constitutional provisions. 
/n re House Resolution #10, 50 
Colo. 71, 114 P. 293, 295 (Colo. 
1911). 
See also 
Committee for Better Health Care v. Meyer, 830 P.2d 884, 
893 (Colo. 1992). The Colorado General Assembly enacted 
its first law implementing the initiative process in 1913. 
1913 Colo. Sess. Laws 310. 
It required that each petition 
contain a warning that it was a felony for anyone to sign a 
petition under a false name, to knowingly sign his or her 
name more than once for the same measure, or to sign a 
petition when he or she was not a qualified elector. 
/d. 
It 
established a procedure by which qualified electors could 
protest the determination that the petition was insufficient. 
The General Assembly also included a provision that 
imposed criminal penalties if any person willfully violated 
any provision of the law. Id. 
In 1941, the General Assembly made it illegal to pay 
petition circulators. 
1941 Colo. Sess. Laws 480. In 1971, 
the General Assembly attempted to change the requirement 
that signers and circulators be registered electors, not just 
qualified electors. The Colorado Supreme Court, however, 
held that this change could not be made without amending 
the Colorado Constitution. Colorado Project-Common Cause 
v. Anderson, 178 Colo. 1, 495 P.2d 220 (1972). 
In 1980, Colorado’s citizens amended the Colorado 
Constitution by adopting a comprehensive list of reforms to 
Colorado’s initiative and referendum process.'

The Tenth Circuit observed

"To subject every petition regulation to exacting 
scrutiny would tie Colorado’s hands in seeking to assure 
equitable and efficient elections on ballot issues." 
/d. at 
A24.

A very important factor in deciding this case is the

Burdick v.

“Common sense, as 
well as constitutional law, compels the conclusion that

‘as a practical matter, there must be a substantial regulation 
of elections if they are to be fair and honest and if some sort 
of order rather than chaos, is to accompany 
the democratic 
process.’" 
Jd. at 434 (quoting Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 
724, 730 (1974)). 
26 
"[T}he rule fashioned by the Court 
to pass on

restrictions that are valid from those that are invidious.

. 
." Storer, 415 U.S. 
at 730. The “analytical process" this 
Court applies is that the Court “must first consider the 
character and the magnitude of the asserted injury to rights 
protected by the First and Fourteenth Amendments that the 
plaintiff 
seeks to vindicate. 
It must then identify 
and 
evaluate the precise interests put forward by the State as 
justifications

460 U.S. 780, 789 (1983). This Court then 
considers "the extent to which those interests make it 
necessary to burden plaintiff's 
rights." Jd. 
When a State’s election-law regulations pose a "severe" 
burden on the constitutional rights asserted by a plaintiff, 
this Court will normally require the State to narrowly tailor

Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party, 117 S.

1364,
