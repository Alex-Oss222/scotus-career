STATEMENT OF THE FACTS 
In 1910, Colorado passed a constitutional amendment, 
Colo. 
Const. 
art. 
V, §1, authorizing 
citizen-initiated 
legislation. 
The amendment provided that at least eight 
percent of legal voters must sign a petition to propose a 
measure; that each signer must be a qualified elector; that 
each signer’s residence and the date of signing must be 
included; and that each circulator must be a qualified elector. 
A qualified elector was a person who was over the age of 
twenty-one, was a citizen of the United States, and “resided 
in the state twelve months immediately preceding the election 
at which he offers to vote and in the county, city, town, 
ward or precinct, such time as may be prescribed by law." 
Colo. Const. art. VII, § 1, 1921 Compiled Laws of Colo- 
rado. 
A qualified elector was not required to register in 
order to sign a petition. 
In 1913, the Colorado General Assembly passed the 
statute implementing the initiative process. 1913 Colo. Sess. 
Laws 310. 
It required that each petition contain a warning 
that it is a felony for anyone to sign a petition under a false 
name, knowingly to sign his or her name more than once for 
the same measure, or to sign a petition when he or she was 
not a qualified elector. 
It also established a procedure by 
which qualified electors could protest the determination that 
the petition was insufficient. 
Criminal penalties were 
imposed for willful violation of the initiative code. 
In 1980, the voters amended Colo. Const. art. V, § 1 in 
two important respects. 
First, the amendment required that 
persons who sign and circulate a petition must be registered 
electors, rather than qualified electors.' 
As noted in the 
comments from the General Assembly ballot analysis, the 
amendment placed greater responsibility upon the circulator 
to determine whether the individuals signing the petitions are 
eligible to sign (Pet. App. E-9).* Second, the number of 
signatures required to place a measure on the ballot was 
reduced from 8% to 5% of the total votes cast for the Secre- 
tary of State at the previous election. 
The reduction in the 
number of required signatures and the extension of the dead- 
line to file petitions were designed to offset the requirement 
that signers and circulators must be registered electors. (Pet. 
App. E-10.) 
'A person may be registered if he is eighteen years of age, is 
a United States citizen, and has been a resident of Colorado for at 
least 30 days prior to the election. 
Colo. Rev. Stat. § 1-2-101 
(1997). 
*The General Assembly's ballot analysis provides important 
insight into the public’s understanding of the amendment when it

In 1988, 
this Court declared 
unconstitutional 
the 
prohibition against payment of petition circulators. Meyer v. 
Grant, 486 U.S. 414 (1988). Subsequently, Colorado exper- 
ienced a surge in the use of paid petition circulators and in 
the number of complaints of abuses committed by circula- 
tors. 
(Pet. App. F-5.) In order to limit the possibility of 
fraud and to enhance public confidence in the initiative 
process, the Colorado General Assembly instituted two re- 
forms. 
First, the circulators must display identification 
badges. 
The badges disclose the name of each circulator, 
the status as paid or volunteer circulators, and, if paid, the 
name and telephone number of the person or entity employ- 
ing the circulator. 
Colo. Rev. Stat. § 1-40-112(2) (1997). 
In addition, the proponents of a petition must sign and 
file reports with the Secretary of State setting forth the 
names of the proponents, the name, residential and business 
address of each of the paid circulators, the name of the 
proposed ballot measure for which petitions are being circu- 
lated by paid circulators, and the amount of money paid and 
owed to each paid circulator for petition circulation during 
the month in question. 
Colo. Rev. Stat. § 1-40-121(2) 
(1997). A final report must identify circulators who were 
paid. Colo. Rev. Stat. § 1-40-121(1) (1997). 
STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
The Respondents are, respectively, a public interest 
organization, and individual Colorado residents. They chal- 
lenge several procedures governing the circulation and 
submission 
of initiative 
petitions. 
In particular, 
the 
Respondents contested the disqualification of petitions circu- 
lated by non-registered 
voters’, 
the disqualification of 
petitions which are circulated by persons without badge 
identification,’ the filing of monthly reports by proponents 
of petitions which are circulated by paid circulators,’ and 
the disclosure of the identity of circulators in the final 
reports.° 
The district court concluded that the requirement that 
circulators must be registered electors 
is constitutional, 
holding that the existence and scope of the initiative and 
referendum are dictated by the Colorado electorate. 
"The 
people are the source of the power and they may exercise 
their sovereign authority to limit the scope of that power so 
long as there is no discriminatory classification." (Pet. App. 
B-22.) However, the district court declared unconstitutional 
the requirement that each circulator wear a badge disclosing 
the circulator’s name and status as paid or volunteer. 
The 
court concluded that the badge identification requirements 
caused a decrease in the pool of available circulators, but did 
not advance a compelling state interest. 
(Pet. App. B-22.) 
The district court also concluded that the required disclosures 
of the name and business and residential address of each said 
circulator was unconstitutional. 
The District Court found 
that "[wjhat is of interest is the payor, not the payees.” 
(Pet. App. B-22-24.) 
Finally, the district court concluded that the monthly 
reporting requirement was unconstitutional because "(p)repa-

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 
The use of the initiative and referendum process has 
grown considerably during the last two decades. 
David B. 
Magleby, “Let The Voters Decide? An Assessment of the 
Initiative and Referendum Process" 66 Colo. L. Rev. 13, 26- 
28 (1995).” 
Indeed, this Court has recently reviewed 
regulations affecting participation by citizens in the initiative 
process. McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Comm'n, 514 U.S. 334 
(1995); Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414 (1988). While these 
cases have analyzed circumstances which directly and Clearly 
impacted the free speech rights of circulators and other 
participants in the initiative process, neither the permissible 
extent of procedural restrictions, Fishman v. Schaffer, 429 
U.S. 1325, 1326-28 (1976), nor the "larger circumstances” 
under which a State may "require the speaker to disclose its 
interest 
by disclosing 
its identity” 
has been defined. 
McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Comm'n, 514 U.S. at 358 
(Ginsburg. J., concurring). 
"Twenty-seven states have a form of the initiative and referen- 
dum. Magleby, supra, at 22. 
This Court has long recognized that the States may 
exercise “substantial regulation of elections if they are to be 
fair and honest and if some sort of order, rather than chaos, 
is to accompany the democratic process". 
Storer v. Brown, 
415 U.S. 724, 730 (1974). 
States have a strong interest in 
protecting 
the stability, 
integrity and fairness 
of their 
political systems. Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party, 
117 S. Ct. 1364, 1373, 1374, 137 L. Ed. 2d 589 (1997). 
The Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals secognized that 
petition circulators perform administrative functions on 
behalf of the State of Colorado which may be regulated. To 
enhance the process, Colorado may impose upon petition cir- 
culators appropriate qualifications, including limits regarding 
age, residency and citizenship. (Pet. App. A-29-30.) Colo- 
rado may also demand that each circulator sign an oath at the 
time that the circulator submits the petition. The oath may 
include the circulator’s address. 
(Pet. App. A-27-28). 
In 
addition, the identity of each circulator, including the circu- 
lator’s address, is disclosed when the proponents submit the 
petitions for verification by the Secretary. (Pet. App. A-34.) 
The Respondents did not contest the statutory provision 
which provides that the affidavit of the circulator of the 
petition presented to the Secretary of State must include the 
circulator’s name and residential address. 
Colo. Rev. Stat. 
§ 1-40-111(2) (1997). Despite acknowledging the important 
ministerial role played by circulators, the Tenth Circuit 
Court of Appeals ruled that Colorado may not require each 
circulator to affirm his age, residency or citizenship by regis- 
tering to vote prior to circulating petitions. 
Moreover, 
Colorado now may not require circulators or proponents to 
disclose the identity of circulators prior to the time that 
petitions are submitted. 
The issue in this case is when, not whether, information 
must be disclosed. Colorado maintains that the identity and 
status of the circulator can be disclosed when the circulation 
5 

process begins. 
The Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals con- 
cluded that the circulator could not be identified until after 
the petitions are submitted. 
Initiatives are assuming a more prominent, and, in some 
instances, a more contentious, role in American politics. Cf. 
Arizonans for Official English v. Arizona, 520U.S. __, 117 
S. Ct. 1055, 137 L. Ed. 170 (1977); Romer v. Evans, 517 
U.S. __, 116 S. Ct. 1620, 134 L. Ed. 855 (1996). 
This 
case is important because the line between appropriate regu- 
lation of the initiative process and prohibited regulation 
directly affecting the discussion of political issues is unclear. 
Thus, this case presents an opportunity to provide necessary 
guidance so that States can develop procedures which will 
ensure the integrity of the initiative process, while preserving 
the right of free speech.° 
I. 
REQUIRING 
CIRCULATORS 
TO BE 
REGISTERED ELECTORS HAS LITTLE, 
IF ANY, EFFECT UPON PROTECTED 
SPEECH. 
The Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals recognized that 
obtaining signatures is an integral part of placing a measure 
on the ballot and that Colorado "has a strong, often compel- 
ling, interest in preserving the integrity of its electoral 
system.” (Pet. App. A-27). 
The Tenth Circuit Court of 
Appeals further acknowledged 
that circulators play an 
"important role" (Pet. App. A-27) in the process because 
they are “entrusted with personal responsibility to prevent 
irregularities in the petition process." (Pet. App. A-28). 
‘The issue is not limited to Colorado. 
Recently, the Eighth 
Circuit Court of Appeals declared unconstitutional a Nebraska 
statute under which a circulator must also be a registe.ed elector. 
Bernbeck v. Moore, 126 F.3d 1114 (8th Cir. 1997). 
Colorado may impose certain restrictions on circulators 
to ensure that they will properly execute their election 
functions. 
Circulators must be eighteen years of age. 
"(b)ecause maturity is reasonably related to Colorado's 
interest in preserving the integrity of ballot issue elections. ° 
Colorado could also impose a residency requirement. 
(Pet. 
App. A-29-30.) 
Registration is litte more than an oath taken by a 
qualified elector in which the circulator swears that he is 
eighteen, a citizen of the United States and a resident of 
Colorado. 
Colo. Rev. Stat. § 1-2-105(2) (1997). 
It is 
minimal assurance that the circulators meet these unobjec- 
tionable qualifications. However, the Tenth Circuit Court of 
Appeals eviscerated 
Colorado's 
ability 
to enforce 
the 
residency and age restrictions by eliminating the requirement 
that circulators must be registered electors. 
The Tenth 
Circuit's conclusion is based on the erroneous assumption 
that registration “bars persons who are not registered voters 
from circulating petitions, excluding that group of persons 
from participating in core political speech." (Pet. App. A-29) 
(emphasis added). 
The Tenth Circuit's analysis is flawed because it incor- 
rectly assumes that the registration requirement creates an 
insurmountable 
barrier to the circulation of petitions. At the 
time 
of trial, approximately 1.9 million persons were regis- 
tered io vote in Colorado; that is, they were 18 years of age, 
a citizen of the United States and a resident of the State for 
30 days (Pet. App. G-1). Registering 
to vote is a simple 
process which does not unduly limit the right to participate 
in the election process or add to the qualifications needed to 
participate 
in the political process. 
Marsten v. Lewis, 410 

U.S. 679 (1973) (per curiam); Dunn v. Blumstein, 405 U.S. 
330, 346 (1972).” 
The registration requirement differs little from proce- 
dures which the Court has deemed constitutional. 
Rosario 
v. Rockefeller, 410 U.S. 752 (1973). In Rosario, this Court 
concluded that a time deadline imposed for registering to 
vote in a party eri ary did not present a constitutional 
impediment. 
In particular, the Court noted that the voters 
merely could take advantage of the available opportunities to 
register. 
/d. at 757. 
Here the Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals disregarded the 
voice of the people of the State of Colorado. The District 
Court correctly noted that the Colorado electorate chose to 
impose the registration, implicitly acknowledging that the 
circulator assumes a public role which can be regulated. 
This Court has long recognized “the authority 
of the People 
of the States to determine the qualifications of their most 
important government officials," Gregory v. Ashcroft, 501 
U.S. 452, 463 (1991), and reiterated that scrutiny “will not 
be so demanding where we deal with matters resting firmly 
within a state’s constitutional prerogative". 
/d. at 469 
(quoting Sugarman v. Dougall, 413 U.S. 634, 647 (1973)). 
The Tenth Circuit's analysis ignored this Court’s mandate 
et to the voters of Colo- 
*At the time that Marsten and Dunn were decided, most 
persons registered at the local clerk and recorder’s offices. 
Recently, the Congress liberalized voting procedure so that 
persons could register at any place where federal benefits are 
obtained. 42 U.S.C. § 1973gg. In addition, Coloradans 
are able 
to register at the county clerk's office by affidavit, by federal 
postcard, at branch offices, and at driver's license facilities. Colo. 
Rev. Stat. §§ 1-2-202, -207, -212, and -213 (1997).

Il. DISCLOSURE 
OF 
THE 
IDENTITY, 
STATUS AND AMOUNT PAID TO CIR- 
CULATORS IS CONSTITUTIONAL. 
Both the district court and the Tenth Circuit declared 
unconstitutional 
the disclosure 
of the identity of each 
circulator prior to the filing of the petitions with the 
Secretary of State, and of 
the paid status of circulators. The 
foundation of the Tenth Circuit's analysis is the assertion 
that the “[cJirculating a petition is akin to distributing a 
handbill." (Pet. App. A-35). The analogy must be rejected. 
A petition is much more than a handbill. It is, in 
essence, a preliminary ballot which determines whether a 
measure has adequate support to qualify for the ballot at the 
general election. Loonan v. Woodley, 
882 P.2d 1380, 1386 
(Colo. 1994). The circulator collects the votes and presents 
them to Secretary of State to be validated and counted. The 
signers depend upon the circulators to collect and submit the 
signatures. The Tenth Circuit itself recognized 
that “circu- 
lators are, in effect, entrusted with personal responsibility to 
prevent irregularities 
in the petition process.” (Pet. App. A- 
28.) The circulators 
are fiduciaries for the petition signers. 
Disclosing the circulator’s name and status as a paid or 
volunteer circulator on a badge is not intrusive; it is neces- 
sary. A signer should have easy access to the name of the 
person who will be submitting the signer’s signature for 
review. 
Similarly, the monthly reports and the disclosure of the 
paid status of circulators are also constitutionally 
unobjec- 
tionable. Given the circulator’s status as a guarantor of the 
integrity of the process, both signers and members of the 
public in general should be allowed to know whether, and 
how much, circulators are being paid to advance an initia- 
tive. 
Such disclosure allows citizens insight into the 
motivation of the individuals purporting to represent the 
ll 

voters, thereby increasing public confidence. 
(Pet. App. B- 
23.) By enhancing their ability to determine the source of 
initial support for the measure, citizens 
can assess whether 
the measure has such significant grassroots support that 
people are willing to volunteer their time.'® As this Court 
has noted, "an informed public opinion is the most potent of 
all restraints upon misgovernment." 
Grosjean v. American 
Press Co., 297 U.S. 233, 250 (1935). Cf. Riley v. National 
Federation of the Blind of North Carolina, 487 U.S. 781, 
800 n.11 (1988) (disclosure of identity and paid status consti- 
tutional).

For the above-stated reasons, the Petition for Writ of 
Certiorari should be granted.

Id. at 422-23, 108

They also cite

Each case, including Meyer, involved restrictions on expen- 
ditures to disseminate information on political issues. 
No 
such restrictions are involved here.

to the voters.

vehicle, as well as an avenue for political expression.

rado maintain a petition process that, in essence, allows 
unregulated access to the ballot. 
Indeed, such a reading 
would conflict with the general rule that states have the 
power to regulate their elections and access to their ballots. 
See, e.g., Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party, -

In Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414, 108 S.Ct. 1886, 100 
L.Ed.2d 425 (1988), the Supreme Court concluded that the 
circulation of an initiative petition involved 
interactive 
communication characterized 
as 
"core 
political speech" 
within the protection of the First Amendment, applicable to 
the states under the Fourteenth Amendment. 
The court 
identified the invalid aspects of the statutory prohibition of 
payment of circulators in these words: 
The refusal to permit appellees to pay petition circula- 
tors restricts political expression in two ways: 
First, it 
limits the number of voices who will convey appellees’ 
message and the hours they can speak, and, therefore, 
limits the size of the audience they can reach. Second, 
it makes it less likely that appellees will garner the 
number of signatures necessary to place the matter on 
the ballot, thus limiting their ability to make the matter 
the focus of statewide discussion. 
Id. at 422-423, 108 
S.Ct. at 1892. 
The burden of the state to justify such a restriction was 
described in the following paragraph: 
We agree with the Court of Appeals’ conclusion that the 
statute trenches upon an area in which the importance of 
First Amendment protections is “at its zenith." 
For 
B-16 
3EST AVAILABLE COPY 
that reason, the burden that Colorado must overcome to 
justify this criminal law is well-nigh insurmountable. 
Id. at 425, 108 S.Ct. at 1894. 
{1} It is familiar 
learning 
that governmental 
in- 
fringements on political speech undergo strict scrutiny to 
determine if they are substantially related to a compelling 
governmental interest and are so narrowly drawn as to be the 
least restrictive means of furthering that interest. Buckley v. 
Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 64, 96 S.Ct. 612, 656, 46 L.Ed.2d 659 
(1976); NAACP v. Alabama, 357 U.S. 449, 463, 78 S.Ct. 
1163, 1172, 2 L.Ed.2d 1488 (1958). 
[2] The Supreme Court has not applied the strict 
scrutiny standard to all legislation affecting the right to vote 
and the freedom of association with others for political 
purposes. 
The state's interest in fair, honest and orderly 
elections has been recognized as a justification for some 
burdens on political liberty. 
In Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S.

The first power reserved to the people under Article 
V, Section 1, Colorado Constitution, is the initiative. Under 
the initiative provision, the qualified electors of Colorado 
may petition to place a proposed 
state 
law or 
state 
constitutional amendment before the voters at a general 
election. 
The second power reserved to the people is the 
referendum, which involves two separate procedures: 1) the 
General Assembly may refer proposed changes in the statutes 
to a vote at a general election; and 2) the referendum also 
establishes a procedure whereby measures, 
or sections 
thereof, adopted by the General Assembly which are not 
necessary to immediate preservation of the public peach, 
health, and safety may be submitted to a vote at the polls 
upon petition. 
For all practical purposes, referendum by 
petition is obsolete because nearly all bills enacted by the 
E-5 

General Assembly contain a "safety clause” stating that the 
measure is necessary for the immediate preservation of the 
public peace, health and safety. 
When a safety clause is 
utilized, the legislation is not subject to a referendum. 
Presently, 
the Colorado 
Constitution 
requires 
that 
signers of initiative or referendum petitions be qualified 
electors or legal voters. 
Based on Colorado Supreme Court 
decisions, qualified elector and legal voter are equivalent 
terms. 
A qualified elector means every person who on the 
date of the next ensuing election has attained the age of 
eighteen years, is a citizen of the Umiied States, and has 
resided in this state and a certain precinct thirty-two days 
immediately preceding the election. 
In order to vote in a 
general election, a qualified elector must first register with 
the county clerk. 
Briefly, registration includes affirming 
citizenship, residency, and signing the registration form. In 
sum, all legally registered electors are qualified electors, but 
not all qualified electors are registered electors. 
In 1978, the number of registered electors in Colorado 
numbered 1,345,006, compared to an estimated 1.8 million 
qualified electors. Thus registered electors constitute a much 
smaller pool of potential signers of initiative measures. The 
amendment, however, would reduce the number of signa- 
tures needed for a statewide initiative from eight percent to 
five percent of the total votes cast for Secretary of State at 
the previous election. 
Based on 1978 data, about 62,200 
signatures of qualified electors are needed on statewide 
initiative petitions—an estimated 3.4 percent of all qualified 
electors. 
The proposal would reduce 
the number of 
signatures required to about 38,800 -- an estimated 2.9 
percent of all registered electors. 
Although fewer persons 
would be eligible to sign petitions, the relative number of 
signatures required would be smaller. 
The initiative and referendum are applicable to cities 
and towns, including home rule municipalities. The proposal 
would require that signatures of not more than 10 percent of 
the registered electors of a city or town would be needed for 
a referendum and not more than 15 percent for an initiative 
measure. 
Under current law these maximum signature 
requirements are applicable to qualified electors rather than 
registered electors. 
Public hearing on initiative proposals. 
The proposed 
amendment would require that the original text of any 
proposed initiative must be submitted to the legislative 
research and drafting offices for review and comment. Both 
offices are agencies of the Colorado General Assembly. The 
review and comment by the two agencies would be presented 
to the proponents of an initiative at a meeting open to the 
public. The proponents could disregard any or all comments 
made by the legislative staff agencies and would not be 
required to amend, modify, or alter any measure prior to 
petition circulation and placement on the ballot. 
The review and comment procedure proposed by the 
amendment would revise the current statutory procedure 
provided for proponents of statewide initiatives pursuant to 
section 
1-40-101, 
Colorado Revised Statutes of 1973. 
Presently, comments made to proponents must be kept 
confidential until the time the ballot title is established. 
A 
purpose of the existing comment procedure is to assist the 
proponents of an initiative in developing technically [p. 3] 
inform the public, as well as proponents, of the potential 
impact of the original draft of any proposed initiative. 
Limitation on articles proposed for amendment by the 
General Assembly. 
While people may initiate amendments 
to the Colorado Constitution under Article V, a separate 
article also authorizes the General Assembly to submit 
proposed constitutional amendments to the Colorado voters 
E-7 

for their consideration. The Colorado Constitution currently 
provides that the General Assembly may 
not propose 
amendments to more than six articles of the constitution in 
any One session. 
The General Assembly is organized on a 
biennial or two-year bases, but convenes in regular sessions 
annually. 
The amendment would 
limit each General 
Assembly to proposing amendments to not more than six 
articles. In past general elections, the General Assembly has 
not offered amendments to more than six articles of the 
Colorado Constitution. 
As recently as the general elections 
in 1974, 1970, and 1954, the General Assembly did offer 
amendments to six articles. 
The Colorado Constitution 
contains 25 articles. 
The amendment would clarify the 
number of articles that the General Assembly could propose 
to amend at any one election, but it would not restrict the 
number of changes proposed for any six articles. 
Popular Arguments For 
1) 
The present procedure in which any qualified 
elector may be eligible to sign an initiative petition makes 
any challenge of the signatures on an initiative petition 
impractical because there is no official record of qualified 
electors. 
Without an inordinate expense of time and 
resources, it is impossible for a citizen, organization, or 
even the Secretary of State to actually determine the validity 
of the 62,200 signatures now required to place an initiative 
petition on the statewide ballot. The likelihood of fraud in 
the petition process would be greatly reduced by requiring 
the signatures of registered electors. 
If a question arises as 
to the authenticity of legality of the signatures gathered on 
any petition, verification could be easily made by comparing 
the signatures and residences on the petition with the names 
and residences of all registered electors as shown in the 
registration books of the county clerks and recorders. 
2) 
The amendment would place greater responsibility 
on the part of those person circulating petitions to determine 
whether the individual signing the petition is eligible to sign 
the petition. 
The proponents would have access to public 
records of registered electors and would ascertain the validity 
of their petitions prior to filing, thus reducing any possibility 
of challenge by those persons opposing the petition. 
3) 
The amendment would reduce 
the number of 
signatures needed to place an initiative measure on the ballot 
and provide more time in the summer preceeding [sic] the 
election for the collection of signatures. 
For the 1980 
general election, for example, sponsors of statewide initiative 
measures must obtain in excess of 62,000 signatures in order 
to place an initiative measure on the statewide ballot. 
If the 
proposed amendment were 
in effect, 
less than 39,000 
signatures would be required. 
The amendment would also 
extend the date for collection of signatures by changing the 
final day for filing signatures from early July to early 
August. 
The increase in the number of days allowed for 
circulation of signatures at this time is important because 
voter interest increases with the proximity of the election. 
Furthermore,
