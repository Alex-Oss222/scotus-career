GREATER DENVER 
Meyer boots anti-tax measure off ballot 
Secretary of state cites insufficient signatures; 
organizer cries foul play 
By John Sanko 
Rocky Mountain News 
Capitol Bureau 
Secretary of State Natalie Meyer stunned supporters of a 
controversial anti-tax measure Friday by knocking it and 
a companion election-reform proposal off Colorado's 
1992 general election ballot because of insufficient signa- 
tures. 
The unexpected decision 
- in which she rejected 
nearly one out of every three names submitted to her 
office - infuriated the measures’ chief architect, Colorado 
Springs businessman Douglas Bruce, who angrily vowed 
to challenge both actions. 
“It’s an outrage,” said Bruce, whose Taxpayer Bill of 
Rights was narrowly rejected by voters last year. “The 
woman is a public menace. There’s no kind way to put 
it.” 
Bruce said his supporters would be in Meyer's office 
Monday checking on the signatures she rejected. 
“| 
wouldn't trust her as far as | could throw her.” 
Meyer — who has had numerous run-ins with Bruce 
over the past several years — was out of town, but issued 

PSA 16 
a terse statement announcing that both petitions were 
being rejected. Each needed signatures of 49,279 regis- 
tered voters to be on the ballot. 
The tax issue, which would have required voter 
approval for higher taxes or any government spending 
that exceeded normal costs due to inflation and growth, 
came closest to making the ballot. Meyer rejected 24,065 
signatures, leaving a total of 47,055. 
On the election-reform measure, which would have 
imposed term limitations on all elected public officials in 
the state, Meyer tossed out 19,538 signatures, leaving 
only 42,382 valid names. 
Election aides said the names were rejected for a 
variety of reasons. Some of the petition circulators, they said, 
were not registered voters. 
In addition, the aides said, there were numerous 
instances of people not living at the addresses that were 
listed, signers who were not registered and, in some 
cases, illegibly written names. 
Bruce said he would challenge Meyer's rejections of 
the names and then take the dispute into court. “If we 
have to go to the U.S. Supreme Court, we'll do that,” 
Bruce vowed. 
“Never underestimate a fanatic,” said Phil Fox, who 
represents the Colorado Associations of School Execu- 
tives and School Boards. “I'm sure he'll be back.” 
Although there still is sufficient time for Bruce to 
collect new signatures, the rejections were a major blow 
to his plans to get a tax initiative on the ballot for the 
third time in four years. 
PSA 17 
Two years ago his ballot proposal failed by less than 
a 2% margin. A poll taken by Talmey-Drake Research last 
month showed 64% of Coloradans support his latest pro- 
posal. 
‘Bad blood’ has boiled since ‘88 
The “bad blood” between Secretary of State Natalie 
Meyer and Colorado Springs tax protester Douglas Bruce 
started in 1988, when he went to court to get his first tax 
initiative on the ballot. 
Bruce angrily accused Meyer of having “stuck a dag- 
ger in the heart of democracy,” violating her oath of office 
and engaging in shameful conduct. 
Meyer accused him of telling “half-truths” and said 
he should be sued for libel and slander. 
In October 1990, Meyer revealed she had fined Bruce 
$370 for failing to file a campaign disclosure statement on 
time in 1988. He refused to pay, saying she mailed the 
form to the wrong person. “I've never met anyone who 
likes him,” Meyer said. “He has a penchant for making 
people mad by the third sentence.” 
In April 1991, Bruce said he would circulate petitions 
for an initiative to limit the authority of the secretary of 
state to rule petition signatures invalid.

Sidetracking petitions 
A hearing in Denver today pits tax-limitation propo- 
nent Douglas Bruce against Secretary of State Natalie 
Meyer to determine whether Bruce collected enough sig- 
natures to put his latest proposal on the ballot. They 
obviously don’t like each other, but it never should have 
come to this. 
What led to this juncture is a 1989 law — by politi- 
cians, for politicians — that makes it much more difficult 
for citizens to place issues on the ballot. One needn't 
sympathize with Bruce or those who would micromanage 
government by referendum to conclude that the law seri- 
ously comprises Colorado citizens’ constitutional right to 
petition. 
Since Meyer rejected more than 19,000 signatures 
collected by Bruce and his supporters in December, hor- 
ror stories have surfaced regularly about lame reasons 
signatures have been rejected. Consider the case of long- 
time De Beque resident Fred Froehlich. 
All of the 50 or so signatures on the petition he 
circulated were declared invalid because Meyer's office 
claimed he wasn’t a registered voter. Mesa County 
records, however, indicate Froehlich has been registered 
since 1972. But there was a minor discrepancy between 
the address Froehlich put on his petition and the county 
record, so his petitions were declared invalid. 

PSA 22 
That’s absurd. The discrepancy hasn’t prevented him 
from voting. (Should every election in which he voted be 
declared invalid?) Nor did anyone at the clerk’s office 
inform him of a problem. 
Montrose County citizens who tried to recall county 
Commissioner Walter Burke ran afoul of the law as well. 
Burke simply hired a law firm to find technical flaws in 
their petitions. 
The upshot is that the law is written so as to allow 
Meyer and Burke, and other politicians, to circumvent the 
will of their constituents, whether in the form of rejecting 
petitions outright or by making them jump through so 
many hoops that it’s practically impossible to put a con- 
troversial issue on the ballot. 
The petition process often times entails serious 
enough consequences that it should require serious leg- 
work. But citizens shouldn’t have to enlist a battery of 
lawyers to prove the state has a defective voter list. Nor 
should politicians be able to shoot down proposals they 
don’t like on flimsy technicalities.

THE LATEST fight over Colorado’s initiative laws is in 
the final stages in the legislature. This time, as in the past, 
it is a classic struggle between the “insiders” and the 
“outsiders.” The smart money is on the insiders. 
Cheered on by Secretary of State Natalie Meyer, the 
legislature is again in the process of “perfecting” the 
initiative laws. Each time the process is refined to achieve 
some high-sounding purpose such as “preventing fraud,” 
the once-clear right of initiative becomes ever more bur- 
dened with procedural requirements. If passed, this latest 
version, with its telescoped timetables and new pro- 
cedural hurdles, may very well put the right of initiative 
beyond the reach of ordinary citizen groups. Instead it 
may be the exclusive playground of special-interest orga- 
nizations able to pay circulators and the army ot lawyers 
necessary to get a measure on the ballot.
