adverse decision even though the shareholder has not 
been formally made a party to the action.*

B. Proceedings in the Court of Appeals. 
The shareholder plaintiffs moved to dismiss the appeal 
on the ground that CalPERS and FSBA could not appeal 
because they had neither been, nor sought to become, 
parties in the District Court. 
J.A. 350. The Court of 
Appeals (Posner, C.J., and Flaum and Easterbrook, JJ.) 
agreed, and dismissed the non-party appeal. 
7 
In an opinion by Judge Easterbrook, the court quoted 
this Court’s opinion in Marino v. Ortiz, 484 U.S. 301 
(1988), that “The rule that only parties to a lawsuit, or 
those that properly become parties, may appeal an ad- 
verse judgment, is well settled.” 484 U.S. at 304, quoted 
at P.C.A. 1. The Court of Appeals also cited Rule 3(c), 
Fed. R. App. P., which provides that “A notice of appeal 
must specify the party or parties taking the appeal... .” 
P.C.A. 2a (emphasis by the court). The court also re- 
ferred to its own previous holding that to allow members 
of a class who were not named plaintiffs to appeal would 
be an “affront to intelligent judicial administration.” Jn re 
Brand Name Prescription Drugs Antitrust Litig., 115 F.3d 
456, 457 (7th Cir. 1997). 
Noting that Marino had rejected appeal by a non-party 
in a class action under Rule 23, Fed. R. Civ. P., the Court 
of Appeals explained that in a Rule 23.1 shareholder 
derivative action, the jurisdictional deficiency of a non- 
party appeal is even more apparent: 
“Although there are differences between class actions 
and derivative suits, these cut against the appellants’ 
position. .. 
. 
It is the fact that each class member 
has a real grievance with the defendant that sets up 
at least an equitable argument for a class member 
who wants to appeal. But in a shareholders’ deriva- 
tive action, the individual investor is not an injured 
party and is not entitled to litigate. 
A derivative 
suit is brought by an investor in the corporation’s

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 
The issue before this Court is not whether shareholders 
objecting to a settlement 
of a derivative 
action may 
appeal. Clearly the Federal Rules provide ample means 
for their doing so. The only issue is whether CalPERS 
and FSBA may appeal on their own terms—without judi- 
cial leave, without the responsibilities of a party, and 
without complying with the procedure the Federal Rules 
establish. 
For nearly 150 years this Court has held on many 
occasions that persons may not appeal the judgment of 
a federal court to which they have not been made or

sought to become a party. Ten years ago this Court 
reiterated that familiar rule in the context of non-parties 
who objected to a class-action settlement and then at- 
tempted to appeal. Marino v. Ortiz, 484 U.S. 301 (1988). 
CalPERS and FSBA argue that the rule, already “well 
settled” in 1850, should not apply to shareholders who 
submitted and argued objections to the settlement of a 
derivative action under Fed. R. Civ. P. 23.1. As legal 
analogy, they rely on cases in which persons became 
parties without intervention, as by order of a court, or 
by submitting to jurisdiction in an ancillary proceeding. 
As an excuse for not complying with the procedure speci- 
fied in Fed. R. Civ. P. 24 for intervention, they and amici 
say that being large institutions they have difficulty reach- 
ing decisions promptly, and that they do not wish to bear 
the costs or obligations of parties. As incentive to grant 
the result they seek, they argue that some settlements of 
derivative actions are unwise or collusive, so that permit- 
ting more persons to appeal to block settlements would 
be a good thing. 
There exists, however, written law that governs who 
may appeal the judgment in a civil action. With respect 
to settlements of derivative actions, Fed. R. Civ. P. 23.1 
provides only that shareholders must be notified of a pro- 
posed settlement, and district-court approval must be ob- 
tained. 
Rule 23.1 does not convert objectors into par- 
ties—a distinction that was clear in the minds of its draft- 
ers—nor confer on them a right of appeal. The method to 
seek the status of a party is prescribed in Fed. R. Civ. P. 24, 
which requires a motion, a showing, and judicial determi- 
nation. 
In addition, Rule 3(c) of the Federal Rules of 
Appellate Procedure in terms reserves the right of appeal 
to parties—as was held not only by this Court in Marino, 
but in many prior cases interpreting the identical language 
in Rule 3(c)’s predecessor. 
The Rules did not prevent 
CalPERS and FSBA from becoming parties—if they had 
moved to do so. 
But they did not want to be parties. 
They wanted instead something the Rules do not allow— 

10 
to avoid party responsibilities and yet become litigants in 
the court of appeals, entirely on their own prerogative, 
simply by filing a notice of appeal. 
CalPERS and FSBA now call themselves de facto par- 
ties, even though neither the District Court nor the parties 
ever treated them as such. They also try to liken themselves 
to persons who have acquired the status of parties in ways 
other than intervention, often unwillingly—those to whom 
a court has directed a contempt citation or injunction, or 
who were litigants in ancillary proceedings like execution 
sales, or attorneys claiming a right to fees. Such situations 
are plainly unlike that of someone who was given a dis- 
trict court’s ear, but who shunned assuming the responsi- 
bilities of a party. 
The core of CalPERS’ and FSBA’s argument is to urge 
what they see as a policy improvement. They believe that 
it would be healthy to have more appeals of derivative- 
action settlements. 
Therefore, they argue, the Federal 
Rules should be interpreted to allow even non-parties to 
file such appeals. There is not, they say, “any counter- 
vailing benefit,” Pet. Br. 12 (emphasis theirs), in restrict- 
ing such appeals to parties. 
Surely that is not so. The first countervailing benefit, 
of course, is faithfulness to the text and historical under- 
standing of the Rules themselves, Rules which embody 
policy judgments already taken. Secondly, as the govern- 
ment’s amicus brief with trepidation recognizes, the crea- 
ture of non-party appeals once loosed would not be easily 
cabined. The same language that would have to be re- 
read to permit non-party appeals in derivative actions 
would also govern other litigation, including class actions; 
in class actions, in fact, class members unlike shareholders 
in a derivative suit have personal claims at stake. Finally, 
surely there is something to be said for encouraging settle- 
ments; for not opening the courts of appeals to litigants 
(here very large and sophisticated ones) who spurned the 
opportunity to become parties but want the benefits; and 
for continuing, as the Rules prescribe, to vest in courts 
11 
some control over who may come from the sidelines to 
demand that the game be replayed. 
Most importantly, whether these two institutional in- 
vestors have an enlightened policy idea is not the point. 
Even if their controversial plan were wise, they have come 
to the wrong forum. Congress twice has recently changed 
procedures for securities litigation. 
It could do so again. 
So could the Rules Advisory Committees, who often rec- 
ommend changes to this Court. 
But not a word in the 
Rules supports the ruling sought here. Whether or not 
_ they may some day be changed, revising them is not this 
Court’s task today.

Rule 37, 226 U.S. 659 (1912), provided: 
PARTIES GENERALLY—INTERVENTION. 
Every action shall be prosecuted in the name of 
the real party in interest, but an executor, adminis- 
trator, guardian, trustee of an express trust, a party 
with whom or in whose name a contract has been 
made for the benefit of another, or a party expressly 
authorized by statute, may sue in his own name 
without joining with him the party for whose benefit 
the action 
is brought. All persons having an interest 
in the subject of the action and in obtaining the 
relief demanded may join as plaintiffs, and any per- 
son may be made a defendant who has or claims 
an interest adverse 
to the plaintiff. Any person may 
at any time be made a party if his presence is neces- 
sary or proper to a complete determination 
of the 
cause. 
Persons having a united interest must be 
joined on the same side as plaintiffs or defendants, 
but when anyone refuses to join, he may for such 
reason be made a defendant. 
Anyone claiming an interest in the litigation may 
at any time be permitted to assert his right by inter- 
vention, but the intervention shall be in subordina- 
tion to, and in recognition of, the propriety of the 
main proceeding.

Rule 39, 226 U.S. 659 (1912), provided: 
ABSENCE OF PERSONS WHO WOULD BE 
PROPER PARTIES. 
In all cases where it shall appear to the court that 
persons, who might otherwise be deemed proper par- 
ties to the suit, cannot be made parties by reason of 
their being out of the jurisdiction of the court, or 
incapable otherwise of being made parties, or be- 
cause their joinder would oust the jurisdiction of the 
court as to the parties before the court, the court 
may, 
in its discretion, 
proceed in the cause without 
making such persons parties; and in such cases the 
decree shall be without prejudice to the rights of the 
absent parties.
