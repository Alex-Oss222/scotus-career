JURISDICTION 
Title 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1), which petitioners invoke for 
this Court’s jurisdiction, Pet. Cert. 1, authorizes review 
of cases in the courts of appeal by writ of certiorari 
“granted upon the petition of any party to any civil or 
criminal case.” Petitioners describe themselves as “par- 
ties in the court of appeals.” Pet. Br. ii. 
This Court has said that “[oJne who has been denied 
the right to intervene in a case in a court of appeals 
may petition for a writ of certiorari to review that 

2 
ruling.” Izumi Seimitsu Kogyo Kabushiki Kaisha v. U.S. 
Philips Corp., 510 U.S. 27, 30 (1993); cf. Hohn 
v. 
United States, __ U.S. __, 118 S. Ct. 1969, 1975-76 
(1998). Petitioners specifically claimed a right to appeal 
as nonparties, J.A. 349, and did not seek to intervene 
in either the district court or the court of appeals. It 
appears that jurisdiction under § 1254(1) may be lack- 
ing.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
The facts which gave rise to this action are not 
disputed. Certain material matters, however, have been 
overlooked by petitioners in their brief. These facts 
should be kept in mind in considering whether to grant 
objecting shareholders, who do not purport to act for all 
shareholders of the Archer Daniels Midland Company, 
the right to delay implementation of a settlement of a 
corporate claim to which more than 99% of the corpora- 
tion’s shareholders do not object. 
These derivative actions were brought by two sepa- 
rate plaintiffs (“representative plaintiffs”) in the name 
of the Archer Daniels Midland Company (“ADM” or the 
“Company”). JA 48. Their claims were consolidated in 
the United States District Court for the Central District 
of Illinois. The representative plaintiffs sought to assert 
3 
claims against the seventeen directors of the Company 
as of June 27, 1995 (“director defendants”) for alleged 
breaches of their fiduciary duties, for abuse of their 
control of the Company and for gross mismanagement 
in failing to prevent ADM employees from violating the 
law. JA 56.’ The claims stemmed from the highly pub- 
licized investigation of ADM, some of its employees, and 
a number of its competitors, for alleged price-fixing in 
the markets for lysine, citric acid and high fructose 
corn syrup. When the Company pleaded guilty to fixing 
prices in the lysine and citric acid markets, paying a 
$100 million fine, the representative plaintiffs amended 
their claims to assert further breaches of fiduciary duty 
by the director defendants in their failure to investigate 
these allegations and to cooperate with the government 
in pursuing two of the three ADM employees personally 
indicted (and ultimately convicted) for their role in the 
lysine scheme. JA 95-105. 
The Company and the director defendants moved to 
dismiss the claims because the representative plaintiffs 
had failed to make the requisite demand on the Compa- 
ny’s board of directors under Rule 23.1 of the Federal 
Rules of Civil Procedure (“Rules”) to pursue these 
claims, and had failed to allege particularized facts why 
such a demand would be futile under Delaware law.

As reflected in the affidavit submitted to the district

PRINCIPLES OF CORPORATE GOVERNANCE: ANALYSIS AND

governance.” JA 281. Based upon this advice, the par- 
ties reached a decision which provides petitioners’ moti- 
vation for this appeal: the representative plaintiffs, the 
Company and the director defendants declined to adopt 
wholesale CalPERS’ governance recommendations. 

The district court preliminarily approved the proposed 
settlement and notice of the settlement was sent to the 
holders of over 525 million shares of ADM stock. The 
notice stated that it was being sent “merely to advise 
you of the pendency and proposed Settlement of the 
Derivative Claims and of your rights thereunder.” JA 
135. The notice also advised that “[a)ny current share- 
holder of ADM may appear at the Settlement Hearing.” 
JA 140. On the day the notice was mailed, CalPERS 
and CII were aware of the specific terms of the settle- 
ment. JA 313. CalPERS, along with petitioner Florida 
State Board of Administration (“FSBA”), filed objections 
to the settlement, supported by two affidavits submit- 
ted to the district court by CII (an organization of 
which CalPERS and FSBA are two of the largest 
members, JA 318). CalPERS and FSBA were but two of 
the Company’s 367 institutional shareholders; none of 
the other 365 objected. JA 343. The total number of 
shares objecting to the settlement comprised less than 
1% of the outstanding holdings of the Company’s share- 
holders. JA 343. All objections were considered by the 
district court on July 7, 1997. 
CalPERS and FSBA objected to two elements of the 
proposed settlement: the amount of the settlement 
escrow to be paid to attorneys; and the governance 
changes themselves, 
particularly the definition by 
which the Company would assess a director’s “indepen- 
dence” of management. Petitioners did not object to the 
principal agreement in the proposed settlement that 
any claims against the only director against whom the 
representative plaintiffs had developed evidence would 
be considered by the Company’s board of directors after 
final disposition of the government’s claims against that 
director. Petitioners also did not object to the establish-

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 
Petitioners made a decision not to join the action 
below as parties and have no right to appeal. Under the 
rule governing federal derivative actions, shareholders’ 
participation is strictly limited, and only those who 
meet the requirements of Rule 23.1 may become parties 
and appeal from the district court’s decision. The rule 
reflects the essential nature of a derivative action, 
which is not an action brought by shareholders for their 
own benefit, but one brought by representative share- 
holders to enforce a right of the corporation. As a 
result, petitioners must have done more than simply 
object to settlement of a suit—which is what the rule 
permits—if they expected to create a right to appeal. 
That avenue, intervention under Rule 24, was readily 
available to petitioners. Despite the fact that they have 
taken advantage of that avenue in other cases, petition- 

12 
ers declined to do so in this case, depriving the Seventh 
Circuit of appellate jurisdiction. 
The court of appeals’ refusal to permit petitioners to 
circumvent the rules governing derivative actions is not 
inconsistent with the precedent, discussed at great 
length in the briefs of petitioners and the government, 
which permits nonparties to appeal under certain cir- 
cumstances. In fact, all of these cases involve non- 
parties who were directly bound or affected by a court 
order. Many of them involved nonparties who were 
effectively named in what may be characterized as proc- 
ess issued by the court. Such cases are distinct from a 
derivative suit, where only the corporation is directly 
bound or affected by the court’s approval of a settle- 
ment. The rules permit a shareholder to receive notice 
of and be heard on a proposed settlement, but because 
of the shareholder’s distance from the suit, they permit 
nothing more.

4. Other Cases. 
Petitioners view the Seventh Circuit’s application of 
Marino as portending great upheaval in “an enormous 
body of settled law.” Pet. Br. 25. Petitioners then cite to 
dozens of cases where nonparties have been permitted 
to appeal in “a wide range of circumstances.” Pet. Br. 
25. None of these cases, however, is threatened by the 
Seventh Circuit’s decision; in fact, the nonparties in 
these three categories of cases were the subject of court 
process, i.e., the courts had issued orders directed to 
the nonparties. The cases are thus consistent with the 
older “quasi-party” cases and distinct from a derivative 
43 
case where nonparty shareholders have no such direct 
involvement. Neither together nor individually do they 
mandate the result petitioners seek. 
a. Cases Binding a Nonparty by Name. 
Petitioners themselves characterize these cases in a 
way that makes them clearly distinguishable from de- 
rivative suits, i.e., “nonparty appeals from judgments 
that bind the nonparty by name.” Pet. Br. 25. Nonparty 
shareholders, of course, are not bound by name in any 
judgment of a district court. The cases cited by petition- 
ers mostly involve injunctions directed to a specific 
party. Where a nonparty is bound by an injunction, it 
may appeal its entry. Thompson v. Freeman, 648 F.2d 
1144, 1148 n.5 (8th Cir. 1981) (citing Zenith Radio 
Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc., 395 U.S. 100, 110 
(1969)). See also 15A WRIGHT, ET AL., supra, § 3902.1, 
at 122 & n.35. Others are far removed from the context 
here. See, e.g., EEOC v. Pan Am. World Airways, Inc., 
897 F.2d 1499, 1504 (9th Cir.) (nonparty in age discrim- 
ination class action has “personal stake in outcome” 
and stood “to recover a substantial amount”), cert. 
denied sub nom. Keith v. EEOC, 498 U.S. 815 (1990); 
Hal Roach Studios, Inc. v. Richard Feiner and Co., 896 
F.2d 1542 (9th Cir. 1990) (judgment actually entered 
against nonparty by name); SEC v. Wencke, 783 F.2d 
829, 834 (9th Cir. 1986) (a “receiver has haled the 
nonparty into the proceeding against his will”). 
Another category identified by petitioners is “nonpar- 
ty witnesses held in contempt for refusing discovery to 
appeal on claims of privilege, lack of personal jurisdic- 
tion, and other matters.” Pet. Br. 25-26. This category, 
however, is really a subcategory of persons specifically 

44 
named in process issued by the court. They all appear 
because they are compelled to do so; for if they do not, 
no one else will speak for them. For example, a person 
summoned to court to show cause why he should not be 
held in contempt of that court stands in a place far 
different than a shareholder who receives notice that a 
corporation in which he owns stock has proposed to 
settle a derivative claim with plaintiffs who have a duty 
to speak for him in asserting the corporation’s rights. 
b. Attorneys’ Fees. 
Petitioners cite to another group of cases where 
courts “allow attorneys and expert witnesses to appeal 
district court denials of fees.” Pet. Br. 26. Again, such 
cases personally address a nonparty and his financial 
interest. While shareholders certainly have a financial 
stake in the fate of corporations whose stock they own, 
any financial recovery in a derivative case goes to the 
corporation, not to the shareholder. 
c. 
Nonparties Obligated To Reimburse De- 
fendants. 
Finally, petitioners point to cases where courts permit 
appeals by nonparties who are “obligated to reimburse 
the defendant for amounts paid under the trial court’s 
judgment.” Pet. Br. 26. One cited example is Fields v. 
Blum, 629 F.2d 825 (2d Cir. 1980), where the court per- 
mitted the state to appeal from an order enjoining a 
reduction in welfare benefits because the state was 
legally requirea to reimburse the city for the payments 
and controlled both the activities complained of and the 
conduct of the local officials. Pet. Br. 26 n.25. As the 
45 
government points out, however, nonparty shareholders 
are quite different from units of government bound to 
make public payments. Shareholders are liable only to 
the extent of their investment—which should not be 
diminished in a derivative suit—and have no direct ob- 
ligation to make payments to corporate creditors. 
C. Rule 23.1 Does Not Embody the Concept of 
Nonparty Shareholders as “De Facto Parties” 
or “Quasi-Parties.” 
Petitioners and the government argue that Rule 23.1, 
because it was based on “existing law,” somehow em- 
bodies the principle that nonparty shareholders are 
entitled to appeal from settlement of a derivative suit. 
Pet. Br. 18; G. Br. 12-19. This argument suffers from 
petitioners’ misperception of the “existing law” defining 
the kind of nonparties who have been permitted to 
appeal. As described in the preceding section, that body 
of law permits a nonparty to appeal where it is directly 
bound or affected by a court’s action. Rule 23.1 does not 
contemplate, and does not in practice have, that sort of 
binding effect on nonparty shareholders who are bound 
only by the limitations placed upon their corporation. 
Nonparty shareholders simply are not in the class of 
persons who, as discussed above, historically have been 
permitted to appeal a judgment to which they were not 
parties. 
Petitioners go to great lengths to distinguish the 
status of a shareholder in a derivative suit from that of 
a shareholder in other suits brought by or on behalf of 
the corporation. These efforts fail because the argument 
boils down to the assertion that shareholders should be 
permitted to appeal because “the shareholders necessar-

11. If such a simplistic test were applied, of course, any 
shareholder could ignore corporate management, corpo-

In a CERCLA settlement, for example, members of

The government’s argument proves only that there are 
other areas where notice to those affected by a settle-

merits of the settlement, does not transform into a 
“summons to appear” giving rise to a right to appeal.
