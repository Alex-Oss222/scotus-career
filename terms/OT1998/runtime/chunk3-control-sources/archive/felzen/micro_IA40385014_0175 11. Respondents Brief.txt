STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
The facts relevant to this petition are simply stated. 
Respondents, shareholders in Archer Daniels Midland 
Company (“ADM”), brought this derivative suit in the 
United States District Court in Illinois, alleging that 
ADM’s directors had violated duties owed to the corpo- 
ration. A settlement was reached, and pursuant to 
Fed.R.Civ.P. 23.1, notice of the proposed settlement was 
given to all the shareholders. Petitioners filed written 
objections to the settlement and appeared at the set- 
tlement approval hearing in opposition to the proposed 
settlement. At the conclusion of the hearing, the district 
court rejected their objections, approved the settlement 
and entered judgment. 
Petitioners never sought to intervene in the district 
court. One week after the district court hearing, how- 
ever, they filed a notice of appeal to the Court of Ap- 
peals for the Seventh Circuit as objectors “entitled to 
appeal.” App. 348-49. Respondents moved to dismiss the 
appeal in light of Seventh Circuit precedent squarely 
holding that nonparty class members who do not seek 
to intervene to become parties may not appeal from dis- 
trict court orders. In re Brand Name Prescription Drugs 
Antitrust Litigation, 115 F.3d 456 (7th Cir. 1997) 
(Posner, C. J.). The Brand Name Drugs decision relied 

on this Court’s holding in Marino v. Ortiz, 484 U.S. 
301, 304 (1988), and made clear that Marino barred 
objectors who were not name plaintiffs in representa- 
tive actions from appealing district court orders. Brand 
Name Prescription Drugs, 115 F.3d at 457. In this case, 
the Court of Appeals (Posner, C. J., and Flaum and 
Easterbrook, JJ.) dismissed petitioners’ appeal, holding 
that petitioners as nonparty objectors were not entitled 
to appeal. The panel’s opinion was circulated to the 
judges of the Seventh Circuit, and no judge favored 
hearing the case en banc. Petitioners sought certiorari 
from this Court, which the Court granted on the limited 
issue whether Rule 23.1 objectors who do not intervene 
can nonetheless appeal. 
Despite the apparent simplicity of these facts and the 
straightforward nature of the legal issue raised by this 
Court’s grant, petitioners’ brief is laced with personal 
attacks upon the conduct of plaintiffs and their counsel. 
This attack is entirely irrelevant to the issue upon 
which the writ issued. Petitioners understand that full 
well. Apparently, they hope that by casting aspersion 
on plaintiffs’ counsel’s conduct, this Court will somehow 
forgive petitioners’ failure to seek to intervene. Perhaps 
we need say no more on this score. But we are un- 
willing to have the Court proceed on the basis of doubts 
about plaintiffs’ counsel’s conduct. The record in this 
case, including affidavits and the district court’s find- 
ings, makes clear that petitioners’ charges are unwar- 
ranted. 
Paul Felzen and Sandra Esner are individual share- 
holders of ADM. They brought this derivative action in 
the United States District Court for the Northern Dis- 
trict of Illinois in July 1995 on behalf of ADM and its 
shareholders against 17 ADM directors after the federal 
government’s investigation of illegal price-fixing at 
ADM was disclosed. Plaintiffs were concerned that the 
ADM board, long criticized as being dominated by 
Chairman Dwayne Andreas, whose son was being in- 
vestigated for price-fixing, would not respond appro- 
priately to the criminal investigation. The case was 
subsequently transferred to the United States District 
Court for the Central District of Illinois. Other individ- 
ual shareholders brought similar derivative actions in 
Delaware State Court. 
Petitioners are two ADM shareholders who own less 
than one percent of the Company’s common stock. Al- 
though aware of this litigation and apprised of settle- 
ment discussions months before the case was settled, 
petitioners did not seek to participate in the litigation 
or make their objections to the proposed settlement 
known to plaintiffs’ counsel until after the parties to 
the derivative actions had entered into a settlement 
agreement.

two years during which time they reviewed all the 
documents ADM produced for various civil antitrust

and briefed successive motions to dismiss and discovery 
motions. The motions were set for hearing when the 
parties agreed to discuss settlement. Plaintiffs’ counsel

Court said:

that properly become parties, may appeal an 
adverse judgment, is well settled. The Court of 
Appeals suggested that there may be exceptions 
to this general rule, primarily “when the non- 
party has an interest that is affected by the

practice is for such a nonparty to seek interven- 
tion for purposes of appeal; denials of such

Relying upon this language, Fed.R.App.P. 3(c), and its 
own earlier decision in Brand Name Prescription 
Drugs, the court below held that Fed. 
R. Civ. P. Rule 
23.1 objectors who had not sought to intervene could 
not appeal from a district court's final judgment ap- 
proving a derivative suit settlement. In this Court, 
petitioners and their amici advance a wide range of 
arguments seeking reversal. Some are plainly irrele- 
vant. The repeated emphasis (Pet. Br. 13-16, 33-34) on 
the alleged importance of the objectors’ “monitoring” 
function surely cannot magically transform “objectors” 
into “parties”. Other arguments appear in a contradic- 
tory fashion. The various briefs inform the Court both 
that an intervention requirement is a meaningless for- 
mality, and that such a requirement is so burdensome 
as to deter the appearance of objectors. 
2. Like this Court’s Rules 12 and 14, Rule 3(c) re- 
flects the principle that only parties may appeal. See 
also 28 U.S.C. §1254(1), which directly governs this 
proceeding and which restricts petitioners to “any 
party”. Accordingly, petitioners must demonstrate party 
status. Although petitioners and their principal amicus, 
the United States, vary in expositional style, both in 
In re Brand Name Prescription Drugs Antitrust Litigation, 
115 F.3d 456 (7th Cir. 1997). There, the court, relying upon 
Marino, held that objectors in a class action who had not inter- 
vened could not appeal. The court below believed that Brand 
Name’s reasoning applied a fortiori in a derivative context. 
(Pet. App. 4a-6a)

the end draw upon two sources: Rule 23.1 and the 
decisions allowing persons not named in the pleading 
captions to appeal—“quasi parties” in the government’s 
terminology’ or, more appropriately, “ancillary parties”. 
Petitioners insist that they are de facto parties by 
virtue of responding to a Rule 23.1 notice (Br. 19), and 
also that they fall within the case law permitting non- 
named parties to appeal. Br. 25-27. Similarly, the 
government argues that Rule 23.1 “quasi-party prece- 
dents do furnish support for appealability” (Br. 16), 
which is reflected by the language of Rule 23.1 itself. 
Br. 12-15. 
But reliance upon either Rule 23.1 or the “quasi 
party” doctrine is misplaced. 
(a) Rule 23.1. (pp. 15-17, infra). The government ac- 
knowledges that at common law some courts held that 
nonparty shareholders had no right to notice of a 
derivative action settlement. Br. 19-20. Rule 23.1 alters 
that legal landscape, providing stockholders with a 
right to notice of the proposed settlement and court 
approval, which in practice carry with them a right to 
appear for the purpose of objecting. In a Rule 23.1 
proceeding, however, an objector does not participate as 
a party. The shareholder cannot dictate litigation strat- 
egy, settle the litigation, or represent the corporation 
on any issue in the litigation. He can only object. Noth- 
ing in Rule 23.1’s language or history remotely suggests 
that the Rule was also intended implicitly to confer 
appeal rights in addition to the two rights that it con- 
fers upon shareholders.

(b) Ancillary or Non Caption Parties. (pp. 17-20, 
infra). Neither Rule 3(c) nor this Court’s Rule 12 
defines “party” but, as the case law and this Court’s 
Rule 14(b) make clear, this term properly includes per- 
sons other than those listed in the pleading captions. 
The decisions of this Court demonstrate that “ancillary” 
parties can be grouped into three general categories. 
First, where the appellant is himself the direct object of 
a judicially enforceable order, such as an injunction or 
a contempt citation. Second, where the court purports 
directly to affect legally established rights or duties of 
named persons, such as determining ownership rights. 
As Marino v. Ortiz makes clear, however, the judicial 
order must do more than simply disadvantage the 
would-be appellant. See also Martin v. Wilks, 490 U.S. 
755 (1989). Rather, the order must operate directly 
upon the rights or duties of named or identifiable per- 
sons. Third, where (as Marino v. Ortiz makes plain) the 
appellant has unsuccessfully sought to intervene in the 
proceeding. 
Neither of the first two categories is applicable here. 
Rule 23.1 provides for the issuance of a notice, not a 
summons. (Compare Fed.R.Civ.P. Rule 4). Objectors are 
not “summoned” to the court, any more than are absent 
class members who receive a comparable notice under 
Rule 23(e). Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Shutts, 472 U.S. 
797, 808-811 (1985). Shareholders can ignore the notice, 
and if they do, their own legal rights are not affected. 
The government’s unsupported argument that the ob- 
jecting stockholder under Rule 23.1 asserts a “personal 
right” (Br. 16) is wrong. A Rule 23.1 proceeding itself 
concerns only the rights of the corporation and all of its 
stockholders, not the rights of or losses suffered by indi- 

12 
vidual stockholders. Kamen v. Kemper Fin. Servs., Inc., 
500 U.S. 90, 95 (1991). 
(c) State Law. An amicus contends that state law 
governs the party status of objectors. (pp. 21-22, infra). 
This issue is not “fairly included” within the question 
presented (Rule 14.1(a) of the Rules of this Court), and, 
in any event, is implausible. 
3.(a) The distinction between the status of objectors 
and intervenors has much to commend it. (pp. 22-25, 
infra). A requirement that an objector move to inter- 
vene neither reflects a meaningless formality nor 
creates an unwarranted obstacle. In a derivative suit, 
the shareholder plaintiff fights for the right to speak 
for the corporation and then to proceed with the 
corporation’s claims. Ross v. Bernhard, 396 U.S. 531, 
538 (1970). An intervenor assumes at least some re- 
sponsibilities and makes some commitments. An ob- 
jector, by contrast, makes no commitments and as- 
sumes no responsibilities. Objectors are not all cut from 
the same bolt of cloth. Some undertake serious work; 
others appear in a more or less desultory fashion. In- 
tervention proceedings thus serve as a screening device 
to deter frivolous or “hold up” appellate challenges to a 
settlement. If the distinction between objectors and par- 
ties is to be altered, it is for Congress to determinate, 
or for the rule-making process. 
(b) Petitioners’ argument that appealability without 
intervention is necessary to prevent collusion is merit- 
less. (pp. 25-27, infra). It assumes not only that a 
district court will rubber-stamp a “collusive settlement” 
in the face of cogent, well-founded objections and deny 
a well-founded motion to intervene, but also that the 
13 
court of appeals will not carefully review the district 
court’s decisions. If this is truly the situation, it will 
make no difference whether a nonparty could appeal 
without seeking to intervene. Either way, the ultimate 
appeal would be futile. Petitioners’ additional argument 
that an intervention requirement would subject them to 
burdens, e.g., discovery, is, at best, speculative, as the 
Court noted of similar claims in Phillips Petroleum Co. 
v. Shutts, 472 U.S. at 810 n.2, and in any event is ir- 
relevant. 
ARGUMENT 
This case has had extensive briefing with respect to 
the meaning and content of Rule 23.1, and this Court’s 
ancillary party cases. We see no reason to duplicate 
that elaborate and meticulous discussion. Rather our 
submission emphasizes the points that we think are 
most salient. They reduce to two: only parties can ap- 
peal and objectors 
are not parties. 
I. Only Parties Can Appeal and Objectors Are Not 
Parties. 
“The rule that only parties to a law suit, or those that 
become parties, may appeal an adverse judgment, is 
well settled.” Marino v. Ortiz, 484 U.S. at 304. Peti- 
tioners’ statement that federal courts “routinely permit

A. Rule 23.1. 
Rule 23.1 can only be understood against the back- 
ground of a derivative suit. A derivative suit is, in 
essence, two suits. Daily Income Fund, Inc. v. Fox, 464 
U.S. 523, 529 n.4 (1984). The first is an effort by a 
shareholder to establish the authority to speak for the 
corporation, when the corporation has failed to enforce 
a right it has. The second, once the authority is estab- 
lished, consists of the shareholder’s efforts, in the 
language of Rule 23.1, “to enforce the right of a cor- 
poration.” See Kamen v. Kemper Fin. Servs., Inc., 500 
U.S. 90, 95-96 (1991). In a derivative action, a share- 
holder has an economic interest, the value of his 
shares, but the shareholder brings the proceeding solely 
in the interests of the corporation and its shareholder 
owners. In discharging that obligation, a derivative 
plaintiff 
may seek to settle or dismiss the case. In such 
circumstances, Rule 23.1 gives shareholders two rights: 
the right to notice and the right to court approval. 
These are valuable rights designed to prevent abuse. 
But these rights do not confer party status upon the 
objector. If the district 
court rejected a settlement, an 
objector could not force the corporation to adopt a dif- 
ferent settlement and could not prosecute or dismiss 
the case. Objectors bear none of the rights and respon- 
sibilities of a party. Indeed, they have no rights what- 
soever if the district court dismisses the case involun- 
tarily. Burks v. Lasker, 441 U.S. 471, 485-86 n.16 
(1978). If objectors seek greater rights, they must move 
to intervene under Rule 24. Unless they do so, objectors 
remain objectors, not parties. 
Petitioners seek to avoid this obvious difficultly by 
insisting they were “summoned” to appear in district

court. They thereby seek to assimilate their status to

summons issues only to defendants and certain plain-

Nat’l Bank v. City of Norwalk, 291 U.S. 431, 441 
(1934), cited with approval in Martin v. Wilks, 490 U.S. 
at 763. Rule 23.1 authorizes the issuance of a notice, 
not a summons.” That notice imposes no legal obliga- 
tion upon the shareholder. The shareholder can ignore 
it, and if he does, none of his own legal rights is deter-

Intervention simply reflects the dual nature of a 
derivative action. A derivative action permits a share- 
holder to “step into the corporation’s shoes and to seek 
in its right the restitution he could not demand in his 
own.” Cohen v. Beneficial Indus. Loan Corp., 337 U.S. 
541, 548 (1949). Intervention properly focuses the court 
and the parties on the threshold issue in a derivative 
action, that is, whether the plaintiff, including the 
intervening plaintiff, has the right to sue on behalf of 
the corporation. Ross v. Bernhard, 396 U.S. 531, 538 
(1970). When properly viewed in the context of what a 
derivative suit is about, intervention is quite obviously 
not a meaningless formality or an unwarranted obsta- 
cle. Because only a shareholder who speaks for the 
interests of the corporation is entitled to litigate and, 
therefore, to appeal, it is logical to require that the 
shareholder first make a showing either that the named 
plaintiff does not adequately represent the interests of 
the corporation and all its shareholders, and that the 
objector does, Rule 24(a); or, at least, that the objecting 
shareholder seeks to represent the interests of the 
corporation in objecting to the settlement and has a 
claim in common with the main action, Rule 24(b). This 
threshold issue is also one that the court of appeals 
should be able to focus on first, regardless of which way 
the district court rules in what has become a dispute 
over which shareholder best represents the interests of 
all and should be heard on behalf of the corporation. 
This case is not the appropriate vehicle for deciding 
the policy argument of petitioners and their amici that 
objectors can play an important role in settlement 
proceedings so long as they are allowed to proceed 
without more. If Rule 23.1 or Fed.R.App.P. 3(c) is to be 
rewritten, see Martin v. Wilks, 490 U.S. 755, 767 
(1989), then the rulemaking process that 28 U.S.C. 
92072 provides for should be invoked. Amchem Prods., 
Inc. v. Windsor, 117 S.Ct. 2231, 2248 (1997). If jurisdic- 
tional statutes, for example 28 U.S.C. 612540), are to 
be amended, Congress must undertake that task. 
Rulemaking, whether under the auspices of Congress or 
the Court, will allow the full consideration of the 
potentials for abuse that exist if any shareholder who 
objects—even by sending a letter—to any part of any 
settlement can appeal and delay for a year or more 
implementation of a settlement that other shareholders 
have accepted. 
B. An Intervention Requirement Does Not Pro- 
mote Collusive Settlements or Impose Undue 
Burdens. 
Petitioners argue that appeals by nonparty objectors 
are necessary to prevent collusive or unfair settlements. 
They express great concern that district court judges 
charged with the responsibility for deciding whether a 
settlement is fair or adequate will be misinformed or 
uninformed. Assuming for argument’s sake that peti- 
tioners are correct about the state of affairs without 
objectors, this argument further assumes that federal 
district court judges will abandon their responsibilities 
as guardians of the class and will rubber stamp a 
“collusive settlement” in the face of cogent, well-founded 
objections. Petitioners totally discount the possibility 

that district courts consider and respond appropriately 
to valid objections.” 
Of course, all of petitioners’ expressed concerns could 
readily be addressed by a motion to intervene. If de- 
nied, objectors could appeal this denial just as they 
could appeal the approval of the settlement. Petitioners, 
however, argue, inconsistently, that district courts will 
deny motions to intervene—a result that could, of 
course, be appealed—or that the courts may instead 
grant these motions, subjecting them to the burdens, 
risks and responsibilities that parties have, but that 
petitioners are unwilling to assume. The fear of discov- 
ery is speculation, as this Court has already observed 
of similar fears, see Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Shutts, 
472 U.S. at 810 n.2, and would appear totally illusory 
in the case of an intervention for purposes of appeal. 
Similarly, any additional pleading burdens are certainly 
not unduly harsh for someone who seeks the time and 
attention of a court of appeals. It is also an obligation 
commensurate with the ability of the objecting share- 
holder to delay the implementation of a settlement. 
Finally, the argument that not all objectors can inter- 
vene, if correct, would not justify a different approach.
