ARGUMENT 
This Case Presents Important Questions of Federal Law 
Fundamentally 
Different 
from 
those 
Presented 
in 
The questions presented by the Petition for a Writ of 
Certiorari (hereinafter, the ‘“Petition”) are fundamentally 
important, involving the standards for the participation of 
minority voices in government-sponsored political debaies. 
While the Court recently deciced Arkansas Educational 
Television Commission v. Ralph P. Forbes, 118 S.Ct 1633 
(1998), this case raises different issues, and the Court’s 
review would not simply be a reexamination of Forbes. 
Respondent urges the Court not to review the facts in 
this case, stating that “the courts need not, and must not, 
become involved in attempting to supervise the editorial 
decisions of broadcast journalists” (Respondents’ Brief in 
Opposition, p. 7-8), citing Fo:bes at 1640. This argument 
minimizes the distinction between the editorial decisions of 
state-owned broadcasters in general, and their decisions about 
which candidates may participate in political debates. 
In 
Forbes, the Court states first that “public broadcasting as a 
general matter does not lend itself to scrutiny under the forum 
doctrine,” Id. at 1639-40, but then concludes, that candidate 
debates present a “narrow exception to the rule,” in part 
because of the “exceptional significarce” of debates in the 
- electoral process, Id. at 1640. 
In advancing their theme that no judicial analysis of 
I 

the facts of this case is necessary, Respondents’ brief, notably, 
is devoid of virtually any analysis of the underlying facts that 
show how 
this 
case 
differs 
from 
Forbes. 
Instead, 
Respondents recite special verd:ct responses and conclusions 
of the District Court that are not inconsistent with the 
Constitutional challenges raised by Petitioners. The District 
Court found (Pet. Apdx. B-29, 30) that the candidate forum in 
question was a debate (special verdict answer 1); that IPTV 
had a predetermined policy in deciding whom to invite to 
debates (special verdict answer 3, which is consistent with 
Petitioners’ claim that no candidate-by-candidate analysis was 
undertaken); that none of the Petitioner candidates was 
newsworthy (special verdict answer 5, which Petitioner 
claims is an impermissibly subjective and content-based 
standard); and that the candidates were not excluded because 
~ 
of disagreement with their political opinions (special verdict 
answer 6). Even this lasi finding does not mean that the 
grounds on which Petitioners were excluded was reasonable 
and not an impermissibly subjective analysis based on the 
content of the Petitioners’ ideas. 
Respondents’ 
principal 
argument, 
with 
which 
Respondents’ brief opens (Introduction) and closes (the next 
to last paragraph, p. 8), appears to be that Petitioner stated to 
the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals that this case is 
indistinguishable from Forbes. 
Respondent’s argument is superficial. 
The eighth 
circuit’s decision in Forbes was premised on that court’s 
holding that the question of whether a candidate is “politically 
2 

viable” (i.e., had sufficient public support) is [too] subjective 
“for the exercise of governmental power consistent with the 
First 
Amendment.” Forbes 
v. Arkansas Educational 
Television, 93 F.3d 497, 505 (8th Cir. 1996). Petitioners have 
consistently argued in the eighth circuit and here that the 
“newsworthiness” standard used by Iowa Public Television 
(“IPTV”), while different from the standard applied in Forbes, 
is also too subjective. However the “indistinguishability” of 
the two cases ends there. 
First, on review, this Court did not find fault with the 
eighth circuit’s decision 
that a subjective -standard 
is 
impermissible; rather, the Court impliedly endorsed the eighth 
circuit’s condemnation of subjective standards for candidate 
selection since the Court based its decision to reverse on a 
finding that Arkansas 
Educational 
Television made 
a 
reasonable decision and that “Forbes own objective lack of 
support...was the criterion.” Id. at 1644. (Emphasis added). 
This case presents, as the first question not settled by 
Forbes, 
whether 
newsworthiness 
is an 
impermissibly 
subjective standard. In this regard, IPTV has admitted that a 
determination 
of newsworthiness 
is 
“extraordinarily 
subjective,” Trial Ex. 20, Miller Depo. at 22, lines 5-18, and 
since a decision about whether a candidate is newsworthy 
depends, in no small measure, on an analysis of the 
candidate’s ideas, the fundamentally subjective nature of the 
decision is apparent. 
This case, then, presents a second important question, 
not present in Forbes, of whether the “newsworthiness” 
3 

standard is impermissibly content or viewpoint-based, since 
the district Court defined the term “newsworthy,” using an 
ordinary dictionary definition, to mean whether the candidates 
or their material were “sufficiently interesting” to the public. 
Jury Instruction No. 15, Pet. Apdx. B-36. Respondents’ brief 
never denies that a determination of newsworthiness involves 
an analysis of a candidate’s ideas, and the testimony from 
IPTV”’s own witnesses shows the content-based nature of the 
analysis. 
IPTV’s Professor Mack testified that newsworthiness 
is evaluated based on whether the interviewee (candidate) has 
“information 
to share 
with viewers 
that 
will 
inform 
them...help them make a better decision...enable them to be 
better taxpayers, better citizens...” Pet. at 7-8. In addition, 
IPTV’s Mike Newell, who excluded the Petitioner candidates 
on the basis of a lack of newsworthiness, testified that 
“newsworthiness would indicate. ..that we have a tendency to 
make news at the lowa Press table, or . 
. 
. to flesh out what 
already is in the news, or to add to what 1s generally known to 
our viewer- ship.” Trial Ex. 19, Newell Depo. at 16-17. IPTV 
evaluated the candidates based on the ideas expected to be 
presented by them, and whether these ideas would “add to 
what is known to the viewership” or “flesh out what is in the 
news.” 
The Marcus case, unlike Forbes, stands for the 
proposition that minority candidates can be prevented from 
participation in debates based on an admittedly subjective 
standard that analyzes whether the candidate’s ideas are 
4 

sufficiently interesting. There is no plausible argument that 
this was the decision of the Court in Forbes. 
Because this case, unlike Forbes, involves an analysis 
of “newsworthiness,” the real basis on which most journalists 
will make their decisions, this case will provide more 
guidance to the lower courts than Forbes on this important 
issue. 
While the District Court held that “it is profoundly 
important that the defendant network [IPTV] and its news 
editors be allowed to exercise independent journalistic and 
editorial judgments based on newsworthiness,” Marcus _v. 
lowa Public 
Television, No. 4-96-CV-80690, 
1996 WL 
764143 (S.D. Iowa, October 9, 1996) (Pet. Apdx. B-33), 
Petitioners contend that it is eve more profoundly important 
that minority voices 
not be silenced 
because 
of the 
government’s “extraordinarily subjective” determination that 
a candidate is not “sufficiently interesting.” 
The Petition should be granted.
