INTRODUCTION 
Petitioners do not establish a compelling reason for 
granting certiorari. 
Petitioners do not, and cannot, point to 
any conflict between courts or to any departure from the 
accepted and usual course of 
judicial proceedings. Sup. Ct. 
R. 10. Neither 
do they identify any "important question of 
federal law that has not been, but should be, settled 
by this 
Court." Sup. Ct. R. 10(c). Just last Term, this Court settled 
the 
same 
issues 
in Arkansas 
Educational 
Television 
Commission v. Forbes, 118 S. Ct. 1633 (1998), and this case 
presents no new important or unsettled questions. Indeed, in 
the court below - before this Court reversed the Eighth 
Circuit's decision in Forbes - Petitioners argued that "[t]his 
case is indistinguishable from the recent eighth circuit [sic] 
case [of Forbes]." Appellants’ Brief 
to the Court of Appeals, 
filed Nov. 14, 1996, at 13.

The decision of the U.S. District Court for the Southern 
District of Iowa is reported at 24 Med. L. Rpt. 2531 (S.D. 
Iowa 1996) and at 1996 WL 764143. The Eighth Circuit's 
denial of emergency injunctive relief is reported at 97 F.3d

Educational Television Commission v. Forbes was entered on

Eighth Circuit affirming the District Court's dismissal is

STATEMENT 
OF THE CASE 
Respondents are a state-owned public broadcaster, lowa 
Public Television ("IPTV"), and its programming 
director, 
Daniel 
K. Miller. IPTV broadcasts a weekly program entitled 
“Iowa Press," in which members of the press question invited 
guests about topics of public interest. In 1996, IPTV decided 
to broadcast a series of programs on Jowa Press with 
congressional candidates as guests. After considering 
which 
guests would be most interesting to the viewing audience, 
Respondents did not invite Petitioners - candidates in the 
1996 election - to participate 
in the programs. Respondents 
made their decision on the basis of a predetermined policy and 
Petitioners (Plaintiffs below) brought an action in the 
United States District 
Court for the Southern District 
of lowa 
alleging infringement 
of their First Amendment 
rights and 
seeking 
injunctive 
relief. 
The District Court denied

Though declining to decide whether the parties were entitled 
to a trial by jury, the District Court submitted eight special 
verdict questions to the jury. The District 
Court found that 
“{eJach of the jury's answers to the special verdict questions 
was fully supported 
by the evidence in the record," 
and the 
court made the same findings based upon an "independent 
consideration of the evidence." 
Marcus v. lowa Public 
Television, No. 4-96-CV-80690, 1996 WL 764143, at *2 
(S.D. Iowa Oct. 9, 1996). 
The jury found, among other things, that Petitioners 
failed to prove that they had been excluded by IPTV based

upon their political viewpoints. Instead, 
the jury found that 
Petitioners 
were excluded "on the basis of independent 
journalistic and editorial judgments the [Respondents] made 
based on newsworthiness." Jd. The court applied the "strict 
scrutiny” standard for limited public fora, and found that the 
“network's exercise of journalistic discretion [met] generally 
about newsworthiness, as required 
by the ‘Statement of 
Principles of Editorial Integrity in Public Broadcasting.'" Jd. 
at *3-*4. 
Petitioners filed an emergency motion for injunctive relief 
in the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit. 
This, too, was denied, as Petitioners failed to demonstrate a 
likelihood of success on the merits. 
Furthermore, though 
acknowledging that a determination of newsworthiness is 
based on journalistic discretion, the Court of Appeals noted 
that "there are clearly objective elements of newsworthiness." 
Marcus v. Iowa Public Television, 97 F.3d 1137, 1143 (8th 
Cir. 1996). The panel concluded "that IPTV's professional 
broadcasters are generally better aware of what constitutes 
appropriate programming than a group of federal judges; it is 
clearly in the public interest in having a state-operated public 
television free of unnecessary interference by a federal court." 
Id. at 1144. 
On March 24, 1997, the Eighth Circuit, on its own 
motion, removed this case from the Court's en banc oral 
argument calendar (App. i). The Court of Appeals ordered 
that further proceedings in this appeal be held in abeyance 
pending 
this Court's decision 
in Forbes. 
In Forbes, this Court held that a political candidate debate 
program produced by a state-owned broadcaster was a non- 

4 
public forum, and that the state-owned broadcast licensee 
could limit participation in the debate program in a viewpoint- 
neutral and reasonable manner. 
Forbes was decided last 
Term, and, shortly thereafter, after receiving supplemental 
briefs but without hearing oral argument, the Eighth Circuit, 
in a two-page en banc opinion, affirmed the District Court's 
dismissal of Marcus, 1996 WL 764143. 
Marcus v. Iowa 
Public Television, 150 F.3d 924 (8th Cir. 1998) (en banc). 
The Court of Appeals held that Marcus is controlled by 
Forbes. Marcus, 150 F.3d at 925. Moreover, the Court of 
Appeals found that the District Court did not clearly err in 
finding that the Petitioners "were not excluded on the basis of 
viewpoint," and that the Petitioners’ "exclusion was otherwise 
reasonable." Jd.

ARGUMENT 
I. 
Forbes Protects the Editorial Decisions of State-Owned 
Public Television Stations that Sponsor Candidate 
Debates. 
The issues raised by the Petitioners in this case were 
resolved in Forbes, 118 S. Ct. 1633, and this case presents no 
unsettled issues. In Forbes, a state-owned public broadcaster 
sponsored 
a debate for Arkansas' Third District candidates for 
the House of Representatives. 
The broadcaster invited the 
major party candidates but, after considering the lack of 
public interest in Forbes' campaign, did not invite Forbes to 
participate. The Eighth Circuit held that Forbes had a First 
Amendment right to participate, and this Court reversed. 
This Court held that the debate was a non-public forum, and 
that Forbes' exclusion was a "reasonable, viewpoint-neutral 
exercise of journalistic discretion consistent with the First 
Amendment." Jd. at 1644. 
"Although public broadcasting as a general matter does 
not lend itself 
to scrutiny under the forum doctrine, candidate 
debates present the narrow exception to the rule." Forbes, 
118 S. Ct. at 1640. A state-owned public broadcaster may 
create a candidate debate that is a non-public forum, where 
access 
is at the broadcaster's 
invitation, based on the 
broadcaster's good-faith editorial judgment. Such a program 
is characterized by selective, rather than general, access. Jd. 
at 1642-43. 
When a debate on a state-owned public 
broadcasting facility is a non-public forum, the editor's 
decision to exclude a legally qualified candidate must not be 
based on the speaker's viewpoint and otherwise must be 
reasonable in light of the purpose of the forum. Jd. at 1643.

In a two-page opinion, the Court of Appeals held that this

for the District Court's holding that the exclusion was 
otherwise reasonable. 
Petitioners assert that IPT V's reliance on newsworthiness 
as a criterion for excluding them is impermissible under 
Forbes. 
Pet. at 8-17. 
Petitioner's Procrustean reading 
ignores this Court's confirmation in Forbes that a public 
broadcaster's 
"journalistic" judgment 
is central 
to the 
congressional framework of broadcast regulation, relying on 
Columbia Broadcasting System 
v. Democratic National 
Committee, 412 U.S. 94, 127 (1973) ("CBS v. DNC"). 
Forbes, 
118 S. Ct. at 1640. 
Among other things, a 
"journalistic" judgment can encompass newsworthiness. 
Petitioners’ invitation to the Court to review the details of 
the District Court's fact-finding, Pet. at 18-20, also ignores 
the reminder in Forbes that courts need not, and must not, 

8 
become involved in attempting to supervise the editorial 
decisions of broadcast journalists. Forbes, 118 S. Ct. at 1640 
("[C]ourts 'would be required to oversee far more of the day- 
to-day operations of broadcasters’ conduct. 
. 
. .'") (quoting 
CBS v. DNC, 412 U.S. at 127). 
Petitioners, citing Regan v. Time, Inc., 468 U.S. 641 
(1984), argue that newsworthiness is a criterion forbidden to 
state-owned public broadcasters. 
Pet. at 10-11. 
Besides 
ignoring Forbes' emphasis on the state-owned broadcast 
licensee's editorial role, this argument misapplies Regan. In 
Regan, the U.S. government was not exercising the editorial 
role of operating a press, and the government's decisions 
about content were not proven to be insulated from political 
pressure (as is the case with IPTV's editorial process). 
Rather, in Regan the government attempted to censor a 
decision by an editor about content. 
Regan therefore is 
inapposite. 
Petitioners’ current theme, that the instant case shows 
unreasonable editorial decision-making, in contrast to the 
reasonable editorial judgments in Forbes, is ironic in light of 
Petitioners' 
argument below 
that, 
"[t]his 
case 
is 
indistinguishable from the recent eighth circuit [sic] case [of 
Forbes]." 
Appellants' Brief to the Court of Appeals, filed 
Nov. 14, 1996, at 13. 
No two cases are likely to have facts that trace each other 
exactly. 
However, comparing facts in the instant case to 
those in Forbes reveals similarities of decisional significance. 
Both broadcasters are networks owned by the state; both 
follow the Statement of Principles of Editorial Integrity in 
Public Broadcasting (Forbes, 118 S. Ct. at 1637; Marcus, 
1996 WL 764143, at *3-*4, Pet. Apdx B-34; Marcus v. Iowa 

9 
Public Television, 97 F.3d at 1142, Pet. Apdx A-18 (noting 
that lowa Code § 256.82(3) specifically adopts the Principles 
of Editorial Integrity)); and both state networks make 
journalistic 
decisions 
that are 
insulated 
from political 
pressure. 
For all these reasons, last Term's decision in 
Forbes settled all the issues raised by the Petition; Petitioners 
fail to present any new unsettled questions.
