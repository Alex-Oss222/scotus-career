ARGUMENT 
1. 
The 
trial 
court 
determined 
that 
the 
Petitioner candidates were excluded by IPTV from 
debates because they were not “newsworthy,” which, as 
applied in this case, was content-based and viewpoint 
based, and an impermissible subjective standard. 
A. 
This is an important issue for the Court 
because 
a decision 
about whether 
a candidate 
is 
“newsworthy” is routinely made by broadcasters in 
making journalistic decisions, yet decisions about a 
candidate’s newsworthiness are subjective and involve an 
analysis of the candidate’s ideas. 
In its recent decision 
in Arkansas Educational 
Television Commission v. Ralph Forbes, _ U.S._, 118 
S. Ct. 1633, 1640 (1998), this Court differentiated between 
public broadcasting as a general matter, where the First 
Amendment “does not compel public broadcasters to allow 
third parties access to their programming,” Id., and “candidate 
debates [which present] the narrow exception to the rule,” Id. 
This Court held the debate in Forbes was a nonpublic forum, 
and that a candidate could be excluded from a debate if the 
exclusion was viewpoint-neutral and otherwise “reasonable 
in light of the purpose of the property.” Id. at 1643. 
The Court said “Forbes was excluded not because of 
his viewpoint but because he had generated no appreciable 
public interest.” Id. at 1644 (emphasis added). While the 
5 

dissenting opinion in Forbes (J. Stevens) noted that AETC 
argued to the Supreme Court that its judgment was based on 
“newsworthiness,” while arguing to the Court of Appeals that 
its judgment was about “political viability,” the majority 
opinion in Forbes did not determine whether “news- 
worthiness” provided a Constitutional standard for the 
exclusion of candidates. The Court determined that AETC’s 
decision was reasonable because Forbes had virtually no 
public support, was not considered a serious candidate, and 
the decision to exclude him from the debate was not based on 
opposition to his views. Id. at 1643-1644. 
The issue of whether candidates can be excluded 
based not on reasonable, objective grounds as in Forbes, but 
because they are not “newsworthy’”— a standard that was 
admitted by IPTV to be “extraordinarily subjective” (Trial Ex. 
20, Miller Depo. at 22, lines 5-18) — is an important issue for 
this 
Court 
since 
journalists 
routinely 
consider 
the 
newsworthiness of a candidate in making their decisions, and 
government journalists need guidance in determining whether 
such -judgments are proper in making decisions about 
candidate selection in political debates. 
Moreover, the district court in this case defined 
“newsworthiness” to mean persons or materials who are 
“sufficiently interesting,” which seemingly requires an 
analysis of the candidate’s ideas. (Jury instruction No. 15, 
Appendix B-36.) 
In fact, a journalist’s decision about a 
candidate’s newsworthiness 
can be divorced from an 
evaluation of the candidate’s ideas and political views. 
6 

Professor Barbara Mack, IPTV’s expert from the Iowa State 
University School of Journalism, provided the only expert 
testimony 
in the case 
on 
the meaning of the term 
“newsworthy.” Professor Mack defined newsworthiness as 
follows: 
When I teach freshmen journalist about what 
is meant by newsworthiness, what makes 
someone 
newsworthy, 
you 
talk 
about 
the - 
- the quality that that person or news 
event has. Is that news event going to have an 
impact 
on 
the people who 
read 
your 
newspaper or who watch your television 
station? 
Is it going to change their lives? 
Does it have the potential to change their 
lives? (Trial Tr. Vol. II at 354-355). 
Evaluating the quality of a political candidate has to be based 
on the quality of the candidate’s ideas, and evaluating whether 
the news event [debate] is “going to have an impact on the 
people” or “change their lives” must depend on the views 
expressed. When Professor Mack was asked at trial about the 
decision of whom to invite to a program like the debates at 
issue, she answered: 
The newsworthiness of the interviewee is the 
ultimate 
test. 
Does 
that person 
have 
information to share with viewers that will 
inform them, that will help them make a 
decision, that will enable them to lead their 
lives better, that will enable them to be better 
taxpayers, better citizens, better residents of 
7 

their community? And—The ability of the 
interviewee to explain that viewpoint and to 
bring viewers information that will inform 
them is the test. 
(Emphasis Added). 
(Trial Tr., Vol. IT at 369). 
Professor Mack’s test requires an evaluation of the 
“information” the candidate has and his or her ability to 
explain that “viewpoint.” 
While the trial judge used a 
somewhat different definition in his instructions to the jury 
(defining “newsworthiness” to be whether the candidate or his 
materials would be sufficiently interesting to the audience), 
both definitions involve an analysis of a candidate’s ideas. 
When the jury answered special verdict question 
number 7 that the Petitioner candidates were excluded from 
the debates “based on newsworthiness” (Appendix, B-30), 
and when the district court decided that the Petitioner 
candidates were excluded from the debates by IPTV because 
they were not newsworthy (Appendix, B-33), it raised the 
important issue of whether such a standard is too vague or 
subjective or too dependent on the candidate’s ideas to 
provide a Constitutional basis for a candidate’s exclusion 
from a debate by a public broadcaste.. 
B. 
Newsworthiness 
is an impermissible 
content-based standard. 
A candidate without significant public support, but 

with novel and appealing ideas, might very well be deemed to 
be an “interesting” and, therefore, newsworthy candidate, 
according to the district court’s definition of the term 
“newsworthy.” At the same time, a candidate with public 
support (based perhaps on name recognition alone), but 
without ideas deemed by the broadcasters to be interesting, 
might very well be deemed to be an “uninteresting” candidate 
and therefore, not newsworthy. 
The newsworthiness of a 
candidate, therefore, is unconstitutionally linked to the 
content of the candidate’s materials and an evaluation of 
whether the candidate’s ideas are “interesting.” 
In Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of the University 
of 
Virginia, 515 U.S. 819, 115 S. Ct. 2510, 132 L. Ed. 2d 700 
(1995), 
this 
Court 
distinguished 
between 
content 
discrimination, which the Court said “may be permissible if 
it preserves the purposes of the limited forum,” and viewpoint 
discrimination, which “is presumed impermissible,” even in 
a non-public forum, when directed against speech otherwise 
within the forum’s limitations. 
Rosenberger at 115 S. Ct. 
2517. 
The Rosenberger Court explained that viewpoint 
discrimination 
is 
“an 
egregious 
form 
of 
content 
discrimination. The government must abstain from regulating 
speech when the specific motivating ideology or the opinion 
or perspective of the speaker is the rationale 
for the 
restriction.” Id; citing Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local 
Educators’ Ass’n, 460 U.S. 37, 46, 103 S. Ct. 948, 955, 74 L. 
Ed. 2d 794 (1983). Here, unlike in Forbes, the jury found the 
Petitioner candidates were excluded from the government 
9 

sponsored debates because 
the government employees 
determined that their political messages, campaigns, and/or 
their candidacies themselves (i.e., their viewpoints), were not 
sufficiently interesting. 
The rulings of the two lower courts are in disregard of 
this Court’s holding in Regan 
v. Time, Inc., 468 U.S. 641, 
648, 104 S. Ct. 3262, 3267 82 L.Ed. 2d 487, 494 (1984). In 
Regan, this Court held in no uncertain terms that the 
determination of newsworthiness is a decision “dependent 
solely on the nature of the message being conveyed,” and that 
to “permit the government to discriminate on the basis of the 
content of the message cannot be tolerated under the First 
-Amendment.” In Regan, which involved the publication of 
photographs of money, this Court stated: 
[U]nder the statute [(18 U.S.C. § 504(1)], one 
photographic reproduction will be allowed 
and another disallowed solely because the 
Government determines that the message 
being conveyed in the one is newsworthy or 
educational while the message imparted by the 
other is not. 
The permissibility of the 
photograph 
is therefore often ‘dependent 
solely on the nature of the message being 
conveyed.” 
Regulations which permit the 
government to discriminate on the basis of the 
message cannot be tolerated under the First 
Amendment. 
Id. (Citations omitted; emphasis added.) 
Although Regan arose in the criminal context, there is 
10 

no reason to believe that its condemnation of the suppression 
of speech based on an analysis of newsworthiness applies 
only in criminal matters. 
The Regan Court held that the 
regulations were impermissible due to the First Amendment, 
not because the defendant was accused of a crime. 
A 
ballot-qualified candidate for 
Congress, seeking to engage in 
political speech, an activity “at the very core of the First 
Amendment” (Forbes v. Arkansas 
Educational 
Television 
Commission, 93 F. 3d 497, 504 (8th Cir. 1996)), should be 
entitled to at least as much protection under the Constitution 
as the criminal defendant in Regan. 
In Regan, once this Court determined 
that the 
“newsworthiness” standard was content based, it simply 
stated 
that it could not be tolerated 
under the First 
Amendment, and that it was “constitutionally infirm.” Regan, 
468 U.S. at 648, 104 S. Ct. at 3267, 82 L. Ed. 2d 487 (1984). 
The Court engaged in no further substantive analysis of the 
constitutionality of the statute to justify its holding. 
By discretionarily determining whether third party 
candidates are sufficiently “newsworthy” to be permitted on 
public television, IPTV is engaging in an unconstitutional, 
content-based evaluation of expression. Such conduct by the 
government 
contravenes 
the 
First 
and _ Fourteenth 
Amendments. 
Cc. 
An evaluation of whether a candidate is 
“newsworthy” is an impermissibly subjective standard 
that cannot be challenged by an excluded candidate and 
il 

can be used as a shield by government to conceal the 
censorship of dissenting voices. 
IPTV conceded that a decision about a candidate's 
“newsworthiness” 
was 
an 
“extraordinarily 
subjective” 
judgment (Trial Ex. 20, Miller Depo. at 22. lines 5-18). Asa 
result, unlike the objective test of reasonableness applied in 
the Forbes case, here there is no standard limiting the 
discretion of government employees to exclude a candidate 
from a debate. 
The Petitioner candidates’ exclusion from the debates 
cannot be said to have been reasonable since no objective test 
was employed. On the other hand, the decision in Forbes was 
objective and was found to be reasonable because the Court 
accepted that AETC excluded Forbes because (1) Arkansas 
voters did not consider him a serious candidate, (2) news 
organizations did not consider him a serious candidate, (3) the 
Associated Press and another reporting service did not plan to 
run his results on election night, (4) Forbes had little, if any, 
financial support, and (5) there was no “Forbes for Congress” 
campaign headquarters except at his house. Forbes at__—iUS.. 
___, 118 S.Ct. 1633 at 1643-1644. The Court’s opinion also 
placed substantial emphasis on Forbes’ own description of his 
campaign organization as “bedlam,” and the media coverage of 
his campaign as “zilch.” Id., 118 S.Ct. at 1644. 
Thus, Ralph Forbes’ lack of support was determined by 
AETC based on the independent evaluations of others (items | 
through 3 above), or objectively determined financial or 
campaign activities (items 4 and 5), and Forbes’ own admissions 
12 
2 

that he had “zilch” in the way of press coverage. These are 
objective factors, and the Court itself stated that “[Forbes] own 
objective lack of support, not his platform, was the criterion.” 
(emphasis added) ]d., at 1644 
In this case, Mike Newell, who made IPTV’s decision 
to exclude the Petitioner candidates, did not consider 
objective factors.

candidates, nor did he ask news reporters or any independent 
journalists’ whether any Petitioner candidate was considered

This case provides an opportunity to render 
a decision that balances the extreme situation in Forbes, 
of a candidate whom the Court found had no support, 
with a situation where candidates had significant support, 
but were excluded 
using an 
admittedly

standard without a fact-finding inquiry. 
A public television station that decides to sponsor 
debates between only the two candidates with the highest 
ratings 
in the polls would 
be making 
an 
objective 
determination, 
but under such 
a standard 
the public 
broadcaster would always exclude all but the major party 
candidates, and minority voices would not have the unique 
opportunity of a debate to challenge the parties in power. 
~ 
This raises the question of what standard is not just 
objective but also reasonable and the perspective from which 
the reasonableness of the decision is to be evaluated. 
The decision in Forbes suggests that reasonableness should be 
considered in light of the purpose of the debates. Forbes at 
118 S.Ct. 1633, 1643 (a candidate can be excluded if the 
exclusion was viewpoint-neutral and otherwise “reasonable 
in light of the purpose of the property.) The objective should 
be to provide candidates who have a modicum of public 
support (this Court said Mr. Forbes had no appreciable public 
support) a fair opportunity to challenge the political status 
quo, at least where there are not so many candidates 
clamoring to gain access to the debates that it shuts down this 
important channel for public education. 
18 

Petitioners submit that the public support necessary 
to allow a third party or independent candidate to participate 
in a government-sponsored debate need not be “massive,” as 
was suggested by Professor Barbara Mack, iPTV’s expert 
(Trial Tr. Vol. Il at 422), but should simply be that support 
that encourages “uninhibited, robust, and wide-open debates,” 
New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254, 270, 84 S. 
Ct. 710, 720, 11 L. Ed. 2d 686, 701 (1964) so that political 
and societal changes “may be obtained through lawful 
means,” Carey 
v. Brown, 447 U.S. 455, 467, 100 S. Ct. 2286, 
2293, 65 L. Ed.2d 263 (1980), and reform ideas from the third 
party camps can be heard. 
As stated in an important treatise on third party 
activities, “Many of America’s noblest and far-reaching 
advances in freedom were third party proposals years before 
major parties touched them even with the longest pole.” J.D. 
Gillespie, Politics at the Periphery: 
Third Parties 
in 
Two-Party America, Univ. of South Carolina Press, 24 (1993) 
(identifying the abolition of slavery and child labor, woman 
suffrage, the graduated income tax, and unemployment 
insurance, among others, as third party proposals). 
Third 
party contributions to democratic ideals and progress in 
society are not just coincidence. The major parties often build 
their platforms and appeal to the electorate based on the most 
“mainstream” ideas because most of the votes are in the 
middle. On the other hand, fundamental reform typically 
comes from outside what is already popular and mainstream, 
strongly suggesting that the health and progress of a 
19 

democracy depend on hearing from minority voices. 
If third party candidates must have the support 
equivalent to that of the major party candidates before they 
can gain access to a state sponsored debate, a formidable 
catch-22 exists, preventing the introduction of new ideas in 
society, since in an electronic age, new ideas and new parties 
need broadcast exposure in order to gain popular support. 
See Greenberg 
v. Bolger, 496 F. Supp. 756, 776 (E.D. N.Y. 
1980). 
CONCLUSION 
The standard IPTV claims to have applied in this case, 
“newsworthiness,” was not addressed by the majority decision 
of the Supreme Court in Forbes, although, as set forth above, 
the Court 
has previously, 
in the Regan 
case, 
held 
“newsworthiness” to be an unconstitutional standard. The 
decision of the lower courts is in conflict with the decisions 
in Regan and in Forbes. The Forbes case, which was based 
on an extreme set of facts (a candidate with no public 
support), requires clarification to encourage appropriate 
minority participation in important political debates.
