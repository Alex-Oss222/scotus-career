## 1. Albertson’s, Inc. v. Kirkingburg — 527 U.S. 555 (1999), No. 98-591

**Historical disposition and topology.** June 22, 1999; Ninth Circuit reversed. All nine joined the judgment and Souter’s Parts I and III. Souter’s Part II was joined by Rehnquist, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Ginsburg: seven Justices. Stevens and Breyer did not join Part II; neither filed a separate writing in this case. Thomas joined the Court’s opinion and filed a solo concurrence.

| Writing/source | Historical rationale and material limits |
|---|---|
| [Souter, full opinion, Parts I–III](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-591.ZO.html), 527 U.S. 558–578 | Part II rejects three features of the Ninth Circuit’s disability analysis: a significant difference in how an individual sees is not necessarily a substantial limitation; mitigating measures include the body’s own compensating mechanisms as well as artificial aids; and monocularity requires an individualized assessment. The statutory focus is the limitation of this individual’s major life activity, including depth perception and visual field. The Court did not announce that monocular people ordinarily fall outside the ADA: it agreed they ordinarily would qualify and expressly disclaimed an onerous evidentiary burden. Part II was unnecessary to the ultimate disposition, although within the first granted question. Part III supplies the unanimous dispositive ground: Albertson’s could insist on the binding DOT vision standard without independently defending that standard under the ADA merely because DOT had granted an experimental waiver. The historical 1992 waiver scheme gathered information for possible future changes; it did not embody a demonstrated alternative safety standard that substantively modified the regulation. Congress was not understood to require an employer to justify the Government’s own applicable safety rule anew or participate in the experiment. |
| [Thomas, concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-591.ZC.html), 527 U.S. 578–580; no joins | Thomas preferred the alternative analysis that the plaintiff had not met his burden to establish that he was a qualified individual: lawful interstate truck driving was an essential function, and the DOT rule bound driver and employer alike. Because the experimental program did not modify the underlying requirements, the proposed accommodation would be unreasonable. He nevertheless joined the Court’s approach given the way the case had been litigated, expressly on the understanding that it left open the importance of federal requirements to the threshold qualified-individual inquiry. |

**Reservations that matter to comparison.** The Court did not decide the unchallenged regarded-as ruling, the validity or deference owed the EEOC disability regulations, the Government’s contention that every safety qualification must satisfy the direct-threat test, or the separate regulatory defense in 29 CFR §1630.15(e), which the parties did not invoke. It also expressly did not decide the effect of the later exemption program. The case therefore does not establish an unconditional rule about every administrative waiver or a holding that Kirkingburg himself lacked a disability. See Part II and nn. 9–11; Part III and nn. 15–16, 21.

## 2. Olmstead v. L.C. — 527 U.S. 581 (1999), No. 98-536

**Historical disposition and topology.** June 22, 1999; Eleventh Circuit affirmed in part, vacated in part, and remanded, six to three in judgment. Ginsburg’s Parts I, II, and III-A were joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, and Breyer: five Justices. Part III-B was joined only by O’Connor, Souter, and Breyer: four Justices. Stevens concurred in part and in the judgment. Kennedy concurred in the judgment, with Breyer joining only Part I of Kennedy’s separate opinion. Thomas dissented, joined by Rehnquist and Scalia. Kennedy did not join Ginsburg’s opinion; Breyer’s limited join of Kennedy does not subtract his join of Ginsburg.

| Writing/source | Historical rationale and material limits |
|---|---|
| [Ginsburg, Parts I, II, III-A and III-B](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-536.ZO.html), 527 U.S. 587–607 | The five-Justice opinion treats unjustified institutional isolation as discrimination under Title II. Congress’s findings expressly identified segregation and institutionalization, and unjustified isolation both perpetuates assumptions of incapacity and diminishes ordinary life activities. Qualified people should not have to relinquish community life to obtain needed services when reasonable accommodation permits otherwise. The Justice Department’s consistent, reasoned interpretation warrants respect; the Court did not decide whether full Chevron deference applied or validate the regulations against a challenge not presented. Community placement is appropriate when state treatment professionals reasonably find it suitable, the person does not oppose it, and it can be reasonably accommodated considering state resources and the needs of others with mental disabilities. The four-Justice Part III-B rejects a cost defense confined to comparing care for these two plaintiffs with the entire mental-health budget: the State must have room to consider unavoidable institutional overhead, its range of services, and equitable allocation among a large and diverse population. A comprehensive, effectively working community-placement plan and a waiting list moving at a reasonable pace, not controlled by an effort to keep institutions full, are given as an example satisfying reasonable modification; litigants would not thereby jump a properly administered queue. |
| [Stevens, concurrence in part and judgment](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-536.ZC.html), 527 U.S. 607–608; no joins | Stevens accepted the discrimination holding and the availability of a fundamental-alteration affirmative defense. He would simply affirm because the Eleventh Circuit had already remanded for that defense; any error in the subsequent district-court decision should be addressed in review of that decision. Because five votes were unavailable for simple affirmance, he joined the Court’s judgment and Parts I, II, and III-A, not Part III-B. |
| [Kennedy, concurrence in judgment](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-536.ZC1.html), 527 U.S. 608–615; Breyer joins Part I only | Part I stresses the danger of inappropriate deinstitutionalization, the limits of available resources, great deference to responsible treating physicians, and appropriate deference to state funding decisions. Part II, Kennedy alone, requires a showing of unjustified differential treatment of a comparable group absent animus or stereotypes. A claim might exist if Georgia ordinarily provides comparably serious medical treatment in an integrated setting but withholds it from mentally disabled people without adequate justification. He would remand to determine whether the pleadings and evidence establish such a claim, permitting amendment if appropriate. He rejects requiring a State to create a community-treatment program where none exists and permits substantial discretion in fixed-cost and overhead accounting. He did not regard discrimination as already established on this summary-judgment record. |
| [Thomas, dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-536.ZD.html), 527 U.S. 615–626; Rehnquist and Scalia join | Thomas reads discrimination in its conventional comparative sense, requiring differential treatment on the protected ground, rather than inadequate services for an individual member of a protected group. He relies on Title VII and Rehabilitation Act precedents, statutory contrasts with Title I, and federalism concerns. In his view, broad congressional findings cannot replace §12132’s operative terms, the integration regulation cannot contradict those terms, and the record shows limited resources rather than exclusion proximately caused by disability. He objects that the majority creates a standard-of-care obligation without an adequate limiting principle. |

**Reservations that matter to comparison.** The historical judgment does not make Part III-B a five-Justice opinion. The decision resolves statutory claims only; the constitutional claims were not reached. It neither orders universal deinstitutionalization nor requires placement against a person’s wishes, inappropriate discharge, a uniform medical standard of care, or a particular level of benefits. The majority’s Part III-A explains that States generally may rely on reasonable professional assessments; n. 14 distinguishes nondiscrimination in services actually provided from a general entitlement to benefits. Kennedy’s separate comparative-treatment theory is not the five-Justice rationale.

## 3. Murphy v. United Parcel Service, Inc. — 527 U.S. 516 (1999), No. 97-1992

**Historical disposition and topology.** June 22, 1999; Tenth Circuit affirmed, seven to two. O’Connor wrote for Rehnquist, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg as well as herself. Stevens dissented, joined by Breyer. There are no partial joins or other writings.

| Writing/source | Historical rationale and material limits |
|---|---|
| [O’Connor, Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/97-1992.ZO.html), 527 U.S. 518–525 | Part II applies Sutton’s rule that mitigating measures are considered. The lower court’s conclusion that Murphy, when medicated, was not substantially limited was outside the grant, so the Court did not decide persistent limitations or medication side effects. Part III holds that the evidence of perceived inability to obtain DOT certification did not create a genuine issue that UPS regarded Murphy as substantially limited in working. Assuming the EEOC regulations valid, exclusion must extend to a class or broad range of jobs. The record showed, at most, exclusion from mechanic work requiring commercial motor vehicle driving; Murphy remained employable in numerous other mechanic jobs using his skills. |
| [Stevens, dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/97-1992.ZD.html), 527 U.S. 525; Breyer joins | Stevens incorporated his Sutton dissent and reasoned that Murphy’s severe unmedicated hypertension, on the uncontested evidence, substantially limited several major life activities and would likely require hospitalization. This lay within the ADA’s core coverage. He would reverse and remand because qualification and ability to perform essential functions had not been resolved below. |

**Completeness correction to the short summary.** This was not a decision that Murphy actually failed every DOT certification route. His retest readings were sufficient for optional temporary certification, and the opinion notes UPS apparently prevented an attempt to obtain it. The Court expressly left actual qualification and any DOT-based defense unresolved because those issues were neither decided below nor within the petition. It also did not hold that a commercial-driving disqualification can never exclude a class of jobs; its regarded-as determination rested on this record and Murphy’s mechanical skills.

## 4. Kolstad v. American Dental Association — 527 U.S. 526 (1999), No. 98-208

**Historical disposition and topology.** June 22, 1999; D.C. Circuit vacated and remanded. O’Connor’s Part I was unanimous. Part II-A was joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer: seven with O’Connor. Part II-B was joined by Rehnquist, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas: five with O’Connor. Rehnquist’s concurrence in part/dissent in part was joined by Thomas; Stevens’s concurrence in part/dissent in part was joined by Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. The approved comparator correctly preserves the different coalitions rather than describing a single undifferentiated merits count.

| Writing/source | Historical rationale and material limits |
|---|---|
| [O’Connor, Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-208.ZO.html), 527 U.S. 529–547 | Part II-A rejects an independent egregiousness requirement. Section 1981a establishes a higher mental-state threshold for punitive damages than intentional discrimination alone: the employer must at least discriminate despite a perceived risk of violating federal rights. Egregious conduct can prove that mental state but is not an additional element. Smith v. Wade and Hazen Paper support the distinction. Punitive liability need not follow when an employer is unaware of the prohibition, reasonably believes an exception applies, or acts under a novel or poorly recognized liability theory. Part II-B separately requires imputation to the employer under agency principles, using Restatement §217C as a starting point: authorization; reckless employment of an unfit agent; a managerial agent acting within scope; or ratification. Managerial capacity depends on authority and discretion, not simply a title or top-management status. Because ordinary scope rules would impose punitive liability even for forbidden acts despite genuine compliance efforts, the Court modifies those rules to preserve Title VII’s preventive incentives: an employer is not vicariously liable in punitive damages for managerial employment decisions contrary to its good-faith efforts to comply with Title VII. The Court regarded this issue as fairly subsumed in the granted question. It left factual application, mental state, managerial status, and compliance efforts for remand. |
| [Rehnquist, concurrence in part/dissent in part](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-208.ZX.html), 527 U.S. 547; Thomas joins | Rehnquist would retain an egregiousness requirement, adopting Judge Randolph’s view that Congress’s two-tier scheme reserves punitive damages for the worst intentional discrimination. Given the Court’s contrary resolution, he joined Part I and the Part II-B agency-law limitations, described in his writing as a significant restriction and sometimes a complete bar to punitive liability. |
| [Stevens, concurrence in part/dissent in part](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-208.ZX1.html), 527 U.S. 547–554; Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer join | Stevens joins Parts I and II-A and agrees that the additional punitive threshold concerns knowing or reckless violation of federal law, not independently outrageous conduct. Drawing inferences in Kolstad’s favor, he views the evidence of a sham selection, pretext, sex-based remarks, and senior decisionmakers as sufficient for a punitive-damages trial. He objects to deciding unbriefed agency limitations which the parties and lower courts had not treated as dispositive and which respondent had said were not before the Court. He would remand directly for trial on punitive damages instead of reopening imputation and compliance issues. |

**Source-text correction.** Cornell’s Rehnquist page omits the words “Part I and” before “that portion of Part II-B.” The final official reporter at p. 547 includes them, consistently with the reporter’s unanimous-Part-I lineup. The final reporter controls this detail. The supplied comparator already has the correct Part-I lineup and requires no alteration. Cornell uses `ZX` and `ZX1`, not the usual concurrence/dissent URL suffixes, for these mixed writings.

## 5. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v. College Savings Bank — 527 U.S. 627 (1999), No. 98-531

**Historical disposition and topology.** June 23, 1999; Federal Circuit reversed and remanded, five to four. Rehnquist wrote for O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas as well as himself. Stevens dissented, joined by Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. No partial joins or additional writings.

| Writing/source | Historical rationale and material limits |
|---|---|
| [Rehnquist, Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-531.ZO.html), 527 U.S. 630–648 | The Patent Remedy Act unmistakably purported to abrogate immunity, but Seminole Tribe foreclosed Article I’s Commerce and Patent Clauses as sources of that power. Valid §5 enforcement legislation could abrogate immunity, and patents are property protected by due process even though federal law creates them. Under City of Boerne, however, Congress identified little state infringement and no substantial pattern of constitutional deprivation. Patent infringement alone is not a due-process violation: remedies must be absent or inadequate, and negligent injury is not a constitutional deprivation. Congress barely examined state remedies and principally sought federal uniformity and equal treatment of public and private infringers, Article I objectives. The Act swept indefinitely across all States and all infringement, including innocent infringement and situations with adequate remedies, without tailoring to arguable constitutional violations. That breadth was not congruent and proportional to the scant supported constitutional injury. Constructive waiver was foreclosed by the companion College Savings Bank decision. |
| [Stevens, dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-531.ZD.html), 527 U.S. 648–665; Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer join | Stevens stresses national uniformity, exclusive federal patent jurisdiction, and Federal Circuit expertise. The suit alleged willful infringement; it was inappropriate to invalidate Congress’s response by demanding later-developed findings about negligent or remedied infringements. Congress had evidence of uncertain or ineffective state remedies and could anticipate future infringement; preemption of state patent jurisdiction reinforced those concerns. Unlike RFRA in Boerne, the Act changed no substantive constitutional definition or state regulatory rule, but removed immunity for a narrow existing federal cause of action. Its operation would grow only with the infringement problem, making prevention proportionate whether violations were rare or frequent. He also maintained his objections to Seminole Tribe and the expansion of sovereign immunity, and in n. 16 endorsed waiver reasoning with particular force for a State invoking the patent system’s benefits. |

**Reservations and source precision.** The majority did not hold patents outside Fourteenth Amendment property. It did not adjudicate infringement or the adequacy of a particular Florida remedy. It declined the Just Compensation Clause basis because Congress had not invoked it in the statute or reports, n. 7. Cornell mistakenly attributes the argument against protecting federally created property to “College Savings”; the final reporter at p. 642 attributes it correctly to **Florida Prepaid**. This correction affects attribution, not the supplied comparator outcome or topology.

## 6. College Savings Bank v. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board — 527 U.S. 666 (1999), No. 98-149

**Historical disposition and topology.** June 23, 1999; Third Circuit affirmed, five to four. Scalia wrote for Rehnquist, O’Connor, Kennedy, and Thomas as well as himself. Stevens filed a solo dissent and also joined Breyer’s dissent with Souter and Ginsburg. There are no partial joins.

| Writing/source | Historical rationale and material limits |
|---|---|
| [Scalia, Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-149.ZO.html), 527 U.S. 668–691 | The Trademark Remedy Clarification Act did not validly abrogate immunity for this Lanham Act false-advertising claim and Florida did not voluntarily waive immunity. Part III invokes Seminole Tribe’s Article I limit but separately rejects the §5 basis: neither freedom from a competitor’s false statements about its own product nor the general activity of doing business or making profits is protected property. Existing business assets, including goodwill, are property, but these allegations did not take such an asset or intrude on an exclusive right. Without a protected deprivation, the Court did not reach the further Boerne question about necessity of prophylactic abrogation. Part IV expressly overrules whatever remained of Parden’s constructive-waiver doctrine. A congressional notice that commercial activity will be deemed consent is not the State’s unequivocal voluntary consent. Treating otherwise lawful participation as waiver would circumvent Seminole Tribe; profitability, traditional private activity, and a market-participant characterization do not remove constitutional immunity. Express consent and voluntarily invoking federal jurisdiction remain possible. Conditions attached to federal grants or congressional approval of interstate compacts are distinguished as offered benefits, subject to the funding-coercion qualification; excluding a State from otherwise lawful activity for refusing waiver is treated as coercion. |
| [Stevens, dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-149.ZD.html), 527 U.S. 691–693; no joins | Stevens joins Breyer and adds that the arm-of-the-State assumption may deserve closer scrutiny for commercial enterprises, suggesting the foreign-sovereign commercial-activity model for future cases. He views doing business and its goodwill as property, with deliberate destruction of a business a deprivation. The validity of prophylactic §5 legislation depends on Congress’s reasonable basis for addressing a category of violations, not whether this particular pleaded act independently violates the Constitution. He also maintains that Union Gas correctly recognized Article I abrogation power. |
| [Breyer, dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-149.ZD1.html), 527 U.S. 693–705; Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg join | Breyer would preserve Parden for clear federal conditions on voluntarily undertaken commercial activity from which a State can readily withdraw. Private competitors need equal regulatory enforcement; the State’s immunity interest is weaker outside core functions. He reads post-Parden cases as principally narrowing required clarity, not rejecting congressional power, and finds analogies in compacts, grants, bankruptcy participation, and commercial exceptions to foreign immunity. The majority’s distinction between withholding benefits and regulating market entry is unpersuasive when grants may exert more pressure. Independently, he rejects Seminole Tribe: constitutional text and history do not prohibit Article I abrogation in federal-question cases, immunity is a defeasible common-law doctrine, and valid federal substantive rights may need private remedies. He emphasizes legislative flexibility, local participation, and the practical inadequacy of replacing private enforcement with a federal bureaucracy or rewritten funding conditions. He would reverse the threshold dismissal without deciding whether the advertising allegations were true. |

**Material completeness addition to the approved summary.** The §5/property ground is indispensable and distinct from the companion patent case, which recognizes patent property and proceeds to congruence and proportionality. This false-advertising decision does not hold trademarks or business assets categorically outside property protection, and does not decide the validity of the TRCA for every possible Lanham Act claim. It preserves genuinely voluntary waiver; it rejects constructive waiver from otherwise lawful commercial participation.

## 7. Alden v. Maine — 527 U.S. 706 (1999), No. 98-436

**Historical disposition and topology.** June 23, 1999; Maine Supreme Judicial Court affirmed, five to four. Kennedy wrote for Rehnquist, O’Connor, Scalia, and Thomas as well as himself. Souter dissented, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer. No partial joins or other writings.

| Writing/source | Historical rationale and material limits |
|---|---|
| [Kennedy, Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-436.ZO.html), 527 U.S. 711–760 | Article I powers do not permit Congress to subject nonconsenting States to private damages suits in their own courts. Part I treats state immunity as a retained constitutional attribute of sovereignty, evidenced rather than created or exhausted by the Eleventh Amendment. English and founding practice, ratification assurances, the response to Chisholm, Hans, and later cases supply the historical argument. Part II rejects reliance on the Supremacy and Necessary and Proper Clauses to override that retained immunity and finds no compelling evidence of surrender in the constitutional plan. Early congressional practice, precedent, state dignity, fiscal and political accountability, and the distinct position of state courts reinforce the conclusion. The power to require state courts to hear ordinary federal claims does not authorize a private action against the nonconsenting State itself. Hilton is confined to its stare decisis and presumed-consent setting; Hall concerns suit in another State’s courts; Reich concerns a constitutionally required promised tax remedy; Howlett concerns an entity without the State’s constitutional immunity. Part IV holds that Maine did not waive immunity and that the record showed no systematic manipulation of immunity to discriminate against federal causes of action. |
| [Souter, dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-436.ZD.html), 527 U.S. 760–814; Stevens, Ginsburg, Breyer join | Souter rejects constitutionalizing state immunity from Article I claims in the State’s own courts. His historical survey distinguishes common-law immunity, defeasible by legislation, from natural-law accounts; colonial charters, state practices, ratification debates, and all Chisholm opinions reveal diverse understandings, not an immutable founding consensus or Tenth Amendment incorporation. Even a natural-law account limiting suit against the maker of the governing law cannot protect Maine from a right created by the national sovereign. Under divided sovereignty, Maine is not sovereign over matters validly regulated by Congress; Garcia permits the FLSA to bind state employers, and the Supremacy Clause requires general-jurisdiction state courts to hear the federal cause. State dignity and budget priorities cannot displace federal rights. The lack of earlier state-court suits reflects former limits on substantive commerce regulation rather than a timeless remedial prohibition. He also disputes the majority’s distinctions of Hilton and related precedents. Eliminating private enforcement undermines Garcia in practice; federal enforcement resources are insufficient, and the traditional connection between a legal right and a remedy supports allowing the employees’ action. |

**Reservations that matter to comparison.** The majority does not excuse noncompliance with valid federal substantive law and does not overrule Garcia. Part III preserves consent, suits by the United States or another State, appropriate Fourteenth Amendment §5 legislation, claims against municipal or other non-arm entities, certain prospective officer suits, and individual-capacity damages claims payable personally. Its treatment of an action prosecuted by the United States depends on political responsibility for bringing that action, not mere federal participation in defense of a statute. The FLSA expressly authorized federal enforcement, which was not the suit before the Court. The opinion’s references to other decisions are historical predicates to be compared with effective simulated law, never silently imported into that law.

## 8. Ortiz v. Fibreboard Corp. — 527 U.S. 815 (1999), No. 97-1704

**Historical disposition and topology.** June 23, 1999; Fifth Circuit reversed and remanded, seven to two. Souter wrote for Rehnquist, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Ginsburg as well as himself. Rehnquist concurred, joined by Scalia and Kennedy. Breyer dissented, joined by Stevens. All majority joins are complete, despite the additional concurrence.

| Writing/source | Historical rationale and material limits |
|---|---|
| [Souter, Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/97-1704.ZO.html), 527 U.S. 821–865 | Applicants for contested mandatory limited-fund settlement certification under Rule 23(b)(1)(B) must demonstrate a fund limited independently of their agreement and allocate it through a process protecting conflicting claimant interests. Traditional limited-fund actions feature (1) an ascertained maximum fund insufficient for the aggregate claims, (2) dedication of the entire fund to those claims, and (3) equitable treatment of all claimants sharing the recovery theory. Those characteristics are presumptively necessary; proponents must justify departures. Rule history, the Rules Enabling Act, and constitutional avoidance counsel caution, particularly over absent claimants’ jury and due-process interests. Rule 23(e) fairness does not replace the separate safeguards in Rules 23(a) and (b). Here the insurance limit lacked independent evidentiary valuation; it was taken from agreements negotiated amid conflicts because counsel represented inventory claimants whose full recoveries depended on a global settlement or insurance resolution. The class excluded substantial claimants with unsatisfied claims during the settlement negotiations. Present and future claimants, and pre-1959 insured versus later uninsured claimants, required separate structural representation; equal nominal treatment itself allocated value and did not erase their conflicts. Settlement urgency and likely cost savings did not excuse those defects. |
| [Rehnquist, concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/97-1704.ZC.html), 527 U.S. 865; Scalia and Kennedy join | Rehnquist agreed that, on a clean slate, Breyer’s solution and the district court’s efforts would have strong practical appeal. He nevertheless joined the Court because the existing Federal Rules controlled, and called for legislative resolution of asbestos litigation. Practical sympathy is not a narrower or judgment-only join. |
| [Breyer, dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/97-1704.ZD.html), 527 U.S. 865–884; Stevens joins | Breyer would defer substantially to the district court’s detailed findings and the Fifth Circuit’s affirmance in an extraordinary mass-tort setting where nominal individual litigation meant delay, high costs, and likely unpaid claims. A fund’s present value can be limited by the risk of an imminent adverse coverage decision. He considered the separate Trilateral negotiation between Fibreboard and the insurers an adequate arm’s-length benchmark, and regarded precision beyond that realistically obtainable before the coverage decision as unnecessary. He accepted the findings that qualified conflict-free counsel could not be obtained in time and that common interests, settlement terms, and circumstances adequately addressed the asserted subgroup conflicts. He also viewed transaction-cost savings exceeding Fibreboard’s retained value as substantial compliance with the whole-fund purpose. He would uphold limited-fund certification. His final part states that respondents would likely prevail on other issues, but expressly does not definitively decide standing, notice, other Rule 23 requirements, or Rules Enabling Act, bankruptcy, jury-trial, and due-process challenges. |

**Reservations that matter to comparison.** The Court treated certification as logically antecedent to the asserted Article III defects; it did not finally decide them. It did not hold that a mandatory mass-tort limited-fund class is always impossible, select between competing probability standards for fund exhaustion, settle the general notice requirement or sufficiency of this notice, or resolve the state choice-of-law questions. Fibreboard’s retention of almost all its net worth raised a third serious defect, but the Court expressly did not decide whether retention alone was fatal or whether a credit for transaction-cost savings might ever be allowed. It did not resolve how close to insolvency a defendant must be brought. An alternative insurance-dispute certification rationale was also foreclosed by existing class conflicts; the possibility of a differently structured later class was left open. The separate judgment approving the Trilateral settlement was final and not before the Court (n. 8). These are limits on the historical decision, not proposed procedural premises for the simulation.
