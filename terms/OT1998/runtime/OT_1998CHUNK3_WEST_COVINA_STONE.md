# City of West Covina v. Perkins

# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Approval Status:** Approved.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** September 28, 1998; no prepared OT1998 position is a completed Court decision.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse the Ninth Circuit's judgment and remand with directions to reject the federal individualized-instruction theory.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** The lower courts shall reject the claim that due process invariably requires personalized legal instructions describing California's published return procedures. Any preserved challenge to the adequacy, accessibility, obstruction, or operation of those procedures remains open on remand.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Content of post-seizure notice

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches only the individualized-instruction theory presented by the judgment below.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Due process requires timely notice identifying the seized property and responsible custodian, but it does not require personalized legal instruction concerning a published, generally available state-law procedure. *Good* is materially different because it addressed whether process must precede governmental occupation of real property.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** When the government gives timely notice identifying seized property and the responsible custodian, due process does not also require personalized instructions describing a published, generally available state-law return procedure. The judgment requiring that additional notice must be reversed.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Perkins knew what was seized and which agency held it. On the presented record, a personalized legal guide adds little protection against error, while the actual adequacy and accessibility of California's procedure were not resolved and cannot be assumed either way.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not approve an inadequate, concealed, misleading, obstructed, or practically inaccessible remedy; unreasonable retention; failure to identify the seizure or custodian; or an unlawful initial seizure.
