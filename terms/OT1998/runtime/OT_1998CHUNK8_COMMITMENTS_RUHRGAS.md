## Common disposition and sufficient rule

All eight Associates would reverse the en banc Fifth Circuit's categorical requirement that subject-matter jurisdiction invariably precede a personal-jurisdiction dismissal in a removed action, and remand for further appellate proceedings consistent with the permitted sequencing choice. They would not conclusively find any asserted removal ground sufficient or insufficient, affirm every district-court contact determination, or direct a state-court remand on this record.

A federal court ordinarily addresses a readily resolved subject-matter objection first. It may instead dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction when that nonmerits question is straightforward and presents no difficult state-law issue while subject-matter jurisdiction raises a difficult question. Such a dismissal declines judicial authority over the defendant rather than exercising disputed authority to decide liability. The choice must respect an actually applicable statutory remand command and covered-remand review bar; convenience permits no merits ruling on assumed Article III power. Difficulty of personal jurisdiction, simplicity of subject-matter jurisdiction, or a substantial state-law issue can require the ordinary order. This is permission in the stated circumstances, not a command always to decide personal jurisdiction first.

The Court's bounded appellate task is to remove the erroneous mandatory sequence. The en banc court withheld any adoption of its vacated panel's resolution of the three removal grounds. Those grounds therefore remain undecided. The current record supports the availability of a personal-jurisdiction-first route, but does not make the accuracy of the District Court's disputed contact findings a new Supreme Court holding. The Court of Appeals can conduct the review still properly before it under the correct rule.

## Enhanced comparison

**Subject-matter-first path:** Retain the en banc direction because a federal court should establish its authority and protect the state forum before issuing a dismissal that could have consequences elsewhere. Its strongest support is the jurisdiction-before-merits requirement in Steel Co. and Jones and the statutory remand obligation when absence of subject-matter jurisdiction is established. Its defect is the proposed extension from contested merits to a second adjudicatory threshold. No supplied statute establishes that universal hierarchy, and no covered district-court state-remand order is under review. The possible effect of a personal-jurisdiction dismissal does not turn it into a decision on tort liability.

**Confined threshold-choice path:** Permit the easier nonmerits personal-jurisdiction disposition while preserving statutory duties and ordinary subject-matter-first practice. The strongest objection is that an amorphous efficiency exception could license deciding merits or displacing difficult state-law questions. The stated limits answer that objection: the alternative itself must deny adjudicatory authority, must not require difficult state-law resolution, and cannot disregard an applicable statutory command. The actual validity of any removal ground or contact analysis is left for the appropriate court. All eight choose this path at that scope.
