## United States v. Rodriguez-Moreno — March 30, 1999

### Reach, entering law and alternatives

Certiorari review reaches the Third Circuit's venue ruling on the charged use-or-carriage count under the applicable §924(c). The continuing kidnapping passed through New Jersey; firearm use against the victim occurred in Maryland. This handoff does not find a separate New Jersey gun-use or carrying act, add an aiding-and-abetting or conspiracy theory, or apply a later possession amendment. The other convictions are not adjudicated.

Article III, the Sixth Amendment, Rule 18 and §3237(a) require venue for the charged offense. Cabrales and the Standard **Venue for completed money-laundering transactions** (effective June 1, 1998) distinguish the offense's conduct from the prior criminal origin of its proceeds. They reserve different actual-conduct allegations and do not enact a verb-counting test. Smith preserves distinct firearm conduct and crime-relatedness requirements; Bailey and Muscarello maintain that distinction without deciding this venue question.

**R1 — incorporated continuing conduct; reverse.** Commission of the ongoing kidnapping is essential conduct of the §924(c) offense because the firearm conduct must occur during and in relation to that crime. Where the charged kidnapping continues, the incorporated conduct occurs; §3237(a) permits trial there even though the firearm is used in another district. The strongest objection is that treating an indispensable predicate as venue conduct could undo Cabrales. The answer is confined to the defendant's continuing criminal conduct constituting the specified predicate, not earlier criminal provenance, remote effects or the mere presence of a necessary circumstance. Proof of kidnapping never substitutes for proof of firearm use or carriage.

**R2 — firearm conduct locates this offense; affirm.** Section 924(c) separately punishes using or carrying the firearm in the specified criminal setting. The kidnapping supplies the qualifying relation but is not itself the firearm conduct charged. On this reading, §3237(a) cannot move a firearm offense to a district lacking its charged conduct merely because a separately punishable kidnapping continues there. The strongest objection is that the statute incorporates an ongoing crime rather than Cabrales's completed antecedent crime. The answer is that temporal overlap and relational importance do not alone convert predicate conduct into conduct constituting this distinct offense. This path is statutory and constitutional venue analysis, not an assertion that only statutory verbs can identify conduct.

## Wyoming v. Houghton — April 5, 1999

### Reach, entering law and alternatives

Review concerns the automobile exception's scope, not the conceded vehicle-level probable cause. The purse was found in the rear seat and identified as Houghton's before the first intrusion. Her false-name discrepancy was discovered after the wallet was opened. Later contents, disclaimers and track observations cannot supply facts retroactively for the first opening. No consent, abandonment, inventory, protective search, independent source or inevitable discovery finding is invented.

**Labron's actual operation:** Its July 1, 1996 rule authorizes a probable-cause search of a readily mobile automobile without additional exigency, reaching places and containers capable of holding the object sought. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer joined that rule. Stevens and Ginsburg's objections concerned review, not an adopted contrary merits rule. All eight joined Wellons's separate closed-luggage privacy and valid-exception distinction. The Standard **Privacy in a rented vehicle and personal luggage** (May 30, 1995) preserves that distinction. Wellons neither creates an ownership exception nor revives Sanders. Knowles and the Standards governing initial stops and passenger-exit directions authorize no general search from a stop or citation alone. Their independent probable-cause grounds remain available.

**H1 — apply object-based scope; reverse.** Conceded probable cause to search the car for drugs reaches this closed purse, a container found in the car capable of concealing those drugs, notwithstanding its known passenger ownership. A protected privacy interest and a justified search can coexist. The strongest objection is the identified nonsuspect passenger's personal privacy and Di Re's refusal to transfer vehicle search power to the passenger's body. The answer is that this purse on the rear seat is a container within the vehicle search, not the passenger's person. Ownership alone does not remove a possible hiding place from otherwise established vehicle probable cause. No presumption that Houghton was guilty or working with the driver is necessary.

**H2 — adopt a limited passenger-effects qualification; affirm.** When vehicle probable cause arises from another occupant and police know before intrusion that a closed personal effect belongs to a passenger, that probable cause alone should not authorize opening the effect absent probable cause connecting it to the contraband or another independently applicable search ground. The strongest objection is that Labron's current object-based scope, Ross and Acevedo encompass capable containers without an ownership condition. H2 therefore candidly proposes a new qualification of that scope. Wellons establishes the privacy interest to be protected, not the proposed exemption; Di Re supplies an analogy to personal protection, not a holding equating every purse with a body. The qualification would leave ordinary vehicle searches, unassociated vehicle containers and independently justified passenger-effect searches available; it would not revive a universal automobile-luggage warrant requirement.

## Barnes v. American Tobacco Co. — April 7, 1999

### Reach, entering law and alternatives

The authorized post-grant merits question concerns certification of this Pennsylvania smokers' medical-monitoring proposal under Rule 23(b)(2). The class ruling and the individual judgments are distinct. Five named claims have separate limitations dispositions; the sixth has a lack-of-monitoring-need disposition. No individual judgment's merits, waiver, reversal or revival is supplied by the granted class question. The review does not create a new representative or eliminate the actual parties' review posture.

Rule 23(a)'s actual prerequisites and (b)(2)'s requirement of generally applicable conduct making final injunctive or corresponding declaratory relief appropriate for the class govern. The Standards **Certification of multistate damages classes** (Castano, January 14, 1997; Rhone-Poulenc's stated component, February 26, 1997) and **Certification of settlement-only classes** (Amchem, June 25, 1997) retain their scopes. Neither imports (b)(3) predominance or superiority into (b)(2). Castano's unappealed (b)(2) denial supplies no Supreme Court decision on medical monitoring. Amchem's actual-prerequisite and representation rules remain relevant; its settlement-specific (b)(3) application is not this case's test.

**B1 — affirm the certification denial on its supported (b)(2) ground.** Common allegations of tobacco-company conduct and a common desire for surveillance do not themselves show that final monitoring relief operates appropriately for this proposed class. On the supplied record, exposure, addiction-related matters, causation, monitoring need and limitations differ in ways affecting entitlement to the requested relief and preservation of defenses. Those are relevant because the undivided monitoring proposal requires answers about who qualifies for it and on what basis, not because the Court counts individual issues or compares them numerically with common issues. No common allegation is converted into an established medical finding. The certification proponents have not demonstrated that the described proposal satisfies the actual classwide-relief requirement.

**B2 — vacate and remand for a proper (b)(2) inquiry.** The strongest case for remand is that a genuinely common monitoring program could remain classwide equitable relief despite individualized eligibility or implementation, and a court must not disguise (b)(3) predominance as a universal (b)(2) rule. Amchem's application cannot answer that question. The authenticated recitation nevertheless identifies individualized entitlement and defense grounds for denying this proposal, rather than only difficult trial management or a numerical comparison of questions. Those actual grounds support B1's limited conclusion. Missing program details are not treated as proof that no program was filed or considered. B1 rejects any categorical proposition that individual differences always defeat (b)(2), and leaves a materially supported different proposal open.

The record is sufficient for B1's limited certification conclusion. It is not sufficient to formulate all Pennsylvania monitoring elements, find particular medical needs or expert deficiencies, decide the named claims, prescribe subclasses, or approve a new program. None of those additional determinations supplies B1's ground. Amchem permits a logically antecedent nonmerits class defect to be resolved without assuming jurisdiction over individual substantive claims; it supplies no permission to decide hypothetical merits or disregard an established live-case defect. No such additional jurisdictional defect is established by the packet.
