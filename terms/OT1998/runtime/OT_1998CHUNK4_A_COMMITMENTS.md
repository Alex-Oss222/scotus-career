## 1. Citizens for a Constitutional Convention v. Yoshina — No. 98-671 — January 21, 1999

### Reachability and live controversy

**Commitment of every Associate Justice:** Reach the substantial federal claims and affirm the rejection of constitutional relief, subject to the precise grounds below. Do not dismiss the whole case as moot merely because the November 1998 question failed. The surviving request to give legal effect to the asserted 1996 approval is concrete and expressly maintained in the December supplement and reply. Act 131 submitted another question; it did not expressly annul an entitlement resulting from the earlier vote. A prospective direction to officials to recognize a federally protected result is a legally intelligible remedial request, even though these plaintiffs cannot establish that entitlement on the merits. Church of Scientology separates possible meaningful relief from ultimate entitlement; Steel Co., NAACP and Paradise prohibit converting predicted failure of a substantial claim into jurisdictional dismissal. No Justice bases continued jurisdiction on fees alone, an invented damages claim or an unverified post-December collateral ruling.

The federal plaintiffs were not parties to the state original action. Amicus participation of organizations does not turn them into state-court losing parties. Rooker-Feldman therefore does not bar their own federal claim. The state supreme court's interpretation of state law remains authoritative: the federal court considers the constitutional consequence of applying that interpretation, not an independent appeal correcting Hawaii law. The requested future implementation of the claimed federal right against responsible officials is distinct from retrospective damages or a Pennhurst direction to obey a different judicial construction of state law. This threshold conclusion does not decide that any particular certification order would be permissible after a proved violation.

**Adversarial paths.** A mootness path has real force because a fully informed later referendum decisively rejected a convention and a new-election request ordinarily has nothing further to obtain. Its defect as a complete disposition is the separate 1996 certification request: a later defeat does not by itself resolve whether earlier approval created a continuing entitlement. A merits path preserves that distinction but must avoid adjudicating state law under a federal label. The bounded federal claim and official-capacity prospective request permit that path; failure to prove a constitutional alteration of the earlier vote defeats relief on the merits. No mootness-based vacatur follows. No Justice orders a third referendum or treats the 1998 outcome as proof about the 1996 electorate.

### Components and sufficient grounds

**Y1 — Due process and retrospective election administration.** Affirm rejection of this claim. Federal protection against fundamentally unfair administration is not confined to rules a state court lacked power to adopt, and legal foreseeability alone does not answer official reliance. But the supplied record does not establish a pervasive frustration of voting that warrants setting aside the 1996 legal result or certifying the proposal as approved. The polling-place sheet did not state that blanks were excluded. The contrary fact sheet and statements to proponents matter, but their limited distribution, the difference between campaigning reliance and a voter's inability to cast an effective choice, the ready yes/no options, and counting and reporting every submitted ballot do not establish the election-wide deprivation asserted. The legal effect of abstention in the denominator is not itself rejection of a ballot expressing an affirmative choice.

This conclusion neither finds that no individual relied nor adopts the lower court's assertion that abstainers simply did not care. Griffin's invalidation of a substantial body of ballots cast through the officially invited absentee procedure illustrates a materially different injury; Roe likewise involved a retrospective validation rule with concrete consequences for voters using or foregoing absentee voting. These lower-court cases are persuasive illustrations, not binding Supreme Court tests. Burdick requires the actual burden and the State's precise interests to be assessed. Hawaii's general requirement of affirmative support among those submitting ballots serves the legitimate decision whether to convene a constitution-making body; the state court's authoritative distinction between the convention provision and ratification provisions is not facially arbitrary. The federal record does not turn the officials' pre-election interpretation into a binding amendment of the state Constitution.

**Y2 — First Amendment and abstention.** Affirm rejection independently of Y1. A neutral approval threshold may give a blank a consequence in the denominator without forcing the voter to espouse opposition. Blanks remained blanks in the published count. Burdick's rejection of a general expressive-ballot entitlement and Gordon's acceptance of nondiscriminatory heightened approval requirements answer this claim. The rule does not compel a public statement, alter the expressed yes/no choices, discriminate among identifiable voters or suppress advocacy outside the ballot. Buckley v. ACLF's actual personal attribution requirement is different. No general permission to attribute a political message to a nonconsenting voter is adopted.

**Y3 — Relief and dependent proceedings.** Affirm the appellate rejection of the federal claims and the vacatur of the new-election injunction. Certification of 1996 approval is denied for lack of the asserted constitutional predicate, not through a new state-law counting rule. The dependent cross-appeal does not produce a separate constitutional entitlement. No disposition of a post-December motion or a new fee amount is inferred; any still-pending collateral matter remains governed by its actual procedural posture and ordinary law. The existing merits judgment is not vacated as moot, and no intervention entitlement is independently adjudicated beyond the supplied scope.

## 2. Census — Nos. 98-404 and 98-564 — January 25, 1999

### Channel, scope and threshold components

The review channel is Pub. L. 105-119 §209(e)(1), with the supplied direct appeals from three-judge final judgments. Section 209 supplies express plan finality, a cause of action and expedition; it does not confer injury. No concrete filing defect is established. NRFU and ICM as used to determine interstate House apportionment are the challenged methods. Postal Vacancy Check sampling, other statistical uses, all possible proxies and every future implementation are outside the merits commitment.

**C1 — Glavin standing.** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer find a sufficiently supported threat to Hofmeister's personal representation to reach the statutory challenge. They do not adopt the District Court's repeated pleading standard or claim of inevitable seat loss. Weber supplies a concrete comparison using Bureau projections and prior measured differential undercounts, predicting a specific Indiana seat effect, rather than merely alleging that somebody might be affected. Hofmeister lives and votes in Indiana. The Government's updated projections, changing methods, overseas-personnel objection and statistical uncertainty seriously qualify the forecast; they do not make an adverse sampling effect on this identified, marginal apportionment position merely an abstract possibility. A prospective challenge requires a substantial threatened personal injury, not a proved eventual allocation or certainty that every component estimate remains constant. The announced plan, its automatic use in apportionment and relief directed at the responsible census officials provide traceability and likely redress. Franklin's actual allocation is a stronger completed showing, not a constitutional requirement to wait until seats are finally assigned. Section 209's defined review point supports timely adjudication only after this independent evidentiary conclusion.

These seven do not need to adjudicate every county-funding or intrastate-redistricting theory. A resident's personal injury is not supplied merely by county funding, and a chain through district boundaries or actual funding formulas needs its own proof. The feared invalid-census/reversion-to-1990 scenario is not an independent ground: it depends on later choices and unproved inability to complete a lawful count.

**Stevens's contrary C1 commitment:** Vacate the Glavin summary judgment and injunction and remand for jurisdictional dismissal without prejudice on the present showing; do not reach contested statutory or constitutional merits in that docket. His broader recognition of concrete interests in Lujan does not eliminate proof at summary judgment. The Indiana prediction combines disputed old population projections and past undercount rates with different planned 2000 methods; the newer projections bear directly on whether Indiana would have the tenth seat even without sampling. The record does not adequately establish that sampling threatens Hofmeister with a particular marginal loss rather than redistributes an unknown allocation. The county theories do not cure the missing causal and personal links merely because census numbers normally influence districting and money. Unlike the specific existing information deprivation in Akins, the alleged injuries depend on unsupported future comparative effects. He does not require mathematical certainty or deny pre-census review categorically.

**C2 — House standing.** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg would vacate the House judgment and injunction and direct dismissal without prejudice for want of the institutional injury asserted. This is the authorized House itself, so Raines's unauthorized individual-plaintiff defect is not imported. Nevertheless, authorization cannot transform disagreement over the legal method for generating a future apportionment into deprivation of particular information or an already impaired institutional power. The House remains a 435-seat body. The dispute concerns which population figures will be lawful, not a refusal to deliver the prescribed report or separately required enumerated-number releases. Akins protects information actually withheld for a concrete use; it does not authorize every statutory recipient to litigate the accuracy or legality of all future official information. The compositional theory additionally concerns later electorates and a successor House, without establishing a present nullification of this House's votes or particular institutional work. These six adopt no universal bar to a House's subpoena-enforcement, property, information or complete-vote-nullification action.

**Stevens and Breyer's contrary C2 commitment:** The House has standing on a combined concrete information-and-institutional-function ground. The present House has a continuing institutional responsibility regarding the apportionment report and lawfully constituted representation, not merely the personal disappointment of dissenting Members. Express authorization identifies the institution and the future information whose lawful preparation it challenges; it reduces the interbranch-representation problem without supplying injury by legislative fiat. Akins supports a concrete statutorily recognized information use, and Raines left an authorized institution distinct. Their own Raines dissents treat actual interference with constitutional work as concrete. A body with legal continuity may protect this function before its next membership is chosen. They do not equate every asserted statutory error with informational injury or claim that the identity of the next House's members is already known.

**Adversarial threshold comparison.** The strongest case against C1 is not the bare futurity of the census but the particular mismatch between the forecast's inputs and the actual planned methods and updated population data. The seven accept the evidence only as a supported threat, with its identified uncertainty, not a finding of the final result. Stevens finds that mismatch defeats the needed showing. The strongest case for C2 is actual institutional authorization and the lawful-information duty. The six reject injury, not authorization; Stevens and Breyer accept the institutional function, not a universal statutory standing grant. Each docket therefore retains its own judgment path.

### Statutory and constitutional components

**C3-P — Statutory prohibition, adopted by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas in Glavin.** Sections 141 and 195, read as one census scheme, prohibit using the challenged statistical sampling to determine population for interstate House apportionment. Section 195 specifically excludes apportionment from the sampling authority/command that otherwise governs carrying out Title 13. Section 141(a)'s express broad census-method language does not erase that subject-specific reservation; §141(b)'s deadline and reporting requirement do not create a contrary sampling grant. The 1976 change to a feasibility-qualified command broadened required use in other census work while retaining the apportionment boundary. This construction preserves a role for each provision rather than making the repeated exception operate only as permission to disregard an otherwise compulsory technique in the very census for which the special restriction was retained.

The contrary textual argument is substantial: an exception from a command ordinarily need not be a prohibition, and §141(a) expressly mentions sampling. These four reject that reading in this particular continuous statutory structure; they do not announce that every exception from a mandate is prohibitory. Nor do they derive a new sampling prohibition from §209, whose disclaimer supplies no substantive authorization. Their decision does not rest on §141(e)(2)'s separate mid-decade bar, turn feasibility into an unconditional duty, adopt a constitutional head-count rule or find that sampling is inaccurate. Affirm the Glavin statutory judgment and its apportionment injunction as applied to the challenged NRFU and ICM methods. Leave the Enumeration Clause question open.

**C3-D — Statutory discretion, adopted by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer in Glavin, and by Stevens and Breyer in the House action.** Section 141(a) expressly authorizes sampling as a census method, and §141(b) directs use of the resulting state tabulation for apportionment. Section 195's exception removes apportionment from the otherwise mandatory, feasibility-qualified sampling direction; it does not say that sampling for apportionment is forbidden. This leaves the exception operative because the Secretary need not use sampling for apportionment even when feasible. The broader census definition and nonapportionment uses explain why Congress imposed the command elsewhere. The special 1997 statute preserves review and preparation for an unsampled census without resolving the underlying permission question. The Secretary has discretion within statutory and constitutional limits, not an obligation to sample and not immunity from review.

The strongest prohibition argument relies on the specific retained exception and the earlier statutory setting. These Justices find it insufficient to convert the enacted negative limitation on a command into a separate ban contrary to §141(a)'s express methods language. They do not treat technical expertise as a substitute for reading the statute. Their own MCI/Smiley positions concern bounded interpretation; Ginsburg's MCI join and concurrence prohibit erasing enacted conditions, which this reading preserves by distinguishing permission and duty.

**C4 — Constitutional question and relief for the discretion path.** These Justices would reverse the statutory prohibition and return the undecided constitutional challenge for consideration under the entering Wisconsin rule on the actual geographic and methodological record. They do not give an unrestricted direction to implement sampling or adjudicate the actual 2000 plan constitutionally valid. Wisconsin unanimously required a reasonable relationship to enumeration and population-based representation but expressly reserved a categorical constitutional sampling prohibition; Franklin rejects a literal census-day physical-presence requirement without deciding this sampling question. The lower courts decided neither the categorical constitutional contention nor this plan's comparative distributional application. A supported administrative account of national improvement is relevant but cannot substitute for examining state and district accuracy, errors and uncertainty at the scale that determines representation. Conversely, block-level percentage error alone establishes no statewide constitutional defect. The confined return preserves both constitutional questions for actual decision; no new evidence, automatic hearing, changed method or guarantee of interim relief is ordered. The statutory injunction cannot remain justified by the reversed ground; any continued temporary protection needs an independently lawful basis in the lower court.

**C5 — Companion consequence.** A final Glavin judgment that actually gives complete enforceable protection against the very apportionment sampling challenged by the House may remove the House's remaining request for prospective relief. Any such mootness disposition must identify the effective protection and apply §2106's equitable disposition to the now-unnecessary House merits judgment; it is not an assumed holding that the House originally had standing. For the six who find original House injury lacking, C2 is the primary independent disposition. Stevens and Breyer would reach House merits on their own positions and adopt C3-D/C4; if assembly instead enters a complete Glavin prohibition, they accept vacatur of the House merits judgment and a nonmerits dismissal of the now-redundant controversy, without joining an adverse original-standing rule. No presidential injunction is necessary or newly approved. The recited House order names the Department, Secretary, Bureau and Director; the actual Glavin order remains separately identified.

## 3. AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Board — eight consolidated dockets — January 25, 1999

### Components, permissible reach and competing paths

The matter includes Nos. 97-826, 97-829, 97-830, 97-831, 97-1075, 97-1087, 97-1099 and 97-1141, and the two lower judgments identified in the packet. The disposition must distinguish the Iowa rule review from California's intraLATA authority and separate numbering-cost ripeness ruling. This handoff makes no merits finding about TELRIC, a confiscatory rate or an unreviewed individual rate. No judicial replacement list of network elements is selected.

**A1 — General implementation power.** Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg would reverse the categorical lack-of-authority premise. Congress added §§251–252 to the Communications Act; §201(b)'s power to make implementing rules applies to those federal obligations. Section 251(d)(1) expressly directs implementation of the requirements, and §252(c)'s duties to apply §251 and the Commission's rules confirm that national standards coexist with state resolution of individual negotiations, arbitrations and rates. Section 152(b) does not remove the local competition provisions from the regulatory authority the amendments themselves create. Louisiana Public Service Commission preserves the prohibition on using a general grant to regulate matters Congress left wholly intrastate; it does not make the newly enacted federal local-competition duties unenforceable by implementing rules. MCI requires an actual statutory delegation; it does not confine every delegation to minor agency action.

**A1-P — Pricing and expressly state-assigned determinations.** O'Connor, Thomas and Breyer would preserve the lower rejection of federal authority to dictate the national rate-setting methodology insofar as it supplants the specific state task under §252(d). They accept federal rules implementing §251's express substantive access duties, but distinguish those duties from setting the detailed framework that governs the state commission's statutory cost determination. The strongest majority argument is that methodology and the actual rate can coexist, and that §201(b) carries through the new Act. Their objection is that the general implementing language cannot make the carefully assigned state pricing function subject to a comprehensive federal rate formula without clearer direction in the specific allocation. Section 152(b)'s retained intrastate protection reinforces this reading; it is not an independent refusal to give §251(d) any effect. They would not decide substantive TELRIC validity even on their own authority disposition.

**A2 — Rural exemption, preexisting agreements, state compliance and §208.** For the five adopting A1, the jurisdictional grounds for vacating Rules 51.405 and 51.303 and displacing otherwise lawful federal rules from state implementation cannot stand. State commissions retain the actual statutory determinations, §251(f)'s exemption predicates, and §§251(d)(3), 252's preservation limits; general federal power does not adjudicate the rules' unresolved substantive objections. Return those objections rather than declaring the burden-of-proof rule, every preexisting agreement or every federal assertion lawful. Section 208's carrier-violation procedure and §252(e)(6)'s review of state determinations must not be conflated. All eight would set aside the lower §208 ruling insofar as it categorically denies FCC complaint authority over a carrier's violation of §251's federal duties: §208 expressly reaches carrier contraventions of the Act, and the new duties are provisions of that Act. That does not authorize the FCC to sit as an appellate tribunal over state commission approval or enforce every agreement as such. The independent statutory-review allocation is retained; a claimed agreement violation must not be equated automatically with an independent statutory violation. No new FCC appellate route over a state determination or private contract-enforcement remedy is created here.

O'Connor, Thomas and Breyer accept binding rules within §251's delegated substantive requirements, while preserving the state-assigned decisional and rate functions. They would not displace the lower protection of state determination under §251(f) or the lower jurisdictional treatment of §252 agreement approval merely by invoking A1. Their objection concerns the identified allocation, not a universal power of States to disregard valid federal law. All eight preserve the specific §208/§252 review distinction and decline to resolve unpresented enforcement proceedings.

**A3 — What qualifies as a network element.** All eight affirm rejection of a physical-component-only definition. Section 153(29) expressly includes features, functions and capabilities furnished through facilities or equipment. Operational-support systems, operator and directory services, and vertical-switching functions are not excluded merely because they are not detachable physical objects or also can be described as services. Eligibility is distinct from compulsory access under §251(d)(2). No conclusion that every eligible function must always be unbundled follows.

**A4 — Necessary, impair and Rule 51.319.** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer would set aside the challenged required-element list and return it for application of the enacted access criteria. The Commission must give independent limiting work to necessity for proprietary elements and impairment for network elements generally, taking account of realistically available alternatives outside as well as inside the incumbent's network. Treating any increased cost or diminished quality as sufficient impairment, while categorically excluding external alternatives, does not supply that limiting inquiry. The proprietary formulation of significant impairment or thwarting is not rejected merely because it is less than absolute indispensability; the defect includes the cramped comparison limited to the incumbent's nonproprietary elements. Neither standard commands an abstract indispensability rule or access only to an irreplaceable natural monopoly. The agency retains room to explain genuine entry impairment, actual availability, costs, quality, service needs and workable criteria under the statute. The Court supplies no invented numerical cost threshold and does not direct which element must be removed.

**Stevens and Souter's A4 contrary commitment:** Uphold the agency's construction and resulting listed access requirements against this statutory attack. The Act makes unbundled access a means of competitive entry, not a last resort after replication. Cost and quality affect the ability to provide the selected service; the separate higher proprietary threshold and nonproprietary alternative give the clauses distinct operation. An outside-source requirement can let incumbents demand duplication of facilities that is theoretically possible but commercially disables entry. Chevron and their MCI dissent support the actual competition rationale within an express delegation. They do not favor absolute mandatory access to every technically available element or endorse the separately invalidated technical-feasibility presumption. Their sufficient ground concerns the reasonableness of this interpretation, not independent judicial findings about every administrative submission.

The strongest challenge to the six-Justice A4 position is that it risks substituting judicial economic judgments for the agency's authorized competitive-entry judgment. The six accept the agency's policy domain but require the words Congress selected to perform the limiting task; expertise cannot turn every disadvantage of purchasing elsewhere into statutory impairment. The strongest challenge to the two-Justice position is that marginal cost or quality differences are virtually ubiquitous. They find the statutory context and retained proprietary and access restrictions sufficient, and do not claim that breadth alone exempts the rule from review. The technical-feasibility presumption receives its own disposition in A9, not an automatic consequence of either A4 vote.

**A5 — No freestanding own-facilities prerequisite.** All eight reject a categorical requirement that a requesting carrier own some local facilities before seeking unbundled access. Section 251(c)(3) reaches any requesting telecommunications carrier and contemplates combination into a service. The separate resale route does not insert a minimum-property requirement absent from the unbundling route. A4 still determines which elements must be supplied, and combining or purchasing elements retains distinct obligations and commercial consequences from buying a finished retail service for resale. No holding promises the entrant the incumbent's entire network regardless of the statutory access criteria or decides rate differences assumed in the parties' policy arguments.

**A6 — Existing combinations, Rule 51.315(b).** All eight would reverse its invalidation. Unbundled access requires separate availability and valuation, not destruction of a configuration already joined in the incumbent's network. Paragraph (b) permits separation when the requesting carrier asks; otherwise a nondiscriminatory duty to leave requested existing combinations intact reasonably implements §251(c)(3). The sentence allowing the requester to combine does not command the incumbent first to dismantle. This holding does not restore paragraphs (c)–(f)'s affirmative new-combination duties, require construction of an unestablished configuration or decide their validity; their separate requested reconsideration is addressed in A11. The technical and operational content of a future particular request remains governed by the Act and valid rules.

**A7 — Section 252(i), Rule 51.809.** All eight would reverse its invalidation. The statutory access to any interconnection, service or network element under an approved agreement permits selection of an individual arrangement on the same pertinent rates, terms and conditions. It is not selection of an isolated price with its relevant obligations discarded. The rule preserves the incumbent's proof that providing the particular arrangement to this requester costs more or is technically infeasible, and limits availability to a reasonable period after public filing. Those safeguards, nondiscrimination and the agency's assessment of negotiation incentives support the construction. The challengers' strongest tradeoff objection does not establish that Congress required every entrant to take an entire agreement or that the agency's contrary practical assessment is irrational. No limitless cherry-picking right follows.

**A8 — California.** The five adopting A1 would reverse the intraLATA dialing-parity vacatur based on absent federal authority and remand any actual remaining issues. O'Connor, Thomas and Breyer also recognize national implementation of §251(b)(3)'s express dialing-parity obligation under §251(d)(1); their specific pricing-allocation objection does not remove that access duty from federal implementation. All eight leave the distinct challenge to Rule 52.17 numbering-cost allocation unripe on this record: the challenged disadvantage depends on whether particular rate decisions permit recovery, and none is before the Court. Reversing the authority premise neither settles those rates nor manufactures an accrued numbering injury. The §271 and other statutory limits retained in the California judgment are not silently eliminated.

**A9 — Rule 51.317's technical-feasibility presumption, separate from its definition and Rule 51.319.** The opening brief expressly seeks corrected-standard reconsideration; this is a supported remedial request, not proof that the lower court decided nothing but jurisdiction. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer affirm the partial vacatur of the presumption and corresponding Order paragraphs 278 and 281 on the following independently sufficient ground. Feasibility can be relevant to access, and the words at a minimum allow the Commission to consider additional matters. But this rule presumptively requires additional elements on a feasibility finding and permits refusal only through the actual incumbent-network-only proprietary alternative or the rule's no-quality-loss/no-cost-increase condition. Those restrictions reproduce the failure to give the required necessity and impairment criteria independent limiting force identified in A4. The result does not invalidate every portion of Rule 51.317 or the separately sustained technical-feasibility definition, and does not hold that the Commission may never use a lawful presumption. A corrected-standard remand cannot cure this already resolved substantive defect without agency reconsideration of the defective criteria.

Stevens and Souter would vacate the lower partial invalidation and remand this presumption for review under the proper delegated-authority standard, without unconditional reinstatement or a final validity ruling. The lower premise that technical feasibility is unmentioned among the minimum criteria is insufficient; the rule has actual exceptions, not feasibility alone. Their A4 position removes the six Justices' independently sufficient objection, but does not decide whether the actual presumption and burdens are reasonably explained or valid in every application. The strongest challenge is that its burden can effectively replace a statutory determination; their remand requires that objection to be considered rather than assuming technical feasibility can never inform selection.

**A10 — Superior quality, Rules 51.305(a)(4) and 51.311(c).** All eight would vacate the reviewed invalidation and remand for reconsideration under the corrected authority and substantive-standard distinction, without deciding final validity or directing immediate unconditional reinstatement. Section 251(c)(2)'s at-least-equal language states a mandatory floor; absence of an express command to provide superior quality does not itself establish that a reasonable implementing rule may never require it. The lower court's existing-network and substantive-conflict grounds remain real objections, not erased by the advocate's claim that nobody challenged statutory consistency. On return, the court must consider the actual requested and technically feasible improvement, the relationship to the statutory duty and the agency's own justification. Compensation alone supplies no authority, and the statutory nondiscrimination floor does not itself require every requested upgrade.

The two rules remain distinct: Rule 51.305(a)(4) concerns interconnection superior to that provided to the incumbent, subsidiary, affiliate or another interconnection recipient; Rule 51.311(c) concerns the element and access superior to the incumbent's own, upon request and to the extent feasible, with the incumbent's technical-infeasibility proof before the state commission. The rule preserves lesser quality at the requester's sole request. The lower necessary-modification exception remains relevant: modifications necessary to accommodate lawful interconnection or access were not forbidden by its ruling. Neither an unconditional right to superior facilities nor a categorical ban on necessary modifications is adopted.

**A11 — Affirmative new combinations, Rule 51.315(c)–(f).** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer would vacate the invalidation and remand for review under the correct implementing-power standard. The sentence requiring provision in a manner allowing requesting carriers to combine elements describes the access capability; it does not unambiguously assign every physical combining task exclusively to the requester. The lower court's substantive reading must therefore be assessed with the available delegation, the incumbent's control, the actual conditions and the agency's explanation. Unlike A6, this does not decide these affirmative duties valid. Subsection (c) reaches requested combinations not ordinarily made only if technically feasible and not impairing other carriers' access or interconnection. Subsection (d) concerns combination with elements possessed by the requester in any technically feasible manner. Subsections (e) and (f) assign the incumbent the relevant infeasibility or other-carrier-impairment showing before the state commission. The (c) other-carrier condition must not be rewritten as an express condition in (d). The remand does not approve each burden or compel a particular configuration.

Thomas would affirm the invalidation of (c)–(f) on a substantive ground independent of the pricing dispute. He reads Congress's direction to make elements available so that the requesting carrier can combine them, together with the distinct resale route, as assigning the new-combination undertaking to the requester; a general implementation grant cannot shift that affirmative task to the incumbent on demand. Necessary accommodation remains distinct from requiring the incumbent to create combinations it does not use. His strongest counterargument is that the statute speaks in terms of enabling, not exclusive labor assignment. His MCI majority and additional-condition joins give decisive weight to the specific allocation; compensation and the rule's feasibility safeguards do not supply the obligation he finds absent. This position permits preservation of an existing combination under A6, which demands no new combining work, and does not forbid voluntary new-combination agreements.

The request to reconsider these three groups is expressly presented in the sanitized opening brief. No certified grant limitation excludes it in the approved neutral packet. The above dispositions neither accept that brief's characterization of the lower reasoning as fact nor resolve every residual regulatory merit simply because general authority has been corrected.

## 4. Hughes Aircraft Co. v. Jacobson — No. 97-1287 — January 25, 1999

### Common result and required claim-specific grounds

**Every Associate Justice would reverse the Ninth Circuit's revival of the six original claims and restore their dismissal on the following limited legal grounds.** The judgment concerns the original complaint reviewed under Rule 12(b)(6); it does not decide later merger allegations merely because an amended complaint appears in the joint appendix. Factual allegations receive the applicable favorable treatment, but the labels separate plan, termination and diversion do not themselves establish the legal predicates. The defined-benefit arrangement continues to fund its promised benefits from the common fund under the contributory and noncontributory benefit structures. No loss of an accrued promised benefit, actual reversion to Hughes, sham payment to a nonparticipant or completed statutory termination is established by the original theories. That conclusion follows from the actual acts alleged and the incorporated plan characterization, not from an assertion that sponsor capacity immunizes everything done with assets.

**H1 — Section 403(c)(1), original first claim and related fifth-claim ground.** Plan assets remain plan property, including surplus attributable to employee contributions. Paying genuine benefits to participants under the amended single plan does not make the assets inure to Hughes merely because Hughes's required future contributions are lower or an early-retirement program reduces payroll. The amendment adds a benefit structure and eligible participants; it is not a transfer from one legally separate trust to satisfy another employer obligation. A contribution holiday alone does not distribute assets to the employer. The anti-inurement restriction applies independently of fiduciary classification; an actual diversion, prohibited reversion or payment outside the lawful plan would require its own analysis. No new exception to §403 is created.

**H2 — Sections 3(21)(A), 404 and pleaded 405 derivative responsibility.** Design and adoption of the 1989 and 1991 amendments are sponsor acts under Spink, even though employees contributed. Contributions do not make every amendment a fiduciary function or participants co-settlors entitled to control the plan's design. Asset management and disposition in implementation remain separately subject to the functional statute, including authority or control over disposition without an additional discretion requirement. Assuming fiduciary implementation of the challenged genuine payments, the original complaint identifies no distinct imprudent management or disloyal diversion beyond the rejected surplus-ownership and separate-plan theories. The derivative cofiduciary label supplies no underlying breach. John Hancock's asset/function/breach distinctions and Varity's separate administration holding remain intact; neither turns amendment design into administration nor exempts actual administration.

**H3 — Sections 203(a)(1), 204(c), and claimed benefit deprivation.** Accrued employee-derived benefits are nonforfeitable, and a contributory defined-benefit plan must satisfy the statutory employee-contribution floor. For mandatory contributions, §204(c) uses accumulated contributions with the statutory interest treatment, expressed as an annual benefit commencing at normal retirement age; a different benefit form requires the prescribed actuarial equivalence. The statute does not treat all actual investment appreciation on mandatory contributions as the employee's separate property. Section 204(c)(4)'s different treatment of voluntary employee contributions remains distinct and is not erased by the defined-benefit label; the original allegations describe required payroll contributions, not an unrecognized voluntary-contribution account. The original claim identifies no reduction below the promised or statutorily protected accrued benefit and no forfeiture merely by use of pooled surplus for lawful plan benefits. No accrued-benefit reduction, anti-cutback violation or recalculation amount is judicially invented. An actual violation of the statutory floor or an independently promised greater benefit is not insulated.

**H4 — Sections 4041 and 4044, original fourth claim.** Closing new enrollment in the contributory structure while continuing the plan, its participants' claims and its ongoing obligations does not constitute the statutory termination that triggers §4044 distribution. Section 4041 specifies standard or distress termination for a single-employer plan, apart from PBGC proceedings under §4042; a common-law wasting-trust label does not supply a substitute termination. If the statutory distribution trigger occurs, the liabilities and §4044 order matter, including its protection for residual assets attributable to employee contributions. Employer residual reversion has its own conditions: satisfaction of all liabilities, consistency with law and authorization by the plan, with the separate enacted limits on a reversion amendment. None creates a present right to distribute all surplus before termination. This is not an unpresented general partial-termination holding and does not use the lower opinion's deleted common-law or mixed-question language.

**H5 — Section 406(a)(1)(D), related fifth claim, and §406(b)(2) argument.** The pleaded asset use is payment of pension benefits to participants in the continuing plan, not a transfer to Hughes or to strangers solely renamed as beneficiaries. Spink's binding genuine-benefit-payment rule defeats the inference of a prohibited use solely from employer savings; it assumes an otherwise lawful plan and preserves sham transfers, kickbacks and independent statutory violations. The original theory supplies no additional transaction of that kind after H1–H4 are resolved. Section 406(b)(2)'s separate bar on a fiduciary acting in a transaction on behalf of a party whose interests are adverse to the plan or its participants is not made out merely because the sponsor adopted a benefit design advantageous to itself. No blanket benefit-payment exemption or general net-benefit defense follows, and no §408 exception is needed to cure an otherwise found prohibited transaction. Souter and Breyer's prior Spink reservation does not constitute a holding that these genuine pension payments are prohibited; they apply the controlling rule to this narrower asserted transaction.

**H6 — Original sixth claim, §404(a)(1)(D), §502(a)(1)(B), and Article V §5.2.** The plan's fair-and-equitable administration, interpretation and application clause does not promise every retired participant an incentive later offered to induce current eligible employees to retire. The actual alleged difference is created by the 1989 amendment's eligibility design, not unequal implementation among participants satisfying that design. Spink disposes of fiduciary liability resting solely on creating that eligibility condition, but that alone is not the answer to §502(a)(1)(B). The benefits-enforcement theory fails separately because the quoted administrative clause supplies no contractual entitlement to an incentive for a retiree outside its eligibility class. The complaint alleges no failure to pay benefits due under the applicable plan terms. Curtiss-Wright preserves actual amendment-procedure requirements and authorized adoption; the original six theories do not allege a distinct defect in who adopted this amendment. The Court decides no case in which an amendment violates a specific anti-discrimination, allocation, benefit or amendment restriction. Selective design is not synonymous with unfair administration, and a broad fairness clause is not judicial authority to redesign the benefit structure.

### Enhanced alternative-path comparison

**Contributory-plan distinction.** The retirees' strongest point is that employee-derived money is protected expressly, rather than being property the sponsor may treat as its own. H1 and H3 accept that premise. It does not follow that participants own every dollar of an ongoing plan's investment surplus, that contributions turn design into fiduciary conduct, or that all surplus must be distributed before the statutory termination trigger. John Hancock preserves plan-asset status and function; it did not adjudicate individual ownership of actuarial surplus. The contrary blanket employer-ownership ground is unavailable.

**Implementation remand.** The strongest return path invokes Spink's express preservation of independently pleaded anti-inurement and implementation claims and Rule 12's treatment of allegations. That path would be required for an actual asset transfer outside the amended plan, a sham, unpaid protected benefits, unauthorized amendment or administrative departure from operative terms. The original complaint's asserted recipient and transfer rest on the claim that the amendment created a legally separate plan, while the early-retirement theory rests on disfavored eligibility design. Resolving those legal predicates leaves no separate supported implementation wrong in these six claims. No dismissal is justified merely by saying that everything Hughes did was settlor conduct.

**Termination path.** The proposed wasting-trust construction would force a distribution because no new entrants can join the contributory structure. Its strongest support is protection of employee-generated residual value against indefinite employer capture. The plan still owes and pays benefits and continues through the new structure; the statutory termination and residual-asset provisions, rather than a judicially invented termination trigger, protect the identified interests. The Court neither finds an actuarial shortage nor treats keeping the assets in the plan as a reversion.

**Plan-enforcement path.** The strongest H6 objection is that an enforceable fair-administration clause has work independent of fiduciary classification. The unanimous commitment expressly gives it that work for administration under actual eligibility terms; it does not turn it into an equality-of-benefit-design promise. Rejection of §502(a)(1)(B) is therefore a claim-specific merits ruling rather than a remedial-category bar under Mertens or an assumption that all contract claims fail when fiduciary design liability fails.
