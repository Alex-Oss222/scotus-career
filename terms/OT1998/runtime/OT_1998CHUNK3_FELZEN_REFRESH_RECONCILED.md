## 3. Governing grounds retained after adversarial review

### A. Limited representative-objector appellate status

The retained reversal ground is confined to a shareholder who appears through the Rule 23.1 settlement-notice procedure, actually presents objections, and has those objections heard and rejected when the district court approves disposition of the corporate claim. That recognized role supplies status for the limited purpose of appealing the disposition over those objections without a separate intervention motion. The ground is a construction of the defined procedural role in the ordinary appellate framework, not an assertion that Rule 23.1 expressly grants an appeal.

Receipt of notice, economic interest, shareholder status without appearance, and possible binding effect alone do not supply this status. The ground gives no general authority to prosecute the corporation's case, introduce new claims, avoid ordinary preservation and appeal requirements, or secure a favorable determination of an objection. It does not decide an independent fee challenge merely because the notice of appeal also names the fee order.

Tryforos, 518 F.2d 1258, Part I and footnote 22, is the most specific support. Stevens distinguished the conditions appropriate to taking over prosecution from those appropriate to an objection to disposition. The footnote expressly included proposed settlements in the limited appeal route. The actual objection concerned a dismissal with prejudice and an apparent failure to serve the corporation; it did not decide the present settlement's fairness. Its separate service, notice-of-appeal and fee rulings do not enlarge the objector rule.

Marino, 484 U.S. 301, 303–304, is the strongest contrary authority. Its objectors were actually heard, yet could not appeal without intervention. Thus a reversal cannot rest on hearing participation by itself. The retained distinction is that this shareholder uses the procedure specifically recognizing objections to disposition of the represented corporate claim; Marino concerned officers asserting their own adverse personal interests in another action. This is a contested legal distinction, not a source fact that automatically makes a shareholder a party. The reversal commitments accept it only for the defined review role.

Izumi's current holding remains intact: financial exposure and an undisturbed intervention denial do not permit review of the parties' underlying judgment under §1254(1). Felzen directly presents the claimed Rule 23.1 appellate role and involves no denied application whose reversal is omitted from the presented question. This distinction allows consideration of the status claim; it does not by itself establish the claim's correctness.

Johnson, 289 U.S. 479, 495–496, supplies a limited equity analogy. Page 495 connects invitation, appearance, objections and appeal in the original receivership proceeding. Page 496 immediately explains why Equity Rule 37 would not have prevented intervention by those litigants. The report also recounts actual formal interventions and a separate receivership appeal statute. Read together, these passages establish neither a universal formal-motion requirement under modern rules nor a holding that mere appearance invariably replaces intervention. The reversal ground must stand on the recognized Rule 23.1 role and the Tryforos distinction; Johnson cannot independently carry it.

The remedy is reversal of the status dismissal and remand for further appellate proceedings. It neither invalidates the settlement nor awards fees, orders discovery, finds collusion or inadequate representation, or guarantees that every contention in the appeal notice is preserved and reviewable.

### B. Intervention required for this appeal

The retained affirmance ground permits a shareholder to object under Rule 23.1 while requiring an otherwise nonparty shareholder properly to become a party through intervention before appealing the settlement judgment. The district-court objection role assists supervision of the corporate claim; it does not on this construction confer authority to continue that litigation on appeal. An actual denial of intervention remains reviewable. No such application or denial exists in the supplied posture.

Marino supplies both the general party-status rule and a concrete heard-objector application. Izumi reinforces the distinct status inquiry and the review route after an actual denial. This ground does not claim that §1291 expressly defines every category of party, that Appellate Rule 3(c) independently resolves status, or that all nonparty appeals in all procedural settings are categorically forbidden.

Tryforos is the strongest contrary source. Its footnote actually recognizes settlement appeals; its presence cannot be omitted or recast as solely a notice-defect rule. The affirmance construction rejects that settlement-appeal proposition insofar as it conflicts with the governing Marino rule as applied here. It explains the disagreement by separating permission to object from authority to continue the action through appeal. The corporate character of the claim does not dispense with the need to justify that separation, just as it does not automatically confer status on the competing path.

Johnson's page 496 discussion weakens any claim that page 495 conclusively eliminates intervention. It does not itself establish the present formal-intervention rule. The affirmance commitments therefore rely principally on Marino's application and Izumi's status distinction, not an overstated receivership holding.

Affirmance leaves the settlement undisturbed because the appeal cannot proceed on this status theory. It supplies no Supreme Court approval of settlement fairness or fees and no holding that an independent personal claim is barred. Requiring intervention for this appeal is distinct from a general duty to intervene in order to preserve an independent claim.
