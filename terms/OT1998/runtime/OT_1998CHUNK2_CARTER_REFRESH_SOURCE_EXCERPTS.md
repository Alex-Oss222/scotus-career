## X03 — Trial memorandum on garden-level viewing, common ground, and curtilage

**Omnibus memorandum, E9–E12.** Source: `petition.txt`, lines 3703–3838; payload SHA-256 `5c6b36c2948d58e809a65f2f9a4e63812654aca0be283c1e8a98e4edf10d9dde`.

```text
I. OFFICER THIELEN’S INITIAL OBSERVATIONS 
THROUGH KIMBERLY THOMPSON'S WINDOW 


Officer Thielen did not violate Defendant's constitutional 
right against unreasonable searches and seizures because the 
officer's observations were made from an area in which 
Defendant had no “reasonable expectation of privacy.” The 
observations made by the officer were made from a common 
area opened to the public and other occupants of the 
apartment complex were limited to articles in plain sight. 

Once Officer Thielen spoke to the anonymous individual 
who identified the apartment in question, he approached the 
apartment which was at garden level. A garden level 
apartment is one that a part of the apartment is below ground 
and if one looked though a window at full height that person 
would have to look downward to see the furniture of the 
apartment. While standing on the grounds outside the window 
of the apartment, the officer peered through the mini-blinds. 
The officer testified that there were gaps in the blinds which 
allowed him to view activity in the apartment. While looking 
downward and through the window, the officer observed 
Defendant and two other individuals bagging white powder. 
After observing this conduct for a period of time, he called his 
supervisor who proceeded to execute search warrants of the 
apartment and a vehicle identified by the anonymous individual 
as belonging to one of the apartment's occupants. 

Unlike a single family dwelling, an apartment complex 
shares its sidewalks, grounds and corridors amongst all its 
residents. While the federal and state constitutions protect 
against unreasonable searches of one's home and surrounding 
curtilage, the Court must determine whether the ground the 
officer stood upon while observing the apartment is the kind 
envisioned to be within the curtilage of the apartment and 
therefore protected from unreasonable searches. 


E-9 


A four factor test has been in Minnesota to 
determine whether an area claimed as curtilage and the 
extension of an individual's expectation of privacy to that area 
is protected. The four factors are: (1) the proximity of the 
area claimed to be curtilage to the home; (2) whether the area 
is included within an enclosure surrounding the home; (3) the 
nature of uses to which the area is put; and, (4) the steps taken 
by the residents to protect the area from observation by people 
passing by. State v. Krech, 403 N.W.2d 634, 636-37 (Minn. 
1987). 

When applying the factors to the instant case it becomes 
apparent that Defendant did not have an expectation of 
privacy from the location where Officer Thielen made his 
observations outside Ms. Thompson's window. First, while 
the proximity of the area to Ms. Thompson's window is quite 
close, the nature of the grounds as being common to all 
residents detracts from the closeness of the claimed curtilage 
area to Ms. Thompson's apartment. Ms. Thompson did not 
own the land immediately outside her window and could not 
claim any particular exclusive rights to said land. Second, the 
area is not within an enclosure surrounding the home. There 
is no fence blocking entrance to this spot from the public. 
There are no bushes or trees planted immediately in front of 
the window to create an enclosure type space that prevents the 
public from gaining entrance to the area. Third, the nature to 
which this area is put favors the position that this ground is 
not curtilage to her apartment. This area is a common area 
open to all residents of the apartment complex. Though the 
area is not a ball field. exhibits 8, 9, 15, 16 and 17 show that 
the public had access to the area directly in front of Ms. 
Thompson's windows to store bicycles. Residents obviously 
must leave the common sidewalk leading up to the building to 
deposit their bicycles and other items. Fourth, while Ms. 
Thompson did make an attempt to protect her apartment from 


E-10 


observation by drawing the blinds, no attempt was made to 
protect the area claimed as curtilage from passerby. 

In support of his argument that the ground stood upon is 
protected as curtilage due to the fact that it was private 
ground in which no invitation was extended to the officer, 
Defendant relies upon Lorenza, et al. v. Superior Court, 9 Cal. 
3d. 626, 511 P.2d 33, 108 Cal. Rptr. 585 (1973). In that case 
an officer approached a single family home and left the 
sidewalk approaching the home to look through a window that 
was not accessible through normal routes. The appellate court 
held that such an observation was unreasonable and violated 
the defendant's constitutional rights. 

The Lorenza case is, however, distinguishable from the 
instant case. The instant case does not involve a single family 
home on private land. The apartment in this case was 
surrounded by property used by the entire apartment complex. 
Exhibits 8, 9, 15, 16 and 17 show a bicycle leaned up next to 
the apartment window of the apartment in question. Someone 
had to have left the sidewalk to put the bicycle there. There 
was also testimony that the complex housed families and it 
was common to see children and others leave the sidewalk 
areas and walk freely on the grassy areas of the complex. 
There does not appear to be any posted signs informing 
apartment residents or there guests to stay off the grass. And 
although bushes are planted at an angle off the sidewalk in 
front of one of the apartment's windows, there was no 
evidence presented that the bushes were planted for privacy 
reasons and not for aesthetic reasons. 

Though Minnesota has no rule which deals with the 
specific facts of the instant case, the supreme court in State vy. 
Buchwald, 196 N.W.2d 445 (Minn. 1972), acknowiedged the 
reduced expectation of privacy over areas adjacent to motel 
rooms. “Granted that a [motel] tenant has standing to protect 
the room he occupies, there is nevertheless an element of 
public or shared property in motel surroundings that is entirely 


E-11 


lacking in the enjoyment of one's home." Id. at 448. Once it 
is determined that the area adjacent to an apartment window is 
not private but is common grounds for the entire complex, 
Buchwald would suggest that such an area would lose any 
constitutional protection. As the exhibits show, residents did 
walk upon and use the grassy area in front of Ms. Thompson's 
window. This evidence demonstrates that the area was 
common ground for the complex and therefore is not within 
the curtilage of the apartment Defendant occupied. As such, 
Defendant had no expectation of privacy in this area and the 
officer's observations did not constitute a search. 
```
