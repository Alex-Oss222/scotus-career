## Record, questions and lawful relief

During regular contract negotiations the union proposed an advance obligation to negotiate over negotiable matters not covered by the agreement. The Survey declared the proposal nonnegotiable; an unfair-labor-practice complaint followed under 5 U.S.C. §7116(a)(1) and (5). The parties waived an administrative-law-judge hearing and stipulated facts. The Authority ordered bargaining over the proposal. Its reported grounds were that the clause restated a statutory duty and, independently, that a contractual duty could be negotiated even if there were no general statutory duty. It invoked the substantial identity of the proposal to an earlier negotiable clause. This case contains no actual Impasses Panel award imposing this clause.

**The recovered proposed clause:**

~~~text
The Union may request and the Employer will be 
obliged to negotiate on any negotiable matters not cov- 
ered by the provisions of this agreement.
~~~

The lower-opinion reproduction uses the equivalent word “obligated”; the saved merits reproduction above uses “obliged.” The source copies preserve that wording difference rather than silently substituting a reconstructed quotation. The general Authority rule separately includes a waiver qualification; that qualification is not written into the quoted proposal. This does not establish that the proposal legally excludes otherwise applicable waiver rules.

NFFE's first question asks whether the statute requires bargaining over union-initiated midterm proposals on matters not covered by the term agreement and not waived. The second asks whether, in any event, the statute requires bargaining over a contract clause creating such an obligation. The Fourth Circuit regarded its *SSA* and *Energy* interpretations as binding circuit law: in its view a general compulsory midterm regime was inconsistent with the statute, so bargaining about a clause imposing that regime could not be compelled. It acknowledged voluntary bargaining while rejecting the asserted compulsory obligation.

The Authority did not renew its general statutory-duty argument in the present Fourth Circuit litigation because that circuit had already rejected it. Its merits brief nevertheless relies on the ground as an actual administrative basis and as the predicate of the circuit's ruling; NFFE expressly includes it in the questions presented. ## Current case law and reach

**MCI** (June 17, 1994) requires courts to determine actual statutory meaning and preserve operative limits before deference. Its tariff holding does not impose a general rule that agencies may make only minor policy choices. **NationsBank** (January 18, 1995) gives effect to permissible, reasoned construction within ambiguity and a genuine delegation, while preserving clear restrictions and requiring an actual reasoned agency judgment. Its opinion by Souter has all eight Associates' support. Those authorities preserve *Chevron*'s inquiry into text and statutory boundaries; neither supplies an answer to this labor statute merely by label.

Current **AT&T v. Iowa Utilities Board** (January 25, 1999) distinguishes implementing authority from the validity of particular instructions. Its Holdings I–II permit national implementation while preserving tasks Congress assigned elsewhere; Holding IV requires actual limiting statutory predicates to do work and returns the matter to the agency without judicial selection of replacement criteria. Its new-combination and superior-quality remands likewise show that rejection of a categorical absence-of-authority ground does not automatically validate all underlying duties. These are useful principles of scope and remedy, not imported telecommunications rules.

Current **Your Home** (February 23, 1999), Holding I, separates statutory construction from additional regulatory deference and makes the agency's label nonconclusive. Its remaining holdings do not decide FLRA authority. *MCI* preserves *Boston & Maine*'s requirement of a discernible agency ground and *Good Samaritan*'s acceptance of permissible interpretive change. A different former interpretation therefore does not automatically defeat deference, while appellate counsel cannot simply supply an unmade policy choice.

**Presented questions, subsidiary arguments and exceptional Supreme Court review** is the applicable exact register title. ## Strongest supported positions and independent decisional paths

1. **A statutory duty exists.** FLRA and NFFE invoke the broad definition, unqualified bargaining language, the agreement definition, required meeting frequency and statutory employee rights. They argue that Congress did not enact the temporal limitation Interior proposes. The strongest opposing argument reads “for the purposes of arriving at a collective bargaining agreement” in its larger term-contract setting, treats meeting-frequency language as governing required negotiations rather than triggering them, and relies on the management-change provisions and the public system's distinctive impasse machinery. This is a genuine text-and-structure dispute; neither side rests only on a singular article. A holding for a duty must state its subject, covered-matter, waiver, management-right and statutory-consistency limits rather than announce unlimited contract reopening.

2. **No general statutory duty, but a negotiable contractual duty.** The Authority's independent ground and NFFE's second question distinguish the absence of a mandatory entitlement from a prohibition on bargaining to obtain one. Petitioners point to ordinary bargaining over lawful agency discretion, subject to sole-and-exclusive statutory reservations and §7106. Interior responds that the statute specifically supplies a contrary rule under §7103(a)(14)(C), and separately that the general clause is inconsistent with federal law under §7117(a); it says public institutional interests prevent advance waiver by an individual agency. This path must resolve those two objections, not assume that rejecting an affirmative statutory duty answers negotiability. It also must address the actual clause as drafted rather than substitute the narrower experimental-schedule examples offered in argument.

3. **The statute forecloses compulsory bargaining over this general advance clause.** Interior defends the circuit's interpretation and the Panel concern, while acknowledging voluntary bargaining on particular issues by present agreement. It reserves the negotiability of a substantially narrower clause that would not commit the employer to the disputed general obligation. Its alternative institutional argument is that even if an agency could voluntarily undertake such a duty, FLRA could not systematically impose the rejected regime through impasse. A decision on this path must identify the statutory prohibition or reservation that does the work; failure to require the regime is not, without more, a prohibition. The reported voluntary-conversation concession is not agreement that every advance contractual promise is negotiable.

4. **Ambiguity and permissible agency construction.** The text, structure and §7105 responsibilities may leave space for a reasonable FLRA choice. The existing record contains an earlier no-duty construction in *IRS I*, the D.C. Circuit's contrary decision, and FLRA's adoption of that reasoning in *IRS II*, followed by the current two-ground order. Petitioners defend the resulting construction as permissible notwithstanding change; Interior says the statute is clear and, independently, that the Authority complied with a judicial command rather than making its own policy choice. If the existing agency grounds are discernible, legally available and sufficient, the Court could sustain the order without inventing additional agency reasons. If a necessary agency choice remains unmade under the correct legal view, a vacatur and agency return may be warranted. *MCI*, *NationsBank*, *Boston & Maine*, *Good Samaritan* and current *AT&T* support this distinction. Ambiguity alone neither commands enforcement of this order nor requires a remand in every case.

5. **A confined judicial construction with agency remand.** The Court may reject the circuit's categorical legal prohibition yet leave the lawful extent of any general duty or the clause's particular statutory constraints for FLRA to address, if those issues require an actual agency determination. Conversely it may decide an established statutory limit and return only matters genuinely left open. The opinion must state what it resolves, what the agency may or must reconsider, and why the existing two-ground order does or does not survive. A remand for an independent construction is distinct from an instruction to enforce the current order and from an instruction to impose a contract term.

6. **Preservation or narrower reach.** The Court may address the first question on the actual presented/predicate/admin-basis record, or explain a lawful limited decision of the second without a final general-duty holding. It cannot treat the prior circuit precedent as itself a Supreme Court statutory command. Nor may it find that the Authority conceded the statute's no-duty construction merely because it did not repeat an already rejected argument in the circuit.

All these paths must retain genuine negotiated-waiver qualifications. FLRA reports covered-matter branches for matters expressly contained in the agreement and matters inseparably bound up with an expressly covered subject, as well as subjects fully explored in term negotiations. These are descriptions of the agency's framework, not findings that an unidentified future proposal satisfies an exception. FLRA acknowledges it has not decided whether zipper clauses themselves are mandatory bargaining subjects; agreed waiver, compelled negotiation of waiver and compelled acceptance at impasse are separate questions. No decision should rely on universal availability of an employer-imposed zipper clause as an established cure.

The circuit and Interior predict that an impasse route will produce an imposed clause. FLRA disputes inevitability and invokes an actual instance of refusal, while Interior supplies a different account of that example. The actual source supports a dispute over prediction, not a finding that the union will obtain an award, withhold subjects strategically or cause quantified inefficiency. Interior's representation of FLRA's “anomaly” comment is also broader than FLRA's actual footnote: the latter addresses negotiability while leaving the general statutory-duty question unresolved, not every decision finding no statutory duty but contractual negotiability. ## Federal Republic of Germany v. United States — No. 127, Original; A-736

### Event, parties, requested action and material record

There is no judgment below. Germany, Ambassador Jürgen Chrobog and Consul General Wolfgang Rudolph seek leave to file an original complaint against the United States and Arizona Governor Jane Dee Hull. The diplomats sue in their official capacities and on Germany's behalf. They invoke Article III's cases-affecting-ambassadors-and-consuls head and 28 U.S.C. §1251(b)(1), which supplies original but nonexclusive jurisdiction over actions or proceedings to which the designated foreign diplomatic officers are parties. Their actual pleaded roles must be examined; neither Germany's foreign-state status alone nor insertion of a diplomatic name resolves the jurisdictional question.

The motion seeks a temporary restraining order or preliminary injunction preventing Walter LaGrand's execution pending the ICJ case. It independently requests, at minimum, a short restraint while the Court considers original leave through ordinary briefing and deliberation. The memorandum's conclusion also requests eventual permanent relief lasting until resolution of Germany's ICJ action. These different stages and durations are not interchangeable. Granting a short jurisdiction-preserving interval would neither grant leave nor determine domestic enforceability; denial of interim relief would not itself decide the leave motion.

The complaint asserts threatened violations of the U.N. Charter and an ICJ ruling, and a separate international-comity theory. Its prayer seeks declarations and prospective restraint against Hull. The United States is named as a defendant, but the operative prayer is directed to the Governor; any relief against the United States must have its own jurisdictional and immunity basis and cannot be inferred from its name in the caption.

Two international acts matter. The acting President's March 2 communication under ICJ Rule 74(4) called attention to preservation of the effectiveness of a future order. The full ICJ's March 3 provisional-measures order is a distinct act under Statute Article 41 and Rule 75. The motion and memorandum expressly rely on the March 3 order even though the complaint's original description begins with the March 2 communication. The authenticated order finds prima facie ICJ jurisdiction, asks the United States to take all measures at its disposal to prevent execution pending final decision, requires information concerning its measures and transmission to the Governor, and retains the matter. It reserves final merits. An international order's issuance, international binding effect, domestic judicial enforceability and the availability of a particular domestic action are separate propositions.

The complaint alleges consular officials learned of the LaGrands' case in 1992, and that Arizona's prior knowledge of their German nationality was disclosed only in late February 1999. It uses February 24; the ICJ order reports an asserted February 23 clemency-proceeding admission and Germany's statement that it became fully aware February 24. The pleading alleges that Hull rejected a clemency panel recommendation to stay execution. The ICJ's paragraph 12 reports the German representative's corresponding account. This is concrete alleged conduct relevant to officer connection; it is not merely the Governor's general duty to execute law. Neither the allegation nor the ICJ's description establishes every state-law power or determines the domestic connection question.

The ICJ order records a March 3 meeting at which the United States objected to proceeding without first hearing the parties. The separate ICJ writings disclose disagreement about emergency procedure and the relationship of interim protection to the international dispute. It is inaccurate to say that no United States position was expressed. Those writings do not decide domestic jurisdiction, cause of action or immunity.

Germany's March 3 reply attributes four objections to the Solicitor General's letter: the Convention does not afford the asserted conviction-vacatur action; doubt concerning the ambassador/consul jurisdictional head; provisional measures may not bind; and an Eleventh Amendment objection. The reply contests them and renews its alternative request for consideration time. The separate letter is unrecovered. These are attributed positions, not a representation that the United States conceded every other point or used a particular unquoted formulation.

### Entering law and issue sequence

1. **A live domestic case and the original channel.** Article III, §1251(b)(1), and the actual diplomatic plaintiffs furnish the asserted channel. The requested domestic restraint can practically prevent execution; whether these plaintiffs have a judicially cognizable right, injury and remedial relationship remains a legal inquiry, not an inference from diplomatic rank. *Steel Co.* (June 2, 1997) forbids deciding contested merits by assuming disputed Article III power, while preserving legitimate nonmerits sequences. Current *NAACP v. Jones* (November 5, 1998), unanimously, requires standing to be determined on the actual pleading and appropriate record, without manufacturing absent allegations. Germany invokes *Ex parte Gruber*, 269 U.S. 302, and *Ohio v. Wyandotte Chemicals*, 401 U.S. 493, in support of the diplomatic head and discretionary exercise of original jurisdiction. These are identified source arguments; §1251's exact text and the actual pleaded capacities must do the jurisdictional work rather than counsel's characterization alone. A decision to decline discretionary original leave must be distinguished from a ruling that constitutional or statutory jurisdiction is absent.

2. **International obligation, domestic duty, cause and remedy.** U.N. Charter Articles 92–94, ICJ Statute Articles 36 and 41, the Optional Protocol and the actual ICJ order supply Germany's treaty argument. Article 94(1)'s promise to comply with an ICJ decision and Article 41's power to indicate provisional measures support Germany's textual and effectiveness arguments; whether those texts require the requested domestic judicial relief remains disputed. The current *Paraguay/In re Breard* application holdings (April 14, 1998) reserved categorical treaty enforceability, final original jurisdiction, separate cause-of-action questions and original leave. They supply no final rule that an ICJ order can never be enforced or that every such order is enforceable. The older consular-notification claim's remedial defect cannot be transplanted to a newly asserted order without examining the new duty. Conversely, a new order's date does not establish a domestic action or an entitlement to relief. *McClellan v. Cablevision* (January 7, 1999), within its implied-damages scope, confirms the distinctness of substantive protection and authority for a particular remedy; it does not decide treaty equity. *Aktepe* (March 10, 1998) requires a claim-specific manageable-duty inquiry and rejects a categorical bar arising merely from foreign or military affairs.

3. **Connected-officer relief and distinct sovereign defendants.** *Seminole Tribe* (March 27, 1996) permits prospective enforcement against its actually connected Governor within IGRA's particular duty, sequence and remedial limits; it creates neither a universal statutory route nor a categorical detailed-scheme exclusion. *Coeur d'Alene* (June 23, 1997) permits prospective relief against officials concretely connected to an ongoing federal violation, without a sovereign-interest balancing exception, while preserving the need to establish the right, violation and lawful remedy. A general law-obedience duty is insufficient. Hull's alleged refusal of a recommended stay is therefore material to the inquiry, but does not itself establish an enforceable treaty duty. *Young*, *Edelman*, *Papasan* and *Milliken* retain their distinct prospective, retrospective and continuing-violation limits. The current Paraguay holding says remaining consequences of old nonnotification do not by themselves establish ongoing denial of presently available consular rights; the new ICJ-order claim requires its own analysis. *Monaco v. Mississippi*, 292 U.S. 313, remains relevant to a direct foreign-sovereign suit against a State. The current *Seminole* judgment establishes no controlling general State-amenability rationale and does not overrule *Union Gas*. Neither an officer caption nor foreign-affairs importance supplies an automatic immunity exception.

4. **Federal immunity is separate.** Current *Blue Fox* (January 20, 1999), unanimously, distinguishes a substantive duty, jurisdiction and consent to the particular remedy; its actual holding concerns an equitable lien functioning as substitute compensation, not a categorical prohibition on prospective treaty relief. A lawful waiver must still be identified for a claim against the United States. Current *Your Home* (February 23, 1999) retains *Califano v. Sanders*: the APA does not independently confer subject-matter jurisdiction. Neither decision substitutes for analysis of an actually invoked waiver, review route or officer remedy here.

5. **Interim relief must be tied to its own channel and purpose.** The certiorari stay standard in *Breard*—reasonable probability of four votes for review, significant possibility of reversal and likely irreparable harm—is not mechanically the original-injunction standard. The companion A-771 holding instead requires an adequate present showing of domestic right, ongoing duty, jurisdictional route and remedy for the requested original-action injunction; its denial was interim and reserved leave. Germany invokes *Doran v. Salem Inn*, 422 U.S. 922, for likely success and irreparable injury, and *University of Texas v. Camenisch*, 451 U.S. 390, to distinguish preliminary likelihood from actual merits success. Execution's irreversibility establishes the character of harm, but cannot itself supply missing power or a right to relief.

6. **The alternative short interval is an independent request.** The current Paraguay holdings preserve *Shipp* and *United Mine Workers* concerning necessary protection while a substantial authority question is resolved, subject to scope and cessation when power is found absent. *Pennsylvania Bureau* keeps §1651 auxiliary. Section 2283's exceptions—express authorization, necessity in aid of jurisdiction, and protection or effectuation of the issuing court's judgments—remain distinct; neither short duration nor an officer caption itself creates an exception. The actual independent-party inquiry under *Hale* and *County of Imperial*, and any properly established §1983/*Mitchum* route, cannot be presumed from the caption. A-738 rejected an additional interval on its older claim because the interim question could already be assessed; A-771 adopted no majority additional §2283-necessity rule. Those holdings require a concrete authority and timing explanation here, not automatic extension of either result.

### Supported alternatives, concessions and limits

- **Original leave:** Grant leave or undertake further ordinary consideration if the actual diplomatic plaintiffs and pleaded interests satisfy the constitutional and statutory head and the original forum is warranted; alternatively deny leave on a supported jurisdictional or discretionary basis. The national importance of the treaty dispute supports consideration but does not establish jurisdiction. Nonexclusive jurisdiction does not itself establish an adequate alternative forum or require abstention.
- **New-order enforcement:** Germany can argue that the treaty undertaking and ICJ preservation power impose a presently enforceable obligation, and that Hull's concrete alleged execution-related conduct threatens an ongoing violation amenable to tailored prospective relief. The opposing path tests international binding effect, domestic enforceability, cause of action and fitted remedy separately. It can reject relief for failure on a necessary domestic link without declaring all treaty rights unenforceable or treating the new order as the old defaulted notification claim. *Paraguay*, *Seminole*, *Coeur d'Alene*, *Aktepe*, the treaty provisions and the actual order support this fork.
- **Comity:** Germany's separate equitable argument invokes the public interest in international judicial cooperation and the treaty-created forum. The contrary position is that equitable discretion operates within existing jurisdiction, sovereign consent and enforceable legal limits and cannot itself create those predicates. *Porter*, 328 U.S. 395, and *Virginian Railway*, 300 U.S. 515, are Germany's identified equitable authorities; the current *Paraguay*, *Blue Fox* and *Your Home* limits prevent a bare public-interest conclusion from replacing the missing legal link.
- **Temporary protection:** A short, expressly ending restraint can be considered on a supported necessary-authority-inquiry basis independently of leave or a longer injunction. ### Entering law, thresholds and limitations

**State-application standard.** *Gomez* (April 21, 1992), *Bowersox* (April 9, 1996) and *Lonchar* (April 1, 1996), copied in the additional reading slice, are necessary additions to the original focused list. *Bowersox* requires substantial grounds on which relief might be granted for a second-or-successive-petition stay, and vacates the identified stay under abuse-of-discretion review; certification, scheduled argument and petition count do not automatically establish those grounds. *Gomez* permits an independent assessment of State enforcement interests and unjustified delay even while assuming a procedural channel remains open, but made no final Eighth Amendment gas ruling, universal alternative-method rule or injunction against all later relief. *Lonchar* preserves independent equitable stay discretion while rejecting an extra-statutory dismissal sanction and requires necessary interim protection only for identified legally required process that lawful expedition cannot finish. The State's vacatur request must be assessed under its own supported posture; *Breard*'s certiorari-stay standard is not automatically imposed on the State as though it were seeking a prisoner's stay.

**Second or successive state-custody habeas applications** (exact register title; present operation effective May 18, 1998, *Martinez-Villareal*, with April 24, 1996 statutory conditions and *Felker*'s June 28, 1996 construction retained). Section 2244(b)(1) requires dismissal of a claim in a second or successive application already presented. A new claim is subject to §2244(b)(2)'s previously unavailable new constitutional rule made retroactive by the Supreme Court, or the combined due-diligence and clear-and-convincing no-reasonable-guilt-factfinder conditions. Authorization from a three-judge circuit panel precedes district filing, on a prima facie showing, within the statutory thirty days; the district independently verifies the conditions. An actual authorization decision is not reviewable through rehearing or ordinary appeal/certiorari under §2244(b)(3)(E). A distinct classification that authorization is unnecessary is reviewable.

*Martinez-Villareal* permits first federal merits consideration without authorization for a Ford competency claim actually presented in the initial merits application, dismissed as premature and expressly preserved, after prematurity ends. **Habeas and §2255 appeals and appellate certificates** (exact title; present operation effective June 15, 1998, *Hohn*, with enacted §2253 and Rule 22 components retained). District refusal permits a circuit-judge request; a certificate does not establish ultimate merits entitlement or defeat successive restrictions.
