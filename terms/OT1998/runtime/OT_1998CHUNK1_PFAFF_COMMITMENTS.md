## Proposed result and rule

Affirm the Federal Circuit's patent-invalidity judgment on the reviewed claims. An invention is on sale for section 102(b) when, before the one-year critical date, it is the subject of a commercial offer and is sufficiently complete to be patented. Physical reduction to practice is sufficient evidence of that completeness but is not indispensable: drawings or descriptions that enable a person skilled in the art to practice the invention can establish it. A promise to undertake future inventive research and a mere incomplete conception do not meet that second requirement merely because a customer offers money.

The substantial Texas Instruments purchase order supplies the commercial commitment; the supplied record establishes drawings complete enough for production before the critical date. The later manufacture of the first physical unit therefore does not postpone the bar. No new factfinding, infringement remedy or damages award follows from this affirmance. This disposition does not invalidate unreviewed claims or decide a distinct experimental-use dispute.

## Competing constructions

**Prototype prerequisite:** A physically proven device gives a clear completion date and avoids loss of rights during uncertain development. Its strongest defect is statutory: section 102(b) concerns an invention being on sale, not delivery of a manufactured specimen. The Telephone Cases recognize an operative invention shown in an enabling specification despite imperfect early physical demonstrations. Making an inventor's manufacturing schedule decisive would permit commercial exploitation of a completed design before the statutory clock begins.

**Commercial offer plus demonstrated completeness:** This construction gives effect to both the sale and invention requirements and Pennock's limit on prolonged commercial exploitation. Its strongest objection is uncertainty about how developed a design must be. Requiring enabling completion answers that objection better than an unbounded assessment of whether work is 'substantially complete': the record must show that the actual claimed invention can be practiced, not just that development is likely to succeed. Elizabeth's controlled experimental use remains distinct; ordinary commercial delivery of a finished design is not made experimental merely by the absence of an earlier prototype.
