## Jurisdiction: shared result, actual rather than hypothetical route

**Commitment of all eight Associates:** The district court has section 2241 jurisdiction to decide this substantial legal claim that an unlawful temporal eligibility rule prevented the agency from considering discretionary relief. This is not a request to reweigh the agency's discretionary equities or retry disputed deportability facts.

Kolster already holds that withdrawal of the ordinary INA review channel does not clearly repeal the independent section 2241 grant and that legal pretermission of discretionary-relief eligibility falls within its conditional scope. Its three nonjoining Associates reserved an unfiled route; their reservation was not a holding against the actual collateral jurisdiction now presented. The ordinary statute reaches custody contrary to federal law as well as the Constitution. No constitutional-label requirement may be inserted into the recognized substantial statutory-authority category.

The additional enactments do not supply a sufficient distinction. Section 401(e) deletes the specialized INA habeas provision, not section 2241. Section 440(a) restricts ordinary direct review. Section 309(c)(4)(G) bars the covered appeal in a transitional case whose final order falls after the statutory date; its appeal language does not silently delete the independent writ. Section 242(g), specially applicable to older proceedings, concerns the three named executive acts under Reyes-Hernandez and AADC, not this legal challenge to completed adjudication. The permanent section 242(b)(9) regime cannot be imported wholesale. These provisions perform different work even when read together.

**Strongest contrary route:** The combined repeal of the express INA habeas provision and broad criminal-alien review restrictions could reflect a complete congressional channel choice; retaining a general writ might defeat it. The obstacle is the independent surviving grant and the already controlling channel-specific construction. AADC's confined constitutional section 1331 route is neither needed nor extended here. Felker, McCardle and Yerger support identifying the particular channel withdrawn. The Court need not decide whether the Suspension Clause independently compels this exact statutory scope.

## Section 440(d): two genuinely different paths

**Pending-application construction:** Ordinary text and structure establish that section 440(d)'s new eligibility restriction does not govern an application filed before April 24, 1996 and still awaiting final administrative action that day. Sections 413(g) and 421(b) expressly reach pending applications, including discretionary relief, while section 440(d) does not. Section 440(f) expressly governs subsection (e); it supplies no borrowed timing clause for (d). Lindh permits this contextual inference at Landgraf's initial statutory-direction stage. The narrower holding concerns Goncalves's September 1994 application and pending administrative appeal, not every earlier crime, plea or later application.

Its strongest objection is that the neighboring provisions have different subjects and that the agency is making a present discretionary decision, so the omission does not necessarily protect pending applications. The affirmative response is that Congress expressly addressed pending discretionary relief in the same statutory undertaking; treating all discretionary restrictions as automatically applicable deprives that deliberate distinction of force. The conclusion does not depend on an invented plea bargain, subjective reliance, a vested right to a waiver, or the mere proposition that pendency always preserves old law.

**Present-decision construction:** Section 440(d) limits the Attorney General's present power to waive deportation; the pending applicant has not received a waiver and no specific completed choice shown here requires preserving that future discretion. Sections 413 and 421 address distinct terrorist/asylum restrictions and do not affirmatively create a saving clause for section 440(d). On this reading the new eligibility restriction governs the unresolved agency decision. The strongest objection is the express treatment of pending discretionary applications elsewhere; the three Associates taking this path conclude that the differently targeted provisions are not close enough to dictate a contrary instruction.

The pending-application majority resolves this case through statutory direction, not a universal completed-event rule. The alternative relevant-activity approach remains the three Associates' position, not current Landgraf law. Earlier section 440(a) decisions rejecting pendency or old convictions alone as sufficient are distinguished because the present amendment changes legal eligibility, and its own contextual timing inference must be addressed. Reyes-Hernandez supplies no automatic protection absent its proved surrendered-defense premise, and no such premise is invented here.
