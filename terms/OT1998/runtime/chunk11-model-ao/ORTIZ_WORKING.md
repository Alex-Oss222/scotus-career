# Ortiz v. Fibreboard — provisional independent non-Stone core analysis

**Status:** Working conclusions only; final June 22 dependency screen pending. See CORE_STATUS.md. No comparator or Stone position has entered this modeling context. Research cutoff is immediately before June 23, 1999. Uncoordinated June 23 outcomes do not govern this case.

**Event:** No. 97-1704; merits review of the Fifth Circuit judgment reported at 134 F.3d 668; argued December 8, 1998; decision anchor June 23, 1999. The eight Associates participate. O’Connor’s nonparticipation in Amchem establishes neither nonparticipation nor a prior vote here. The Chief’s position belongs to a later stage.

## Record, review and law entering

The objecting Ortiz class members intervened and seek relief from certification and approval; the proponents defend the judgment. No rule governing every nonintervening class member’s appellate status is necessary. The existing adverse positions and proposed releases supply the concrete certification dispute. Under Amchem’s antecedent nonmerits rule, denial of this representative device may precede the particular standing questions arising from inclusion of future or absent claimants. That does not establish standing for each class member or permit substantive damages adjudication on assumed jurisdiction.

The class covers asbestos personal-injury claims against Fibreboard not brought in suit, settled or included in a settlement agreement before August 27, 1993. It includes presently ill persons with unfiled claims, exposure-only persons, and the derivative categories described below. It is not synonymous with a class of exclusively uninjured persons. The prior inventory settlements, including roughly 20,000 Ness Motley claims and contingent back-end payments, are distinct from the class.

The proposed trust contains $1.535 billion, comprising $1.525 billion insurer money and $10 million from Fibreboard. Insurer policies had no aggregate ceiling, but coverage and its value were disputed; neither victory nor defeat in that litigation may be assumed. The separate Trilateral settlement contemplated $2 billion in insurance proceeds without settling these tort claims. Fibreboard’s ordinary assets were not all devoted to the Global class.

The district court heard evidence and found likely exhaustion of even $2 billion plus about $235 million in noninsurance value within five to nine years. The legal sufficiency of that finding for this mandatory arrangement remains contested. Calling the finding nonexistent is incorrect; accepting an economic prediction does not resolve the legal boundaries of (b)(1)(B). A later acquisition figure reported by the dissent is a valuation objection, not an authoritative replacement appraisal.

Class members cannot opt out. The trust procedures permit individualized claims valuation and eventual jury proceedings against the trust, but retain the $500,000 cap, no punitive recovery, and other payment restrictions. That mechanism is not unrestricted resort to Fibreboard or insurers. Counsel represented inventory claimants and the proposed class during bargaining; the Trilateral agreement later secured the inventory back-end payments, and the guardian was appointed after agreement in principle. Those later protections and the findings of vigorous bargaining are genuine evidence, not permission to assume that the original bargaining interests were independently protected.

The governing settlement rule is Amchem’s actual simulated holding: all applicable Rule 23(a) and (b) requirements apply; settlement may inform them but cannot replace them; materially conflicting bargaining interests need adequate protection. It does not categorically disqualify every difference or prescribe universal subclasses. The (b)(3) predominance holding does not enter (b)(1)(B). Barnes unanimously confirms this category-specific discipline. Abate likewise protects genuine common adjudication while rejecting efficiency as a substitute for substantive rights. Richards, Matsushita, South Central Bell and Tice preserve lawful authority to bind absent persons; they do not independently decide this settlement’s adequacy or impose every federal class rule as constitutional law.

No existing controlling holding supplies the proposed three-part formula of independent fund, all assets and equitable treatment as a settled conclusive test. The Rule’s practical-impairment language, its traditional limited-resource category, the Rules Enabling Act and the actual settlement structure must support any construction adopted now. The working positions below distinguish the narrower proposition they support from a comprehensive formulation they do not need to adopt.

## Adequacy: sufficient application and competing path

For O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, the challenged bargain makes the material conflict concrete. Currently ill members have reason to favor prompt larger payments; later-manifesting members have reason to conserve a finite fund and protect later value. Pre-1959 exposure gives access to materially different insurance arguments from post-1959 exposure. A uniform cap and process make allocative decisions across those interests; postponing individual valuation does not undo them. The common representatives and counsel were not separately responsible for protecting the divergent groups in negotiating those tradeoffs. The lower conclusion that common objectives overwhelm the differences therefore answers the wrong legal question. The full record of competent, vigorous negotiations and later guardian review does not establish the missing independent protection.

Counsel’s simultaneous inventory representation reinforces the concern. The lower court’s phased account deserves accurate treatment: no assumption is made that a fixed competing aggregate cap existed throughout early negotiations, and the Trilateral agreement did later secure back-end payments. But inventory clients’ preexisting stake in obtaining a global deal remained during the August agreement in principle. Later removal of a contingent financial interest does not retroactively establish independent representation in those negotiations. The six do not find fraud, collusion or unethical conduct merely from this structural defect. Nor do they make that external-counsel issue the sole basis for reversal; the identified intraclass conflicts independently suffice.

The strongest affirmance argument combines the concrete contrary findings with the distinction from Amchem: the settlement contains no fixed disease-category awards, all class members eventually receive individualized valuation, insurance uncertainty threatens both groups, the Trilateral agreement removed the inventory contingency, and a guardian reviewed the bargain. Those points may show economic benefit and diligence. They do not align conflicting preferences over the funding, cap, conservation and release provisions already negotiated. Amchem’s rule applies to (a)(4) in each (b) category, and the (b)(1)(B) premise that claimants compete for scarce resources makes representation necessary rather than automatically adequate. Equal treatment is one contested allocation choice, not proof that no allocation occurred.

Stevens and Breyer agree that “common interests outweigh conflicts” and “all members are treated alike” do not supply the legal answer. They select a different appellate consequence. Their Amchem position gives substantive weight to the agreement’s operation and the district court’s detailed findings and favors appellate application under the correct standard. The 1998 majority’s reliance on uniform awards and a supposed disappearance of all uncommon questions does not perform that review. They would vacate and require it, considering the preserved phased negotiations, later Trilateral protection, future-payment mechanics and insurance distinctions. They would neither assume that the settlement is adequate because it is beneficial nor decide that the six Justices’ structural conclusion follows on the present appellate treatment. This is a specific remedial reservation, not an unqualified affirmance preference.

## Limited-fund construction: precise common ground and reserved extension

O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg would adopt this confined proposition if the issue is discussed: **a defendant’s and insurers’ negotiated willingness to contribute a stated amount, combined with the attractiveness of resolving claims, does not by itself establish Rule 23(b)(1)(B)’s qualifying risk of disposition or substantial impairment of other claimants’ interests. A proposed mandatory limited-fund class must be justified by the actual limiting resource and the relation of the class treatment to the interests said to require collective protection, independently of the bargain’s own ceiling.** This is a proposed construction in this case, grounded in the Rule’s language, traditional unitary limited-resource adjudication and the Enabling Act; it is not mislabeled entering law.

The resource here cannot simply be equated with $1.535 billion because the parties agreed to that number. The lower court’s predictions concerning $2 billion plus corporate value must be distinguished from the smaller Global trust and its releases. The legal review must account for the disputed policies without pretending they have an aggregate ceiling, the noninsurance value left outside the trust, and the inventory claims excluded by the class definition. These matters are relevant to whether the plan collectively addresses the asserted limited resource; they do not establish that every previously settled claimant must be forced into every later class or that every corporate asset must invariably be surrendered.

The six do **not** rely on “no asset findings” or treat the class definition as proof of a complete inventory of all legally eligible claimants. They do not presently require a universal all-assets condition, proof of insolvency to certainty, a preexisting segregated res in every case, or categorical exclusion of disputed insurance and potential insolvency from the Rule. A comprehensive traditional-category or all-assets rule would require a distinct supported construction and proposition-level determination. None is needed for their independently sufficient adequacy disposition, and no such comprehensive formula is frozen here by implication.

Stevens and Breyer accept that a cap cannot prove itself, but reserve the six Justices’ more specific construction if it is understood to require independent redetermination of insurance value or availability beyond proper review of the evidence already taken. The risk/practical-impairment wording can encompass prospective insolvency rather than only a fixed segregated res. The lower court’s credited evidence of depletion is therefore legally relevant. Their remand would require the court of appeals to distinguish the actual findings from the settlement’s ceiling and apply the Rule without an invented certainty requirement. They withhold a present ultimate conclusion that the demonstrated financial risk necessarily fails (b)(1)(B).

The strongest broad-certification path rests on that risk wording, the contemporaneous Advisory Committee discussion quoted below, supported depletion findings and insurance-litigation risk. It fails as an automatic affirmance ground because (a)(4) remains independent and financial benefit cannot supply lawful representation. The strongest categorical traditional-fund path emphasizes a unitary fund, equitable claimant treatment and preservation of substantive rights. It cannot be treated as preexisting controlling law or applied as if the trial court made no financial inquiry. The present six-Justice sufficient ground avoids deciding whether every element of that broader construction should be adopted.

## Judgment components and compatible joins

All eight support the antecedent nonmerits route, continued force of actual Rule 23 requirements, the distinction between uniform processing and adequate protection of materially conflicting bargaining interests, correction of the lower legal analysis, and the conclusion that the existing appellate affirmance cannot remain in force on that analysis.

Six—O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg—support reversal of the reviewed certification/approval affirmance with a conclusive holding that this undivided representative structure fails (a)(4), followed by remand. Stevens and Breyer support vacatur and remand for proper review, withholding that conclusive application. Their votes share removal of the affirmance but are not joins in a finding of final inadequacy, and the different remand instructions must not be conflated. The Chief’s position and any final Court judgment are not supplied here.

The limited-fund discussion is not silently elevated into an independently sufficient alternative holding. The six support the confined construction above; they presently resolve the judgment through adequacy without deciding every disputed financial and equitable predicate. If a later authorized opinion makes (b)(1)(B) independently dispositive or adds an all-assets requirement, that is a material proposition requiring supported affected modeling and reconciliation, not editorial implementation.

No present position directs a replacement subclass architecture. Adequacy may be supplied in a materially different proposal by a lawful structure that actually protects the competing interests, but this Court does not negotiate its terms. If only the shared legal-rule and corrective-remand portions are written, Stevens and Breyer can join those exact portions while preserving their application reservation. They do not become full joiners in an opinion declaring the actual representative structure conclusively inadequate.

## Remaining constitutional questions, remedy and source limits

Rule 23 supplies sufficient grounds to set aside or require reconsideration of certification. The six need not decide a universal constitutional opt-out requirement for limited funds, the notice rights of each future claimant, all personal-jurisdiction objections, every derivative claimant’s injury, or substantive tort liability. Richards and Hansberry provide the constitutional background for adequate representation without making every federal rule requirement constitutional. The two remand proponents likewise do not authorize affirmative substantive relief without the needed jurisdiction and representative authority.

Removal of this certification prevents the existing arrangement from binding through it. No disposition orders insurers to contribute a specified sum, adjudicates individual damages, compels Fibreboard bankruptcy, declares an improved settlement approved, or automatically voids every provision of the separate Trilateral agreement or Rudd judgment. Remand concerns the reviewed Global certification and its approval; ancillary releases and injunctions dependent on that certification must receive corresponding treatment within the actual judgment’s scope. No unsupported general injunction or special mandate date is added.

The permitted lower opinions establish the facts used here, including the class definition, actual financial findings, negotiation phases, insurance dispute, trust limits and different proceedings. The exact quoted Rule 23(a)(4), (b)(1)(B) and §2072(b) text, together with controlling simulated law, supports the core analysis. The missing separately recovered complete Rule instrument is not a defect in a litigant’s file and does not support a production order. Any result depending materially on exact unquoted language would need that specific supplement. Completed Albertson’s and Kolstad law changes no certification or representation predicate. Final baseline completeness awaits the controller’s clean dated release.
