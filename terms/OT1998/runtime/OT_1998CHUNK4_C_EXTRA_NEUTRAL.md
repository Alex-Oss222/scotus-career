## John Crane, Inc. v. Abate, No. 98-873

### Posture and the actual adjudication below

The supplied event is post-grant merits consideration on February 18, 1999, without a newly invented argument date or grant text. The federal question is whether this state's consolidated trial afforded the defendant due process, not whether all consolidation is forbidden or whether Federal Rule 23 governed the trial.

The Maryland Court of Special Appeals' *ACandS, Inc. v. Abate*, 121 Md. App. 590, 710 A.2d 944 (1998), reviewed final judgments involving **five trial plaintiffs**, while common issues affecting approximately 1,300 other plaintiffs remained for later individual mini-trials. Its preliminary finality analysis, petition appendix 9a–13a, appears in [C_VALIDATED_SOURCE_PASSAGES](../sources/chunk4_c/C_VALIDATED_SOURCE_PASSAGES.md). Common-issue rulings alone did not dispose of an entire damages claim under Maryland Rule 2-602(b). They were reviewable in this appeal only insofar as they bore on the final trial judgments or demonstrated a defect in the consolidation. **There was no final liability-and-damages judgment for each of the 1,300 claimants.**

The appellate mandate, appendix 108a–109a, reversed specified judgments against U.S. Mineral, Stebbing and Hampshire for insufficient evidence; vacated Ciotta's judgment for reduction under the damages cap; vacated specified Hampshire judgments for proceedings concerning the predominant-purpose instruction; directed corresponding judgment-share adjustments; and otherwise affirmed trial-plaintiff judgments. Crane's final judgments are not erased by relief granted to other defendants. Nor does review of Crane's due-process objection reopen every other party's separate judgment or issue.

The [Maryland highest-court order](../sources/chunk4_c/ABATE_STATE_REVIEW_ORDER.md), petition appendix 137a, establishes that the Maryland Court of Appeals denied petitions for discretionary review. The clean image has been checked: it prints July 26, 1998. The source receipt reports a conflicting 1998 date elsewhere in the petition, which this preflight has not independently read or resolved. The denial itself and its occurrence before the supplied February 18, 1999 event are supported; no exact federal filing deadline is computed from the unresolved typography. The intermediate decision is therefore not silently treated as a final state judgment without checking availability of further state review. The supplied post-grant merits premise and actual final trial judgments support the ordinary §1257 channel; this date discrepancy alone proves no untimely filing.

### Supported record, not a statistical verdict for absent plaintiffs

Maryland Rule 2-503(a)(1), quoted at the start of the [complete lower Part I](../sources/chunk4_c/ABATE_LOWER_PART_I_EXACT.md), allows a joint hearing, trial or consolidation of any or all claims, issues or actions sharing a common legal or factual question or subject matter. That procedural authorization remains subject to due process. Maryland Rule 2-522(c) permits special findings appropriate to the pleadings and evidence; the state court reviewed form under its abuse-of-discretion standard. Neither state rule authorizes loss of a meaningful defense to a material issue.

The lower court described three phases. Phase I combined common issues against relevant defendants with the trial plaintiffs' individual claims, and included some cross-claims. Phase II resolved earlier Abate I third-party claims and other designated cross-claims; Phase III concerned punitive damages. Evidence pertinent to more than one phase was received together to avoid repeated witnesses. The number of phases alone says nothing about the constitutional adequacy of the boundaries.

The strongest supported defense objection is concrete: many defendants, cross-defendants, products, worksites, time periods and diseases were presented together; the plaintiffs selected the five trial claimants, including two mesothelioma cases although those cases had been severed from the common-issue group; the product-category forms could obscure differences in fiber release, encapsulation, warnings and relevant time periods; and erroneous verdicts might reveal a loss of individualized consideration. Petitioners' description of over 10,000 claims is an account of direct and cross-claim complexity, not proof of 10,000 final plaintiff recoveries. See [the separate petition and opposition statements](../sources/chunk4_c/PRIMARY_SOURCE_EXTRACTS.md), original petition lines 510–641 and opposition lines 191–303.

The lower court's contrary grounds are equally material. It relied on actual common state-of-knowledge evidence, attorney identification of each represented client before examination, opening explanations and jury instructions differentiating earlier defendants from presently contested defendants, and the preservation of later individualized exposure and causation proceedings. It concluded that the selection of trial plaintiffs served illustration rather than statistical representation or extrapolation of each claimant's injuries. It examined the five claimants' workplaces, jobs, exposures and diseases rather than assuming that the word “illustrative” made them adequate class representatives. Those are the lower court's findings and reasons; they are not an independent constitutional conclusion of this preflight.

The common-issue verdicts determined defendant-specific negligence and product-category defect, including relevant dates; the later juries were **not** to retry those determinations. They were to determine each claimant's exposure to the defendant's product, disease, legally sufficient causation and damages. Thus “mini-trials remain” is not by itself a complete answer: the reviewing court must identify whether the issues conclusively resolved were supported genuinely common issues or whether a contested individual defense was already lost.

### Category evidence, Crane-specific facts and actual errors

The lower court's defense of product categories was expressly record-bound. It relied on testimony that visible dust from an asbestos-containing product signified excessive exposure, the absence of directed contrary evidence for the products implicated, and the need for each trial or future claimant to prove actual exposure to such dust and resulting harm. It acknowledged that products differ in danger and that all asbestos products cannot simply be grouped together. Its footnote 27 did **not** find that a product incapable of emitting respirable fibers could never exist; it said no such product was identified as implicated in this appeal. A later nonemitting or materially different product cannot be declared defective solely by expanding that finding.

Crane's verdict sheets distinguished **packing** from **gaskets** and found negligence/defect for packing but no liability for gaskets. Crane argued that Theis and Glensky described only gaskets, making the individual verdicts inconsistent. The lower court identified the admitted Crane catalog listing sheet packing from which gaskets could be cut, together with testimony that the material was cut from sheets and generated dust. It therefore read those verdicts as supported by packing exposure, not as an unexplained contradiction. This catalog explanation is supplied at appendix 39a–40a; it cannot be omitted when evaluating Crane's strongest record-specific objection.

Errors nevertheless occurred. The appellate court ordered correction of the starting liability dates for Stebbing and Hampshire to conform to uncontroverted product-use evidence. It distinguished Rapid's continuing warning duty for earlier products from liability for a different successor's post-1967 conduct. It described Harbison-Walker errors corrected by judgment notwithstanding the verdict and separately reversed exposure findings against certain defendants because the relied-on “fiber drift” inference did not meet Maryland causation requirements. It regarded these errors as remediable without proving systemic jury incapacity. A neutral assessment must preserve both propositions: errors were found and corrected; whether they demonstrate a constitutional aggregation defect is contested. Neither “no errors” nor “errors automatically invalidate everything” accurately states the record.

### Entering law, alternatives and remedy limits

*Richards v. Jefferson County*, June 10, 1996, preserves due-process limits on binding persons without a constitutionally sufficient relationship and rejects common objectives as a substitute. Its exact holdings distinguish genuine representative arrangements and ordinary state procedure from the federal Rule 23 device. It does not declare every common-issue proceeding unconstitutional. Its §1257 finality holding turns on the actually conclusive federal claim and preordained implementation, not a universal permission to review interlocutory state rulings.

*Amchem Products v. Windsor*, June 25, 1997, independently requires representative adequacy and predominance for its **Rule 23(b)(3) settlement class** and preserves individual proof and governing-law differences. It does not impose that federal certification rule on Maryland Rule 2-503 consolidation. Its warning about conflicts and individualized claims can inform the analysis without turning a settlement holding into a categorical ban on trials of genuinely common issues. The defendant's opportunity to contest evidence and material defenses, the exact binding issues and the individual proceedings actually preserved must do the legal work.

The current-term *Felzen* public rule, January 20, 1999, distinguishes nonparty appeal status from constitutional authority to bind; it does not cure defective representation or supply a party-status obstacle for Crane as an actual judgment defendant. The [neutral projection](../workspace/neutral-projection.md#california-public-employees-retirement-system-and-florida-state-board-of-administration-v-paul-felzen-et-al--1999-01-20) supplies that limited update.

Supported decisional paths include:

- Sustain the reviewed judgments against the due-process objection on a record-specific account of common proof, differentiated verdicts, actual defense opportunities and preserved individual causation, while leaving different products, proceedings and future applications open.
- Find that a demonstrated binding category determination or other identified procedure deprived Crane of a material defense despite later mini-trials, and reverse or vacate only the affected judgment components with a remand fitted to that defect. Trial size and efficiency alone do not answer either side.
- If a material uncertainty concerns the actual preclusive reach of a common finding, require lawful clarification or reconsideration rather than invent a universal factual determination. Any remand must identify the exact issue; missing archival possession alone does not justify discovery or retrial.
- Limit review or dispose of a genuine channel defect without deciding constitutional merits. An anticipated future common-issue application supplies no advisory jurisdiction over every claimant.

The supplied issue is the Fourteenth Amendment objection. The lower opinion records a reply-stage Seventh Amendment reexamination argument, but that reference neither enlarges the question granted nor makes the federal civil-jury provision automatically applicable to state trials. Preservation, scope and applicable constitutional law must be established before making that a separate holding.

## National Collegiate Athletic Association v. Smith, No. 98-84

### Posture and relief must be stated at the pleading stage

The supplied event is merits consideration on February 23, 1999; argument occurred on the supplied January 20, 1999 date. The Third Circuit decision, 139 F.3d 180 (March 16, 1998), concerns dismissal and leave to amend, **not a finding that Smith proved discrimination or that the NCAA owed damages**.

The [clean lower discussion](../sources/chunk4_c/NCAA_CLEAN_INPUT.md#third-circuit-title-ix-discussion-and-conclusion-petition-appendix-12a20a), appendix 12a–20a, establishes the precise route. The majority agreed that the original complaint failed to allege receipt of federal assistance; Judge McKee would have read the pro se complaint more liberally. The district court had denied amendment as “moot” after dismissal. The appellate court held that postjudgment amendment was discretionary rather than available as of right, but that dismissal alone did not make the motion moot; it relied on Rule 15 and *Foman v. Davis*, 371 U.S. 178 (1962), including futility, undue delay, bad faith, dilatory motive and prejudice as legitimate grounds.

The appellate court treated Smith's proposed indirect-recipient allegation as encompassing dues from federally funded members and held that allegation legally sufficient. It therefore **vacated the Title IX dismissal, reversed denial of leave to amend, and remanded**; it also directed reinstatement of the supplemental state contract claim. It affirmed dismissal of the Sherman Act claim. Neither the antitrust merits nor the contract merits is within the supplied Title IX coverage question merely because those claims appeared below.

Smith seeks monetary and declaratory relief for the alleged denial of volleyball eligibility and discriminatory refusal of waivers. The end of the seasons does not by itself moot a live legally available compensation claim. Conversely, coverage alone proves no discrimination, causation, recipient responsibility, compensable loss or right to a prospective order. The statutory recipient issue must not be labeled an Article III defect solely because some pleading papers use “jurisdiction” loosely; *Steel Co.* preserves the distinction between federal jurisdiction and a disputed statutory liability element.

### Facts and three separate coverage routes

The clean party excerpts are preserved in [PRIMARY_SOURCE_EXTRACTS](../sources/chunk4_c/PRIMARY_SOURCE_EXTRACTS.md) and [NCAA_CLEAN_INPUT](../sources/chunk4_c/NCAA_CLEAN_INPUT.md). Smith played two undergraduate seasons at St. Bonaventure, completed that degree, and pursued programs at Hofstra and Pittsburgh. The rule allowed a student with remaining eligibility to compete while undertaking specified further study **at the same institution**, subject to the applicable five-year or ten-semester limit. Smith alleges that she met other eligibility conditions and that waivers were denied discriminatorily. No coverage determination establishes that allegation.

The NCAA describes itself as a voluntary association of approximately 1,200 members, with approximately $200 million in other annual revenues and $900,000 in dues. Those figures are its briefing account; their relative size does not itself supply or defeat a statutory recipient threshold. Its assertion that it receives no federal aid is disputed in legal significance and must not be adopted as an uncontested finding.

The distinct routes are:

1. **Membership dues:** Whether ordinary dues paid by federally assisted institutions make the association itself a recipient of federal assistance. The lower court relied on indirect receipt and its view that the NCAA acts as a surrogate for member athletics, rather than an incidental commercial beneficiary.
2. **Actual NYSP assistance:** Whether the NCAA directly or indirectly receives assistance through its administration of the National Youth Sports Program or relationship to the NYSP Fund. Smith invokes agency letters and other date-eligible lower litigation; she cites her appellate argument that the program receives direct funding. These assertions and citations show a real alternative contention, not a verified finding of the grant recipient, transfer mechanism or extent of NCAA control.
3. **Delegated control:** Whether the members' delegation of control over federally funded athletics independently subjects the NCAA to Title IX. Its rulemaking and enforcement role is established as background, but its legal characterization is disputed. NCAA constitutional provisions retaining institutional responsibility and its sometimes-adverse relationship with members are relevant contrary material. A general rulemaking role is not automatically the same factual proposition as complete control over receipt and administration of federal aid.

The lower court discussed surrogate status in support of its **dues** rationale. It did not separately adjudicate the complete NYSP factual route or an independently sufficient control-only theory. The parties dispute preservation and the significance of the Marshall affidavit. The clean source set supports acknowledging and preserving those issues for an appropriate lower-court determination; it does not support declaring either alternative proven, definitively waived, or automatically available as a new claim.

### Statute, regulation and operative law

The official [1997 §1681 text](../sources/chunk4_c/usc_1997_20_1681.plain.txt) makes receipt of federal financial assistance part of the prohibition on sex discrimination under an education program or activity. Its admissions, religious, military, specified membership and other exceptions remain part of the statute, but none is identified as a live defense to this dues question. Section 1681(b) rejects required preference solely from numerical imbalance while allowing relevant statistical evidence; it does not itself resolve the alleged waiver discrimination.

The [1997 §1687 text](../sources/chunk4_c/usc_1997_20_1687.plain.txt) defines a covered program or activity through the operations of specified entities, including colleges and universities and an entity established by two or more listed entities, **any part of which receives assistance**. Its religious qualification remains. The Civil Rights Restoration Act's institution-wide definition and its express nonextension to previously excluded ultimate beneficiaries must be read together; enlarging the operations covered once an entity receives aid is distinct from making every economically connected entity a recipient.

The official [1998 regulation](../sources/chunk4_c/cfr_1998_34_106_2.txt), [primary PDF](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-1998-title34-vol1/pdf/CFR-1998-title34-vol1-sec106-2.pdf), §106.2(h), defines a recipient as an identified person or entity **to whom assistance is extended directly or through another recipient and which operates an education program or activity receiving or benefiting from that assistance**, including subunits, successors, assignees and transferees. The first requirement cannot be silently dropped when quoting the second. Section 106.2(g) describes assistance through grants, loans, specified property or personnel support and other assistance arrangements, with its insurance/guaranty exception. That definition does not say that all payments made by a recipient are themselves federal assistance.

*Grove City College v. Bell*, 465 U.S. 555 (1984), supplies indirect receipt through federal student aid intended for payment to the college; Congress later broadened program scope through §1687. *Department of Transportation v. Paralyzed Veterans of America*, 477 U.S. 597 (1986), distinguishes receipt from economic benefit in the analogous §504 wording and connects coverage to acceptance of assistance. The NCAA invokes that distinction; Smith invokes the dues' educational-governance purpose, the regulatory language and the association's institutional relationship to distinguish the airline example. The Third Circuit's actual treatment of both authorities is in the clean lower opinion. The regulatory phrase “benefits from” must be analyzed in its whole sentence rather than treated as automatic displacement of the recipient condition.

The [operative Kelley and Gebser holdings](../entering-law/OT_1998CHUNK4_C_READING_SELECTIONS.md) preserve the funding-recipient premise but do not decide dues, NYSP receipt or control-only coverage. *Kelley*, May 22, 1995, concerns equal athletic opportunity within covered institutions and does not guarantee every particular team. *Gebser*, June 22, 1998, concerns attribution of intentional discrimination to an established recipient; its particular school-conferred-authority and recipient-fault route is not a general definition of who receives assistance. Its preserved *Pennhurst* funding-notice principle and *Franklin v. Gwinnett County* compensation principle do not prove receipt or damages here. Their precise coalition and authority limits remain those in the reading copy; no complete framework or later rule is imported.

### Supported paths and limits of the mandate

- A dues-based coverage holding can sustain the legal sufficiency of the proposed allegation and the appropriate amendment/remand result, while leaving the funding facts, discrimination and remedy to later proceedings. It must explain why the particular transfer constitutes assistance rather than ordinary downstream expenditure, and how the entire regulatory definition and *Paralyzed Veterans* are satisfied or distinguished.
- Rejection of dues alone can remove the lower court's legal premise without deciding that the NCAA can never be covered. An appropriate vacatur or reversal and ordinary remand may permit the lower courts to address **properly preserved** NYSP or control theories, amendment standards and the resulting supplemental-jurisdiction consequences. It supplies no automatic right to amend again, no instruction that an unproved theory succeeds, and no reopening of the concluded antitrust issue.
- A request for final rejection or final acceptance of an alternative requires its actual preservation, the relevant allegation/evidence and the correct procedural standard. The existing clean excerpts do not settle the NYSP grant mechanics or all facts of delegated control; a merits finding on those facts would require controlled extraction of the pertinent affidavit, funding/organizational documents and preserved pleadings. That conditional need does not block deciding the dues question or returning genuinely unresolved issues under ordinary procedure.
