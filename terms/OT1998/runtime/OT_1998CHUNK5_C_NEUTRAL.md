## Gable v. Patton / Patton v. Gable — Nos. 98-897 and 98-1072

### Verified posture, statutory operation, and scope

The Sixth Circuit's April 30, 1998 judgment, 142 F.3d 940, affirms a mixed summary judgment in a facial challenge. Its complete opinion and Cudahy's partial dissent are in [the exact archived opinion extract](../sources/chunk5_c/gable_sixth_exact.txt), copied from petition Appendix A, pp. 1a–27a. The district court decision and partial declaratory judgment are included in the same original petition, Appendices B and C. Gable was a nonparticipating candidate in the 1995 Republican gubernatorial primary. State enforcement officers and the Registry are defendants. The lower court upheld the responsive-financing trigger and the 28-day rule for outside contributions; it invalidated the latter rule insofar as it prohibits **nonparticipating candidates' own-money financing**. The cross-petition seeks reversal of that last component.

**Scope resolved.** The [mechanically copied supplied question](../runtime/OT_1998CHUNK5_GABLE_SCOPE_EXACT.md) controls: the financing trigger and whether the 28-day contribution ban may reach a candidate's own funds. The broader lower-review description does not expand that question. Outside-donor contributions are statutory context only; their independent validity is outside the supplied grant and remains reserved. Sponsor identification and compulsory gubernatorial slating also remain outside review. The outside-contribution judgment is left undisturbed within this review, without a new endorsement of its merits. Both dockets and the own-funds cross-petition retain their distinct requested relief.

**Correct the shorthand “public funds matching an opponent's expenditures.”** The enacted operation is not dollar-for-dollar matching of the nonparticipant's expenditures. Ordinarily a participating slate raises qualifying private contributions from $300,000 to $600,000, receives a two-for-one public match, and accepts a $1.8 million ceiling separately for primary and regular election. The lower opinion also reports that an opposing slate must raise at least $300,000 before a participant receives matching funds (§121A.060(13)), preventing payment in an uncontested race. For a runoff the lower opinion identifies a $300,000 ceiling/trigger and fourteen-day window; that is not the regular-election rule being challenged here.

The petition reproduces §121A.030(5)(a) and §121A.080(4)–(5): after the relevant Registry certification that a nonparticipating slate has **received contributions or made expenditures** above the ceiling, the participating ceiling is lifted and further qualifying contributions are eligible for the same two-for-one public match. Section 121A.080(4)(a)'s quoted route requires a factual finding after a public hearing with notice to all slates; subsection (5) separately permits written notification of exceeding the limit. Do not describe receipt alone as the only statutory trigger, or omit certification from a purported exact account.

The Sixth Circuit construes activation to lift the 28-day window for **all** slates. The Governor's cross-petition repeats that construction. Gable disputes it in petition p. 12 n.2, arguing that the quoted subsection addresses qualifying participating slates. Preserve this as a statutory-construction dispute: it is not an agreed finding that only subsidized slates regain fundraising rights. The lower construction is the operation reviewed unless the Court lawfully resolves that question otherwise. The saved [statutory quotation extract](../sources/chunk5_c/gable_statutory_petition_excerpt_exact.txt) retains the petition's ellipses and OCR defects; it is not certified as the complete statute. Published recitations adequately establish the particular adjudicated operation under Engine §14; an opinion purporting to settle every unquoted exception would require further text verification.

The 28-day command prohibits knowing acceptance of new campaign contributions during the final 28 days before primary or regular election. It reaches a candidate's deposit of personal funds, but does not bar spending funds already in the campaign account. The original window was thirty days and was amended in 1996 to twenty-eight to coincide with the reporting date. These facts matter to the State's notice/administration argument; they do not establish that a ban is only a reporting requirement. The lower majority identifies the real cost of committing funds before the final campaign develops, nonliquidity, activation of the trigger by an early deposit, and restrictions on loans/recovery of unused money. Its footnote 11 quotes an unexpended-funds rule described as applying to participating slates; do not indiscriminately apply that rule to nonparticipants. The general personal-funds burden does not depend on silently importing that disputed application.

### Entering law and strongest supported alternatives

**Day v. Holahan (May 15, 1995)** is indispensable and is not a categorical controlling rule against responsive financing. Its responsive-financing judgment was 5–4. Kennedy's plurality, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, and Thomas, treated the programmed proportional adverse benefit as a legal speech burden. The judgment concurrence supplied a different ground. No common rationale controls under Marks. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer would uphold that trigger because the independent speaker retained uncapped spending and public support enlarged advocacy, while reserving materially coercive or punitive arrangements. The separate independently majority-supported Day holdings on special notice, intermediary contributions, and MCCL remain controlling at their own scope. No numerical comparison of Minnesota's trigger to Kentucky resolves this case.

**Buckley v. Valeo** preserves the contribution/expenditure distinction, protection of personal expenditures, lawful voluntary public financing, scrutiny of contribution/disclosure rules, and rejection of equalizing resources as a sufficient basis to suppress advocacy. **Colorado Republican** requires attention to an actual candidate-resource transaction rather than treating independent expression as a contribution. **Kruse** unanimously rejects a source-independent total candidate-spending ceiling on its accepted record; adequate remaining speech and campaign-cost/equalization claims do not cure that ceiling. It expressly reserves voluntary financing. **Suster** unanimously applies demanding expenditure review to a judicial candidate's total spending ceiling after separately evaluating impartiality; institutional interest is not categorically ignored, but the particular fit must be shown. **Buckley v. ACLF** preserves differentiated review for speech, administration, and disclosures; it does not decide late contribution prohibitions. **Kentucky Right to Life** preserves fitted contribution controls and actual-source analysis without a permanent dollar safe harbor.

For the **trigger**, the challenger can argue that Kentucky conditions unlimited responsive subsidies and release from restrictions on his exercise of protected fundraising/spending, creates a state-imposed disincentive distinct from an ordinary unconditional subsidy, and cannot justify that burden through equalization. The State can answer that participation remains voluntary, the nonparticipant retains his own funds and right to spend, the public payment depends on the participant's own qualifying fundraising, and preserving a usable voluntary anticorruption program is a legitimate purpose. Each side must confront the other: no speaker has a right to absence of competing public-supported speech, but a subsidy label does not immunize practical coercion. The factual basis for actual coercion or program necessity cannot be manufactured from legislative generalities. The difference from Day's proportional response to independent expenditures must be tested, not ignored.

For **personal funds during the window**, the challenger invokes Buckley, Kruse, and Suster: forbidding new own-money advocacy at the decisive campaign stage is a substantive speech burden despite the word “contribution”; a candidate cannot corrupt himself, and facilitating a subsidy system or giving opponents time to answer is not automatically sufficient. The State's strongest alternative, articulated in Cudahy's dissent and the cross-petition, is a neutral timing condition that preserves unlimited predeposited expenditure, permits advance notice/fundraising, and makes the trigger operational before the final election. It must answer why timely disclosure or another less restrictive administration mechanism is insufficient and why the window's responsive-opportunity objective does not become forbidden equalization. The challenger must address the State's real program-administration objective rather than falsely claim that every timing rule is a dollar ceiling.

**Outside-contribution context, not a separate question:** the lower court treated rearranged fundraising as justified by the program's anticorruption function while acknowledging that Buckley's marginal-restriction rationale for large donor limits does not directly resolve a complete late ban. Gable's objection to small late gifts and the State's notice/response argument explain the statutory system. They do not enlarge the own-funds question. A disposition on personal funds neither decides nor necessarily invalidates the outside-donor branch. Remedy must distinguish the trigger, ceiling release, subsidy entitlement, own-funds window, ordinary disclosure, and voluntary participation; severability and state legislative intent require their own supported treatment.

The exact archived text of **Wis. Stat. §119.23 (1995–96)** is [preserved separately](../sources/chunk5_c/jackson_statute_exact.txt), Appendix pp. 192a–195a. A pupil in kindergarten through grade 12 residing in Milwaukee must meet the 1.75-times-federal-poverty income ceiling and the stated prior-enrollment condition: district enrollment, existing program attendance, K–3 private-school enrollment, or no school enrollment in the previous year. The school must give its participation/space notice by May 1 of the prior school year, comply with §2000d racial nondiscrimination and public-school health/safety requirements. Program enrollment is capped at seven percent in 1995–96 and fifteen percent from 1996–97. Selection is random, with a permitted sibling preference. These are actual statutory qualifications, not a judicial finding that every eligible family has an available seat at every desired school.

**Payment is not simply a tuition reimbursement.** Under subsection (4), after proof of enrollment, the Department pays the lower of the MPS state-aid amount per member or the private school's per-pupil educational operating and debt-service cost. It pays in four 25-percent installments in September, November, February, and May. A check is payable to the parent/guardian, sent to the school, and restrictively endorsed for the school's use. The formula does not cap payment at the tuition the school otherwise charges. Trial findings and stipulated materials describe many participating religious schools as charging tuition below actual cost because of church subsidies; state payment may replace that difference. Do not turn the Wisconsin opinion's shorthand about tuition into a nonexistent statutory ceiling.

Subsection (7)(c) bars requiring a participating pupil to engage in religious activity **if the parent or guardian submits a written exemption request to the teacher or principal**. It does not categorically prohibit religious instruction, automatically opt out every pupil, or require that funded educational costs be secularly segregated. Participating schools must meet at least one of four specified performance criteria (70-percent grade advancement, 90-percent attendance, 80-percent significant academic progress, or 70-percent family involvement under school criteria); they are subject to uniform accounting and annual independent financial audit. Subsection (7)(b) provides performance monitoring and next-year exclusion for failure to meet a criterion. The 1995 amendments removed the old annual evaluation/report and particular Superintendent audit authorities, while preserving these other duties and adding a Legislative Audit Bureau evaluation due January 15, 2000. Do not say all oversight disappeared or that the retained audit is a secular-use restriction.

The record includes agreed school mission/handbook materials, described as accurately representing their purposes and operation. The handoff identifies the trial discussion at pp. 147a–152a. The [available exact copied excerpt](../sources/chunk5_c/jackson_integrated_curriculum_exact.txt) begins within p. 149a and continues through p. 152a; it preserves concrete integrated-curriculum examples and the payment-versus-tuition point, without certifying that the earlier pages were copied into this safe excerpt. ### Current-law replacement and competing paths

The historical lower opinion uses Lemon's three independent prongs and treats historical Zobrest as an aid-merits holding. **Neither is current law here.** The controlling **Agostini (June 23, 1997)** preserves Lemon through one inquiry: a specific prohibition/distinct doctrine; an established permissible relationship; otherwise a legally sufficient unresolved claim examined through attribution and function. Purpose, effect, and entanglement are evidence. For expenditure, the object is what government purchases or performs, without attributing every recipient activity to government. Publicly controlled, separately administered secular work may be purchased with reliable controls against devotional use/diversion. Ordinary monitoring is not itself forbidden entanglement. Neutral recipient selection alone does not excuse devotional financing. The decision expressly reserves tuition-voucher/provider-redemption programs and preserves Witters and Mueller at their actual scope. Zobrest supplies **no Establishment Clause merits holding**.

**Rosenberger** permits a real financial printing benefit, not merely free space. Broad neutral availability, independently edited and attributed student expression, and controlled vendor payments do joint work. It supplies no rule that every public payment creates a forum or that all neutral payments may buy devotional instruction. **Pinette, Chabad, and Bronx Household** protect genuinely private expression/access within their actual contexts. A rented school forum does not decide financing of an integrated curriculum. **Nyquist, Witters, Mueller, Everson, Allen, and surviving Ball components** must be distinguished by the service/benefit and attribution they actually decided, not mechanically classified by the number of intermediaries. Agostini displaces Ball's corresponding Shared Time rationale, not its distinct Community Education judgment.

**Path supporting the program:** treat the neutral educational entitlement, authentic parental choice among meaningful public/nonsectarian/sectarian alternatives, and independent school operation as a permissible private-benefit relationship analogous at its material level to Witters/Mueller; the public action is purchasing educational opportunity for the child rather than selecting religious instruction as governmental work. The strongest counterargument is that this restricted cost-based payment buys the school's integrated instructional service, without secular-use controls, and private routing cannot answer Agostini's reserved expenditure question. The path must explain that difference and Nyquist, not merely quote historical Zobrest or assert that parental choice ends the inquiry.

**Path against the challenged religious-instruction financing:** apply Agostini's retained prohibition on public purchase of devotional function to the actual cost-based benefit where supported record facts show integrated devotional instruction and no enforceable separation. Parental selection and a restrictive endorsement do not change the function purchased; neutral eligibility and an opt-out on written request cannot themselves make every funded instructional activity secular. The strongest counterargument is that private choice and ordinary educational support do not make the private school's speech governmental, and preserved Witters/Mueller permit aid reaching religion. The path must explain the particular expenditure rather than adopt a categorical religious-provider exclusion or treat every religious viewpoint as devotion.

**Narrower path:** if either application depends on an unresolved fact about a materially separable funded service, the nature of the claimed facial component, or available relief, vacate the federal disposition and remand under current Agostini with supported limits. No new evidence or hearing is automatically required merely because the legal test changed. The record's integrated-curriculum materials cannot be erased; they also do not support declaring every religious school's every expense devotional. Severability remains a state-law task if a partial federal violation is found. A permissible secular-services path is different from compelling schools to restructure theology or their ordinary private curriculum. Preserve lawful secular schools/services and the reserved state-law issues; no general parental subsidy entitlement, monetary award, student transfer, or instantaneous school closure follows.

## National Committee of the Reform Party v. Democratic National Committee

### Verified posture and statutory corrections

The complete Ninth Circuit opinion, 168 F.3d 360, February 9, 1999, is saved as [reform_ninth.txt](../sources/chunk5_c/reform_ninth.txt), from the Public.Resource.Org reporter reproduction and cross-checked against OpenJurist. District Judge Walker dismissed on March 2, 1998. Plaintiffs include the national committee, 1996 campaign committees, California party, and voter John Place; **Perot himself is not a plaintiff**. Defendants include the national/state major-party organizations, campaign entities, and FEC. The opinion distinguishes damages, FEC membership, financing, and certification. The approved grant includes financing and FEC membership; the other damages judgments remain background and do not become new questions.

The financing record reports approximately $29 million for the 1996 Reform campaign and $62 million for each major party, based on prior presidential support. The asserted as-applied defect is inability, with the $1,000 individual candidate-contribution limit, to make up the funding gap. The appellate court says plaintiffs did **not** allege that these constraints reduced their voting strength or ability to field candidates in the different manner reserved in Buckley. Preserve this as its description of the pleaded claim, not a judicial finding that the program can never impair minor parties.

The official **1998 Code, enacted through January 5, 1999**, is saved; exact sections [9002](../sources/chunk5_c/usc1998_26_9002_exact.txt), [9003](../sources/chunk5_c/usc1998_26_9003_exact.txt), [9004](../sources/chunk5_c/usc1998_26_9004_exact.txt), and [9011](../sources/chunk5_c/usc1998_26_9011_exact.txt) are mechanically extracted. These correct two significant shorthand errors in the lower opinion:

* A major party has **25 percent or more** of the preceding presidential vote; a minor party has **at least 5 percent but less than 25 percent**. New parties are neither. The minor pre-election amount uses its prior votes divided by the average prior major-party votes, multiplied by the major entitlement. Section 9004(a)(2)(B) supplies a candidate-based continuity route; (a)(3) permits post-election entitlement for a minor/new candidate reaching five percent, with the stated credit for pre-election entitlement. Do not say a new party can never obtain public funds.
* Section **9003(c)** permits a minor/new-party candidate's qualified expenses up to the **major-party entitlement**, with private contributions covering expenses beyond public receipts. It does not cap that candidate's total spending at its smaller subsidy. The $1,000 individual-candidate contribution limit is a separate FECA rule. Section 9004(b) separately caps combined entitlements after accounting for received/expended/retained contributions; public money remains restricted to qualified expenses and designated restoration/loan repayment. Section 9004(d)'s $50,000 combined candidate/immediate-family spending certification is a condition of public funding and includes the vice-presidential candidate's expenditures. The dispute is inability to raise the difference under the contribution rule, not a nonexistent statutory command to stop at $29 million.

Section **2 U.S.C. 437c(a)(1)** requires presidential appointment with Senate consent of six voting members and says no more than three may affiliate with the same party. It names no party and reserves no seat to Democrats or Republicans. The reported equal major-party division is practice, not the statute's command. The Code still prints ex-officio legislative members; do not treat that printing as authority for present unconstitutional participation. **Legi-Tech** already addresses a lawfully reconstituted Commission after congressional participation ended and preserves real reconsideration requirements. This case challenges the affiliation limitation, not that earlier defect. Current text and original pages are saved as `usc1998_2_437c_exact.txt` and the associated HTML.

### Threshold, channel, and available relief

The lower court **does not reach the affiliation-cap merits**. It finds no showing that invalidating a cap designed to prevent one party's domination would make minor-party representation more likely: the same six persons could be appointed without it. Personal concrete injury, traceability, and likely redress must be assessed separately. An asserted exclusion from an actual appointment opportunity or a concrete defective enforcement adjudication would pose different questions, but neither may be invented. A political disagreement with the Commission's makeup does not alone establish a right to a seat, nor does its makeup alone establish partisan enforcement.

The funding claim has an identified financial disadvantage; that does not establish success on equal protection. Facial support-based funding and this pleaded as-applied gap claim are separable. Buckley's reserved future challenge requires attention to actual pleaded harm and statutory operation, not an irrebuttable rule that enacted financing always survives or that every unequal subsidy fails.

The original channels matter if a constitutional claim is found substantial. **Section 437h**, saved from the 1998 Code, authorizes the national committee and an eligible presidential voter among the specified plaintiffs and directs certification of FECA constitutional questions to the circuit en banc. **Section 9011(b)** authorizes appropriate implementation/construction suits by the Commission, national committees, and presidential voters; ordinarily those proceedings go to a three-judge district court with direct Supreme Court appeal. The lower court relies on California Medical's standing/frivolousness exception and distinguishes these channels. Review of a single judge's refusal to convene or certify does not authorize bypassing a necessary statutory tribunal after rejecting that refusal's premise. Section 9011(a) separately governs review of Commission action in the D.C. Circuit with its thirty-day filing condition; it is not a substitute for the subsection (b) implementation/construction suit at issue. A tribunal ruling must identify whether the claim arises under FECA or the Fund Act and which route follows from that classification. Certification is not a new ungranted merits controversy, but an antecedent remedial consequence if the Court finds a live substantial statutory constitutional claim.
