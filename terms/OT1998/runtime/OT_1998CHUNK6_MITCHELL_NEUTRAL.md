### Issue 1: Privilege and adverse inference at sentencing

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** No simulated holding withdraws the privilege after a guilty plea. Existing criminal-procedure law preserves proof requirements for punishment-determining facts.
* **Other Governing Law:** Fifth Amendment; Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 11; applicable sentencing statutes and guidelines.
* **Material Other Authority:** Estelle v. Smith and Griffin v. California protect against compelled use of silence; the privilege continues until the risk of additional penal consequences ends.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Controlling Reasoning Below:** The Third Circuit upheld the sentence, concluding that Mitchell’s plea waived the privilege and that adverse inferences were permissible at sentencing.
* **Material Positions and Limits:** Mitchell argues that the guilty plea did not admit disputed drug quantity and that silence could not supply proof at sentencing. The government relies on the plea’s consequences and the character of sentencing factfinding. Waiver concerning admitted guilt must be distinguished from still-disputed punishment facts.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

If the privilege remains as to disputed quantity, the sentence must be reconsidered without an adverse inference from silence. An independently supported evidentiary finding may still affect punishment within lawful limits. No withdrawal of the plea or automatic minimum sentence follows.
