# In re Sealed Case

# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Approval Status:** Approved.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** September 28, 1998; no prepared OT1998 position is a completed Court decision.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the judgment of the D.C. Circuit.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** No separate remedial instruction is required. The order compelling testimony remains enforceable subject to ordinary evidentiary objections and any lawful protective measures imposed by the supervising court.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Recognition of a protective-function privilege

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the merits. The compulsion order and Rule 501 question are properly before the Court.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies Rule 501 without creating the proposed privilege. The asserted relationship is operational rather than a historically protected confidential relationship, the proposed rule would cover observations as well as communications, and the record does not establish that ordinary compelled testimony materially impairs the Secret Service's protective function. Institutional importance cannot substitute for the legal and evidentiary predicate required to create a privilege.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Federal Rule of Evidence 501 does not recognize, on this record, a Secret Service protective-function privilege against federal grand-jury testimony concerning information obtained while protecting the President.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Privileges withhold relevant evidence and therefore require a source-grounded justification and workable boundary. The Secret Service's prediction that testimony could reduce presidential cooperation deserves serious consideration, but no record finding establishes that consequence, and the proposed contemporaneous-felony exception is poorly matched to later-discovered criminal significance. Ordinary confidentiality practices and tailored judicial protections can address genuine security concerns without creating categorical immunity from the grand jury. Rule 501 does not authorize the Court to convert an important executive function into an evidentiary privilege merely because testimony may be inconvenient or sensitive. Swidler & Berlin strengthens protection once a recognized confidential legal relationship exists, but supplies no automatic extension to operational observations. Rejecting this novel privilege therefore does not balance away an existing attorney-client or psychotherapist privilege. The Secretary of the Treasury asserted the proposed privilege, and its contemporaneous-felony exception would withhold observations whose criminal significance emerged later; those features weaken its claimed protection of presidential confidence.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding is confined to federal grand-jury testimony and does not decide classified information, state secrets, executive privilege, physical security details, or a materially different proceeding and record.

### Issue 2: Absolute or qualified scope and overcoming need

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone does not reach the conditional choice between an absolute and qualified privilege because Issue 1 rejects recognition of the asserted privilege.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** No special showing of investigative need is required to overcome a privilege that does not exist. The grand jury remains subject to ordinary relevance, legality, and court-supervision rules.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Because Rule 501 recognizes no protective-function privilege on this record, the Court establishes no absolute or qualified form and no privilege-specific overcoming standard.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Selecting an overcoming test after declining to recognize the underlying privilege would create an advisory rule. The supervising court may still protect operational security through ordinary lawful means without altering the evidentiary entitlement.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** A future claim under another recognized privilege or a materially different statutory or national-security source must be analyzed under that source's own elements and limits.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Notice-prejudice rule

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches saving-clause coverage and asserted conflict with ERISA's plan-document, civil-enforcement, and claims provisions.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** The notice-prejudice rule regulates the insurer-insured bargain by making actual prejudice part of enforcement of a policy deadline. Ward invokes it as the rule of decision inside § 502, not as a rival state cause of action, and it does not conflict with the cited ERISA provisions.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** California's notice-prejudice rule regulates insurance, may supply the rule of decision in Ward's ERISA benefits action, and does not conflict with the cited ERISA provisions.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The rule changes when an insurer may enforce a notice deadline, a core policy term, and applies specifically to the insurance relationship. It neither supplements ERISA's remedial scheme nor directs the plan administrator to follow a separate state cause of action.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding creates no independent state remedy outside ERISA and does not save laws affecting insurance only incidentally.

### Issue 2: Employer-as-agent rule

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the categorical agency rule because the Ninth Circuit relied on it as an alternative ground.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** The rule directly reallocates plan-claim administration by treating the employer-plan administrator as the insurer's agent in every case. That command relates to plan administration and is preempted under *Travelers* and *De Buono*.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** California's categorical employer-as-agent rule relates to plan administration and is preempted; actual prejudice remains for remand.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Unlike notice-prejudice, the agency rule does not define an insurance-policy obligation. It determines who receives claim notice for an ERISA plan and therefore governs the plan's administrative structure.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not decide voluntarily assumed agency, fact-specific actual authority, or apparent authority established under nonpreempted principles.

# Redmon v. United States

# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Approval Status:** Approved.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** September 28, 1998; no prepared OT1998 position is a completed Court decision.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Vacate the judgment of the Seventh Circuit and remand.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** The Seventh Circuit shall return the case for determination, under the correct Fourth Amendment framework, whether the containers were within curtilage or other protected residential space and whether officers entered or exploited that space to inspect them. If a constitutional search occurred, the lower courts shall apply the ordinary warrant-fruit, independent-source, attenuation, suppression, and plea consequences without presuming the outcome. If no protected space was entered or exploited and the garbage was exposed for collection in the manner governed by *Greenwood*, the judgment may be reinstated.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Reach of Greenwood near the home and garage

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the legal question but does not resolve the disputed location as an appellate fact. The preserved suppression issue is properly before the Court.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies *Greenwood* at its stated boundary and rejects its categorical extension into protected residential space. Garbage placed for collection outside curtilage and exposed to ordinary public access may fall within *Greenwood*. Closed containers immediately beside an attached garage do not lose Fourth Amendment protection merely because they are designated for later collection if police must enter or exploit the home's protected space to inspect them.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** *California v. Greenwood* does not categorically authorize warrantless inspection of closed garbage containers located within, or at a disputed boundary of, a home's curtilage; the court must first determine whether police entered or exploited constitutionally protected residential space.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** *Greenwood* relied on public exposure outside curtilage, not on a rule that all refuse is constitutionally abandoned at the instant an owner intends future collection. The immediate proximity to an attached garage, the common-driveway configuration, and the local collection rule are legally relevant to curtilage, exposure, and access, but the current record does not permit Stone to declare the ultimate location. The en banc judgment treated collection intent and accessibility as sufficient without first resolving the protected-space question. Vacatur is therefore required.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not create privacy in ordinary curbside garbage, forbid collectors or other private persons from handling exposed refuse, or decide containers located outside curtilage and freely accessible from a public route.

### Issue 2: Abandonment, private access, and police intrusion

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the legal effect of collection intent and limited private access but leaves disputed spatial facts for lawful factfinding.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** An intent eventually to surrender garbage to an authorized collector is not, by itself, an abandonment of every Fourth Amendment interest before collection. Nor does limited accessibility to collectors, neighbors, or other private persons grant police a general license to enter or exploit protected residential space. The constitutional question concerns the location, exposure, and intrusion actually undertaken.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Future discard intent and limited third-party accessibility do not eliminate Fourth Amendment protection when police obtain access by entering or exploiting the home's curtilage or another protected residential area; absent such protected-space use, ordinary public exposure may bring the search within *Greenwood*.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The Government cannot derive public police access from every private person's practical capacity to touch an object. A collector's limited access for a defined purpose and time does not necessarily place the contents before the public at large. At the same time, the Amendment does not protect refuse genuinely exposed beyond residential protection. The record must therefore be classified rather than assumed, and the remedy must follow only after that classification and the warrant-fruit analysis.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not decide whether a lawful collector's own inspection, a private search later reported to police, or garbage placed at the curb would present the same constitutional issue.

# Saenz v. Roe

# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Approval Status:** Approved.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** September 28, 1998; no prepared OT1998 position is a completed Court decision.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the injunction against California's first-year lower-benefit rule.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** California shall treat otherwise eligible bona fide residents equally without using recent arrival or the benefit level in a former State to reduce first-year benefits. Bona fide residence verification and equal prospective eligibility rules remain available.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Equal state citizenship after migration

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the facial classification applied to established bona fide residents.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** The Fourteenth Amendment makes a United States citizen a citizen of the State of bona fide residence. California may not make that current state citizenship inferior for twelve months solely by reference to recent arrival or former residence.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A bona fide new resident is entitled to equal state citizenship and may not receive lower generally available welfare benefits solely because of recent arrival or the benefit level in a former State.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The plaintiffs are current California residents who satisfy the State's ordinary eligibility rules. The reduced payment is triggered only by recent migration and the former State's benefit level, the kind of unequal treatment *Shapiro*, *Dunn*, and *Zobel* forbid.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not prevent bona fide residence verification, equal prospective requirements, or distinctions tied to services or contributions earned elsewhere.

### Issue 2: Congressional authorization

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches Congress's asserted permission because California relies on it to defend the classification.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Congress may structure and condition a federal welfare program, but it may not authorize a State to violate the Fourteenth Amendment's equal state-citizenship guarantee.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Congress may not authorize a State to violate the Fourteenth Amendment guarantee of equal state citizenship.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The federal provision cannot lower the constitutional floor applicable to state administration. Because the state classification is unconstitutional, federal permission does not save it.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not decide whether Congress could restructure the underlying federal program through a constitutionally distinct nationwide rule.
