Background 


Petitioner Melvin Tyler, a prisoner currently incarcer- 
ated in the Louisiana state Penitentiary, Angola, Louisi- 
ana, was convicted on February 19, 1976 of one count of 
second degree murder and sentenced to life imprison- 
ment without the benefit of parole, probation, or suspen- 
sion of sentence. Tyler directly appealed his conviction to 
the Louisiana Supreme Court.! Following that appeal, he 
was resentenced to life imprisonment without the benefit 
of parole, probation, or suspension of sentence for a 
period of twenty (20) years. 


Tyler subsequently filed six (6) applications for post- 
conviction relief in state court, all of which were denied.

same two claims he raised in his final post-conviction

Magistrate Judge Louis Moore, Jr. issued a Report 
and Recommendation in which he recommended that 
Tyler’s petition for a writ of habeas corpus pursuant to 28 
U.S.C. § 2254 be denied. Because petitioner has filed 
timely objections to the magistrate’s Report and Recom- 
mendation, a de novo reviéw of those portions of the 
findings to which petitioner has objected is required.

However, it is not before me to decide whether Tyler is 
permitted to file this successive habeas application in accordance 
with 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(2)(A). The Fifth Cipcuit has already

Since I am not deciding whether to grant an application to 
apply for successive writs, 1 am not governed by 28 U.S.C. 
2244(b)(2)(A). Thus, I am not limited to Supreme Court 
determinations on this issue as Smith was. 


Since the time the Fifth Circuit granted authorization 
for Tyler to file his habeas application, that court had an 
opportunity to rule on the issue of whether Cage and 
Victor should be applied retroactively on collateral 
review. In Humphrey v. Cain, 138 F.3d 552 (5th Cir. 1998), 
adopting the panel opinion, 120 F.3d 526 (5th Cir. 1997), 
the Fifth Circuit en banc held that Cage, as clarified by 
Victor, announced a new rule of constitutional law made 
retroactive to cases on collateral review. Id. and See Mes- 
sick v. Cain, 1998 WL 473942, (E.D. La). 


Therefore, the only remaining question is whether 
the jury instruction given at Tyler’s trial was unconstitu- 
tional. In Cage, the Supreme Court held that an instruc- 
tion containing questionable reasonable doubt language 
was unconstitutional, because it permitted finding guilt 
based upon a degree of proof below that required by the 
Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. 


Tyler claims the trial court erroneously instructed the 
jury on reasonable doubt, and that this instruction vio- 
lated the Fourteenth Amendment's due process clause by 
reducing the state’s burden of proof against him, as dis- 
cussed in Cage v. Louisiana, 498 U.S. 39, 111 S.Ct. 328, 112 
L.Ed.2d 339 (1990) and Victor v. Nebraska, __ U.S. __, 114 
S.Ct. 1239, 127 L.Ed. 583 (1994). 


Cage and Victor set the standard by which courts 
examine the “reasonable doubt” instructions. Cage and 
Victor affirmed the principle that the charge must not 
allow the jury to convict unless it is convinced that the 
evidence demonstrates guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. 
Victor modified Cage by leaving no uncertainty that the 
inquiry is “not whether the instruction ‘could have’ been 


applied in [an] unconstitutional manner, but whether 
there is a reasonable likelihood that the jury did so apply 
it.”> 


Using this standard, the Fifth Circuit decided Hum- 
phrey v. Cain, 138 F.3d 552. In Humphrey, the Fifth Circuit 
sitting en banc held that the complained-of reasonable 
doubt instruction was unconstitutional, over-.uling prior 
cases. The jury charge given at Tyler’s trial is identical to 
the charge on reasonable doubt given in Humphrey. The 
issue in this case is whether Tyler’s habeas application 
was filed too late to apply the Humphrey ruling. 


In Humphrey, the petitioner filed his habeas applica- 
tion prior to the April 24, 1996 effective date of the Anti- 
terrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act (AEDPA), 
which established a new standards of review for state 
court decisions in habeas cases. Thus, Humphrey’s claims 
were analyzed under the old version of the habeas stat- 
ute. The court noted this distinction in Humphrey when it 
stated. 


Prisoners who file petitions after April 24, 1996, 
must surmount the formidable barriers erected 
by the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Pen- 
alty Act. Of course, we do not have the occasion 
to measure how high those barriers might be. 
We note only that the. . . heightened standard of 
review for state prisoners, see 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2254(d)(1), could shut out future petitioners in 
Humphrey’s situation. 


Tyler’s petition here was filed on March 18, 1997, 
after the April 24, 1996 effective date of AEDPA. Hence,

Petitioner argues that this “good reason” limitation on 
reasonable doubt, in conjunction with other errors, low-

In analyzing this charge under a more stringent 
AEDPA standard, the charge is able to pass constitutional 
muster. Terms such as “substantial basis” in connection 
with “grave uncertainty” has been recently allowed by 
the Fifth Circuit, as has “grave uncertainty” used with 
“moral certainty”. See Weston v. Ileyoub, 69 F.3d 73 (5th Cir. 
1995); and Gaston v. Whitley, 67 F.3d 121 (5th Cir. 1995), 
cert. denied, 518 U.S. 1023, 116 S.Ct. 2561, 135 L.Ed.2d 
1078 (1996). The Fifth Circuit has also upheld instructions 
using the phrases “substantial doubt” together with 
“moral certainty.” Schnider v. Day, 73 F.3d 610 (5th Cir. 
1996); and Bias v. leyoub, 36 F.3d 479 (5th Cir.1994). 


Moreover, other circuits have refused to reverse con- 
victions with articulation demanding reasonable-doubt 
instruction such as the one in this case. See Chalmers v. 
Mitchell, 73 F.3d 1262, 1268-69 (2d Cir. 1996); ___ U.S. ___, 
117 S.Ct. 106, 136 L.Ed.2d 60 (1996); Beverly v. Walker, 118

(2d Cir. 1996), cert. denied, __ U.S. __, 117 S.Ct. 240, 136 
L.Ed.2d 169 (1996). The Second Circuit held that an 
instruction survived due process scrutiny in spite of its 
characterization of reasonable doubt as “doubt for which 
you can give a reason if called upon to do so by a fellow 
juror in the jury room.” Vargas at 1277-79.

PER CURIAM:* 


Melvin Tyler, Louisiana prisoner # 81668, appeals the 
dismissal of his petition under 28 U.S.C. § 2254. In 1976, 
Tyler was convicted of one count of second degree mur- 
der. He is continuing to serve his sentence of life 
imprisonment without the benefit of parole, probation, or 
suspension of sentence for a period of twenty years. 


* Pursuant to 5TH Cr. R. 47.5, the court has determined that this 
opinion should not be published and is not precedent except 
under the limited circumstances set forth in 5TH Cir. R. 47.5.4. 


15 


Tyler has requested federal habeas relief following 
the denial of his numerous state habeas petitions. This 
court has previously given Tyler permission to file a 
successive § 2254 application on the issues whether Cage 
v. Louisiana, 498 U.S. 39 (1990), should be applied retroac- 
tively on collateral review and whether the jury instruc- 
tion on reasonable doubt given to his jury was 
unconstitutional under Cage and Victor v. Nebraska, 511 
U.S. 1 (1990). The district court determined that, based 
upon Humphrey v. Cain, 138 F.3d 552 (5th Cir.) (en banc), 
cert. denied, 119 S. Ct. 348, 365 (1998), Cage was to be 
applied retroactively, but it denied relief under the stan- 
dard set forth by 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). 


The district court erred in not determining first 
whether Tyler’s petition satisfied AEDPA’s successive 
habeas standard, 28 U.S.C. § 2244 (b) (2) (A). Pro- 
cedurally, this case is governed by Brown v. Lensing, 171 
F.3d 1031 (5th Cir. 1999), which relies on In re Smith, 142 
F.3d 832 (5th Cir. 1998). Under circumstances indis- 
tinguishable from this case, petitioner Brown was denied 
successive habeas relief because he could not show that 
any Supreme Court decision renders the Cage decision 
retroactively applicable to cases on collateral review, as 
AEDPA now requires. Moreover, the Brown case speci- 
fically disapproved the district court’s approach in this 
case. Brown, 171 F.3d at 1032 n.9. 


Like cases demand like treatment. Tyler’s successive 
petition fails according to Brown and § 2244 (b) (2) (A). 


For these reasons, only, the judgment of the district 
court denying habeas relief is AFFIRMED.
