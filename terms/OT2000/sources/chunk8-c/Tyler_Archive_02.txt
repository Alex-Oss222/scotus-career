STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
I. INTRODUCTION 

Two years ago, in August, 1998, undersigned counsel, acting on behalf of the petitioner 
Gregory Smith, filed a petition for a writ of habeas corpus with this Honorable Court. In that 
petition, docketed as proceedings #98-5804, Gregory Smith sought to invoke the original jurisdiction 
of this Court, to present the same two questions being presented in this petition. 

In response to Gregory Smith's petition, this Court directed the State of Louisiana to file a 
brief, and invited the Solicitor General of the United States to file a brief. In the Brief for the United 
States as Amicus Curiae, the Solicitor General concluded that “[{t}he application for a petition for 
a writ of habeas corpus should be set for full briefing and argument on its merits.” 

This recommendation notwithstanding, the Court denied Gregory Smith’s petition on June 
7, 1999, with a notation that “Justice Stevens, Souter, and Breyer would set the case for full 
briefing.” 

As a result of this denial, an additional two years have passed with no final resolution of 
issues of great legal import: did this Court’s decision in Cage v. Louisiana, 498 U.S. 39 (1990), 
announce a new rule of constitutional law, one that must be applied retroactively to cases on 
collateral review? Although most federal courts that have considered the question have concluded 
that it did, see e.g., Humphrey v. Cain, 138 F.3d 552 (Sth Cir.(en banc), cert. denied, 525 U.S. 935, 
119 S.Ct. 348, 365 (1998), for most circuits, it is this Court's determination that is critical. 

During these two years, many federal courts have been denying relief to petitioners with 


‘Cage claims,’ because of the absence of a clear statement from this Court that Cage did announce 


a new rule of law, one that must be applied retroactively. See, e.g., In re Gregory Smith, 142 F.3d 


832 (Sth Cir.1998), Brown v. Lensing, 171 F.3d 1031(Sth Cir.1999). State courts in these circuits. 
in turn, have been denying relief, because they do not consider themselves bound by any federal 
court decisions other than those coming directly from this Court: 


The decision in Humphrey|[v. Cain, supra\(or Wilson [v. Cain, 199 F.3d 439 
(Sth Cir.1999)) might constitute persuasive authority but will not bind this Court in 
our interpretation of what federal constitutional law generally requires. * * * 
(citations omitted). * * * 


Unless and until the United States Supreme Court resolves the status of a 
Cage/Humphrey instruction after Victor [v. Nebraska, 511 U.S. 1, 114 S.Ct. 1239 
(1994)], our decisions in [State v. ] Smith [637 So.2d 398, cert. denied, 513 U.S. 
1045, 115 S.Ct. 641 (1994)] and [State v.] Williams, [708 So.2d 703-(La.1998] as 
well as our decision in State ex rel. Taylor v. Whitley, 606 So.2d 1292 
(La.1992)(Cage not retroactively applicable to final convictions subject only to 
collateral attack), and not the decision in Humphrey, bind the district courts of this 
state in reviewing claims that trial judges have read erroneous instructions on 
reasonable doubt. See generally Robert M. Cover and T. Alexander Aleinikoff, 
Dialectical Federalism: Habeas Corpus and the Court, 86 Yale L.J. 1035, 1046- 
1054 (1977). -

The net result of these two lines of cases has been to turn this Court’s decision in Cage into 
an historical artifact, one with no relevance or meaning to those petitioners whose convictions were 
obtained through the use of constitutionally flawed jury instructions. This petition presents the Court 
with the opportunity to revivify Cage.

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 


On its face, the opinion of the Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit purports to be a simple 
and straightforward application of a clear statute to an undisputed set of facts. Behind this opinion. 
however, are dilemmas which are one part Lewis Carroll, and one part Franz Kafka. Because of the 
holding in Humphrey v. Cain, 138 F.3d 552 (Sth Cir.1998)(en banc), the Fifth Circuit, and every 
federal district court in the Fifth Circuit must apply Cage retroactively, unless, however, the federal 
petition is a successive one, in which case relief will be denied, because this Court has never held 
that Cage is to be given retroactive application -- at least in the eyes of the Court of Appeals for the 
Fifth Circuit. 

Every other court of appeals to have addressed the issue has also interpreted this Court's 
rulings to require the retroactive application of Cage. But under the Fifth Circuit’s application of 
the law, the unanimous view of the federal courts on this issue is to be disregarded. Indeed, the very 
unanimity of the federal courts on the question of retroactivity may have the effect of making it more 
difficult to for a successive habeas petitioner to surmount the procedural obstacles created by the 
AEDPA, given this Court’s disinclination to decide issues as to which there is no disagreement 
among the courts of appeals. 

These circumstances are legally absurd. They are, however, the inevitable result of 
interpreting the statute to direct the courts of appeals to disregard any judicial finding of retroactivity 
that does not come from the Supreme Court -- even if the finding is one of their own. Such 


legislation stands Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. 137 (1803) on its head.

I. THE COURT OF APPEALS FOR THE FIFTH CIRCUIT HAS DECIDED AN 
IMPORTANT FEDERAL QUESTION IN A WAY WHICH CONFLICTS WITH 
RELEVANT DECISIONS OF THIS COURT, AND IN A WAY WHICH IS IN 
CONFLICT WITH TWO OTHER CIRCUIT COURTS OF APPEALS. 


In Cage v. Louisiana, 498 U.S. 39 (1990), this Court held that a jury instruction which 
defines reasonable doubt in a manner that allows a finding of guilt based on a degree of proof below 
that required by the Due Process Clause is improper, and grounds for reversing a conviction obtained 


using such an instruction. 


Three years later, in Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275 (1993), this Court held that a 
constitutionally deficient reasonable doubt instruction, such as the one given in Cage, cannot be 
harmless error, but is instead “structural error,” and thus outside the entire harmless error analysis. 

In the words of the Court: 


In [Arizona v.] Fulminante, [499 U.S. 279 (1991)] we distinguished between, on the 
one hand, “structural defects in the constitution of the trial mechanism, which defy 
analysis by ‘harmless-error’ standards,” 499 U.S., at 309, and, on the other hand, trial 
errors which occur “during the presentation of the case to the jury, and which may 
therefore be quantitatively assessed in the context of other evidence presented,” id., 
at 307-308. Denial of the right to a jury verdict of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt 
is certainly an error of the former sort, the jury guarantee being a “basic protectio[n]” 
whose precise effects are unmeasurable, but without which a criminal trial cannot 
reliably serve its function, Rose [v. Clark, 478 U.S. 570 (1986)], supra, 478 U.S. at 
577. The right to trial by jury reflects, as we have said, “ a profound judgment about 
the way in which law should be enforced and justice administered.” Duncan v. 
Louisiana, 391 U.S., at 155. The deprivation of that right, with consequences that 
are necessarily unquantifiable and indeterminate, unquestionably qualifies as 
“structural error.” 


Sullivan, 508 U.S. at 283.

Together, Teague, Cage, Sullivan, and this Court's action in Adams point ineluctably to the 
conclusion -- which the courts of appeals have unanimously adopted -- that Cage applies 
retroactively. Indeed, the Fifth Circuit itself has acknowledged that “the Supreme Court has made 
it plain that Cage-Victor errors fit within the second Teague exception.” Humphrey v. Cain, 120 
F.3d 526, 529 (Sth Cir.1997)(panel opinion adopted by en banc court in Humphrey v. Cain, supra). 
Under any reasonable construction of the AEDPA, where this Court's decisions so clearly necessitate 
retroactive application of a new rule, that rule has indeed been “made retroactive to cases on 
collateral review by the Supreme Court.” The Fifth Circuit's contrary decision in this case thus 
conflicts with controlling decisions of this Court. 

Moreover, as indicated above, the Fifth Circuit's decision that a successive petition raising 
a Cage issue may not be filed under 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(2)(A) is in direct conflict with the decision 
of the Ninth Circuit in Nevius v. Sumner, 105 F.3d 453 (9th Cir. 1996), and in direct conflict with the 
decision of the Third Circuit in West v. Vaughn, 204 F.3d 53 (3rd Cir.2000). 

In Nevius, a petitioner sought to raise a Cage issue in a successive petition. The Ninth Circuit 
granted leave to file the petition under § 2244(b)(2)(A), noting that the history of this Court's cases 
on Ye Cage issue indicated that Cage must be retroactive, and finding that the petitioner “has made 
a ‘prima facie showing that the application satisfies the requirements of this subsection."” /d at 462. 

West also presents an instance of a petitioner seeking to raise a Cage issue in a successive 
petition. After finding that Cage did indeed announce a new rule, the Third Circuit concluded that 
this Court had never explicitly held Cage to be retroactive -- “ We are similarly unaware of any such 


precedent [explicitly stating that the Cage rule is to be applied retroactively]” West, supra, p.59.

However, the West court found that this was not the end of the inquiry, because the court was 
“not convinced that [the phrase] ‘made retroactive’ [as used in § 2244(b)(2)(A)] deserves the 
restrictive gloss applied by these [other circuit] courts in construing the term.” West, supra, p.59. 
The West court then went on to find that this Court’s decision in Sullivan compelled retroactive 
application of Cage. West, supra, pp.61-63. 

The Fifth Circuit, however, has specifically rejected the Ninth Circuit's decision in Nevius 
v. Sumner, aligning itself instead with the First Circuit's decision in Rodriguez v. Superintendent, 
Bay State Correctional Center, 139 F.3d 270 (1st Cir.1998), and the Eleventh Circuit's ruling in /n 
re Hill, 113 F.3d 181 (11th Cir.1997). In Rodriguez, the First Circuit rejected the filing of a habeas 
petition raising a Cage claim. Like the Fifth Circuit. the First Circuit specifically disagreed with 
Nevius v. Sumner, rejected the petitioner's reliance on Sullivan and Adams, and held that the statute 
required a decision by this Court directly addressing the question of retroactivity. Similarly, in Jn re 
Hill, the Eleventh Circuit held that its own prior ruling that Cage applies retroactively was 
unavailing in a successive habeas petition, and denied the petitioner leave to file. 

The effect of these varying decisions is that in the nine states of the Ninth Circuit, and the 
three states and the other territories of the Third Circuit, a state prisoner can file a successive habeas 
petition raising a Cage claim, whereas in the five states that make up the First Circuit, the three 
comprised in the Fifth Circuit, and the three in the Eleventh Circuit, such a petition may not be filed. 
This Court should grant plenary review of this petition to resolve this conflict over an important 


issue of federal law. 


is 


Il. THE RETROACTIVITY ISSUE IN THIS CASE PRESENTS AN 
IMPORTANT QUESTION OF FEDERAL LAW 


If the Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit is correct in its position that this Court has never 
determined (for purposes of § 2244 (b)(2)(A)) whether the rule of law announced in Cage is to be 
applied retroactively, then the reasons for granting this petition become all the more compelling. At 
least five circuits, the Third, the Fourth, the Fifth, the Tenth, and the Eleventh, have determined that 
Cage is to be given retroactive effect. Nevertheless, even if every other circuit follows their lead, 
federal relief will be unavailable to any state prisoner filing a successive petition. While one might 
hope that state courts addressing post-conviction petitions under their own procedures will adhere 
to rulings made by the federal Courts of Appeal. it is plain from the decision of the Louisiana 
Supreme Court in State v. Penns, supra, that this is not going to happen -- at least in Louisiana. 
Thus, in Louisiana, there is no remedy available to state prisoners -- in any court. 

In short, whether any given habeas petitioner receives the constitutional protections to which 
he or she is entitled by virtue of Cage and Sullivan has become a question of judicial roulette, not 
a principled application of the laws. : 

Petitioner's claim that Cage is retroactive is surely substantial, indeed, every federal court 
to address the issue after Sullivan has so held. Ordinarily, such unanimity might be a reason for this 
Court to deny review of the issue. But here, the need for resolution of the retroactivity issue by this 
Court follows directly from the interpretation of the AEDPA adopted by the court below, which 
places a premium not on what the proper retroactivity rule is, but on who has announced the rule. 
If the AEDPA is to be so interpreted, then this Court has a special role and responsibility with 


respect to the resolution of retroactivity issues -- at least where, as here, the claim of retroactivity is 


16 


weighty. 

Failure by this Court either to reverse the Fifth Circuit's interpretation of the AEDPA or to 
take on the merits of the retroactivity question would lead to an absurd result. The effect of allowing 
a ruling like the one below to stand unreviewed would be to create a system where, if a retroactivity 
question 1s so close and debatable that it engenders division in the lower courts that this Court 
ultimately resolves in favor of retroactivity, then the issue may be presented in successive habeas 
petitions. If, on the other hand. the case for retroactivity is so easy and obvious that the courts of 
appeals all agree and the issue is never addressed by this Court, a successive petition raising the 
issue is barred. In short, the better the petitioner's claim, the less chance he will have to raise it.” 

No rational legislature could have intended to create such a system. To restore reason to the 
law. this Court has two choices: it may interpret the AEDPA to permit the courts of appeals to give 
effect to the clear import of this Court's decisions when they dictate (but do not directly rule on) the 
retroactive application of a new rule, or it may decide the retroactivity question itself. Leaving the 


Fifth Circuit's decision intact is not an acceptable third alternative. 


The Fourth Circuit, in an opinion denying a prisoner leave to file a successive petition 
raising a non-Cage claim because this Court had not directly decided the retroactivity issue involved, 
took note of the jurisprudential awkwardness its interpretation of the AEDPA created in cases 
presenting easy retroactivity questions: “Of course, it seems unlikely that the Supreme Court would 
grant certiorari to declare the applicability of a rule announced on direct review to collateral 
proceedings . . . lower federal courts uniformly rule in favor of collateral availability.” Jn re Vial, 
115 F.3d 1192, 1196, n. 8 (4th Cir.1997). Despite its recognition of this “Catch 22," the Fourth 
Circuit in Vial felt constrained to require a direct retroactivity ruling by this Court as a prerequisite 
to a successive petition.
