the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit because 
the circuit court held "resident aliens" and "excludable aliens" to

1. Historically Resident Aliens Possess 
Greater Rights than Do Excludable Aliens. 

Petitioner is not excludable. He is a former permanent 
legal resident alien subject to a final order of deportation. Once 
aliens are admitted, their constitutional status changes so that 
they enjoy due process rights including a fundamental liberty 
interest in personal freedom under the Fifth Amendment. Reno vy. 
Flores, 507 U.S. 292, 315-316 (1993) (J. O’Comnor, concurring). In 
reversing the district court, the appellate court reasoned that 
this Court’s more recent decisions recognizing the greater rights 
of resident aliens in relation to excludable aliens were limited to 
procedural rights. The circuit court therefore incorrectly held 
that a resident alien’s substantive due process right to personal 
liberty was the same as that afforded excludable aliens. 

Admittedly, the authorities relied on by the circuit 
court are procedural due process decisions. However, the broad 
language in those cases recognizes the constitutional rights of 
resident aliens and contains no such limitation. These Supreme 
Court pronouncements dsuensseens an expansion of due process rights 
for resident aliens, not, as the circuit court concluded, a 
narrowing of the protections afforded to such persons by the Fifth 


Amendment.

2. The Plenary Power Doctrine Does Not 
Require Courts to Defer to the INS’ 
Prolonged Detention of Resident Aliens. 

The plenary power doctrine places questions involving 
aliens outside the purview of the judiciary - deferring immigration 
matters to the political branches’ discretion. Such agencies have 
unfettered authority to deport non-citizens. Although deference is 
accorded to the political branches in immigration matters, this 
Court now scrutinizes an individual’s right to due process of law. 
Reno v. Flores, 507 U.S. 292, 305 (1993). The circuit court 
incorrectly invoked the plenary power doctrine where no national 
sovereignty or foreign relations concerns exist. 

The government’s sovereignty interest is greater in the 
case of an excludable alien than it is in the case of a resident 
alien. When a country like Cuba deposits thousands of aliens on 
our shores, the governmental interest in effectuating their 
expulsion (and not permitting them to enter) necessarily affects 
the United States’ sovereignty. In contrast, this case concerns 
only a single deportable alien who the United States has already 
admitted. He cannot be deported because his purported country of 
origin will not accept him. The circuit court gives no explanation 


as to why a foreign government’s refusal to accept such an alien 


10 


would be changed by either his confinement or his release. In sum, 
this case presents no national sovereignty or foreign relations 


issues. 


3. The Circuit Court Failed to Recognize the 
Fundamental Nature of  Petitioner’s 
Liberty Interest to be Free From Physical 
Restraint. 

The circuit court relegated the petitioner to a status it 
characterized as less than that of a citizen with respect to his 
Fifth Amendment right to personal liberty. This conclusion relies 
on rights that are far less fundamental than the right at issue 
here. The decisions cited involved protections such as a person’s 
right to Medicare, employment, government services and freedom of 
speech. As is evident, none of those decisions held that 
continued, indefinite confinement of a resident alien was 
constitutionally permissible. Because the substantive due process 
protection at issue here is far ase fundamental than the above 
rights, the petitioner submits that the circuit court’s analogy 
simply does not apply under the facts of this case. 

Since detention is one of the most serious deprivations 


of liberty that the government can impose, it is ordinarily 


reserved in this country as punishment for those convicted of 


11 


serious criminal offenses. Moreover, the Immigration and 
Naturalization Service (INS) does not possess the general detention 
power to incarcerate aliens in order to protect society; that is 
the role of the criminal justice system. The INS can only detain 
aliens for the purpose of facilitating their removal. Thus, 
because the INS is unable to effectuate petitioner’s deportation, 
the INS no longer has a legitimate immigration interest in the 
petitioner’s continued detention. Petitioner’s continued detention 
is therefore excessive in relation to the government’s immigration 
purpose. Petitioner’s continued detention, with no end in sight, 


accordingly violates his right to due process of law.

Petitioner has lived in the United States continuously 
for the past forty-three years. His wife, daughter, mother, 
numerous brothers and sisters all reside in the United States. 
These ties, along with his territorial presence in the United 
States, are the basis for his constitutional status as a holder of

This Court has long acknowledged a resident alien’s 
constitutional rights. Given such longstanding acknowledgment, the 
Fifth Circuit errs in equating excludable aliens with resident 
aliens. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding "(t]he need to expel 
[resident aliens) is identical from a national sovereignty 
perspective [as that of excludable aliens)" Zadvydas, at p.296. 

Excludable aliens and resident aliens are not identical. 
To reach the conclusion that the two groups are identical, the 
circuit court hinged its holding on a legal fiction that solely 
applies to excludable aliens, i.e. "“[a]lthough an [excludable] 
alien is physically present in the U.S., ‘his detention... is 
only a continuation of exclusion.’" Thien Van Vo v. Joseph Green, 
63 F.Supp.2d 1278, 1283 (U.S.D.C. D.Colo. 1999). 

By equating excludable aliens with long term permanent 


residents, the Zadvydas court ignores his legal admission into this 


country, his forty-three years in this country, and most 


importantly, his constitutional status acquired based on the above. 
If someone has never been excluded, there can be no continuation of 
exclusion. The case law favors resident aliens precisely because

their interests are so much stronger than that of someone who is 
attempting to gain entry into the United States. Thien Van Vo, 63

The circuit court further reasoned that "resident aliens" 
are not entitled to a substantive due process right to be free from 
physical restraint because to hold otherwise requires that 
"excludable aliens" be viewed “as non-persons wholly unprotected by 
the Constitution." Zadvydas v. Underdown, 185 F.3d 279, 296 (Sth 
Cir. 1999). The circuit court stated that such a holding would 
conflict with two of its previous decisions involving "“excludable 
aliens" making "“excludable aliens" subject to the rack and the 


screw, the Eighth Amendment notwithstanding. Zadvydas, at 296. 
Citing, Gisbert v. United States, 988 F.2d 1437 (5™ Cir. 1993); 
Lynch v. Cannatella, 810 F.2d 1363 (5™ Cir. 1987). 


Indeed, there is no conflict. Holding resident aliens to 
possess a substantive due process right.to be free from indefinite 
detention is fully consistent with the holdings in Gisbert and 
Lynch. Both decisions employ a balancing test. In Lynch, an 
excludable alien’s interest in being free from gross physical abuse 
outweighed the government’s minimal immigration interest. Gisbert 
balanced excludable aliens’ liberty interest against’ the 
governmental interest in protecting the national sovereignty from 


15 


Cuba depositing thousands of aliens on our shores. Here, Lithuania 
has not deposited even one individual on our shores. On the 
contrary, this country in 1956-57 decided to admit the Zadvydas’ 
family - when they resided in Germany - it was the actions of the 
United States, not Lithuania, that brought the petitioner to this 
country. The circuit court gives no explanation as to how the INS’ 
decision forty years later to deport the petitioner protects 
mational sovereignty, or impacts relations between the United 
States and Lithuania. 

The holding is based on the assumption that Congress’ 
interest in detaining excludable aliens and its interest in 
detaining deportable aliens involve the same national sovereignty 
concerns. This is not so. Congress’ interest in the two 
situations is extremely different. In the case of excludable 
aliens, Congress must prevent other nations from unilaterally 
shipping their citizens here. Without the ‘etention of such 
persons, they could enter the United States simply by virtue of 
reaching United States’ soil. Unlike excludable aliens - aliens 
who the United States has never agreed to admit - deportable aliens 
have, by definition, "entered" the United States ir accordance with 
law. Congress has elected to admit such persons with, or without, 
the interference from other nations. While Congress may later 


16 


decide to remove those persons, its decision to admit is not 
influenced by the will of other nations; the “national 
sovereignty" interests at stake in the detention of excludables are 
not applicable to the detention of deportables. Accordingly, 
national sovereignty interests and foreign relations between the 
United States and Lithuania, do not depend in any manner on whether 
petitioner is confined or released. 

As the circuit court candidly conceded, detention of 
resident aliens who cannot be removed is based on Congress’ fear 
that the aliens "may commit crimes against the general population." 
Zadvydas v. Underdown, 185 F.3d 279, 296 (Sth Cir. 1999). General 
crime reduction is not an immigration matter, and legislation that 
Congress passes in furtherance of crime reduction should not be 
governed by the reduced constitutional standard applicable to 
“excludable aliens."

A. History of Habeas Corpus 


It is appropriate at this point to review the historical 
development of habeas corpus. The history of the 
right of personal liberty began before the writ of 
habeas corpus originated. The right was granted, at 
least to some English subjects, by Article 39 of 
Magna Carta, which provided that no free man 
should be arresied, unpnsoned, or destroyed except 
by the judgment of his peers or by the law of the 
land. Magna Cara, An. 39. The essence of this 
article has been waced back through English law 
books of the Twelfth Century © documents of the 
Holy Roman Empire of the Eleventh Century. 
Richard L. Perry and John C. Cooper, Sources of 
Our Liberties, p. 5 (1978). Although there has been 
scholarly dispute on *1017 the subject, as early as 
the Fourteenth Century the “law of the land” 
provision was considered to be the equivalent of 
“due process of law*. Richard L. Perry and John 
C. Cooper, Sources of Our Liberties, p. 189 (1978); 
William S. Hollsworth, A History of English Law, 
4th ed., Vol. 1, pp. 60-63 (1931). In addition, the 
protection of this provision was extended to all 
citizens and not just to the “freemen” who were a 
limited group of land barons. William S. 
Holdsworth, A History of English Law, Vol. 1, p. 
227 (4th ed. 1931); Ex Parte Yerger, 75 U.S.(8 
Wall.) 85, 96, 19 L.Ed. 332 (1868). 


In America the Colonist claimed the writ as one of 
the “rights of Englishmen” to which they were 
entitled under thew charters and by reason of the 
common law. As 4 result, the privilege of the writ 
of habeas corpus was incorporated into the 
constitutions adopted by the states at the time of the 
American Revolution. See, ¢.g., Constitution of 
Pennsylvania, Am. [X (1776). Constitution of 
Maryland, Art. XX] (1776).

1. Statutory Authority 


(25}[26] It is necessary to note that contro! over

Congressional power in their area is plenary, subject

430 U.S. 787, 97 S.Ct. 1473, 52 L.Ed.2d SO

entitled to the constitutional protection of due 
process, equal protection and reasonable bail. Id. 
Also, legal aliens subject to deponation—the 
category in which petitioner falls—- generally are 
granted greater substantive rights than are 


. excludable aliens. Landon, 459 U.S. at 26-27, 103

review the petitioner's detention to determine

rights.

2. Constitutional Challenge 


This Court agrees with the Court in Tran, that 
“{wyhile there is statutory authority allowing 
indefinite detention of an alien who is classified as 
an aggravated felon under immigration law where 
the alien is unable to overcome the presumption 
which is established by 8 U.S.C. § 1252(aX2), it is 
unclear how long this detention can continue without 
becoming violative of an alien's constitutional 
rights.” Thus, “it is necessary for this Court to 
analyze the Petitioner's detention in light of his 
constitutional challenge tw determine whether 
Petitioner's detention has continued beyond 
permissible constitutional limitations." Id. 


{28} At the outset this Court again notes that the 
Fifth Amendment's mandate that “no person shall ... 
be deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due 
process of law" extends due process protection to 
persons within the territorial United States and not 
just to U.S. citizens. See Plyler v. Doe, 457 U.S. 
202, 210-12, 102 S.Ct. 2382, 2391-92, 72 L.Ed.2d 
786 (1982); Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 
228, 16 S.Ct. 977, 41 L.Ed. 140 (1896). Thus, the 
petitioner is entitled to have his detention reviewed 
by this Court to make sure it comports with the 
potions of due process. 


The constitutionality of the detention must be 
considered in light of its purpose. The Fifth Circuit 
bas indicated that the focus must be on “whether the 
detention is imposed for the purpose of punishment 
or whether it is merely incidental t another 
legitimate governmental purpose.“ Gisbern v. 
United States Attorney General, 988 F.2d 1437, 
1441 (Sth Cir.1993), citing Schall v. Marin, 467 
U.S. 253, 268-69, 104 S.Ct. 2403, 2412, 81 
L.Ed.2d 207 (1984). Absent “any expression of 
intent to punish on the part of °1026 the 
government, that determination will generally turn 
oo ‘whether an alternative purpose to which [the 
detention} may rationally be connected is assignable 
for it, arid whether it appears excessive in relation to 
the alternative purpose assigned [to it).' * Id. at 
1441-42, citing Schall, 467 U.S. at 268-69, 104 
S.Ct. at 2411-12. 


The legislative history and text of Section 1252 


Page 15 


clearly shows that Congress did not contemplate 
permanent detention as a means of punishment for 
aliens convicted of aggravated felonies who have 
already served their sentence. Tran, 847 F.Supp. ai 
474. Section 1252(aX2B) of the INA and § 
303(>3)(B) of the IIRIRA provides for the release 
of a lawfully admitted alien who has been convicted 
of an aggravated felony where the alien has 
demonstrated that he is neither a threat to others nor 
a flight risk. Thus, the purpose behind detention of 
these aliens is to protect the community from 
aggravated felons and to prevent aliens from 
absconding before deportation can be accomplished. 
id., Caballero v. Caplinger, 914 F.Supp. 1374 
(E.D.La.1996). Accordingly, the question of 
whether petitioner's detention complies with 
substantive due process ultimately turns on whether 
the detention in petitioner's case is excessive in 
relation to these goals. 


The court in Tran determined that continued 
detention of a non-excludable alien under § 1252 
pending deportation is not an excessive means of 
accomplishing the government's purpose. Tran, 847 
F.Supp. 469. The Tran court recognized the 
heightened interest of Congress in immigration 
matters and noted that the INS had limited 
application of § 1252(aX2) detention to those aliens 
who have already been judicially determined to have 
committed a felony. Id. at 475. The Court also 
notes that in Gisbert the Fifth Circuit using 
essentially the same rationale as the court in Tran 
found that the continued detention of an excludable 
alien pending deportation was not an excessive 
means of accomplishing the purposes sought to be 
served by § 1252(a). See also Caballero, 914 
F.Supp. at 1374. 


It is clear that protecting the community from 
aggravated felons and preventing aliens from 
absconding before deportation are legitimate 
governmental policy goals that, “can warrant a 
deprivation of liberty under the proper 
circumstances.” U.S. v. Salerno, 48] U.S. 739, 
107 S.Ct. 2095, 95 L.Ed.2d 697 (1987).

the future. Detention is intended for the sole

The district court granted the writ of

permitted by TrexCove Crim. Proc. art 
44.2%). or (2) to vacate the habeas peti- 
tioner’s sentence and to impose a sentence 
less than death. Granviel v. Estelle, 655 
F 2d 673 «Sth Cir. Sept.1981); Whitley, 977 
F.2d at 161; Jones, 788 F.2d at 1103. We 
therefore remand with instructions to en- 
ter such an order.
