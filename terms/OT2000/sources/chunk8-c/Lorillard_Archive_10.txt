OTHER CONSTITUTIONAL OR STATUTORY 
PROVISIONS INVOLVED 


In addition to the constitutional, statutory and 
regulatory provisions set forth in the Brief of Petitioners 
Lorillard Tobacco Co., et al. in No. 00-596 (hereafter “Pet. 
Br.”), and in the appendix to the Petition for Writ of 
Certiorari in No. 00-596: (hereafter “Pet. App.”), the 
following statutory provision is involved. 


Section 2 of the Federal Cigarette Labeling and 
Advertising Act, as amended, 15 U.S.C. § 1331, provides: 


It is the polity of the Congress, and the 
purpose of this Act, to establish a 
comprehensive Federal Program to deal with 
cigarette labeling and advertising with respect 
to any relationship between smoking and 
health, whereby — 


(1) the public may be adequately informed 
about any adverse health effects of cigarette 
smoking by inclusion of warning notices on 
each package of cigarettes and in each 
advertisement of cigarettes; and 


(2) commerce and the national economy may 
be (A) protected to the maximum extent 
consistent with this declared policy and (B) 
not impeded by diverse, nonuniform, and 
confusing cigarette labeling and advertising 
‘regulations with respect to any relationship 
between smoking and health.

INTRODUCTION 


This case concerns the power of a State to take 
reasonable measures to limit advertising near schools and 
playgrounds promoting a harmful product that is illegal for 
sale to minors. None of the parties before the Court disputes 
that the vast majority of tobacco use begins with children, 
that children are exposed to the selling message of tobacco 
advertising each day, and that prevention of youth tobacco 
use is a paramount governmental concern. Indeed, none of 
the petitioners claims any right to advertise to children, nor 
could they. Thus, under the First Amendment, the 
petitioners raise the relatively narrow issue of whether 
Massachusetts has chosen appropriate means to protect an 
unintended audience from exposure to tobacco advertising, 
while still permitting adults ample access to tobacco product 
information through other channels. The limited nature of 
Massachusetts’ restrictions — which prohibit only publicly 
visible tobacco advertising near schools and playgrounds — 
makes plain that the challenged regulations are appropriately 
tailored. For similar reasons, the regulations are clearly 
outside the scope of preemption under the Federal Cigarette 
Labeling and Advertising Act, wherein Congress precluded 
the States from regulating the health-related content of 
cigarette advertising, but indicated no intention to foreclose 
the States’ historic power to limit the locations in which 
advertising may be displayed. 


STATEMENT 


l. Tobacco use, in particular cigarette smoking, 
is the leading preventable cause of disease and premature 
death in the United States. Joint Appendix (hereafter “JA”’) 
188. Overwhelmingly, smoking and smokeless tobacco use 
begin, and become established, in adolescence. JA 177, 187, 
194, 195, 210; State’s Lodging (hereafter “Ldg.”) 8. The 
average age at which smokers try their first cigarette is 14 %

years. JA 210. Nationally, 82% of adult smokers started 
smoking before the age of 18, and approximately 70% of 
them had become regular smokers by that age. JA 210, 214. 
The Surgeon General has found that “[mjost young people 
who smoke regularly are already addicted to nicotine, and 
they experience this addiction in a manner and severity 
similar to what adult smokers experience.” JA 190. Thus, 
addiction to the nicotine in tobacco occurs almost 
exclusively during adolescence and young adulthood. JA 
194, 195. 


The consequences of youth tobacco use are severe. 
The younger a person begins to smoke, the more likely he or- 
she is to become a heavy smoker. JA 189, 190, 214. An 
adolescent who continues smoking into adulthood 
substantially increases his or her risk of dying from cancer, 
heart disease or lung disease. JA 189-190, 214, 218. 
Continued use of smokeless tobacco is associated with a 
range of oral cancers. JA 190. Likewise, regular cigar 
smoking causes cancer of the lung, oral cavity, larynx and 
esophagus. JA 244. As this Court recently recognized, 
“tobacco use, particularly among children and adolescents, 
poses perhaps the single most significant threat to public 
health in the United States.” Food\and Drug Admin. v. 
Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp., 529 U.S. 120, 161 
(2000). 


A person who does not begin smoking in childhood 
or adolescence is unlikely ever to begin. JA 214. Thus, the 
Surgeon General Has stated that “young people are the chief 
source of new consumers for the tobacco industry, which 
each year must replace the many consumers who quit 
smoking and the many who die from smoking-related 
diseases.” JA 192. Yet “efforts to prevent the onset or 
continuance of tobacco use face the pervasive challenge of 
promotion activity by the tobacco industry.” Ldg. 18. 
Cigarettes and smokeless tobacco are among the most

The Surgeon General has concluded that tobacco 
advertising influences children and adolescents in at least 
two ways. First, tobacco advertising uses images — rather 
than information — “to portray the attractiveness and 
function of smoking.” JA 197. The featured themes include 
adventure seeking, peer approval, sex appeal, success, 
sophistication and independence, all of which resonate 
especially with adolescents as they strive to define their 
identities during the transition to adulthood. JA 192, 197; 
see JA 211, 219. Because tobacco advertising portrays, in 
smokers, “attributes that children and adolescents would like 
to have,” it implies that smoking may “close the gap” 
between an adolescent’s actual self-image and the ideal for 
which he or she strives. JA 197, 206. Second, highly 
repetitious advertising exposure leads minors to overestimate 
the prevalence and “normalcy” of smoking among both peers

In Massachusetts, children are routinely exposed to a 
significant volume of tobacco advertising. Two 
Massachusetts studies have documented that the density of

advertisements, including those inside stores and visible 
from the outside, were at children’s eye level (i.e. below 3.5 
feet). JA 105; JCA 2384. 


3. In 1993, Massachusetts established its 
statewide tobacco control program, funded by a 1992

State’s tobacco control efforts include extensive school- 
based tobacco prevention programs, intensive enforcement 
of youth access restrictions, a sustained counter-advertising 
campaign, local licensing requirements for tobacco retailers, 
and scores of community based anti-tobacco programs, 
which implement a wide range of youth education, access 
restriction and cessation programs. JA 90-96; Ldg. 13-15. 
Between 1994 and 1998 local boards of health conducted

of the state tobacco sales law. JA 93. Massachusetts also 
maintains one of the highest cigarette taxes in the country. 
JCA 671, 784. In 1999, Massachusetts was recognized by 
the CDC as maintaining one of the two leading tobacco 
programs in the Nation. JA 91, 97.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


1. The court of appeals correctly held that a 
State law limiting the locations for display of tobacco 
advertising is not preempted by the Federal Cigarette 
Labeling and Advertising Act. Pub. L. 89-92, 79 Stat. 282, 
as amended, 15 U.S.C. §§ 1331-1340. The Act’s purpose is 
clear: to mandate a uniform national health warning for 
cigarette packages and advertising while avoiding the burden 
to commerce from the imposition of conflicting health 


14 


warning requirements under state law. See 15 U.S.C. 
§§ 1331. Section 5(b) of the Act preempts only those state 
law requirements or prohibitions that are “based on smoking 
and health . . . with respect to the advertising or promotion of 
[properly labeled] cigarettes ... .” Nothing in this text, or 
the Act’s purpose or legislative history, suggests that 
Congress intended to intrude upon the traditional exercise of 
state and local zoning authority over commercial advertising. 
Rather, Congress created a program “to deal with cigarette 
labeling and advertising with respect to any relationship 
between smoking and health,” 15 U.S.C. § 1331 (emphasis 
added), thus seeking to maintain exclusive federal control of 
the health-related informational content of cigarette 
packaging and advertising. Congress provided for 
preemption under §5(b) to implement this statutory 
objective. This distinction is reflected in the result reached 
in Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc., 505 U.S. 504 (1992), 
where the plurality held preempted only those of the asserted 
claims that challenged the adequacy of the warning in the 
manufacturers’ advertising. Jd. at 524-531. Although the 
amendments to §5(b) in 1969 expanded the forms of 
advertising content regulation that can be preempted, such as 
to include common law claims in addition to “positive 
enactments,” see id. at 524, the 1969 amendments left in 
§ 5(b) the critical “smoking and health” language limiting 
preemption to health-related advertising content. The 
Massachusetts regulations, which restrict only the location of 
cigarette advertising while leaving its content unaffected, are 
therefore not preempted. 


2. Intermediate scrutiny is the appropriate 
standard for judicial review under the First Amendment of 
regulations that merely zone publicly visible tobacco 
advertising away from schools and playgrounds. In the 
regulations, Massachusetts has addressed the specific harms 
to children presented by tobacco advertising they 
unavoidably confront in the areas they spend time each day. 


15 


At the same time, petitioners remain free to communicate 
product and price information to adults through advertising 
in other locations, and in magazines, newspapers, by direct 
mail or other media. The regulations clearly do not “ban” 
commercial speech, but merely regulate its “place.” 
Consequently, they should be reviewed consistently with 
various forms of “time, place and manner” restrictions on 
speech, for which intermediate scrutiny is well established. 


That these locational restrictions are limited to the 
advertising of tobacco, but not other products, does not 
require heightened review. The State has drawn an entirely 
permissible distinction. The regulations are intended to 
further the State’s law against tobacco sales to minors, and 
thus exhibit a close connection to a commercial transaction 
the State has prohibited. In addition, because tobacco 
advertising exerts an “undue influence” on children — who 
constitute the vast majority of new tobacco users — it can be 
singled out for regulation. Unlike the “blanket prohibition” 
on speech to adults that the Court unanimously stuck down 
in 44 Liquormart, Inc. v. Rhode Island, 517 U.S. 484 (1996), 
the Massachusetts regulations simply zone advertising away 
from the areas that children congregate. 


3. The Massachusetts regulations satisfy each of 
the criteria for regulation of commercial speech under 
Central Hudson, 447 U.S. at 566. The governmental 
interests that support the regulations are compelling — the 
promotion of Massachusetts’ policy against tobacco sales to 
minors and, ultimately, protection of the well-being of the 
State’s children. Substantial evidence demonstrates the 
significant influence of tobacco advertising in promoting and 
maintaining tobacco use by minors. Thus, restricting 
tobacco advertising in areas where children attend school 
and play will directly and materially advance the State’s 
interest. 


16 


Moreover, the regulations are narrowly tailored under 
the Central Hudson standard, even if they prevent some 
tobacco advertising from reaching adults. Petitioners remain 
free to advertise to adults in areas beyond the school and 
playground zones, and through other permitted channels. 
The petitioners themselves determined in 1990 to cease 
billboard advertising in areas within 500 feet of schools and 
playgrounds. The State’s determination to set this distance 
at 1,000 feet (approximately three blocks) and to restrict 
certain interior retail tobacco advertising in these zones is 
entitled to deference. There is also no “obvious” alternative 
to a restriction of speech. Aggressive enforcement of the 
youth tobacco sales law, which Massachusetts has pursued, 
does not address the stimulation of youth demand for 
tobacco by petitioners’ advertising. Especially given the 
magnitude of the problem Massachusetts seeks to address, its 
determination to restrict tobacco advertising near its 
communities’ schools and playgrounds should be upheld.
