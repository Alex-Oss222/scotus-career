(2) A claim presented in a second or successive 
habeas corpus application under section 2254 that was 
not presented in a prior application shall be dismissed 
unless - 


(A) the applicant shows that the claim relies on

Court, that was previously unavailable; or

found the applicant guilty of the underlying

permitted by this section is filed in the district court, the 
applicant shall move in the appropriate court of appeals 
for an order authorizing the district court to consider the 
application. 


(B) A motion in the court of appeals for an order 
authorizing the district court to consider a second or 
successive application shall be determined by a three- 
judge panel of the court of appeals. 


(C) The court of appeals may authorize the filing of 
a second or successive application only if it determines 
that the application makes a prima facie showing that the 
application satisfies the requirements of this subsection. 


(D) The court of appeals shall grant or deny the 
authorization to file a second or successive application 
not later than 30 days after the filing of the motion. 


(E) The grant or denial of an authorization by a 
court of appeals to file a second or successive application 
shall not be appealable and shall not be the subject of a 
petition for rehearing or for a writ of certiorari. 


(4) A district court shall dismiss any claim pre- 
sented in a second or successive application that the court 
of appeals has authorized to be filed unless the applicant 
shows that the claim satisfies the requirements of this

STATEMENT OF FACTS 


Sometime near 2:00 a.m. on March 20, 1975, Melvin 
Tyler telephoned his estranged lover, Gloria Augustine 
wanting to know if she would return to him, or if she 
planned on reuniting with her husband. She replied no to 
each question and informed him that she was hanging up 
the telephone. (Tr. p. 66) Approximately five to ten min- 
utes later, without warning, Melvin Tyler fired five shots 


from a twenty-two caliber revolver into the bedroom of 
Gloria Augustine. (Tr. p. 57) Tvler’s own child, a new- 
born, Melba Augustine, was struck by three separate 
bullets, in the head, chest and hand, and died. (Tr. pp. 
3-6) The bullets were fired through a square piece of 
cardboard covering a broken segment of the bedroom 
window. (Tr. pp. 12, 66) Powder burns on the cardboard 
indicated the gun was fired from a distance of one to 
three inches. (Tr. p. 32) The child was laying on a bed 
directly in front of and against the window. Gloria and 
her husband, Joseph Augustine, were also in the room on 
the bed or an adjacent bed. (Tr. pp. 57, 65) 


Shortly after the shots were fired, Tyler began throw- 
ing bricks through nearly all the windows of the residence 
including the window adjacent to where the child lay. (Tr. 
pp. 10-11, 57, 67- 68) Gloria Augustine went to the aide of 
the newborn and then called out to Tyler, “Melvin my 
baby is dead.” He responded, “Bitch I am going to kill 
you” and threw another brick through a window. (Tr. pp. 
57, 68) In the house next door, Gloria Augustine’s sister, 
Erma Damango, hearing all that was transpiring, called 
out to Tyler to stop, that the children were in the house. 
He responded by telling her to shut up, that he was “going 
to get the bitch,” either “next” or “first.” (Tr. p. 48) 


Upon seeing that Tyler was about to gain entry into 
the house through the front door, Gloria Augustine, carry- 
ing the child, and Joseph Augustine fled out of the rear of 
the house. (Tr. p. 10-11, 74) Gloria entered her sister’s 
house next door. When the two women heard shaking at 
the back door, the sister moved the refrigerator in front of 
it. (Tr. p. 69) Whereupon, Tyler threw a brick through a 
window at that residence. (Tr. p. 69) In the alleyway, 


Joseph Augustine confronted Tyler and the two struggled 
until Tyler fled the area. (Tr. p. 58) The gun was recovered 
in the rear of a neighbor’s residence. (Tr. p. 78) 


At trial Tyler testified that he had been drinking and 
that after talking to Gloria he was “steamed” and did not 
have the intent to kill or hurt anyone especially not his 
daughter. (Tr. pp. 91-92)

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The clear intent of 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(2)(A) is that 
only rules that have been explicitly determined by this 
Court to fall within one of the two exceptions to Teague’s 
prohibition against the retroactive application of new 
rules of constitutional law may serve as the basis for a 
successive writ of habeas corpus. Every court to have 
considered the issue, with the exception of the Third 
Circuit in West v. Vaughn, 204 F.3d 53 (3d Cir. 2000), has 
determined that the plain implication of the statute, not- 
withstanding the reviewing court’s own consideration of 
the issue, is that only this Court can make new rules 
retroactive for purposes of succesive petitions by holding 
so directly. 


The interpretation adopted by the Third Circuit, and 
advanced by petitioner, is inconsistent with the sections 
respect for the finality for criminal judgments. Absent an 
affirmative statement from this Court, the courts of 
appeals will no doubt be lead to varying and inconsistent 
determinations of the availability of new rules to pris- 
oners attempting to file successive petitions. Further- 
more, that Congress did not anticipate the interpretation 
advanced by petitioner, is reflected in the concurrent 


— 


requirements of § 2244 regarding a prima facie showing 
that the requirements of the section be satisfied, and that 
the motion to file successive petitions be ruled on within 
thirty days, and that the district court dismiss any claim 
that fails to satisfy the requirements of the section. Finally 
had Congress sought to employ the interpretation 
advanced by petitioner, it would have employed the Tea- 
gue standard directly rather than including the require- 
ment of action by this Court. 


The two courts to address the retroactivity of Cage v. 
Louisiana, 498 U.S. 39 (1990) prior to this Court’s decision in 
Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275 (1993), determined that 
Cage did not satisfy the second Teague exception because Cage 
did not alter the elements essential to the fairness of a 
proceeding and was not a watershed rule of criminal pro- 
cedure. They were correct. Cage provides an additional mea- 
sure of protection to an existing guarantee of due process 
protection- against fundamental unfairness and is therefore 
not a watershed case.

ARGUMENT 


I. THE PLAIN LANGUAGE OF 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2244(b)(2)(A) REQUIRES THAT THIS COURT 
SPECIFICALLY DECLARE THE COLLATERAL 
AVAILABILITY OF NEW RULES OF CONSTITU- 
TIONAL LAW. 


The issue of whether 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(2)(A) 
requires a specific declaration as to the collateral avail- 
ability of new rules of constitutional law by this court or 
whether the statute can be fairly read to include the 
ability of federal courts to assess the availability of the 


new rule on their own (by applying a traditional Teague 
analysis) has been raised on numerous occasions. In In re 
Smith, 142 F.3d 832 (5th Cir. 1998), an inmate serving a life 
sentence for second degree murder sought to file a suc- 
cessive petition raising a Cage claim. The Fifth Circuit 
held that while Cage constituted “a new rule of constitu- 
tional law”, the Supreme Court had not itself made Cage 
retroactive to cases on collateral review, as required by 
2244(b)(2)(A). See also Brown v. Lensing, 171 F.3d 1031 (5th 
Cir. 1999) (although Court of Appeals had granted per- 
mission to file a successive petition, district court prop- 
erly dismissed petition since Brown failed to identify any 
decision by the Supreme Court authorizing collateral 
review of his Cage claim). 


In In re Hill, 113 F.3d 181 (11th Cir. 1997), a death row 
inmate sought to file a successive habeas petition, claim- 
ing that his Cage claim was exempt from the successive 
petition bar because it relied upon a new, retroactively 
applicable rule of constitutional law under 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2244(b)(2)(A). The Court held that even though the 
Eleventh Circuit had previously held! that Cage should 
properly be retroactively applied to cases on collateral 
review, that conclusion did not justify review of a succes- 
sive habeas action: “Hill’s Cage claim [did] not satisfy the 
requirement that any new rule of constitutional law relied 
upon must have been ‘made retroactive to cases on collat- 
eral review by the Supreme Court,’” since the United 
States Supreme Court had never so ruled. Id., at 184 
(emphasis in original).

II. CAGE V. LOUISIANA ANNOUNCED A “NEW 
RULE” 


Respondent concurs with petitioner that the rule 
announced in Cage v. Louisiana is a new rule of constitu- 
tional law within the meaning of Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 
288 (1989). The issue was one of first impression in Skel- 
ton v. Whitley, 950 F.2d 1037 (5th Cir. 1992) where the 
court considered that “ ‘a case announces a new rule if 
the result was not dictated by precedent existing at the 
time the defendant’s conviction became final,’” and 
stated, “This is emphatically such a case,” Id. at 1043, 
quoting Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. at 301. The Louisiana 
Supreme Court, addressing the retroactivity of Cage, like- 
wise determined that Cage announced a new rule. State ex 
rel. Taylor v. Whitley, 606 So. 2d 1292 (La. 1992) In Adams v. 
Aiken, 965 F.2d 1306 (4th Cir. 1992) the court stated, 
“Whether a trial court’s unconstitutional misdescription 
of the burden of proof in a criminal case violates the Due 
Process Clause was certainly an open question before 
Cage.” The Fifth Circuit’s opinion remained unchanged 
following Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275 (1993), which 
prompted the court to state, “It is undisputed that Cage 
announced a new rule of constitutional law.” In re Smith, 
142 F.3d 832, 835 (5th Cir. 1998). Indeed every court to 
reach the issue has determined that Cage announced a 
new rule. See Brief for Petitioner 13.

Ill. CAGE V. LOUISIANA IS NOT A “WATERSHED 
RULE” OF CRIMINAL PROCEDURE 


Prior to this Court’s decision in Sullivan v. Louisiana, 
508 U.S. 275 (1993) the two circuits that addressed the 
retroactivity of Cage v. Louisiana, 498 U.S. 39 (1990) found 
that the decision did not fall within the second exception 
of Teague v. Lane for watershed rules of criminal pro- 
cedure. In Adams v. Aiken, 965 F.2d 1306 (4th Cir. 1992) the 
Fourth Circuit concluded without hesitation that 
although Cage “eliminates confusion and improves the 
accuracy of the trial, ... it does not alter our under- 
standing of the bedrock procedural elements essential to 
the fairness of the proceeding.” Adams, supra at 1312, 
citing Sawyer v. Smith, 497 U.S. 227, 242 (1990) (omitting 
citation and interior quotation marks). The court stated 
that these bedrock procedural elements remain the same. 
“The burden of proof is not changed. Cage does not alter 
the elements, it criticizes their dilution.” Ibid. Similarly, 
the Fifth Circuit, in Skelton v. Whitley, 950 F.2d at 1045 
(1992), the first decision to address the issue following 
Cage, concluded that Cage failed to conform to the second 
Teague exception as it was not a watershed case in the 
vain of In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970) and Gideon v. 
Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963). Id. at 1045. 


Both courts’ analysis were correct. If there was any 
doubt following Teague that the second exception requires 
conformity on two levels, it was clearly established in 
Sawyer v. Smith: 


Petitioner contends that the second Teague 
exception should be read to include new rules of 
capital sentencing that “preserve the accuracy 
and fairness of capital sentencing judg- 
ments.” . . . But this test looks only to half of our

definition of the second exception. Acceptance 
of petitioner’s argument would return the sec- 
ond exception to the broad definition that Jus- 
tice Harlan first proposed in Desist but later 
abandoned in Mackey under which new rules 
that “significantly improve the pre-existing fact- 
finding procedures are to be retroactively 
applied on habeas.” Desist v. United States, 394 
U.S. 244, 262, 89 S.Ct. 1030, 1041, 22 L.Ed.2d 248 
(1969). In Teague, we modified Justice Harlan’s 
test to combine the accuracy element of the Des- 
ist test with the Mackey limitation of the excep- 
tion to watershed rules of fundamental fairness. 
It is thus not enough under Teague to say that a 
new rule is aimed at improving the accuracy of 
trial. More is required. A rule that qualifies 
under this exception must not only improve 
accuracy, but also ” ‘alter our understanding of 
the bedrock procedural elements’ ” essential to 
the fairness of a proceeding. Teague, supra, 489 
U.S. at 311, 109 S.Ct. at 1076 (plurality opinion) 
(quoting Mackey, 401 U.S., at 693, 91 S.Ct., at 
1180).
