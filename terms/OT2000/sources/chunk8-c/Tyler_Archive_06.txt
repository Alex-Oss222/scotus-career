SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


Under the AEDPA, a habeas petitioner may obtain 
relief in a successive petition if, but only if, he or she 
satisfies the requirements of 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(2). That 
subsection requires dismissal of a successive petition 
unless the petitioner establishes that he or she is legit- 
imately entitled to rely on either newly established rules 
of law or newly discovered facts warranting relief. As to 
newly established law, the statute provides that the suc- 
cessive habeas applicant must show that his claim “relies 
on a new rule of constitutional law, made retroactive to 
cases on collateral review by the Supreme Court, that was 
previously unavailable.” 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(2)(A). 


The AEDPA neither defines the term “new rule of 
constitutional law” nor provides standards for determin- 
ing when a “new rule” applies retroactively. This statu- 
tory silence, viewed against the backdrop of this Court’s 
habeas corpus jurisprudence, indicates an intent to incor- 
porate this Court’s body of cases concerning the defini- 
tion of new rules and the circumstances under which they 
are retroactively applicable in habeas proceedings. 


The underlying principles of retroactivity are, of 
course, set forth in this Court’s decision in Teague v. Lane, 
489 U.S. 288 (1989). Under Teague, a “new rule” of crimi- 
nal procedure is one not compelled or dictated by prece- 
dent at the time of its announcement by this Court. Such 
new rules are retroactively applicable only if they either 
place substantive conduct beyond the reach of criminal 
punishment or announce “watershed rules” that are 
essential to the fairness and reliability of criminal pro- 
ceedings. When a new rule within the meaning of Teague 


has been “made retroactive” by the Court under the 
criteria set forth in Teague, a successive habeas petition 
invoking that rule is not subject to dismissal under 
§ 2244(b)(2)(A). 


Here, the rule that petitioner Tyler sought to invoke 
in his successive habeas application is the holding of Cage 
v. Louisiana, 498 U.S. 39 (1990), which condemned the 
reasonable-doubt instruction given to the jury in Tyler’s 
case. There is no doubt that, from Tyler’s standpoint, Cage 
announced a “new rule” under Teague, for it postdates the 
affirmance of his conviction on direct appeal, and it was 
not dictated by precedent in existence at the time his 
conviction became final. , 


Nonetheless, the Fifth Circuit held that Tyler’s peti- 
tion was subject to dismissal under § 2244(b)(2)(A) - not 
because the Cage rule is not retroactive (indeed, the Fifth 
Circuit agrees with the other courts of appeals that it is 
retroactive), but because, in the Fifth Circuit’s view, the 
Cage rule has not yet been “made” retroactive by this 
Court. 


In so holding, the Fifth Circuit has given 
§ 2244(b)(2)(A) a constricted meaning that is not justified 
by its language. The statute does not require a direct 
holding by this Court that a particular rule is retroactive, 
but only action by this Court that has the effect of “mak- 
ing” it retroactive. Where precedents of this Court compel 
the conclusion that a particular new rule must be retroac- 
tive, that standard is satisfied. 


That is the situation in this case. In Sullivan v. Louisi- 
ana, 508 U.S. 275 (1993), this Court held that Cage error is 
a “structural error” that is so fundamental to the fairness 


10 


and accuracy of a criminal proceeding that it can never be 
harmless error. The reasoning of the Court in Sullivan 
demonstrates without doubt that Cage must also be retro- 
actively applicable in habeas proceedings, for the test for 
“structural error” is substantively identical to the one 
used to identify “watershed rules” under Teague. Sullivan 
held that Cage error necessarily vitiates a conviction, and 
the reasoning that leads to that result also dictates retro- 
activity. Indeed, the conclusion is so evident that every 
court of appeals to consider the issue after Sullivan has 
agreed. 


Thus, in a very real sense, this Court has “made” Cage 
retroactive. The Court of Appeals’ ruling, which is prem- 
ised solely on the view that Cage has not been made 
retroactive, must therefore be reversed. 


Alternatively, even if this Court has not yet “made” 
Cage retroactive within the meaning of § 2244(b)(2)(A), 
the reasoning of Sullivan plainly indicates that it should 
now do so explicitly. Once the Court takes that step, Cage 
will without question have been “made” retroactive by 
this Court, and Tyler’s successive petition will be proper 
under § 2244(b)(2)(A). Thus, even if the Court agrees with 
the Fifth Circuit’s construction of § 2244(b)(2)(A), reversal 
of the decision below will still be required once this Court 
resolves the question of Cage’s retroactivity.

A. Cage Announced a “New Rule” 


There is no question but that Cage v. Louisiana 
announced a “new rule” within the meaning of Teague v. 
Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), and the AEDPA. As Teague 
makes clear, “a case announces a new rule when it breaks 
new ground or imposes a new obligation on the States or 
the Federal Government” — that is, when “the result was 
not dictated by precedent existing at the time the defen- 
dant’s conviction became final.” Teague, 489 U.S. at 301. 
This definition of a new rule has been repeatedly reaf- 
firmed by the Court. See O'Dell v. Netherland, 521 U.S. 151, 
156 (1997); Graham v. Collins, 506 U.S. 461, 466-467 (1993). 
As the Court put it in Goeke v. Branch, 514 U.S. 115 (1995), 
the question is “whether a state court considering [the 
defendant's] claim at the time his conviction became final 
would have felt compelled by existing precedent to con- 
clude that the rule {he] seeks was required by the Consti- 
tution.” Jd. at 118 (quoting Saffle v. Parks, 494 U.S. 484, 488 
(1990)). If an issue was “susceptible to debate among 
reasonable minds,” the resolution of the issue constitutes 
a new rule. Butler v. McKellar, 494 U.S. 407, 415 (1990). 


Although the rule of Cage was based upon and was 
consistent with existing precedent, it was not dictated by 
precedent, and was hardly beyond debate by reasonable 
jurists. While the theoretical foundations for Cage were

C. The Rule in Cage Has Been “Made Retroactive” 
to Cases on Collateral Review by this Court 


Under Teague, the new rule of constitutional law 
announced by the Court in Cage is retroactively applica- 
ble to cases on collateral review if it falls within one of 
two “narrow exceptions” to Teague’s general nonretroac- 
tivity principle. The first exception, inapplicable here, is 
for rules that “plac[e] ‘certain kinds of primary, private 
individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law- 
making authority to proscribe.’” Teague, 489 U.S. at 307 
(quoting Mackey v. United States, 401 U.S. 667, 692 (1971) 
(opinion of Harlan, J.)). The second exception applies to 
“watershed rules of criminal procedure” that “implicate 
the fundamental fairness of the trial” and are “central to 
an accurate determination of innocence or guilt.” Teague, 
489 U.S. at 311, 312, 313. This Court’s decision in Sullivan 
v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 276 (1993), compels the conclusion 
that the Cage rule falls squarely within the second excep- 
tion. 


In Teague, the Court expressly adopted Justice Har- 
lan’s view of retroactivity for cases on collateral review, 
as set forth in his opinion in Mackey v. United States, 401 


23 


U.S. 667, 675 (1971) — including Justice Harlan’s view that 
there were but two exceptions to the rule. Teague, 489 U.S. 
at 311-12. Teague elaborated on the nature of the second 
exception, which in Justice Harlan’s formulation applied 
to new rules requiring the observance of “ ‘those pro- 
cedures that... are “implicit in the concept of ordered 
liberty.” ’” Teague, 489 U.S. at 311 (quoting Mackey, 401 
U.S. at 693). According to the Teague Court, Justice Har- 
lan’s language left “no doubt” that this second exception 
was “to be reserved for watershed rules of criminal pro- 
cedure.” !d. 


Teague went on to emphasize that the “watershed 
rules” exception was limited to new rules that combined 
two essential characteristics, both derived from Justice 
Harlan’s opinions on retroactivity. See Teague, 489 U.S. at 
312. First, a rule falling within the second exception must 
be “central to an accurate determination of innocence or 
guilt” — that is, it must establish “procedures without 
which the likelihood of an accurate conviction is seri- 
ously diminished.” Id. at 313. Second, “the procedure at 
issue must implicate the fundamental fairness of the 
trial.” Id. at 312. In Justice Harlan’s words, the new rule 
must “ ‘alter our understanding of the bedrock procedural 
elements that must be found to vitiate the fairness of a 
particular conviction.’ ” Id. (quoting Mackey, 401 U.S. at 
693-94) (emphasis in Teague). 


Subsequent decisions of this Court have reiterated 
these basic elements of the second Teague exception. For 
example, in Sawyer v. Smith, 497 U.S. 227 (1990), the Court 
summarized the exception as follows: “A rule that quali- 
fies under this exception must not only improve accuracy, 


¥ 24 


but also ‘ “alter our understanding of the bedrock pro- 
cedural elements” ’ essential to the fairness of a proceed- 
ing.” Id. at 242 (citation omitted). See also, e.g., Graham v 
Collins, 506 U.S. 461, 478 (1993); Butler v. McKellar, 494 
U.S. 407, 416 (1990). 


A rule that satisfies these stringent limitations is in 
select company indeed. That the rule in Cage falls within 
this “small core of rules” (Graham v. Collins, 506 U.S. at 
478), however, has already been effectively determined 
by this Court’s holding in Sullivan v. Louisiana that the 
Cage rule is in the equally exclusive company of those 
rules that are so fundamental to the fairness of a proceed- 
ing and so essential to the accurate determination of guilt 
or innocence that their violation can never be harmless 
error. 


In Sullivan, the Court held that a jury instruction 
identical to the one held unconstitutional in Cage was one 
of those few “structural defects in the constitution of the 
trial mechanism, which defy analysis by harmless-error 
standards.” Sullivan, 508 U.S., at 281 (quoting Arizona v. 
Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279, 309 (1991)). The Court rested 
this conclusion on the role of a constitutionally adequate 
reasonable doubt instruction in ensuring the fundamental 
right to trial by jury, which “includes, of course, as its 
most important element, the right to have the jury, rather 
than the judge, reach the requisite finding of ‘guilty.’ ” Id. 
at 277. Moreover, the Court emphasized, “the jury verdict 
required by the Sixth Amendment is a jury verdict of 


guilty beyond a reasonable doubt” —- a verdict that cannot | 


ever come into being when an improper reasonable-doubt 
instruction is given. Id. at 278. As the Chief Justice put it 
in his concurring opinion, “A constitutionally deficient

reasonable-doubt instruction will always result in the 
absence of ‘beyond a reasonable doubt’ jury findings.” Id.

tion of the burden of proof” “vitiates all the jury’s find-

The conclusion that the Cage rule is “structural error” 
that is not amenable to harmless error review necessarily 
implies that the Cage rule falls within the second Teague 
exception, for the two tests are essentially identical. Both 
are designed to isolate a very small class of procedures so 
fundamental that this Court will not tolerate a conviction

when they have been violated.!° And both have the same 
focus on identifying procedures that are simultaneously 
essential to the fundamental fairness of a proceeding and 
to the reliability of the determination of innocence or 
guilt. As the Court explained in Arizona v. Fulminante, 499 
U.S. 279 (1991), which established the standard for identi- 
fying structural errors that was applied in Sullivan, a 
structural error is one that deprives the defendant of 
those “basic protections” without which “a criminal trial 
cannot reliably serve its function as a vehicle for deter- 
mination of guilt or innocence, and no criminal punish- 
ment may be regarded as fundamentally fair.” Id. at 310 
(quoting Rose v. Clark, 478 U.S. at 577-78). The twin 
requirements of reliability and fundamental fairness are 
perfectly congruent with the elements of the second Tea- 
gue exception.
