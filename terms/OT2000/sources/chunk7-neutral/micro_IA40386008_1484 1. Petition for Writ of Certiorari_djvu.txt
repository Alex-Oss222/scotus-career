QUESTIONS PRESENTED FOR REVIEW 
Preface 


On May 20, 1996, Judge Jackson L. Kiser, Chief 
Judge of the U.S. District Court for Virginia, Western 
Division, issued a court order (App. at 3a) to the Virginia 
State Board of Elections which enjoined it from using its early 
June deadline for filing petitions for the U.S. Senate in the 
1996 general election. Judge Kiser directed the Board to set 
a new filing deadline no earlier than 90 days before the 
general election, sometime in early August. 

The Board’s Motion to Stay (App. at Sa) was denied 
by the District Court on June 20, 1996, and later by the 4th 
Circuit on Aug.7, 1996 (App. at 6a). 

On June 25, 1996, the Board held a news conference 
at which it announced the ballot line and the order of 
contents on the ballot for U.S. Senate in the general election 
of 1996. It had still not set anew deadline, as the federal 
court order had ordered it to do more than a month before. 

On July 25, the Board, including an officer, was telling 
people who inquired about the filing deadline that the deadline 
had passed in early June, 1996. The Board blatantly admits to 
doing so and admits that when mention of the federal court 
order was made, it would change its mind and tell that caller 
that the deadline was Aug. 7, 1996. 

The Board never set a new deadline until after the 
new deadline, nor was the new deadline announced until after 
the new deadline, and that was done nearly three months after 
the federal court order had directed the Board to set a new 
deadline. 

Instead of addressing the question at hand, whether 
the State Board of Elections had failed to comply with the 
court order, the Fourth Circuit said the Board of Elections 


“_.no longer needs to comply with the district court’s prior 
order” (App. at 1a), which it demonstrably never did, the crux 
the argument in the first place that is not addressed by the 
Fourth Circuit. Additionally, questions presented for 
review include 


1. Whether all federal court orders involving 
fundamental, Constitutionally guaranteed rights can be safely 
ignored pending appeal despite denials of stay pending appeal, 
in this instance also ignoring the First and Fourteenth 
Amendment rights of all voters in an entire state despite their 
being under the further protection of a federal court order. 


2. Whether Elrod v. Burns 427 U.S. 347, 373 
(1976) still stands as meaning “[t]he loss of 1st Amendment 
freedoms, for even minimal periods of time, unquestionably 
constitutes irreparable injury,” and whether Bullock v. 
Carter 405 U.S. 134 (1972) still means that inherent with the 
right to vote is the right to run, within reasonable limitations 
based on administrative burden, never the case in this case, 
the Board’s admitting that it takes only 30 days to process all 
petitions (sans Presidential). 


3. Whether a U.S. Senate petition filing deadline 
more than five full months before the general election is, 
indeed, violative of the First and Fourteenth Amendments.

CONCISE STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Culminating a federal court dispute started in the 
summer of 1994 to secure the First and Fourteenth 
Amendment rights for all the voters of the Commonwealth of 
Virginia, petitioner won Summary Judgement against the 
State Board of Elections for the Commonwealth of Virginia 
that enjoined the Board from enforcing the declaration of 
candidacy and petition filing deadline imposed on independent 
candidates for the office of U.S. Senator as established by Va. 
Code 24.2-507(1), a code requiring petitions for ballot 
inclusion to be filed by the second Tuesday in June of the 
general election year, more than five full months before the 
general election and for which the Board demonstrated no 
administrative necessity. 

The court order of May 20, 1996, (App. at 3a) 
directed the Board to set a new deadline of no earlier than 
ninety (90) days before the general election...sometime in the 
second week of August. 

The relief ordered by the federal court order was ; 
never delivered by the Virginia State Board of Elections; the 
effect of the Order was denied by the Board when inquiries 
were made, and the Board announced the content and order 


3 


of conterits on the ballot long prior to the court-ordered 
deadline. 

Although the district court declared the Virginia State 
Code involving ballot access un-Constitutional, McCarthy y. 
Briscoe, 429 U.S. 1317 (1976); McInerney v. Wrightson, 
421 F. Supp 726 (1976); Hail v. Austin, 495 F. Supp 782 
(1980); and Goldman-Frankie v. Austin, 727 F 2nd 603 
(1984) were not applied. 

No un-aligned independent has ever met the 
requirement set forth in Va. Code 24.2-507(1), requiring that 
some 15,000 signatures be submitted some 150 days before 
the general election. These signatures, unlike those for major 
party primaries, had to be certified by being sent to respective 
registrars throughout Virginia. That the number of required 
signatures is currently 10,000 has no effect on the fact that 
just after major party primaries/conventions, all ballot access 
slams shut five months before the general election, denying 
the voters any chance to select other candidates to run, nor 
does the reduction to 10,000 signatures affect the weather, 
either literally or in a political sense during the time presently 
allocated to collect signatures. 

The Board admitted that it took only 30 days to 
certify all petitions for all offices except Presidential petitions. 

Anyway, this Court, in Bullock v. Carter 405 U.S. 
134 (1972) held that a mere argument of burden does not 
override fundamental Constitutional rights. 

On June 25, 1996, the Board held a news 
conference/lottery to determine the order of appearance of 
candidates’ names on the ballot for U.S. Senator in the 
November, 1996, general election, although it had no 
regulatory authority to do so and even though it was under a 
federal court order to keep the ballot line open until the 
second week in August. The only regulatory authority it had 
(Va. Code 74.2-613, app. at 17a ) was to announce the

All the media present disseminated the contents and

Voters throughout the state were given the content

set by the Board). 

A person calling the Board to inquire about the

passed, had been on the second Tuesday in June, 1996. 

If a caller happened to have heard about the court 
order and mentioned it, immediately the caller was given a 
new deadline, sometime in August. 

The Board admits freely to having both announced the 
content and order of the ballot on June 25, 1996, despite a ; 
federal court order to keep the ballot open until the second 
week in August. The Board admits freely to giving out what 
it knew was false information regarding the deadline, only 
correcting it in instances when the caller happened to have

The Board never set a new deadline as directed by the

The respondent’s not following the federal court order

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 


Among and in addition to the afore-mentioned special 
and important reasons this Court should grant this Writ of 
Certiorari are 

1. Such decisions by the 4th Circuit depart from 
accepted and usual course of judicial proceedings and 
decisions, and sanction such a departure by the lower court. 

2. Such a decision that allows people entrusted with 
the public trust to circumvent a federal court order dilutes the i 
force of a federal court order, particularly one so directly | 
concerned with the First and Fourteenth Amendment rights of 
all the voters of the Commonwealth of Virginia. 

3. The matter of federal judicial authority is so clearly 
(and so far successfully) challenged by the respondent that 


7 


judicial review by this Court is warranted and 
should and can be settled only by this Court. 

4. The Fourth Circuit order of February 17, 1998. 
(the crux of the reason for this petition) (App. at 1a) evaded 
and missed the basic argument at hand beginning with the 
assumption and assertion that the Board of Elections had ever 
complied with the May 20, 1996, district court order...”the 
Commonwealth no longer needs to comply with the district 
court’s prior order.” The argument is that the Board never 
complied with the’ order, and that assertion was the bone of 
contention from the outset and has yet to be addressed. 

5. The public importance of these issues affects and 
ranges far beyond the interests of the parties to this case. 

6. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit’s 
decision runs contrary to Elrod v. Burns, 427 U S. 347, 373 
(1976) and to Bullock v. Carter, 405 U.S. 134 (1972). 

7. The Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals ignored and 
circumvented petitioner’ s attempt to seek judicial redress 

8. U.S.C. 42 (1983) applies.

independent candidates for the United States Senate by Va. 
Code Ann. & 24.2-507(1). The placement of independent 
candidates’ names on the ballot is governed by Va. Code Ann. 


1. This uncapitalized term should not be confused with Independent 
candidates, who are members of a party recognized by the Commonwealth pursuant 
to Va. Code Ann. & 24.2-101,

Code Ann. & 24.2-534. Section 613 does not direct a specific date.

such drawing is held. Plaintiff's complaint is one of public

Virginia Code 24.2-534. Returns tabulated by State 
Board; when nominee declared. --- As soon as possible 
after the receipt of the certified abstract and not later than 
fourteen days after the day of the election, the State Board 
shall open and tabulate the returns. Upon completion of the 
tabulation the Board shall declare the nominee in the manner 
and form as it does in general elections. (Code 1950, 24-389; 
1952, c. 4; 1970, c. 462, 24.1-192; 1978, c. 778; 1993, c. 
641.)

Virginia Code 24.2-613. «Form of the ballot. ---The ballots 
shall be white paper without any distinguishing mark or 
symbol and shall contain the.names of all the candidates 
qualifying to have their names printed on the official ballot as 
provided by law. Their names shall be printed in black ink, 
immediately below the office for which they have qualified as 
candidates. The names on the ballot shall be in clear print; 
each name shall be on a separate line; and the type used in 
printing the ballots shall be plain Roman type, not smaller 
than twelve-point pica. Immediately to the left of and on the 
same line with the name of each candidate shall be printed a 
square, not less than one-quarter, nor more than one-half inch 
in size, printed thus: 
[] JOHN DOE 

Except as provided for primary elections, the State 
Board shall determine by lot the order of the political parties, 
and the names of all the candidates for a particular office shall 
appear together in the order determined for their parties. In 
an election district in which more than one person is 
nominated by one political party for the same office, the 
candidates’ names shall appear alphabetically in their party 
groups under the name of the office, with sufficient space 
between party groups to indicate them as such. For the 
purpose of this section and 24.2-640, except as provided for 
presidential elections in 24.2-614, independent candidates 
shall be treated as a class under “Independent”; their names 
shall be placed on the ballot after the political parties; and 
where there is more than one independent candidate for an 
office, their names shall appear alphabetically. 

No names of political parties shall appear on the 
ballot, except as provided in 24.2-614 for presidential 
elections.
