STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


The Good News Club (“Good News”) is a religious 
organization, sponsored by the Children Evangelism Fellowship 
Inc., a worldwide Christian ministry, which is committed to the 
spreading of the gospel of the New Testament. In the fall of 
1996, a local chapter of the Good News Club, which was 
sponsored by Rev. Stephen Fournier, a minister, and his wife 
Darlene, requested to use the Milford Central School District 
building for weekly meetings of Good News. The activities of 
Good News consisted of religious instruction and religious 
indoctrination and the Milford Central School District rejected 
the application. This lawsuit resulted. 


Milford Central School District (“Milford”) is a public 
school district and municipal corporation located in Otsego 
County, New York. Milford consists of a single school building 
that houses all grades of the district, K-12. Milford educates 
approximately 530 students of ages 5 through 18. As a public 
school district in New York, its facilities are open for use by the 
public in accordance with New York Education Law § 414. 
Milford had a Community Use of School Facilities policy which 
was in effect at the time that the Petitioners applied for 
permission to utilize Milford’s facilities. Petition for Writ of 
Certiorari at D1-D3. 


Milford denied the Petitioners’ application for use of the 
building following a thorough review of the program, upon the 
ground that the program was the “equivalent of religious 
worship” which was prohibited under the Respondent’s policy. 
Joint Appendix (“J.A.”) at H1-H2. Upon the completion of 
pre-trial discovery, both parties moved for summary judgment 
upon facts which were largely stipulated. The District Court 
found, as a matter of fact, that Good News was in a different 
genre than Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Clubs in that Good 
News dealt specifically with religious subject matter, not as 
Petitioners contend, a religious perspective on a secular subject 
matter. See Petition for Writ of Certiorari at C30-C31. 
The Circuit Court made the same factual findings and affirmed

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Milford presents two principle arguments in opposition to 
the Petitioners’ appeal. First, Respondent contends that the 
Establishment Clause of the First Amendment was violated by 
the proposed use and the actual use pursuant to the preliminary 
order of the District Court. The evidence in this case established 
that the Petitioners engaged in religious worship and instruction 
when they conducted meetings of Good News. The evidence 
also established that the Petitioners sought the use of the Milford 
school building only at times and on occasions when the students 
were actually assembled. Petitioners sought the use of the school 
building at 3:00 p.m. on Tuesday afternoons, during the school 
year. Petitioners limited their request for use of the building to 
times when the students were already assembled and readily 
accessible for purposes of solicitation and proselytizing. 
Respondent submits that it conducted a good faith, thorough 
investigation of the details of the program offered by Good News 
and correctly concluded that to permit this club involved Milford 
in impermissibly endorsing, promoting and aiding a specific 
religion. People of the State of Illinois, ex rel: McCollum v. Board 
of Educ., 333 U.S. 203 (1948). 


The second argument advanced by Milford is that in denying 
access to Good News, it had not engaged in impermissible 
viewpoint discrimination. The evidence established that there 
was no group that had been permitted to use Milford’s limited 
open forum for purposes similar to those of Good News. The 
evidence established that meetings of Good News were 
qualitatively different from the meetimgs of other groups 
permitted to use the school facilities. The meetings of Good 
News were decidedly religious and specifically Christian in their 
onentation. No other group, club or activity engaged in any 
similar conduct. The Respondent contends that it properly and 
lawfully excluded Good News from the limited open forum in

of certain subjects.” Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense 
and Educational Fund, Inc., 473 U.S. 788, 802 (1985). 
See also Bronx Household of Faith v. Community School Dist. 
No. 10, 127 F.3d 207 (2d Cir. 1997). 


This Court has recognized that in the context of a limited 
open forum a school district or the state may exclude certain 
subjects. In Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University 
of Virginia, 515 U.S. 819 (1995), this Court observed: 
“The necessities of confining a forum to the limited and 
legitimate purposes for which it was created may justify the 
state in reserving it for certain groups or for the discussion of 
certain topics.” Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 829. This Court has 
also observed that the state or a school district may exclude 
speech where the content of that speech exceeds the lawful 
boundaries which the state or the school district has set for itself. 
See Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 829. In this case, we contend that 
the forum created by Milford was not and had never been open 
to purely religious instruction, indoctrination and worship. 
The Petitioners seek to promote the use of adult-led, adult- 
initiated prayer and worship of a particular religious faith in a 


30 


public elementary school. Those who did not accept this 
religious doctrine were essentially compelled to avoid this 
program. 

The record in this case reflects that Milford consistently 
excluded certain types of speech, including purely religious 
worship and proselytizing speech. Rev. Fournier testified that 
the former superintendent advised him that he had denied 
previous requests for purely religious uses and, therefore, could 
not allow Good News its intended use. J.A. at P44. The policy 


enacted by Milford was consistent with New York Education 
Law § 414. 


The evidence in the record did not establish a single incident 
or instance at Milford of comparable instruction by any of the 
groups to which the Petitioners have compared themselves. 
We note, in particular, that there was no evidence in the record 
that explains what character education was actually provided 
by the Boy Scouts or Cub Scouts at Milford. We also note that 
the record contains references to the fact that the meetings of 
the Boy Scouts held at the school district were for a limited 
purpose and on limited occasions. Specifically, the record 
reflects that only leadership meetings and organizational 
meetings were held at Milford. Thus, there was no direct 
evidence that any instruction in “moral education” or in 
“spiritual development” ever took place on the Milford premises. 
Further, such instruction, if it even took place on an incidental 
basis, was vastly different from the religious worship and 
instruction involved here. 


Furthermore, there was no development in the record of 
precisely what it was that the Cub Scouts did by way of 
providing “moral education.” The only reference to the Cub 
Scouts having provided any “moral education” was found in 
the Joint Appendix at Y3 which states that one of the purposes 
of the Cub Scouts was to “influence a boy’s character, 
development and spiritual growth; develop habits and attitudes 
of good citizenship, etc.” Absent from the record was any 
specific evidence to demonstrate that the Cub Scouts or the 


31 


Boy Scouts engaged in any form of similar religious instruction 
or worship. The record evidence in this case failed to 
demonstrate how the Cub Scouts or Boy Scouts conducted their 
meetings. There was no evidence of what was done at the 
meetings, and whether any instruction or education in regard to 
moral, spiritual or similar issues was ever actually presented 
by such organizations in Milford. On this record, we respectfully 
submit that there was no instance of any group or club which 
conducted an activity similar to Petitioners. 


We respectfully submit that the Petitioners’ “Free Speech - 
Viewpoint Discrimination” argument must yield to the 
overriding Establishment Clause concerns presented here and 
the lack of evidence of discrimination by Respondent. 
Lee v. Weisman, 505 U.S. 577, 587 (1992); Berger v. Rensselaer 
Cent. School Corp., 982 F.2d 1160, 1168 (7th Cir. 1993). 
The Court in Berger observed: 


Defendant is also wrong as a matter of law that the 
First Amendment interest in free expression 
automatically trumps the First Amendment 
prohibition on state sponsored religious activity. The 
reverse is true in the coercive context of public 
schools. 


Berger, 982 F.2d at 1168. We categorically deny the claim that 
the Respondent had engaged in “prohibited viewpoint 
discrimination.” Milford had not discriminated against the 
Petitioners on the basis of Petitioners’ viewpoint. Milford had 
rejected the Petitioners’ use of the school building for the simple 
reason that the Petitioners sought to engage in religious 
instruction and religious indoctrination on school premises 
immediately at the end of the school day. We contend that this 
result was not only permissible, it was required. 


The principle that government may accommodate 
the free exercise of religion does not supercede 
the fundamental limitations imposed by the 
Establishment Clause. It is beyond dispute that, at a 
minimum, the Constitution guarantees that 


32 


government may not coerce anyone to support or 
participate in religion or its exercise or otherwise 
act in a way which “establishes a [state] religion or 
religious faith, or tends to do so.” 


Lee v. Weisman, 505 U.S. 577, 587 (1992). 
We also note and strenuously oppose the argument 


advanced by the Petitioners that Milford would exclude all 


activity by Good News. See, Petitioners’ Brief at 13. Such a 
Suggestion is contrary to the record evidence. In the Joint 
Appendix at H1-H2, the Superintendent of Schools 
acknowledged this Court’s decision in Lamb’s Chapel and stated 
uses consistent with that decision would be permitted. However, 
the Superintendent, after a detailed review of Petitioners’ 
program concluded that its program was the equivalent of 
religious worship (J.A. at H1-H2) and denied the request. 


The decision to deny access to Good News was based upon 
a well-founded belief that Good News was engaging in the 
practice of religion and religious indoctrination in the school 
through an adult-led and adult-initiated program. The basis for 
this exclusion was approved by the lowers courts in this case. 


Milford maintains that its decision to exclude was based 
solely upon its investigation of the activities of Good News and 
its determination that Good News was engaged in religious 
instruction and religious indoctrination in the school. The record 
established that the decision of Milford was based entirely upon 
consideration of the content of the speech, not its viewpoint. 
The limited open forum was properly closed to this program of 
adult-led, adult-initiated proselytizing of Milford’s most 
vulnerable students. 


C. Milford Did Not Become Excessively Entangled 


with Religion When it Inquired into Good News’ 
Intended Use. 


We note that the Petitioners and several of the amici make 
the argument that the Respondent cannot separate religious 
instruction and religious services from a secular activity carried 


33 


out from a religious viewpoint. Petitioners’ Brief at 22-26. 
We disagree and respectfully submit that if this argument were 
accepted then all religious activity must be permitted at any 
time in the schoolhouse. As discussed in Point I above dealing 
with the Establishment Clause, the issue of presenting religious 
material from a non-sectarian point of view or a non-theological 
point of view was rejected in School Dist. of Abington Township 
v. Schempp, supra. We respectfully submit that the argument 
that offering up this religious instruction and indoctrination as 
nothing more than “teaching morals from a Christian 
perspective” is to ignore the stark reality of the facts of this 
case. The Petitioners have engaged in, and have actively pursued, 
an agenda of religious instruction and religious indoctrination. 
This was not changed by the Petitioners’ facile characterization 
of its intent. An investigation of the purpose of Good News’ 
intended use of the school revealed the plainly religious purpose 
of the Petitioners’ request. 


This Court has often recognized that drawing the lines of 
distinction in Establishment Clause cases is not a precise 
science. In Lynch v. Donnelly, 465 U.S. 668 (1984), this Court 
stated: 


In each case, the inquiry calls for line drawing; no 
fixed, per se rule can be framed. The Establishment 
Clause like the Due Process Clause is not a precise, 
detailed provision in a legal code capable of ready 
application. The purpose of the Establishment 
Clause “was to state an objective, not to write a 
statute.” Walz, supra, at 668. The line between 
permissible relationships and those barred by the 
clause can no more be straight and unwavering than 
due process can be defined in a single stroke or 
phrase or test. The clause erects a “blurred, indistinct, 
and variable barrier depending on all the 
circumstances of a particular relationship.” 


Lynch, 465 U.S. at 678-679. 


34 


We contend that it is not a difficult task for school districts 
to determine whether a certain activity is “quintessentially 
religious.” Nor does such an inquiry “entangle” the Respondent 
with the religion of the Petitioners. Such line drawing does not 
involve the school district or the courts in impermissible 
intrusion into religious activity nor does it entangle the school 
district with religion to make such determinations. The school 
district is responsible to examine and oversee what takes place 
in its school buildings, both before, during and after the school 
day. For example, this Court has held that school officials must 
make “certain that ... subsidized teachers do not inculcate 
religion.” Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602, 619 (1971). Schools 
are required to make these determinations and have done so 
often. Marchi v. Board of Co-op. Educational Services, 173 
F.3d 469, 475 (2d Cir. 1999) where the Court prohibited a teacher 
from instructing in religious matters. Peloza v. Capistrano 
Unified School Dist., 37 F.3d 517 (9th Cir. 1994) where a school 
district properly prohibited a teacher from discussing his 
religious believes with students during the school day on school 
grounds. Helland v. South Bend Community School Corp., 93 
F.3d 327 (7th Cir. 1996) where the Court ruled that a school 
district correctly removed a substitute teacher because of 
his efforts to teach religion in the school during the day. 
See also Bishop v. Aronov, 926 F.2d 1066 (11th Cir. 1991); 
Roberts v. Madigan, 921 F.2d 1047 (10th Cir. 1990); Webster v. 
New Lenox School Dist., 917 F.2d 1004 (7th Cir. 1990). 


Milford was obligated to proceed with “heightened 
concern” for protecting the freedom of conscience of the 
students in its district from subtle and coercive pressure. 
See Lee v. Weisman, 505 U.S. 577, 592 (1992). This Milford 
attempted to do by asking for information from Good News 
that was no different than what was requested from others who 
sought use of the building. 


Contrary to assertions by Petitioners, there were no probing 
questions asked of any member of Petitioners’ Club. Petitioners’ 
Brief at 25. Those inquiries only occurred after the litigation 


35 


was commenced and during an Examination Before Trial. 
The process which Respondents followed consisted of an 
examination of the materials to be used in the program and an 
analysis of the Petitioners’ own description of the event on a 
standard form. J.A. at W1. This inquiry represented a non- 
discriminatory examination of the purpose of the request for 
use of the building; similar to that of every other user. 


The Respondent made a reasoned and careful judgment” 
regarding the Petitioners’ requested use of the building. 
Respondent followed the same procedure in regard to 
Petitioners’ application as it did with all others. Respondent 
did not become unduly entangled with Petitioners’ religious 
practices. 


D. The Impressionability of the Young Students in this 
Case Has Been Recognized by the Court. 


Respondent also rejects the argument that the 
impressionability of elementary age students, ages 5 to 12, is 
something which must be proven by evidence in the record. 
Amicus Brief of Laycock at 5. We respectfully submit that such 
an assertion is without foundation. This Court and numerous 
other courts have recognized and taken notice of the 
impressionability of young students in elementary and 
secondary public schools. See Lee v. Weisman, supra; School 
Dist. of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203 (1963), 
Edwards vy. Aguillard, 428 U.S. 578 (1987); Board of Educ. of 
the Westside Community Schools v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226 
(1990). 

As this Court observed in School Dist. of the City of Grand 
Rapids v. Ball, 473 U.S. 373 (1985): 


It follows that an important concern of the effects 
test is whether the symbolic union of church and 
state effected by the challenged governmental action 
is sufficiently likely to be perceived by adherents of 
the controlling denominations as an endorsement, 
and by the nonadherents as a disapproval, of their 
individual religious choices. The inquiry into this 


36 


kind of effect must be conducted with particular 
care when many of the citizens perceiving the 
governmental message are children in their formative 
years. Cf. Widmar v. Vincent, supra, at 274, 102 S.Ct., 
at 276; Tilton v. Richardson, 403 U.S. 672, 685- 
686, 91 S.Ct. 2091, 2099, 29 L.Ed.2d 790 (1971). 
The symbolism of a union between church and state 
is most likely to influence children of tender years, 
whose experience is limited and whose beliefs 
consequently are the function of environment as 
much as of free and voluntary choice. 


School Dist. of the City of Grand Rapids, 473 U.S. at 390. 


Congress has also recognized the vulnerability and 
impressionability of elementary age children. It specifically 
excluded elementary age children from coverage under the Equal 
Access Act because of fear expressed by various members that 
such children would be unable to distinguish school sponsored 
prayer and speech from private speech. 20 U.S.C. §§ 4017-4074; 


see also S. Rep. No. 98-357 at 43-49 (1984), as reported in 


1984 U.S.C.C.A.N. 2348, 2389-94. 


Respondent respectfully submits that it acted properly when 
it rejected the Good News application based upon its policy 
determination that to allow religious instruction in the schools 
would be inappropriate and would expand the forum. 
The impressionability of these students has been recognized 
by this Court. There was no need for Milford to separately prove 
the impressionability of elementary age students. Milford was 
required to take special care to insulate these students from the 
adult-led proselytizing of the Petitioners. The decision of the 
court below should be affirmed. 


E. The Cases of Lamb’s Chapel, Widmar and Rosenberg 
Do Not Support Petitioners’ Arguments. 


The Petitioners have written extensively in regard to 
comparing this case to Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union 
Free School Dist., 508 U.S. 384 (1993); Widmar v. Vincent, 454 


37 


U.S. 263 (1981) and Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of 
University of Virginia, 515 U.S. 819 (1995). We respectfully 
submit that these cases are inapposite to the present 
circumstance. 


In Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School 
Dist., supra, the majority of this Court ruled that it was not 
considering, and did not consider, the issue of religious 
instruction or Sunday school in a public school in its decision. 
At footnote 2 of the Majority Opinion, at 389, this Court 
observed as follows: 


Shortly before the first of these requests, the Church 
had applied for permission to use school rooms for 
its Sunday morning services and for Sunday school. 
The hours specified were 9 a.m. to | p.m and the 
time period one year beginning in the next month. 
959 F.2d 381, 383 (CA2 1992). Within a few days, 
the district wrote petitioner that the application 
“requesting use of the high school for your Sunday 
services” was denied, citing both New York 
Education Law § 414 and the district’s Rule 7 barring 
uses for religious purposes. The Church did not 
challenge this denial in the courts and the validity 
of this denial is not before us. 


Lamb's Chapel, 508 U.S. at 389 n.2 (Emphasis supplied). 


We respectfully submit that the issue in the instant case is 
the issue identified in Footnote 2 and which this Court did not 
address in Lamb’s Chapel, supra. We respectfully submit that 
the issue before this Court is whether a Sunday school type 
program, replete with religious instruction and religious 
indoctrination is permissible in a public school setting. 
We respectfully submit that under the established facts of this 
case Good News’ activity was impermissible and violated the 
Establishment Clause. 


The facts of this case established that Milford was not 
dealing with a secular activity presented from a Christian 


38 


perspective. See Decision of District Court which distinguished 
Lamb's Chapel, Petition for Writ of Certiorari at C29-C30. 
The District was faced with activity which paralleled a religious 
worship service and constituted religious instruction. 
As described by the court below, this activity was 
“quintessentially religious.” Good News Club y. Milford Cent. 
School, 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2000). There was little doubt that 
the activities engaged in by Rev. Fournier in the meetings of 
Good News were decidedly religious in nature. Although Good 
News sought to defend its activities on the ground that it 
endeavored to teach morality from a Christian perspective, Good 
News did little more than to change terminology without 


affecting the content of their message. This definitional coup 
must fail. 


Good News seeks by an alteration of language to change 
this case into something it is not. Good News seeks by verbal 
sleight of hand to change religious instruction and religious 
- worship into morality and character education. Although 
religious instruction may have its moral benefits and may be 
beneficial to the students’ character development, it nonetheless 
remains purely religious instruction and indoctrination. 


The logical extension of Petitioners’ argument that its 
activity is protected speech from a religious viewpoint would 
be that the Establishment Clause be overruled by the Free Speech 
Viewpoint Discrimination Doctrine. This would be a result at 
odds with long standing First Amendment Law. School Dist. of 
Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203 (1963); Berger vy. 


Rensselaer Cent. School Corp., 982 F.2d 1160, 1168 (7th Cir. 
1993). 


Milford contends that Lamb’s Chapel is not dispositive of 
this case. Although Lamb’s Chapel may provide guidance to 
schools in viewpoint discrimination cases, this case is purely 
an Establishment Clause case in which a religious club sought 
to engage in purely religious worship and instruction under the 
guise of a free speech argument. By contrast, Lamb’s Chapel

also involved an evening program for adults concerning a secular

Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), also is not 
controlling in this case. In Widmar, the University denied 
permission to a group known as “Cornerstone” which sought 
to hold its meetings in classrooms or in the student center. 
The meetings were open to the public and attracted 
approximately 125 students. The typical Cornerstone meeting 
included prayer, hymns, bible commentary, and a discussion of 
religious views and experiences. It is significant that this activity 
was entirely student led and student driven. Second, it was 
conducted on a college campus where young adults, rather than 
children of tender years, were present. In Widmar, supra, the 
majority of this Court observed as follows: 


University students are, of course, young adults. 

They are less impressionable than younger students 

and should be able to appreciate that the University’s 

policy is one of neutrality toward religion. 

Widmar, 454 U.S. at 274 n.14. Thus, the Court’s opinion 
distinguishes Widmar from the facts involved in the Good News 
activity. In Good News, we were dealing with the youngest 
children registered in a public school. Unlike Widmar, we are 
not dealing with mature young adults expressing themselves in 
the marketplace of ideas. | 

Respondent argues that there is simply no comparison 
between dealing with well-educated adults in a college setting 
who gather together for purposes of some religious prayer and 
the gathering of elementary age students for religious 
indoctrination conducted by a frocked minister of an established 
church, while the students are still assembled through the means 
of compulsory attendance laws. 

Respondent also notes that in Widmar, supra, this Court 
held that the University had created an open or public forum. 
This, of course, was unlike the situation at Milford where the 
school established a limited public forum only. Since the University 
in Widmar could show no compelling reason for closing the 


40 


forum to this religious activity, the Court found a constitutional 
violation in the exclusion of that activity. In this case, Milford 
had a limited open forum in which no similar activity had 
ever been demonstrated and where compelling reasons have 
been shown for closing the forum to the Petitioners’ requested use. 


In Widmar v. Vincent, supra, this Court observed: 


But this Court has explained that a religious 
organization’s enjoyment of merely “incidental” 
benefits does not violate the prohibition against the 
“primary advancement” of religion. 


Widmar, 454 U.S. at 273. 


Unlike Widmar, the benefits to the Good News Club from 
the use of the classroom, immediately after school, were not 
incidental. Benefits to Good News were significant. Good News 
had ready access to a group of very young, public school children 
for purposes of proselytizing. The evidence established that the 
State's compulsory attendance laws were utilized to support 
Good News and its religious activities. This conclusion was 
supported by the fact that Good News’ attendance had increased 
from 8 to 28 by moving from its church to the Milford public 
school. Milford contends that this was not incidental assistance 
to Good News and its religious pursuits. Milford urges that this 
was the very type of unconstitutional assistance which the First 
Amendment Establishment Clause prohibited. 


The Petitioners have also placed heavy reliance upon the 
case of Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University 
of Virginia, 515 U.S. 819 (1995). Rosenberger is likewise 
inapposite. Rosenberger involved a university setting. 
There were mature young adults in a college setting who 
were enmeshed in a vibrant marketplace of ideas. Students 
in Rosenberger established a student newspaper called 
“Wide Awake” which received funding from the University as 
did numerous other student activities and student run 
newspapers. The University’s objection to this particular 
newspaper was found in the fact that it was published from a 


41 


religious and Christian perspective. The Court found that the 
refusal to fund “Wide Awake” amounted to unconstitutional 
viewpoint discrimination. . 

Rosenberger is markedly different from the present case. 
First, Rosenberger involved a college setting versus Milford’s 
elementary school setting. There is a world of difference between 
the maturity and intellectual development of college students 
versus students, ages 5 to 12. 

In Milford, there was no similar activity permitted or 
conducted in the school district which was similar to or which 
paralleled the religious instruction and religious indoctrination 
offered by the Petitioners. Milford established that there was 
no record evidence that substantiated a comparison between 
the activities described in the record of the Boy Scouts, Cub 
Scouts, Girl Scouts, 4-H Clubs or Little League which could be 
said to compare to that of the Petitioners. Further, there was no 
evidence that the school permitted any other religious, atheistic 
or similar presentation to occur in Milford. Rosenberger dealt 
with the issue of viewpoint discrimination while the present 
case deals with an Establishment Clause issue. The two are 
substantively distinct. Rosenberger is, therefore, inapposite. 


F. Milford Was Not Hostile to Religion When It 
Excluded Petitioners’ Request for Use of the 
Building. 

When Milford rejected the Petitioners’ application for use 
of its building, it was applying established law. As set forth in 
detail above, it would be impermissible to allow Good News to 
conduct its religious instruction, religious indoctrination and 
religious worship on the premises of Milford. The group of 
students involved were the youngest and more vulnerable in 
the school district. To permit this religious group into the school 
to conduct its religious exercise would be potentially divisive 
within the small community of Milford, New York. Maintaining 
government neutrality toward religion is not hostility toward 
religion. The enmeshing of government with religion harms both 
government and religion. 


42 


The answer to the argument by Petitioners that the 
Respondent was hostile to religion is found in the following 
passage from McCollum, supra, by Mr. Justice Black: 

To hold that a state cannot consistently with the First 
and Fourteenth Amendments utilize its public school 
system to aid any or all religious faiths or sects in 
the dissemination of their doctrines and ideals does 
not, as counsel urge, manifest a governmental 
hostility to religion or religious teachings. 
A manifestation of such hostility would be at war

CONCLUSION 


In the fall of 1996, Rev. Stephen Fournier and his wife, 
Darlene, requested the use of the Milford Central School District 
for meetings of its club known as the “Evangelism Good News 
Club.” The Good News Club intended to meet on Tuesdays, 
immediately after school. The School Superintendent conducted 
an appropriate inquiry into what the Good News Club proposed 
to do inside the school building on Tuesday afternoons. After 
reviewing the materials submitted by Good News and examining 
the content of its application for building use, the Superintendent 
concluded that its services were the equivalent of religious 
instruction and religious worship. Milford determined that it 
was obligated, under law, to deny access to Good News for the 
program of religious instruction and religious worship, it offered. 


43 


The Respondent had not engaged in impermissible 
viewpoint discrimination. Milford has protected its youngest 
children from adult-led, adult-sponsored, sectarian proselytizing 
by limitiag its forum as it is required by law to do. 

The District Court, upon the submission of a complete 
record, concluded that Good News was engaged in religious 
worship and religious instruction. The United States Court of 
Appeals for the Second Circuit affirmed that finding and further 
concluded that the activities of Good News were 
“quintessentially religious.” In order to preserve the wall of 
separation between church and state, Respondent urges this 
Court to affirm the judgment of the court below.
