## Material comparator correction

The supplied comparator's claim that the discriminatory Social Security application was rejected **6–1** is incorrect. All seven historical participants agreed that the application was unconstitutional: Breyer, Rehnquist, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg through the Court opinion; Scalia expressly through his judgment concurrence; and Thomas through his position affirming the Federal Circuit's judgment in its entirety. The historical Social Security judgment is therefore **7–0**, with materially different grounds. There is no historical Justice voting to uphold the Social Security application.

The historical Medicare judgment is correctly described as **5–2**: Breyer, Rehnquist, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg upheld the tax; Scalia and Thomas would have held its application unconstitutional.

## Substantive nuances to preserve in later reconciliation

1. The Court overrules *Evans v. Gore* only insofar as it bars generally applicable nondiscriminatory taxation of judicial salaries; it retains the protection against direct salary reductions and discriminatory indirect reductions. Scalia also supports overruling *Evans*, but on the sovereign-versus-employer distinction. Thomas expressly rejects overruling it.
2. The Social Security conclusion rests on the statute's combined features: federal employees as the relevant comparison class; practical ability of nearly all current employees other than judges to avoid an added payroll burden; substantial costs with little or no substantial benefit for most judges; and the inadequacy of equalizing away an advantage tied to constitutional judicial protection. These are the Court's explanation of this statute, not an automatically enacted four-prong test.
3. The Court does not find hostile congressional intent. It accepts benign motives and holds that proof of intimidation, influence, or punishment is unnecessary to the constitutional conclusion. The President's similar treatment does not cure the discrimination identified against judges.
4. Scalia's footnote 2 specifies that the case does not present elimination of a tax exemption in exchange for a benefit different in kind but equivalent in value. His separate reasoning must not be expanded to decide that unpresented exchange.
5. The salary-cure analysis relies on the absence of evidence that the increases sought to make these judges whole and their relationship to inflation. The Court distinguishes *United States v. Will*: the limited damages period there was not contested or decided as the question presented here.
6. The opinion's introductory sentence at page 561 refers to Social Security rules enacted in 1984, while its detailed statutory history identifies the 1983 enactments effective January 1, 1984. The detailed history and disposition distinguish enactment and effective dates; later work should not reproduce the introductory shorthand as the enactment date.
7. The earlier Supreme Court no-quorum action is historical Supreme Court procedure. Its existence in this source does not admit it into the alternate timeline. Its relevance and any substitute current procedural premise await the fresh neutral packet.
