Petitioners respectfully reply to the Respondents’ Brief in 
Opposition (“Oppos.”) to the School Board’s petition for a writ 
of certiorari. In an exaggerated manner, the Respondents distort 
the factual record in this case seeking to distract this Court from 
the plain language of the disclaimer and the myriad of reasons 
supporting this Court’s review of this case. 

Respondents begin with a misleading and inaccurate 
reading of the disclaimer itself. Contrary to Respondents’ 
suggestion, the disclaimer does not “instruct students not to 
believe their science teachers and the state-mandated 
curriculum,” nor does it instruct students “to adhere to religious 
interpretations of life’s origins.” Oppos. at 1. Neither does the 
disclaimer encourage “students to adhere to the Biblical version 
of creation,” id. at i, or “impose upon schoolchildren a 
religiously-based and doctrinal interpretation of life’s origins.” 
Id. at 1. Instead, the disclaimer advises students that evolution 
should be taught, and encourages critical thinking on the 
complicated issue of life’s origin. In the text of the disclaimer 
itself, the Board expressly acknowledges each student’s right 
to form opinions or maintain beliefs about life’s origin, 
notwithstanding the intention of the school system to teach only 
evolution. This is not religious indoctrination, as the 
Respondents suggest; the disclaimer is instead the School 
Board’s effort to render the classroom a more comfortable 
environment for all students to learn, including those students 
whose views about life’s origin may differ from the lesson 
presented to them. In an era when “political correctness” is being 
chalienged as an inauthentic substitute for genuine pluralism 
in our country, a school board may surely be allowed to avoid 
the imposition of official orthodoxy by encouraging students 
to think critically about controversial ideas and to form their 
own conclusions about them. That is what the disclaimer at 
issue in this case does.

I. RESPONDENTS MISCHARACTERIZE THE FIFTH 
CIRCUIT’S LEGAL DECISION AS FACT-BASED TO 
‘DISTRACT THIS COURT FROM THE PLAIN 

LANGUAGE OF THE DISCLAIMER. 

The issue presented in this case is whether the disclaimer, 
which has never been read, endorses religion or has the primary 
effect of advancing religion. None of the alleged “facts” recited 
by Respondents is relevant to this issue. The only facts that are 
relevant are the words of the disclaimer itself. In particular, as 
was recognized by Judge Barksdale and the other dissenters 
from the Fifth Circuit’s en banc denial, the apparently 
determinative “fact” for the panel was the disclaimer’s mention 
of the Bible. Petitioners have demonstrated in their Petition that 
this illustrative reference to the Bible cannot render 
unconstitutional a disclaimer the Fifth Circuit observed would 
otherwise be constitutional. Petition at 15-16. 


This case should turn on the wording of the disclaimer, not 
the motives that may have impelled some of the members of 
the School Board to vote in favor of it. Thus, this Court should 
not be distracted by Respondents’ detour into the irrelevant. In 
a passing reference, Respondents note their belief that the Fifth 
Circuit erred in holding that the disclaimer satisfied the 
“purpose” prong of the Lemon test, Oppos. at 10 n.7, but they 
did not preserve this issue for further review since they failed 
to file timely a cross-petition for a writ of certiorari. Accordingly, 
the constitutionality of the disclaimer’s purposes is now a part 
of the law of the case, and Petitioners’ belated effort to insinuate 
the issue is both untimely and irrelevant.

II. THIS COURT SHOULD GRANT THE WRIT. 
A. This Case Is Of Exceptional Importance. 


Anyone familiar with the American experience of teaching 
evolution in public schools, from Scopes to Aguillard, fully 
appreciates the exceptional nature of this case. This controversy 
shows no signs of waning, certainly so long as the concerns of 
certain parents and schoolchildren are perceived as being 
frustrated or left unaddressed. While several states and local 
school authorities use or have attempted to use disclaimers to 
address the controversy,'® other jurisdictions have employed

B. To Avoid Repetition, The Fifth Circuit’s Erroneous 
Application of the Endorsement Test and Lemon’s 
“Effects” Prong Should Be Corrected. 

That the Fifth Circuit erred in its application of the endorsement 
test and the “effects” prong to the disclaimer at issue is demonstrated 
in the Petition. Petitioners note only that this Court should 
grant review to correct this error now, so that other courts will 
not resort to the “effects” prong as a means of striking down 
other governmental acts that have never been implemented, 
are imbued with permissible, secular purposes, and are unlikely 
to have any effect of advancing or endorsing religion. 

C. The Decision Below Stands In Conflict With 

Interpretations Of The “Effects” Prong By This 
Court, Other Circuits, And The Fifth Circuit Itself. 


Petitioners have reviewed the manner in which the Fifth 
Circuit’s application of the “effects” prong in this case conflicts 
with decisions of this Court, other Circuits, and the Fifth Circuit 
itself. Petition at 22-24. Petitioners observe here only that this 
case is easily distinguished from the cases cited by Respondents 
in their Opposition, which cases involved a ban on the teaching 
of evolution, see Epperson v. Arkansas, 393 U.S. 97, 89 S. Ct. 
266 (1968), balancing the teaching of evolution with the teaching 
of creation science, see Aguillard, a teacher’s refusal to teach 
evolution on grounds of his personal, religious beliefs, 
see Peloza v. Capistrano Unified School Dist, 37 F.3d 517 
(9th Cir. 1994), cert. denied, 515 U.S. 1173 (1995), and a 
teacher’s insistence on teaching creationism. See Helland v. 
South Bend Community School Corp., 93 F.3d 327 (7th Cir. 
1996), cert. denied, 519 U.S. 1092 (1997); Webster v. New Lenox 
School Dist, 917 F.2d 1004 (7th Cir. 1990). In contrast to these 
cases, the disclaimer at issue recites the School Board’s 
conviction that evolution should be taught, and it does not require 
or condone the teaching of creationism. The disclaimer merely 
acknowledges that students may adhere to “other concepts” 
about life’s origin without sacrificing their standing in the school 
community. 


10 


Respondents mischaracterize this case as the latest in a line 
of anti-evolution cases, merely because it deals with the issue 
of teaching evolution in public schools. Thus, Respondents paint 
the religious materials inquiry as a conspiracy to introduce 
creation science into the public school curriculum, and the 
disclaimer as a step toward this end. But the disclaimer contains 
no reference whatsoever to creation science, and the religious 
materials inquiry simply involved the School Board’s efforts to 
clarify legitimate issues that had also caught the attention of 
the highest levels of the federal government. See supra at 3-6. 


Aguillard does not stand for the proposition that evolution 
must be taught as the official orthodoxy of the state on the issue 
of life’s origin. Instead, Aguillard holds that religion may not 
be advanced by banning evolution or, alternatively, requiring 
the teaching of a religious theory. Te disclaimer does neither. 
Thus, the disclaimer is not “anti-evolution” at all, but reinforces 
the teaching of evolution in the public school curriculum, while 
clarifying that the government has no business imposing an 
official orthodoxy upon those who either decline to accept 
evolution, or who wish to harmonize it with “other concepts” 
of life’s origin. Evolution is not discredited, but, in teaching it, 
religious or other beliefs are not intended to be disparaged; 
religious alternatives are not proposed, but the school system 
does disclaim its intent to impose a single, orthodox view of 
life’s origin on its students. The Establishment Clause is not 
and should not be made an impediment to a school board’s 


attempt to make all schoolchildren feel welcome in the school 
community. 


CONCLUSION 


The petition for a writ of certiorari should be granted.
