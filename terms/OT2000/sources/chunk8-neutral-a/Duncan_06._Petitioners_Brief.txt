QUESTION PRESENTED 


Whether a prior federal habeas corpus petition is an 
“application for State post-conviction or other collateral 
review” within the meaning of 28 U.S.C. § 2244(d)(2), which 
provides that the one-year statute of limitations for federal 
habeas corpus petitions set forth in the Antiterrorism and 
Effective Death Penalty Act (“AEDPA”) is tolled during the 
pendency of such an application.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


A. Introduction 


The Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996 
(“AEDPA”), Pub. L. No. 104-132 § 101, 110 Stat. 1214, 1217 
established for the first time a one-year statute of limitations 
applicable to any application for a federal writ of habeas corpus. 
In most cases, the period runs from the “conclusion of direct 
review” of the state court judgment confining the petitioner to 
state custody. 28 U.S.C. § 2244(d)(1)(A) (Supp. 2000). 


The one-year limitations period is tolled, however, during 
the pendency of “a properly filed application for State post- 
conviction or other collateral review.” Jd § 2244(d)(2). With 
respect to convictions that became final prior to AEDPA’s 
effective date of April 24, 1996, all of the circuits to address 
the issue, including the Second Circuit, established a one-year 
grace period, allowing prisoners to file new federal habeas 
petitions seeking review of pre-AEDPA convictions up to one 
year after the effective date of the statute. See Ross y. Artuz, 
150 F.3d 97, 100-02 (2d Cir. 1998) (citing cases). The tolling 
provision contained in section 2244(d)(2) applies to this one- 
year grace period as well. See, e.g., Bennett v. Artuz, 199 F.3d 
116, 119 (2d Cir. 1999) (holding that “AEDPA’s pending-state- 
petition tolling provision does apply to a petition challenging a 
pre-AEDPA conviction”), cert. granted and aff'd on other 
grounds, 121 S. Ct. 361 (2000) (interpreting “properly filed” 
language in tolling provision). 


The issue in this case arises because Walker filed a federal 
habeas petition shortly after his State conviction became final. 
His petition was dismissed without prejudice three months later 
by the district court (during which time AEDPA became law) 


4 


for failure to specify which claims had been exhausted in 
the state courts in compliance with the requirements of 
28 U.S.C. § 2254(b). Rather than filing a renewed federal 
habeas petition after correcting the pleading defects, or else 
initiating collateral review proceedings in the State courts 
to exhaust all claims asserted, Walker waited almost eleven 
months. He then filed a new federal habeas petition, the 
subject of this case, but by this time more than one year had 
passed since AEDPA’s effective date. The district court thus 
dismissed Walker’s new petition as time-barred under 
28 U.S.C. § 2244(d)(1). 


Departing from all of the other United States Courts of 
Appeals to have considered the issue, the Second Circuit 
reversed the dismissal. It held that Walker’s earlier petition 
in the federal court qualified as an application for “State post- 
conviction or other collateral review” within the meaning of 
the tolling provision applicable to AEDPA’s statute of 
limitations, 28 U.S.C. § 2244(d)(2). Accordingly, the Second 
Circuit concluded that Walker’s earlier petition tolled the 
one-year grace period for the duration of its pendency (almost 
three months) in the federal court, rendering Walker’s new 
federal petition timely. The question presented in this case 
is whether the Second Circuit erred in interpreting the tolling 
provision of section 2244(d)(2) to encompass federal 
petitions for habeas review.

Respondent Sherman Walker was convicted and

a jury trial in the New York Supreme Court, New York

Thereafter, on F ebruary 27, 1992, Walker was convicted

Walker then pleaded guilty in the New York Supreme 
Court, Queens County, to a charge of robbery in the first 
degree, arising out of a September 1990 holdup at a jewelry

unrelated charges at the time he was identified by a

(App. 41a) 


The Legal Aid Society was appointed to represent

C. Other State Court Proceedings 


On April 22, 1992, even before his third conviction 
(in Queens County), Walker filed a pro se petition for State 
habeas corpus relief in the New York State Supreme Court, 
Appellate Division. (Walker was already in State custody 
for prior unrelated convictions.) Among his included claims, 
he alleged that he had been denied counsel that he had 
retained in other matters when he appeared at the lineup 
leading to his third conviction, and that the court which 
ordered his appearance in that lineup had lacked personal 
jurisdiction over him. (App. 259a-282a) The Appellate 
Division denied the petition on May 7, 1992. (App. 257a- 
258a) 


Following his convictions, Walker initiated repeated 
challenges in the State courts to the validity of his two Queens 
County convictions. He twice moved in the trial court 
(on December 21, 1992 and on May 27, 1993) to vacate both 
convictions, and the motions were denied by the trial court. 
(App. 220a-221a, 222a-223a) In August 1993, he moved 
again in the trial court to reargue the motion to vacate the 
convictions, in part on grounds of newly discovered evidence 
in the form of an affidavit from the person identified as the 
confidential informant. (App. 196a-219a) That motion, to 
which no response by the State was filed, was denied in an 
oral decision from the bench. (App. 171a) 


8 


Walker thereafter filed in the Appellate Division a 
proceeding for a writ of mandamus against the trial court 
judge, seeking an evidentiary hearing or new trial on the 
basis of the claims in his motion to reargue. (App. 176a- 
189a) The Appellate Division denied the requested relief on 
January 17, 1995. (App. 174a-175a) Walker initiated a 
similar mandamus request in the New York State Court of 
Appeals (App. 168a-173a), which that court denied in June 
1995. (App. 167a) 


Finally, on November 24, 1995, Walker filed an 
application for a writ of error coram nobis in the New York 
State Appellate Division, Second Department, with respect 
to his two Queens County convictions on grounds of 
ineffective assistance of counsel. (App. 150a-160a) Walker 
claimed that: (1) appellate counsel had improperly failed 
to investigate that the police information from the 
person identified as the confidential informant was false, and 


(2) he was deprived of adequate appellate counsel of choice . 


because the Legal Aid Society had thus improperly 
withdrawn based on its prior representation of the informant. 
The Appellate Division denied the petition on March 18, 
1996, finding no ineffective assistance of appellate counsel. 
See People v. Walker, 639 N.Y.S.2d 932 (N.Y. App. Div. 
1996). (App. 148a-149a)

Three weeks later, on April 10, 1996, Walker filed a 
single document, constituting a complaint pursuant to 
42 U.S.C. § 1983 against the Legal Aid Society and 
corrections officials, as well as a petition for habeas corpus 
relief pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 2254, in the United States

aa.

(App. 79a-90a) Walker claimed that his detention for each

issues have been properly exhausted in state court, the

Following the dismissal of his habeas corpus petition, 
Walker took no further action with regard to his convictions 
for nearly eleven months. On April 24, 1997, the one-year 
grace period for filing a federal habeas corpus petition 
applicable to pre-AEDPA convictions expired, without 
Walker having taken any further steps to preserve his rights.

year grace period (and nearly eleven months after the federal

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Under the guise of giving full meaning to the phrase 
“State post-conviction or other collateral review” within the 
tolling provision, the Second Circuit erroneously equated 
“other collateral review” with federal habeas review. The 
Second Circuit’s reading of section 2244(d)(2) is contrary 
to the plain text of the provision, applicable canons of 
statutory interpretation, and the principles of comity and 
federalism underlying this Court’s habeas jurisprudence and 
AEDPA. 


First, the Second Circuit’s interpretation would require 
rewriting the statutory text. Contrary to basic rules of 
grammar, the Second Circuit disjoined the unitary phrase 


14 


“State post-conviction or other collateral review” into distinct 
parts, thus effectively inserting the only noun, “review”, at 
a second, additional point in the phrase. In so doing, the 
Second Circuit also rendered the word “State” superfluous. 
Had Congress intended to toll the statute of limitations for 
the filing of federal habeas petitions, it simply could have 
omitted “State” altogether. 


The Second Circuit based its interpretation on the 
erroneous assumption that no state “other collateral review” 
exists other than state “post-conviction” review. In fact, state 
courts make available a range of state collateral proceedings 
that are not “post-conviction” challenges, including 
challenges to confinement in a state mental facility or to 
pretrial detention. It is to state proceedings like these that 
the phrase “other collateral review” refers. 


Second, the Second Circuit’s interpretation is 
inconsistent with longstanding principles of statutory 
construction. Section 2263(b)(2), the parallel tolling 
provision for habeas petitions in capital cases, expressly 
limits its terms to state proceedings. It defies logic that 
Congress would grant greater tolling, and thus more 
expansive opportunities for federal habeas review, in non- 
capital cases than in capital cases. The Second Circuit further 
ignored the fact that when Congress sought to bring both 
federal as well as state proceedings within AEDPA’s scope, 
it did so expressly. See, e.g., 28 U.S.C. §§ 2263(b)(2), 
2254(i), 2261(e), and 2264(a)(3). Indeed, the loose statutory 
interpretation that led the Second Circuit to construe the word 
“other” as referring to “federa!” is particularly inappropriate, 
given that references to sovereign entities should be strictly 
construed and implied references to sovereign entities should 
be avoided. 


15 


Finally, the Second Circuit’s construction of section 
2244(d)(2) undermines the goal of AEDPA’s statute of 
limitations and the total exhaustion requirement established 
in Rose v. Lundy, 455 U.S. 515 ( 1982). A petitioner could 
circumvent the statute of limitations by deliberately or 
inadvertently filing an unexhausted federal habeas petition 
because, under the Second Circuit’s interpretation, the one- 
year limitations clock of section 2244(d)(1) would freeze as 
soon as the petition were filed. This result would prolong 
indefinitely the review process and unnecessarily burden 
federal courts with piecemeal litigation — an outcome wholly 
inconsistent with this Court’s habeas jurisprudence and 
AEDPA’s objectives of lending finality to state court 
judgments, fostering exhaustion of state court procedures, 
and enhancing the efficiency of federal habeas review. 


The judgment of the Second Circuit should be reversed.

II. Traditional Principles of Statutory Construction 
Confirm That Section 2244(d)(2) Does Not Encompass 
Pending Federal Habeas Petitions 


That the plain language of Section 2244(d)(2) does not 
encompass federal habeas applications is confirmed by 
traditional principles of statutory construction: first, that similar 
provisions in the statute should be examined to shed light upon 
the meaning of the provision at issue; second, that the breadth 
of the language at issue should be compared to other more 
specific references in the statute to determine whether it would 
be consistent with the statute as a whole to infer specific meaning 
from a broad phrase; and third, that statutory references to 
sovereign entities should be specified by Congress and should 
not be lightly inferred from broad textual language, especially 
in the context of habeas corpus, where delineation of the 
respective roles of the federal and state sovereigns is paramount.

isolation” is “untenable in light of” statute “as a whole”’). 


Such is the case here. By stating clearly that the tolling of 
the limitations period in capital cases is to apply only to pending 
state court proceedings, Congress left no doubt as to the proper 
interpretation of the tolling provision applicable in non-capital 
cases as well. It defies logic to believe that Congress intended 
to allow for unexhausted federal habeas petitions to toll the 
limitations period in all cases except capital cases, where the 
consequences of constitutional error are of the highest potential 
order of gravity. 


B. Where Congress Intended In AEDPA To Reference 
Both State And Federal Collateral Proceedings, It 
Did So Expressly And Explicitly 


An examination of other provisions of the federal habeas 
statute, as amended by AEDPA, confirms that section 
2244(d)(2)’s use of the words “State post-conviction or other 
collateral review” does not contemplaie that the filing of federal 
habeas petitions will toll the one-year period of limitations. 
See Gutierrez v. Ada, 120 S. Ct. 740, 744 (2000) (“Words 
and people are known by their companions.”). Where Congress 
meant in AEDPA and the habeas statute to refer to both state 
and federal proceedings, it did so explicitly. 


28 


For example, AEDPA provided that “[t]he ineffectiveness 
or incompetence of counsel during Federal or State collateral 
post-conviction proceedings shall not be a ground for relief in 
a proceeding arising under section 2254.” 28 U.S.C. § 2254(i) 
(emphasis added). The parallel provision for habeas procedure 
in capital cases states not only that “[t]he ineffectiveness or 
incompetence of counsel during State or Federal post- 
conviction proceedings in a capital case shall not be a ground 
for relief,” but also that “[t]his limitation shall not preciude the 
appointment of different counsel .. . at any phase cf State or 
Federal post-conviction proceedings.” Id. § 2261(e) (emphases 
added). Likewise, AEDPA provided that, in habeas petitions in 
capital cases, a district court may consider a claim not raised 
and decided on the merits in the state courts if, inter alia, the 
failure to raise the claim properly is “based on a factual predicate 
that could not have been discovered through the exercise of 
due diligence in time to present the claim for State or Federal 
post-conviction review.” Id. § 2264(a)(3) (emphasis added). 


Provisions such as sections 2254(i), 2261(e), and 
2264(a)(3) underscore that when Congress intended in 
AEDPA to refer to both state and federal post-conviction or 
collateral review, “it knew how to do so.” Custis v. United 
States, 511 U.S. 485, 492 (1994); see also Martin v. Hadix, 
527 U.S. 343, 354 (1999) (rejecting construction of statute 
where Congress “could have used language more obviously 
targeted” to alleged statutory purpose). “It simply isn’t 
plausible” that Congress, which was so clear in the other 
sections of AEDPA, “would be so delphic” in section 
2244(d)(2). Lindh v. Murphy, 521 U.S. 320, 331-32 (1997); 
see also Bank of America Nat'l Trust & Sav. Ass'n v. 203 N. 
LaSalle St. Partnership, 526 U.S. 434, 450 (1999) (“It is 
unlikely that the drafters of legislation so long and minutely 
complicated ... would have used two distinctly different 


29 


forms of words for the same purpose.”); Russello v. United 
States, 464 U.S. 16, 23 ( 1983) (“Where Congress includes 
particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in 
another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed 
that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the 
disparate inclusion or exclusion.”) (internal quotation marks 
omitted); id. (refraining from concluding that “differing 
language” in two subsections of statute “has the same 
meaning in each” and refusing to ascribe “this difference to 
a simple mistake in draftsmanship”).

III. Tolling The Statute Of Limitations For Pending 
Federal Habeas Petitions Would Be Contrary To The 
Values And Purposes Underlying AEDPA 


As the Second Circuit acknowledged, AEDPA’s 
establishment of a statute of limitations applicable to federal 
habeas petitions “was a dramatic change in the law and was 
Congress’ primary vehicle for streamlining the habeas 
review process and lending finality to state convictions.” 
Walker, 208 F.3d at 361. (Pet. App. 8a) In the ten years prior to 
AEDPA’s enactment, more than 80 bills proposing a statute of 
limitations for habeas corpus petitions had been introduced in 
Congress. See Lonchar v. Thomas, 517 U.S. 314, 333 (1996) 
(Appendix listing the bills). When Congress finally reformed 
the habeas corpus laws in 1996, the new one-year limitations 
period for filing habeas petitions was a key element of the reform 
effort to address “acute problems of unnecessary delay” in 
habeas review, see H.R. Conf. Rep. 104-518, at 111 (1996), 


reprinted in 1996 U.S.C.C.A.N. 924, 944, so as to promote 


finality to and respect for state court judgments. 


The Second Circuit justified its reading of the tolling “~ 


provision on the basis that the congressional goal in enacting 
the limitations period was “to spur defendants to file their 
federal habeas petitions more quickly,” that is, to give 
defendants “an incentive to file their federal petitions right 
away because this filing will toll the one-year statute of 
limitations.” Walker, 208 F.3d at 361. (Pet. App. 8a) This 
view of congressional goals fails to come to terms with the 
fundamental values and purposes underlying AEDPA and 
its statute of limitations. 


The statute of limitations enacted in AEDPA was written 
to balance twin goals of comity in tension, i.e., to balance 


33 


the goal of reducing delays to the finality of state convictions, 
with the goal of ensuring full exhaustion of state court 
remedies. See H.R. Rep. No. 104-23, at 9 (1995) (explaining 
the statute of limitations provisions are designed to “curb 
the lengthy delays in filing that now often occur in federal 
habeas corpus litigation, while preserving the availability 
of review when a prisoner diligently pursues state remedies 
and applies for federal habeas review in a timely manner”) 
(Judiciary Committee report accompanying H.R. 729, an 
immediate precursor of AEDPA in the same Congress, which 
contained the identical statute of limitations enacted under 
AEDPA). 


AEDPA did so by enacting a statute of limitations which 
allows for exhaustion of state remedies in two ways: 
by commencing the limitations period, at the earliest, 
after the direct appellate review process is over, 
see 28 U.S.C. § 2244(d)(1)(A), and by enacting a tolling 
provision for properly filed applications for State post- 
conviction or other collateral review of the judgment or 
claim, see id. § 2244(d)(2). Tolling the statute of limitations 
for state court collateral proceedings furthers comity, because 
it allows and encourages full exhaustion of state court 
remedies, thereby affording state courts the first opportunity 
to consider prisoners’ federal claims as the case advances 
toward finality. 


By interpreting the tolling provision as encompassing 
federal habeas petitions, however, the Second Circuit 
undermined AEDPA’s purpose of promoting the finality of 
state court judgments without furthering any competing 
purpose of comity. See Jiminez v. Rice, 222 F.3d 1210, 1214 
(9th Cir. 2000) (“Applying § 2244(d)(2) to a federal petition 
does nothing to further Congress’s intent to promote 
exhaustion of state relief.”). 


34 


If, as the Second Circuit held, tolling were permitted 
for federal habeas petitions, then habeas petitioners would 
be encouraged to make federal court their first stop. But in 
Rose v. Lundy, 455 U.S. 509 (1982), this Court construed 
pre-AEDPA sections 2254(b) & (c) to require a district 
court to dismiss federal habeas petitions containing both 
exhausted and unexhausted claims (i.e., “mixed” petitions). 
See Rose, 455 U.S. at 522; see also Coleman v. Thompson, 
501 U.S. 722, 731 (1991) (“[A] state prisoner’s federal 
habeas petition should be dismissed if the prisoner has not 
exhausted available state remedies as to any of his federal 
claims.”) (emphasis added). This rule requiring exhaustion 
of all claims in a federal peiition was adopted with several 
purposes. One was to “encourage state prisoners to seek full 
relief first from the state courts, thus giving those courts the 
first opportunity to review all claims of constitutional error.” 
Rose, 455 U.S. at 518-519. Another purpose was “to 
encourage habeas . . . petitioners to present the federal court 
with a single petition.” Jd. at 520. The Court in Rose reasoned 
that its total exhaustion rule would benefit “both the courts 
and the prisoners” to the extent it “reduces piecemeal litigation” 
and “provide[s] for a more focused and thorough review.” Jd. 


Under the Second Circuit’s interpretation, there would be 
no practical incentive, despite Rose v. Lundy, for a petitioner to 
evaluate his or her claims for exhaustion prior to filing in federal 
court, because the one-year limitations clock would freeze as 
soon as the federal petition was filed, fully exhausted or not. 
Moreover, if the petitioner wished to buy additional time prior 
to filing in state court, he or she simply could file a series of 
unexhausted petitions in federal court, tolling the statute 
indefinitely. 


35 


The Second Circuit’s reading of the tolling provision 
as encouraging early filings of federal habeas petitions, 
see Walker, 208 F.3d at 360-61 (Pet. App. 8a), thus creates an 
incentive out of step with this Court’s habeas jurisprudence. 
If, as the Second Circuit suggested, section 2244(d)(2) were 
intended to “reward” a petitioner who files any federal habeas 
petition by tolling the one-year period of limitation, id., then a 
petitioner easily could “circumvent the limitations period 
imposed by the AEDPA,” Warren vy. Garvin, 219 F.3d 111, 
114 (2d Cir.), cert. denied, 121 S. Ct. 404 (2000), by filing 
unexhausted claims in federal habeas petitions as a means of 
prolonging the review process. This clearly would undermine 
the very purpose of the total exhaustion “rule,” i.e., to reduce 
“piecemeal litigation” by presenting all of their claims to a 
federal court in “a single habeas petition.” Rose, 455 U.S. at 
520; see also Harris v. Reed, 489 U.S. 255, 269 (1989) 
(O’Connor, J., concurring) (“[TJhe [exhaustion] rule furthers 
the interest in the efficiency of federal habeas corpus, by assuring 
that, in general, the factual and legal bases surrounding a 
petitioner’s constitution claim or claims will have been 
developed in a prior adjudication.”) 


Such an interpretation, by encouraging early, ill- 
considered, or repeat federal habeas filings, moreover, would 
place a substantial burden on federal courts. “Federal 
collateral litigation places a heavy burden on scarce federal 
judicial resources, and threatens the capacity of the system 
to resolve primary disputes.” McCleskey v. Zant, 499 U.S. 
467, 491 (1991). Indeed, in the year ending September 30, 
1999, prisoners filed 23,821 habeas corpus petitions 
(not involving the death penalty) in the federal courts, 
comprising almost 10% of the total number of civil cases 
filed. See 1999 Director’s Ann. Rep., Jud. Bus. of the U.S. 
138, 140. 


36 


The broader ramifications of the burden that even readily 
dismissed petitions place on the federal courts and on petitioners 
with meritorious claims has long been recognized. In 1953, after 
541 habeas petitions challenging state convictions had been filed 
in 1952, Justice Jackson criticized this Court’s habeas decisions 
as enabling 


floods of stale, frivolous and repetitious petitions 
[to] inundate the docket of the lower courts and swell 
our own. Judging by our own disposition of habeas 
corpus matters, they have as a class become 
peculiarly undeserving. It must prejudice the 
occasional meritorious application to be buried in a 
flood of worthless ones. 


Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 536-37 & n.8 (1953) (Jackson, 
J., concurring in the result) (footnotes omitted); see also Keeney 
v. Tamayo-Reyes, 504 U.S. 1, 7 (1992) (“Federal habeas 
litigation also places a heavy burden on scarce judicial resources, 
may give litigants incentives to withhold claims for manipulative 
purposes, and may create disincentives to present claims when 
evidence is fresh.”); Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 
260 (1973) (Powell, J., concurring) (same). 


By encouraging the early and repeat filing of 
federal habeas petitions, therefore, the Second Circuit’s 
interpretation would encourage delay and undermine the finality 
of state court convictions, without advancing any principles of 
exhaustion and comity, while also burdenizg scarce federal 
judicial resources and federal petitioners with meritorious 
claims. This is contrary to the goals and purposes of AEDPA. 
The Second Circuit’s interpretation of the tolling provision must 
be rejected under the plain language of the statute, principles of 
statutory construction, and the values and purposes underlying 
AEDPA.
