STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


A. Introduction 


Before 1996, there was no statute of limitations for 
federal habeas corpus proceedings. The only provision 
dealing with the effect of the passage of time between a state 
conviction and the filing of a federal habeas corpus petition 
was Rule 9(a) of the Rules Governing Section 2254 Cases, 
which permits dismissal of a petition if a State “has been 
prejudiced in its ability to respond to the petition by delay 
in its filing.” 28 U.S.C. § 2254 Rule 9(a) (1994). The 
Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996 
(AEDPA), P.L. 104-132, §§ 101, 106, 110 Stat. 1214, 1217 
(1996), introduced 28 U.S.C. § 2244(d) into the law of habeas 
corpus. That provision establishes a one-year statute of 
limitations for applications for the writ, running from the 
date on which the judgment of conviction became final. With 
respect to convictions that became final prior to AEDPA’s 
effective date of April 24, 1996, all the circuits to address 
the issue, including the Second Circuit, have established a 
one-year grace period after the effective date of the statute 
in which prisoners may apply for the writ. See Ross v. Artuz, 
150 F.3d 97, 100-101 (2d Cir. 1998) (citing cases). 


Section 2244(d)(2) tolls the one-year limitations period 
during the pendency of a “properly filed application for State 
post-conviction or other collateral review.” The circuits have 
agreed that the tolling provision of § 2244(d)(2) applies to 
the one-year grace period as well as to the one-year 


Pr ne 


3 


limitations period. See Bennett v. Artuz, 199 F.3d i16, 119 
(2d Cir. 1999) (citing cases), cert. granted sub nom, Artuz v. 
Bennett, 120 S. Ct. 1669 (2000) (No. 99-1238). 


The questions presented in this petition involve the 
construction of the tolling provision applicable to the one- 
year AEDPA limitations and grace periods: can prior federal 
habeas corpus petitions toll the limitations and grace periods 
during their pendency, and if so, what is the meaning of the 
“properly filed” requirement as applied to such prior federal 
petitions so as to invoke the toll? 


B. Facts and Procedural History 


Respondent Sherman Walker was convicted and 
sentenced for three distinct acts of and indictments for 
robbery. He initially was sentenced on February 13, 1992, 
following his conviction of two counts of robbery after a 
jury trial in the New York Supreme Court, New York County. 
The judgment was affirmed by New York State Supreme 
Court’s Appellate Division, First Department. See People v. 
Walker, 632 N.Y.S.2d 68 (N.Y. App. Div.), leave to appeal 
denied, 641 N.Y.S.2d 238 (N.Y. 1995). Thereafter, on 
February 27, 1992, after a jury trial in the Supreme Court, 
Queens County, Walker was sentenced following another 
conviction for robbery. This judgment similarly was affirmed 
by the Appellate Division, Second Department. See People 
v. Walker, 628 N.Y.S.2d 951 (N.Y. App. Div. 1995), appeal 
dismissed, 641 N.Y.S.2d 608 (N.Y. 1996). Finally, on 
June 16, 1992, pursuant to a plea of guilty in the Supreme 
Court, Queens County, Walker again was convicted of 
robbery and sentenced. This judgment was likewise affirmed 
by the Appellate Division, Second Department. See People 
v. Walker, 628 N.Y.S.2d 950 (N.Y. App. Div. 1995), appeal 
dismissed, 641 N.Y.S.2d 608 (N.Y. 1996).

Walker filed an application for a writ of error coram nobis 
with respect to the latter two convictions in the Appellate 
Division, Second Department, which determined against him 
on March 18, 1996. See People v. Walker, 639 N.Y.S.2d 932 
(N.Y. App. Div. 1996). Thereafter, in a single document dated 
April 10, 1996, Walker filed a complaint pursuant to 
42 U.S.C. § 1983 and a petition for habeas corpus relief 
pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 2254 in the United States District 
Court for the Eastern District of New York. He claimed that 
his detention for each of his three robbery convictions was 
attributable to a violation of his federal constitutional right 
to the effective assistance of counsel. The district court 
dismissed the petition and civil rights complaint without 
prejudice in a Memorandum and Order dated July 9, 1996 
(Appendix C). The court dismissed the habeas corpus petition 
“for [Walker’s] failure to adequately set forth his claim 
* * * * Because this court cannot determine which issues 
have been properly exhausted in State court, the essential 
allegations of a habeas corpus action have not been 
presented.” (App. C, 20a). 


On May 20, 1997, Walker filed a second habeas corpus 
petition in the district court for the Eastern District of 
New York. The petition challenged only the third conviction, 
dated June 16, 1992, claiming that Walker’s right to counsel 
had been denied at the lineup and that the People had failed 
timely to provide a copy of the detective’s affidavit attesting 
to the circumstances warranting petitioner’s inclusion in the 
lineup. (App. C, 20a). 


On August 22, 1997, petitioner herein moved to dismiss 
Walker’s petition as untimely under the one-year limitations 
period fixed by 28 U.S.C. § 2244(d). The basis of the motion 
was that more than one year had elapsed since Walker’s state- 
court conviction had become final on January 5, 1996, when 
the Court of Appeals denied leave to appeal in People v. 
Walker, 641 N.Y.S.2d 608 (N.Y. 1996).

The district court dismissed the second petition as time- 
barred in a Memorandum and Order dated May 6, 1998 
(Appendix B). The court applied the then-applicable rule of 
the Second Circuit, see Peterson v. Demskie, 107 F.3d 92, 
93 (2d Cir. 1997), that petitions challenging convictions 
which became final prior to AEDPA must be filed within a 
“reasonable time” after AEDPA’s effective date of April 24, 
1996. The district court held that the petition was not filed

one year and four months after the completion of [Walker’s] 
direct appeal, over one year after the effective date of AEDPA, 
and almost one year after his first habeas petition was 
dismissed.” (App. B, 15a). 


By order entered December 1, 1998, the Second Circuit 
granted petitioner a certificate of appealability. See 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2253(c) (1996). The parties were ordered to brief the

1. Whether the petitioner-appellant’s first petition 
for writ of habeas corpus pursuant to 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2254 tolled the period of limitations set forth in 
28 U.S.C. § 2244(d)(2), and 2. Whether the 
limitations period in section 2244(d) may be

1. Shortly after the district court’s decision, the Second Circuit 
in Ross v. Artuz, 150 F.3d 97 (2d Cir. 1998) adopted the one-year 
grace period for habeas corpus petitions challenging pre-AEDPA

AEDPA.

The court further ordered that counsel be appointed to 
represent the petitioner-appellant. In briefing the first issue, 
the parties addressed whether federal habeas corpus petitions 
are “applications for State post-conviction or other collateral 
review” as stated by § 2244(d)(2). 


In an opinion dated March 27, 2000, the Second Circuit 
reversed the district court (Appendix A). It concluded that a 
federal habeas corpus petition is an “application for State 
post-conviction or other collateral review” under 
§ 2244(d)(2). Therefore, the court held, Walker’s second 
petition was timely because the one-year grace period was 
tolled during the three-month pendency of his initial federal 
habeas petition. In finding that the tolling provision 
encompasses federal habeas corpus petitions, the court 
reasoned that 


“State” modifies only the word “post-conviction,” 
and the phrase “other collateral” is to be given its 
naturally broader meaning. The disjunctive “or” 
in the statute creates a distinct break between the 
two kinds of review [whereas] applying “State” 
to both of the disjunctive phrases would create a 
linguistic oddity * * * * “State other collateral 
review” is an ungainly construction that we do 
not believe Congress intended. 


208 F.3d at 360. Moreover, the Second Circuit added, 
“ “ ‘State post-conviction review’ means all collateral review 
of a conviction provided by a state and the phrase “other 
collateral review” would be meaningless if it did not refer to 
federal habeas petitions.” Jd. at 360. 


The Second Circuit also concluded that including federal 
habeas petitions within the scope of § 2244(d)(2)’s tolling 
provision would not be inconsistent with the overall objective 
of AEDPA to place finite restrictions on the time for such 


7 


petitions. In the court’s view, tolling the limitation period during 
the pendency of a first federal petition would not create a 
loophole permitting easy evasion of the limitations period, 
because successive petitions would not satisfy the “properly 
filed” proviso of the statute if the first petition had been 
dismissed on the merits or with prejudice. /d. Instead, according 
to the court, including federal habeas corpus petitions within 
‘the scope of § 2244(d)(2) will reward petitioners for filing as 
soon as possible, thus promoting the Statutory objective of 
efficiency in the review process. See id. at 360-361. 


REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


I. There is a conflict among the lower federal courts on 
the significant question whether the AEDPA tolling 
provision encompasses federal habeas corpus petitions. 


This Court should grant certiorari to resolve the division 
among the federal courts over whether the phrase “State post- 
conviction or other collateral review” in AEDPA’s tolling 
provision encompasses federal habeas corpus review. The 
decisions are squarely in conflict on this important issue. In the 
present case, the Second Circuit held that “State” does not 
modify the term “other collateral review” in the language of 
the statutory provision. Thus, the court reasoned, a prior federal 
habeas corpus petition falls within § 2244(d)(2), and serves to 
toll the one-year grace period for convictions preceding 
AEDPA’s effective date (as well, therefore, as the one-year 
limitations period for post-enactment convictions). As the court 
below acknowledged, see 208 F.3d at 359, its decision squarely 
conflicts with the decision of the Third Circuit in Jones y. 
Morton, 195 F.3d 153 (3d Cir. 1999). The Third Circuit in Jones 
concluded that “Congress clearly intended that the word ‘State’ 
would be read to modify both ‘post-conviction’ and ‘other 
collateral,’ so that tolling would be afforded under § 2244(d)(2) 
for various forms of state review only.” Jd. at 159. 


8 


The conflict between the present case and Jones extends 
beyond the results reached by the two courts to the particulars 
of their reasoning. The Second Circuit stated it was reading 
the provision in question “naturally.” 208 F.3d at 359. The 
Third Circuit was persuaded that its own opposite 
interpretation was the “ ‘natural reading of the statute. 
195 F.3d at 158 (quoting Sperling v. White, 30 F. Supp. 2d 
1246, 1250 (C.D. Cal. 1998)). The Second Circuit stated that 
“including federal petitions within the meaning of Section 
2244(d)(2) ... promotfes] the statutory objective of 
efficiency in the review of State criminal judgments.” 
208 F.3d at 360-61. The Third Circuit, by contrast, found 
“nothing in the policy concerns behind AEDPA’s enactment” 
to suggest that the “collateral review” of § 2244(d)(2) 
encompasses federal habeas corpus petitions. Jones, 195 F.3d 
at 159. 


> 99 


Other circuits also have taken a view of § 2244(d)(2) 
that inescapably conflicts with that of the Second Circuit. 
For example, in Rhine v. Boone, 182 F.3d 1153 (10th Cir. 
1999), the Tenth Circuit considered whether the one-year 
grace period under § 2244(d)(2) was tolled during the 
pendency of a petition filed with this Court for a writ of 
certiorari to review a state court’s denial of post-conviction 
relief. The court held it was not tolled, concluding that 
“in the wording of § 2244(d)(2), ‘State’ modifies the phrase 
‘post-conviction review’ and the phrase ‘other collateral 
review, ” and a petition for certiorari “is simply not an 


application for state review of any kind.” /d. at 1156; 
see also Ott v. Johnson, 192 F.3d 510, 513 (Sth Cir. 1999) 
(following Rhine in concluding that petition for writ of 
certiorari in state-court post-conviction proceeding “is not 
an application for ‘State’ review that would toll the 
limitations period’’). 


The conflict among the circuit courts is mirrored by 
conflicting decisions of the federal district courts of 
California, which (like the Second Circuit and Third Circuit 
in Walker and Jones, respectively) have reached opposite 
conclusions while invoking the same principles of statutory 
construction. Compare Barrett v. Yearwood, 63 F. Supp. 2d 
1245, 1250 (E.D. Cal. 1999) (“{t]he plain meaning of 
§ 2244(d)(2) is that the statute of limitations is tolled during 
the pendency of any properly filed federal habeas corpus 
petition”), with Sperling v. White, 30 F. Supp. 2d 1246, 1250 
(C.D. Cal. 1998) (“{a] natural reading of the statute concludes 
that section 2244(d)(2) does not contemplate tolling 
limitations during the time a habeas petition is pending in 
federal court’). 


Nor does the conflict between the present decision and 
those of the Third, Fifth and Tenth Circuits pertain only to 
convictions that became final prior to the effective date of 
AEDPA and to which the judicially-created “grace period” 
applies. As Rhine and Barrett — both of which deal with 
State convictions finalized after April 24, 1996 — make clear, 
tolling pursuant to § 2244(d)(2) applies — indeed, more 
literally — to the one-year statute of limitations for habeas 
corpus petitions with respect to such post-AEDPA 
convictions. Pre-AEDPA convictions receive the benefit of 
the one-year “grace period” pursuant to judicial rule, while 
later convictions are subject to a one-year limitations period 
mandated by statute. Both periods are tolled during the 
pendency of “State post-conviction or other collateral 
review.” The interpretation of § 2244(d)(2) arrived at in one 
context applies equally in the other. 


The importance of the issue presented here is indicated 
by the number of cases potentially affected by its resolution. 
There were 20,251 habeas corpus proceedings commenced 
in the federal courts during the year ending September 30, 
1999. See 1999 Director’s Ann. Rep., Jud. Bus. of the U.S. 


10 


Cts 146. The number was in the same range for the preceding 
years — 20,897 in 1998 and 21,621 in 1997. See id. 
Exhaustion of state remedies is required before any of these 
petitions may be entertained in the federal courts. 
See 28 U.S.C. § 2254(b)(1)(A), (c) (Supp. 2000); O'Sullivan 
v. Boerckel, 526 U.S. 838, 845 (1999). Studies have suggested 
that a significant number of such federal petitions are 
dismissed for failure to exhaust. See Richard H. Fallon, et 
al., Hart and Wechsler’s The Federal Courts and The Federal 
System, 162 (4th ed. Supp. 1999) (citing Allen, Schachman 
& Wilson, Federal Habeas Corpus and its Reform: An 
Empirical Analysis, 13 Rutgers L.J. 675, 695, 703 (1982); 
Shapiro, Federal Habeas Corpus: A Study in Massachusetts, 
87 Harv. L. Rev. 321, 356-61 (1973)). Thus, the issue of the 
tolling effect of an initial federal habeas corpus petition on 
the timeliness of a subsequent petition “may not be 
uncommon.” Kethley v. Berge, 14 F. Supp. 2d 1077, 1079 
(E.D. Wis. 1998). It will arise whenever an inmate files a 
habeas petition after his prior petition containing unexhausted 
claims has languished in the district court before dismissal, 
or when the original unexhausted petition was filed with only 
a short time remaining in the one-year limitations period. 
See 1 James S. Liebman and Randy Hertz, Fed. Hab. Corp. 
Prac. & Procedure § 5.16 at 229-231 (3d ed. 1998) 
(discussing circumstances in which second petition will be 
time-barred). 


Thus, there is an inescapable conflict among the federal 
courts over the interpretation of § 2244(d)(2). Resolution of 
the issue will produce a rule of decision applicable in many 
cases, and perhaps dispositive on the significant matter of a 
state prisoner’s access to federal habeas corpus review. The 
issue is, moreover, binary in its possible resolutions: the 
statute either does or does not encompass federal habeas 
corpus petitions within “other collateral review.” Both 
possible views have now been taken by the circuit courts, 
with no chance that a middle way or a different approach 


1] 


will emerge from further lower-court consideration. Only a 
decision by this Court can produce a consistent statutory 
interpretation on this important and recurrent issue. 


II. The Decision Below Is Legally Erroneous. 


The court below erred in interpreting § 2244(d)(2), 
misapprehending both its literal terms and the provision’s 
connection with other sections of AEDPA. 


The Second Circuit first erred in concluding that its reading 
of the statute is more “natural” than that of the courts with which 
it disagrees. It is true that the disputed phrase of § 2244(d)(2) 
— “State post-conviction or other collateral review” — should 
be given its “most natural reading.” McNeil v. United States, 
508 U.S. 106, 112 (1993). That reading requires that the words 
of the phrase be read in order and together rather than uncoupled 
from each other, as the Second Circuit attempted. In the language 
at issue “[t]here is a unity of verbai structure that is a symptom 
of an inner unity, a unity of plan and function.” George Moore 
Ice Cream Co. v. Rose, 289 U.S. 373, 378 (1933). 


The Second Circuit stated that “[t]he disjunctive ‘or’ in the 
statute creates a ‘distinct break’ between the two kinds of 
review.” 208 F.3d at 359. In fact, the grammatical 
incompleteness of the first half of the phrase renders the break 
less than “distinct.” To the court below, 


applying “State” to both disjunctive phrases would 
create a linguistic oddity because the statute would 
refer to “‘a properly filed application” for “State post- 
conviction * * * review” or “State * * * other 
review.” “State other collateral review” is an 
ungainly construction that we do not believe 
Congress intended. 


Id. at 359-360. It is the Second Circuit’s reading, however, 
that is unnatural, for it disturbs the unity of the phrase’s 
structure. 


12 


If Congress had wanted to create a complete disjunction 
between the two halves of the phrase “State post-conviction 
or other collateral review,” it would have achieved 
grammatical closure by inserting the word “review” after 
“post-conviction” in the first half of the phrase. The word 
“review,” however, appears only once, at the end of the 
phrase. The most “natural” reading of the statute is that 
Congress intended the entire phrase as a single unit, with 
“State” modifying the only noun in the phrase — “review” 
— at the point at which the noun appears. 


As the Second Circuit acknowledged, its own 
interpretation effectively inserts the word “review” before 
“or.” See 208 F.3d at 360. Congress easily could have drafted 
such a statute, but it did not, and its failure to do so means 
that the interpretation that such a statute would have 
demanded must be rejected. See, e.g., West v. Gibson, 527 
U.S. 212, 220 (1999) (rejecting an interpretation that 
Congress “could easily” have embodied in the plain language . 
of a statute). 


That the reading of the statute offered herein is the 
natural reading is suggested by the failure of scholars to see 
even a possible disagreement here. Pertinent treatises state 
unequivocally that section 2244(d)(2) contemplates only 
state-court proceedings. See Larry W. Yackle, Postconviction 
Remedies 346 (ist ed. Supp. 2000); Richard H. Fallon, et 
al., Hart and Wechsler’s The Federal Courts and the Federal 
System 162 (4th ed. Supp. 1999); 1 James S. Liebman & 
Randy Hertz, Federal Habeas Corpus Practice and 
Procedure 229-30 (3d ed. 1998). 


Comparison to AEDPA provisions further demonstrates 
that Congress meant to confine the “review” contemplated 
by § 2244(d)(2) to state-court proceedings. 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2263(b) (Supp. 2000), for example, parallels § 2244(d)(2) 
in tolling the 180-day statute of limitations fixed by 


13 


§ 2263(b)(1) for habeas corpus proceedings in capital cases. 
Section 2263(b)(2) provides for tolling “from the date on 
which the first petition for post-conviction review or other 
collateral relief is filed until the final state court disposition 
of such petition.” By providing for tolling only until the final 
state-court disposition, Congress made clear that only state 
“post-conviction * * * or other collateral” proceedings are 
contemplated by this provision. The identical words 
appearing in 2244(d)(2) should receive the same 
interpretation, for it is a “ ‘normal rule of statutory 
construction that “ ‘identical words used in different parts 
of the same act are intended to have the same meaning. ”’ ” 
Sullivan v. Stroop, 496 U.S. 478, 484 (1990) (citations 
omitted); see also Rhine v. Boone, 182 F.3d at | 156 (limiting 
§ 2244(d)(2) to state-court post-conviction review); Ott v, 
Johnson, 192 F.3d at 513 (same). 


Moreover, when Congress intended in other provisions 
of AEDPA to refer to both state and federal post-conviction © 
proceedings, it did so unambiguously. Thus, 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2254(i) (Supp. 2000) provides that “[t]he ineffectiveness 
or incompetence of counsel during Federal or State collateral 
post-conviction proceedings shall not be a ground for relief 
in a proceeding arising under section 2254.” 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2261(e) (Supp. 2000) makes the identical limitation in 
Capital cases, Providing that “[t]he ineffectiveness or 
incompetence of counsel during State or Federal post- 
conviction proceedings ina capital case shall not be a ground 
for relief,” and that “[t]his limitation shall not preclude the 
appointment of different counsel * * * at any phase of State 
or Federal post-conviction proceedings.” Likewise, 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2264(a)(3) (Supp. 2000) specifies that a district court may 
only consider a claim not raised in the State courts if, inter 
alia, the claim is “based on a factual predicate that could not 
have been discovered through the exercise of due diligence 
in time to present the claim for State and Federal post- 
conviction review.” Thus, when Congress intended in AEDPA 


14 . 


to refer to both state and federal post-conviction or collateral 
review, it did so expressly. Had Congress intended 
§ 2244(d)(2) to apply to federal habeas proceedings, it would 
“have used language more obviously targeted” to the purpose. 
Martin v. Hadix, 527 U.S. 343, 354 (1999). 


The natural meaning of the phrase “State post-conviction 
or other collateral review” confines the “review” in question 
to “State” proceedings. This reading of § 2244(d)(2) is 
reflected in the use of both the same and contrasting terms 
elsewhere in AEDPA. Accordingly, the Second Circuit erred 
in its interpretation of § 2244(d)(2), and this Court should 
grant certiorari to correct the legal error. 


III. The Interrelationship of the Issues In Artuz v. 
Bennett and the Present Case Suggests That This 
Court Should Either Grant the Petition or Hold the 
Petition Until Disposition of Bennett. 


This Court’s grant of certiorari in Artuz v. Bennett, 120 
S. Ct. 1669 (2000) (No. 99-1238), underscores the 
importance of the questions presented in this case. Like the 
present case, Bennett requires interpretation of the tolling 
provision of § 2244(d)(2) and involves a question of when a 
prisoner’s effort to challenge a final state-court conviction 
tolls the AEDPA statute of limitations. It is possible, 
moreover, that the Court’s interpretation of the statutory 
provision, purpose, and the specific terms at issue in Bennett 
will prove dispositive for the present case, which involves 
adjacent statutory language. 


At issue in Bennett is the meaning of the portion of 
§ 2244(d)(2) requiring that applications for review be 
“properly filed” in order to toll the statute of limitations. 
The Second Circuit in Bennett held that a state-court 
application for post-conviction review is “properly filed” 
merely if it is submitted in accordance with state procedural 


15 


requirements, such as those governing the time and place 
for filing, even if it is subject to a mandatory procedural bar 
under state law. 199 F.3d at 121-123. The question presented in 
Bennett is whether a “state court post-conviction application 
[is] ‘properly filed’ [under § 2244(d)(2)] if it is filed in [a] court 
that does not have power to hear [the] merits of [the] 
application.” 68 U.S.L.W. 3649 (April 18, 2000). 


This Court’s determination in Bennett of whether and when 
an application for review of a state-court conviction is “properly 
filed” will have bearing on the present case. Even if Walker’s 
first federal habeas corpus petition was an application for 
“collateral review” encompassed by § 2244(d)(2), it also must 
have been “properly filed” in order to toll the Statute of 
limitations. If, however, a “properly filed” petition is one of 
which dismissal is not required by federal law, then Walker’s 
petition was arguably not “properly filed,” because its dismissal 
for failure to exhaust state remedies was mandatory as a matter 
of federal law. See, e.g., Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722, 
731 (1991) (“This Court has long held that a state prisoner’s 
federal habeas petition should be dismissed if the prisoner has 
not exhausted available state remedies as to any of his federal 
claims.”). If Walker’s first federal petition was not properly filed, 
the question of whether it is covered by the “other collateral 
review” portion of § 2244(d)(2) would be entirely academic 
with respect to this case. 


The Second Circuit has recognized the connection between 
the issue presented in Bennett and the issue it addressed in the 
present case. The issue was subsumed within the first question 
framed by the court in its order granting a certificate of 
appealability, which predated the decision in Bennett: “whether 
[Walker’s] first petition tolled the period of limitations set forth 
in 28 U.S.C. § 2244(d)(2).” Accordingly, the issue whether or 
nor Walker’s first federal habeas petition was “properly filed” 
was an alternative basis for affirming the district court’s 
dismissal of Walker’s present petition. 


16 


Moreover, this Court’s interpretation of the scope of the 
“properly filed” requirement and the tolling provision in 
general necessarily will affect the consequences of including 
or excluding prior federal petitions within the tolling 
orovision in AEDPA. The text of the Second Circuit’s 
decision in the present case recognizes the connection 
between the two issues. In rejecting the argument that 
including federal habeas petitions within the scope of 
§ 2244(d)(2)’s tolling provision would be inconsistent with 
AEDPA’s objective of placing finite restrictions on the time 
within which habeas petitions may be filed, the court 
reasoned that 


allowing Walker to exclude the time during which 
his first federal habeas petition was pending does 
not create a loophole in the law. The toiling 
provision by its terms already pertains only 
to “properly filed application[s].” 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2244(d)(2). Thus, for example, successive 
petitions arguably would not toll the one-year 
period. 


208 F.3d at 360. In other words, in the Second Circuit’s view, 
a particular interpretation of the term “properly filed” helps 
justify its statutory interpretation of the “other collateral 
review” provision. Indeed, the Second Circuit’s potentially 
“saving” interpretation of “properly filed” herein may not 
be consistent with its own holding in Bennett, in which it 
squarely held that an inquiry into whether a state-court 
application was “properly filed” entails no examination of 
the merits of the application. 


Central to the Second Circuit’s decision in Bennett was 
its view of federal-state comity. In the Second Circuit’s view, 


[djetermining whether a pending state-court 
motion is procedurally barred under a state law 


17 


. is beyond the appropriate role of a federal 
court in addressing whether a state post- 
conviction application was “properly filed.” It 
would involve federal courts in making 
determinations as to the proper outcome of 
pending state proceedings based on state law when 
a state court is positioned to make the very same 
decision. 


Bennett, 199 F.3d at 122. The same consideration will not 
arise in a federal court’s determination of whether a federal 
habeas petition is procedurally barred, for it is not beyond 
the appropriate role of a federal court to make such an 
assessment. Indeed, Rule 4 of the Rules Governing Section 
2254 Cases contemplates just such a role. Depending on the 
outcome of Bennett and this Court’s view of the “other 
collateral review” provision, this distinction may be critical 
in applying the “properly filed” requirement to prior federal 
habeas petitions. The present case offers an opportunity either 
to resolve the issue or indicate that it need not be resolved. 


The issue this Court already has agreed to resolve in 
Bennett thus arises in and has unavoidable implications for 
the present case. Especially in view of the compelling 
considerations otherwise warranting review by this Court, 
the instant petition should be granted so that the entirety of 
2244(d)(2) is before the Court in resolving the important 
question whether the pendency of a federal habeas petition 
dismissed for nonexhaustion tolls the limitations period. At 
the very least, the Court should hold the petition pending its 
decision in Bennett for consideration in light of that case. 


18 


CONCLUSION 


For the foregoing reasons, the petition for a writ of 
certiorari should be granted or held for consideration in light 
of this Court’s decision in Artuz v. Bennett, No. 99-1238.

DISCUSSION 


Walker’s appeal presents a narrow question regarding 
proper interpretation of Section 2244’s statute of limitations 
and tolling provisions. At issue is whether the AEDPA’s 
mandate to toll its one-year limitations period during the 
pendency of “other collateral review” applies to properly 
filed federal habeas petitions or only to applications for state 
review. See 28 U.S.C. § 2244(d)(2). 


In general, the AEDPA restricts the ability of prisoners 
to seek federal review of their state criminal convictions. 
Section 2244(d) created a new one-year statute of limitations 
in which state prisoners could file applications for a writ of 
habeas corpus. See 28 U.S.C. § 2244(d)(1). The one-year 
period generally runs from the date on which the state 
criminal judgment became final. See id. Prisoners like 


4a 
Appendix A 


Walker, whose convictions became final prior to the 
AEDPA’s effective date of April 24, 1996, have a one-year 
grace period in which to file their habeas corpus petitions, 
or until April 24, 1997. See Ross v. Artuz, 150 F.3d 97, 102-03 
(2d Cir.1998). Section 2244 also has a tolling provision that 
applies to both the statute of limitations and the one-year 
grace neriod. See Bennett v. Artuz, 199 F.3d 116, 119 (2d 
Cir.1999) (holding that the “AEDPA’s pending-state-petition 
tolling provision does apply to a petition challenging a 
pre-AEDPA conviction”), petition for cert. filed, _ 
U.S.L.W. _ (U.S. Jan. 24, 2000) (No. 99-1238). The tolling 
provision at Section 2244(d)(2) states: 


The time during which a properly filed application 
for State post-conviction or other collateral review 
with respect to the pertinent judgment or claim is 
pending shall not be counted toward any period 
of limitation under this subsection. 


28 U.S.C. § 2244(d)(2). 


Walker interprets Section 2244(d)(2), specifically the 
phrase “other collateral review,” to mean that we should 
exclude from the one-year grace period the time during which 
his first, timely habeas corpus petition was pending in federal 
court. That exclusion of approximately 80 days would render 
his second habeas petition timely. Respondent interprets the 
phrase “other collateral review” to mean only state 
procedures or remedies. Under this view, no tolling takes 
place and Walker’s second habeas petition is untimely 
because he filed it after the one-year grace period ended on 
April 24, 1997. Both Walker and respondent claim that their

Appendix A 


interpretations rely on the plain language of Section 
2244(d)(2) and are consistent with the legislative goals of 
the AEDPA. 


Although a number of federal district courts have 
addressed this question, only one other circuit court of 
appeals has decided the issue. In Jones v. Morton, the Third 
Circuit held that Congress “intended that the word ‘State’ 
would be read to modify both ‘post-conviction’ and ‘other 
collateral,’ so that tolling would be afforded under 
§ 2244(d)(2) for various forms of state review only.” Jones 
v. Morton, 195 F.3d 153, 15° (3d Cir.1999). The Third 
Circuit relied upon a California federal district court case, 
Sperling v. White, 30 F.Supp.2d 1246, 1250 (C.D.Cal.1998), 
as well as other district court cases adopting Sperling’s 
interpretation. See Jones, 195 F.3d at 158-59. In Sperling, 
the district court adopted the report and recommendation of 
a magistrate judge holding that Section 2244(d)(2) “does not 
contemplate tolling limitations during the time a habeas 
petition is pending in federal court.” Sperling, 30 F.Supp.2d 
at 1250. But see Barrett v. Yearwood, 63 F Supp.2d 1245, 
1249-50 (E.D.Cal.1999) (reaching opposite result). We 
respectfully disagree with the Jones approach. 


It is possible to interpret the word “State” in Section 
2244(d)(2) to modify both “post-conviction” and “other 
collateral.” Close analysis of the statute language, however, 
shows that “State” modifies only the word “post-conviction,” 
and the phrase “other collateral” is to be given its naturally 
broader meaning. The disjunctive “or” in the statute creates 
a distinct break between the two kinds of review Jones and 
Sperling would tie together. In contrast, applying “State” to 


6a 


Appendix A 


both of the disjunctive phrases would create a linguistic 
oddity because the statute would refer to “a properly filed 
application” for “State post-conviction . . . review” or “State 
. other review.” “State other collateral review” is an 
ungainly construction that we do not believe Congress 
intended. If Congress had intended the meaning that the 
Sperling court imputed, it might more naturally have written 
“a properly filed application for State post-conviction or 
collateral review.” We choose to adopt an interpretation that 
gives full meaning to the phrase “State post-conviction 
review,” which includes all state remedies, and the phrase 
“other collateral review,” which means federal habeas 
petitions. As the district court held in Barrett, “ ‘State 
post-conviction review’ means all collateral review of a 
conviction provided by a state,” and the phrase “other 
collateral review” would be meaningless if it did not refer to 
federal habeas petitions. Barrett, 63 F.Supp.2d at 1250. 


In addition, the district court’s interpretation in Sperling 
strains to hold that “State post-conviction review” means 
state judicial remedies and “other collateral review” means 
non-judicial state remedies such as petitions for clemency 
filed with the governor. See Sperling, 30 F.Supp.2d at 1251. 
This reading would require us to interpret at least one of 
these terms differently from its normal usage, because both 
“collateral review” and “post-conviction review” 
conventionally refer to habeas corpus, coram nobis and 
similar writs or judicial orders that courts issue after a 
conviction is final, overturning the conviction by reason of 
some error of law. Sperling read “collateral” to apply to 
clemency and pardon applications in order to avoid having 
it refer to the same scope of proceedings as “post-conviction 


7a 


Appendix A 


review” describes, but we can see no logical reason why 
Congress would have thought those applications to be 
included in the term “collateral” but not “post-conviction.” 
The only legislative history upon which the Sperling decision 
relies for its equation of “other collateral review” with 
petitions for clemency is a statement of Senator Orrin Hatch 
concerning a totally different proposed amendment to the 
legislation and discussed in the context of capital cases. See 
id. at 1251 (citing 141 Cong. Rec. $7803-01, S7825-26 (June 
7, 1995) as “appearing to describe a state petition for 
clemency as a form of collateral review, but distinct from 
judicial post-conviction review”). The Sperling court’s own 
characterization of the legislative history provides only a 
slender reed upon which to rest its holding. Because “post- 
conviction review” is a broad term that seems to encompass 
all review a prisoner seeks after conviction, we can see no 
reason why Congress should have believed that there were 
“other” forms of “collateral review” that did not come within 
the scope of “post-conviction review.” 


The Sperling court also held that its interpretation was 
consistent with the overall objective of the AEDPA “to place 
finite restrictions upon the time within which a petitioner 
may file a petition in federal court.” Jd. at 1251. However, 
allowing Walker to exclude the time during which his first 
federal habeas petition was pending does not create a 
loophole in the law. The tolling provision by its terms already 
pertains only to “properly filed application[s].” 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2244(d)(2). Thus, for example, successive petitions 
arguably would not toll the one-year period. Even if they 
did, the act’s restrictions on successive petitions, see 
28 U.S.C. § 2244(b), would not allow a petitioner to file 


8a 
Appendix A 


another petition if the district court dismissed a first petition 
on its merits or with prejudice. We therefore disagree with 
respondent that our reading would frustrate the AEDPA’s 
purpose by allowing petitioners to indefinitely toll the statute 
of limitations by filing a series of unexhausted petitions. 


Moreover, including federal petitions within the meaning 
of Section 2244(d)(2) will reward petitioners for filing their 
federal petitions as soon as possible, thus promoting the 
statutory objective of efficiency in the review of state 
criminal judgments. Congress enacted the limitations period 
to spur defendants to file their federal habeas petitions more 
quickly, and reading the tolling provision to encompass 
federal court consideration of the petitions furthers this goal. 
Under our reading of the statute, defendants will have an 
incentive to file their federal petitions right away because 
this filing will toll the one-year statute of limitations. This 
tolling is particularly useful for a petitioner if, upon review, 
the federal court determines that he must return to state court 
to exhaust his claims. Meanwhile, if the claims are properly 
before the federal court, this incentive leads to speedier 
completion of collateral review. 


We must examine the issue before us in the historical 
context of the AEDPA. This statute created a one-year statute 
of limitations where none previously existed. See Ross, 150 
F.3d at 98. This in itself was a dramatic change in the law 
and was Congress’ primary vehicle for streamlining the 
habeas review process and lending finality to state 
convictions. By reading the tolling provision of Section 2244 
to include pending federal habeas petitions, we do not detract 
from the overall purpose of the AEDPA because the statute 


9a 
Appendix A 


of limitations remains enforceable and intact. Our 
interpretation merely avoids penalizing state prisoners who 
properly have filed federal habeas petitions and are awaiting 
a response from the court. 


In adopting the contrary position, the Third Circuit did 
not “dwell” on the issue before it because it believed the 
majority of courts considering the issue adopted the Sperling 
v. White approach. See Jones, 195 F.3d at 158-59. We do 
not see a consensus emerging. It is true that three other 
California district court cases are consistent with the Sperling 
decision issued in the Central District of California. See 
Harrison v. Galaza, No. C 98-3371 TEH (PR), 1999 WL 
58594, at *2 (N.D.Cal. Feb. 4, 1999); Vincze v. Hickman, 
No. CIV.S-98-044 WBSGGHP, 1999 WL 68330, at *1 
(E.D.Cal. Jan. 13, 1999); Babcock v. Duncan, No. C 97-2740 
VRW (PR), 1997 WL 724450, at *2 (N.D.Cal. Nov. 12, 
1997). As noted above, however, we find the analysis of the 
California minority position in Barrett to be more persuasive. 
See Barrett, 63 F.Supp.2d at 1249-50. In addition, one of 
the district court cases upon which both Jones and Sperling 
rely does not clearly support their position. In the Wisconsin 
case of Kethley v. Berge, the district court indeed held that 
“time is not tolled while an improperly filed federal habeas 
petition is gathering dust on district court shelves” pursuant 
to Section 2244(d)(2). See Kethley v. Berge, 14 F.Supp.2d 
1077, 1079 (E.D.Wis.1998). However, the Wisconsin court 
also recognized the inequity resulting from its interpretation, 
stating that “the procedural dismissal of petitioner’s action 
in the present case should not later be construed to produce 
the unintended effect of barring federal habeas review under 
the AEDPA statute of limitations provision.” Jd. The Kethley 


10a 
Appendix A 


court remedied this problem by permitting the petitioner to 
reopen his petition after exhausting state remedies and 
preserve his original case number and filing date as “a form 
of equitable tolling of the AEDPA’s limitations period.” /d. 
at 1079. Cases purporting to foliow Kethley’s interpretation 
of Section 2244(d)(2) have not adopted this second part of 
the solution. Although we agrec with the Wisconsin court’s 
assessment of the inequity its interpretation of Section 
2244(d)(2) creates, we find its solution to the problem — 
preserving the original petition’s case number and filing date 
— to be artificial and cumbersome. A much simpler approach 
is to interpret the words of Section 2244(d)(2) to allow tolling 
outright in cases such as Walker’s. 


Because we decide that Section 2244(d)(2) by its terms 
tolled the one-year period of limitations while Walker’s first 
federal habeas petition was pending, we need not reach his 
alternative argument that the court should exercise its 
equitable powers to exclude the approximately 80 days 
during which the first petition was pending. 


CONCLUSION 


For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the district court’s 
dismissal, reinstate Walker’s petition for habeas corpus 
relief, and remand the matter to the district court for further 
proceedings.

MEMORANDUM AND ORDER 
JOHNSON, District Judge: 


Sherman Walker (“plaintiff” or “Walker”), appearing pro 
se, brings this action pursuant to 42 U.S.C. § 1983 alleging 
that a number of lawyers associated with the Legal Aid 


18a 
Appendix C 


Society and in private practice violated his right to counsel 
and due process of the law. Plaintiff also seeks habeas corpus 
relief pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 2254. For the reasons stated 
below, the motion to proceed in forma pauperis is granted, 
but the complaint is dismissed sua sponte in accordance with 
28 U.S.C. § 1915(d). 


BACKGROUND 


In three separate state court trials — one in New York 
County, and two in Queens County — Plaintiff was convicted 
on robbery charges. In each case, the Legal Aid Society was 
appointed to represent plaintiff in the appeal of his 
conviction. In the course of representing Walker, however, 
it became apparent that the Legal Aid Society already 
represented a confidential informant who had been a primary 
source of information leading to plaintiff's arrest. Because 
representing both clients would pose a conflict of interest, 
the Legal Aid Society formally moved to be relieved as 
counsel on plaintiff's appeals. The state appellate courts 
granted this motion and appointed new counsel to prosecute 
plaintiff's appeals. 


Walker now argues that no confidential informant 
existed and thus that there was no conflict of interest 
preventing the Legal Aid Society from representing him. 
Plaintiff contends that, although he notified the Legal Aid 
Society of this fact, they failed to investigate his claim. 
Plaintiff argues that the Legal Aid Society’s improper 
withdrawal denied him of his right to the counsel of his 
choice and due process of the law. 


19a 


Appendix C 


Accompanying plaintiffs complaint was an application 
to proceed in forma pauperis. 


DISCUSSION 


Under 28 U.S.C. § 1915, a district court may dismiss a 
case in which the plaintiff has applied to proceed in forma 
pauperis “if the allegation of poverty is untrue, or if satisfied 
that the action is frivolous or malicious.” 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1915(d). The Second Circuit follows a two-step approach 
in establishing eligibility to proceed in forma pauperis by 
first assessing a plaintiffs financial status, followed by a 
determination as to the frivolousness of the action. See 
Anderson v. Coughlin, 700 F.2d 37, 41 (2d Cir.1983). 


Applying the two-step approach to the present case, this 
Court finds that plaintiff satisfies the financial requirements 
to proceed in forma pauperis. Based on the allegation of 
poverty stated in his declaration, the Court is convinced that 
Walker is unable to pay the costs and filing fees in connection 
with this suit. Thus, plaintiff's motion for leave to proceed 
in forma pauperis is granted. 


Nevertheless, plaintiff's complaint must be dismissed. 
First, to establish a claim under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, a plaintiff 
must demonstrate that the conduct complained of was 
committed by a person acting under the color of state law 
and that the conduct deprived the plaintiff of a federal right. 
Bernheim v. Litt, 79 F.3d 318, 321 (2d Cir.1996). The United 
States Supreme Court has stated unequivocally that 
court-appointed lawyers do not act under color of state law 
within the meaning of § 1983. See Polk County v. Dodson, 


20a 
Appendix C 


454 U.S. 312 (1981) (public defender does not act under 
color of state law); Cassidento v. Hammer, 1995 WL 722536 
(2d Cir.1995) (court-appointed attorney does not act under 
color of state law). As such, plaintiff's claims against all of 
the individual attorneys as well as the Legal Aid Society 
must be dismissed. See Pino v. Ryan, 49 F.3d 51 (2d 
Cir.1995) (sua sponte dismissal may be based on an 
affirmative defense that appears on the face of the complaint). 


Plaintiff's claim for habeas corpus relief also must be 
dismissed for failure to adequately set forth his claim. For 
instance, although plaintiff refers to state appellate 
proceedings relating to his robbery convictions, he fails to 
specify the claims litigated in those appeals. Because this 
court cannot determine which issues have been properly 
exhausted in state court, the essential allegations of a habeas 
corpus action have not been presented. See 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2254(b) (writ of habeas corpus “shall not be granted unless 
it appears that the applicant has exhausted the remedies 
available in the courts of the State’). Therefore, this claim 
is dismissed without prejudice. 


CONCLUSION 


For the reasons stated herein, plaintiff's motion to 
proceed in forma pauperis is granted. However, the Court 
finds plaintiff's complaint to be frivolous within the meaning 
of 28 U.S.C. § 1915(d), since it fails to state a cognizable 
claim under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 or 28 U.S.C. § 2254. 
Accordingly, plaintiff's complaint is dismissed without 
prejudice. 


SO ORDERED.
