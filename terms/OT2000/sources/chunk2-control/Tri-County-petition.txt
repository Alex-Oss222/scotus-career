QUESTION PRESENTED 
Gasperini v. Center for Humanities, Inc., 518 U:S. 415 
(1996), holds that the Seventh Amendment does not preclude 
district courts from exercising substantial discretion in grant- 
ing new trials but does constrain appellate review of such 
orders. The question presented is whether, as some Circuits 
have recognized, Gasperini invalidates the contrary approach 
of other Circuits, including the D.C. Circuit here, which hold 
that the Seventh Amendment strictly circumscribes a district 
court’s discretion to grant a new trial and thereby expands the 
scope of appcllate review.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
I. INTRODUCTION. 
This appeal involves the award of damages for a procedur- 
al due process violation -- the suspension, without a prior 
hearing, of a permit to construct a soil remediation cility in 
the District of Columbia. See Tri County Industries, Inc. Vv. 
District of Columbia, 104 F.3d 455, 460-62 (D.C. Cir. 1997). 
On remand from that appeal, the case was tried to a jury 
which awarded $5,000,000 in damages. Following the award, 
the district court, the Honorable James Robertson, ordered a 
3 
new trial because the damages award was excessive and he 
had disabled the jury from performing its job by excluding 
and striking evidence from its consideration. A second jury, 
which considered a fuller evidentiary record in light of Carey 
v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247, 266-67 (1978), and the rule that a 
party must mitigate its damages arising from an unlawful 
injury, awarded nominal damages of $100. 
On appeal, citing Seventh Amendment concerns, the D.C. 
Circuit transformed an abuse of discretion standard of appel- 
late review into a “more searching inquiry" standard. App. 
5 & 10. Under that revised standard, the court set aside the 
district court’s order granting the District of Columbia a new 
trial. We submit that in doing so, the D.C. Circuit contra- 
vened this Court’s teaching in Gasperini v. Center for Human- 
ities, Inc., 518 U.S. 415 (1996). Gasperini holds that district 
court judges retain substantial discretion under the Seventh 
Amendment to order new trials and that appellate courts must 
exercise restraint in applying the abuse of discretion standard 
of appellate review of new trial orders. The decision here 
turns Gasperini on its head.

F. The D.C. Circuit’s Decision. 
Tri County appealed the order granting the new trial. 
Citing McNeal v. Hi-Lo Powered Scaffolding, Inc. , 836 F.2d 
637 (D.C. Cir. 1988), it argued that, on appeal, under the 
abuse of discretion standard, “[t}he trial court’s decision to 
grant a new trial will be given close scrutiny in order to 
protect the litigants’ right to a jury trial." Brief for Plaintiff- 
Appellant at 30. 
The District countered that, under an abuse of discretion 
standard, “‘[a} trial court has wide discretion in setting aside 
a jury verdict where . . . the motion granted is for a new trial, 
not judgment n.o.v.; the court’s decision concerns matters of 
fact, not law; and the new trial is ordered because’ the first 
trial ‘created a potential for injustice.’" Brief for Appellees 
at 24-25, quoting Schneider v. Lockheed Aircraft Corp., 658 
F.2d 835, 849 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (footnote omitted), cert. 
denied, 455 U.S. 994 (1982). The District also countered that 
““it is the duty of the judge to set aside the verdict and grant 
a new trial, if he is of the opinion that the verdict is against 
the clear weight of the evidence, or is based upon evidence 
which is false or will result in a miscarriage of justice, even 
though there may be substantial evidence which would prevent 
the direction of a verdict.’" Brief for Appellees at 24, quoting 
Eastern Air Lines, Inc. v. Union Trust Co., 239 F.2d 25, 29 
(D.C. Cir.), cert. denied, 353 U.S. 942 (1956). 


=== PDF PAGE 11 ===
12 
The D.C. Circuit rejected the District’s position. Instead, 
the court applied (App. 5 & 10) its recently developed “more 
searching inquiry" standard. See, e.g., Langevine v. District 
of Columbia, 106 F.3d 1018, 1023 (D.C. Cir. 1997); Hutchin- 
son v. Stuckey, 952 F.2d 1418, 1421 (D.C. Cir. 1992). This 
standard arises from the court’s “‘concern that a judge’s 
nullification of the jury’s verdict tay encroach on the jury’s 
important fact-finding mission.’" App. 5, quoting Langevine, 
106 F.3d at 1023. Accordingly, “close scrutiny is required in 
order to protect the litigants’ [Seventh Amendment] night to

There is an intolerable conflict among the Circuits over 
the standard of appellate review of new trial orders. This 
conflict continues despite Gasperini, whose significance has 
eluded several Circuits, including the D.C. Circuit. On one 
side are the Circuits which~ understand that the Seventh 
Amendment affords district courts substantial discretion to 
order new trials; on the other are the Circuits which hold that 
the Seventh Amendment strictly constrains the district court’s 
discretion in this respect and requires close appellate scrutiny 
of new trial orders. 
The significance of Gasperini in granting broad discretion 
to district courts to order new trials and in constraining 
appellate court review of such orders has been recognized by 
the Seventh Circuit, which previously had followed a standard 
similar to the D.C. Circuit’s current approach. In Medcom 
Holding Co. v. Baxter Travenol Laboratories, Inc., 106 F.3d 
1388 (7th Cir. 1997), the Seventh Circuit applied Gasperini’s 
abuse of discretion standard to review a district court’s grant 
of a new trial. In so ruling, the court observed that, prior to 
Gasperini, it had “applied this standard ‘somewhat more 
exacting[ly]’ when the district court granted a new trial 
because of Seventh Amendment considerations." Jd. at 1397. 
Given Gasperini, however, the court, pursuant to its Circuit 
Rule 40(e), overruled its prior jurisprudence; abandoned its 
exacting scrutiny standard for appellate review; and announced 
that it would review rants of new trials "with somewhat more 
deference to the district court, utilizing an ordinary abuse of 
discretion standard .. .." Jd. at 1397. The Seventh Circuit 
subsequently explained that “[g]enerally, an abuse of discre-

We review the district court’s grant of a new trial for abuse 
of discretion. See Langevine v. District of Columbia, 106 
F.3d 1018, 1023 (D.C. Cir. 1997) (citing Hutchinson v. Stuck-

searching inquiry is required” if the new trial is granted than 
if denied, however, because of “the concern that a judge’s 
nullification of the jury’s verd*-t may encroach on the jury’s

its discretion in excluding the evidence. In light of our 
findings that Tri County’s evidence was properly before the 
jury and the District’s evidence was properly excluded, we 
conclude that the district court, in granting a new trial based 
on a revised view of its original rulings, did abuse its discre- 
tion. See Langevine, 106 F.3d at 1023 (a “more searching 
inquiry” is particularly necessary “when the motion [for new 
trial] is granted on the ground that the verdict is against the 
weight of the evidence”). 
For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the district court’s 
order of July 23, 1998, reinstate the original jury verdict of 
$5,000,000 and vacate the verdict and judgment resulting 
from the second trial.

Before the Court is defendants’ motion for judgment 
notwithstanding the verdict or in the alternative for a new trial 
[#115]. 
The motion for JNOV (now, of course, properly styled 
judgment as a matter of law, Fed. R. Civ. P. 50), will be 
denied. The most important grounds for defendants’ motion, 
set forth as well in several pretrial motions, is that plaintiff 
failed to mitigate its damages. The mitigation question turns 
upon what would have been reasonable for Tri-County to do 
by way of mitigation. Plaintiff adduced proof that it would 
have had to make capital expenditures, continue in possession 
of a lease, and incur substantial legal expenses over a long 
period of time to have a chance of going forward with its 
business. The jury might reasonably have found that it would 
have been unreasonable to spend nearly a million dollars to 
recoup the $536,421 in expenses Tri-County had already 
incurred. In any case, the party raising mitigation as an 
affirmative defense must prove that there is a standard for 
reasonableness and that plaintiff did not meet it. Norms v. 
13 
Green, 656 A.2d 282 (D.C. 1995). The District of Columbia 
did not sustain that burden at trial. 
The jury’s award of future profits is another matter. 
Plaintiff's position is that it was entitled to recover 
$11,628,174 of future profits (the jury apparently awarded 
$4,463,579) without investing a penny to seek reinstatement 
of the unlawfully suspended building permit. No proof of a 
standard of reasonable mitigation efforts is required to evalu- 
ate that proposition: it is unreasonable per se. 
The jury’s award of future profits, moreover, was based 
on expert testimony that should not have been received -- or, 
at least, should not have been received without allowing the 
District of Columbia to adduce proof that plaintiff's soil 
remediation facility would never have been permitted to 
operate. Plaintiff's proof of future profits consisted of what 
amounted to a pro-forma statement of income and expense. 
Plaintiff had no experience in the business of “burning” soil 
for remediation. Plaintiff's expert testimony acknowledged 
the recent failure of a similar remediation facility in Virginia, 
but the impact of that failure upon plaintiff's hoped-for future 
profits was not analyzed, nor was the implication of the 
diminishing market price per ton for cleaning petroleum- 
contaminated soil. Upon reflection, I am convinced that the 
evidence of lost future profits was too speculative and remote 
and that it failed to meet the required standard of reasonable 
certainty. 
In addition, the jury’s award of some $4.5 million in 
assertedly lost profits was in any event grossly excessive and 
shocks the judicial conscience, particularly in view of the 
realistic prospect, which I did not permit defendants to prove 
at trial, that there would be continued community resistance 
to increased dump truck traffic along Bryant Street, where the 


=== PDF PAGE 27 ===
14 
remediation facility was located, and a very real likelihood 
that the District of Columbia would have closed down the 
remediation facility sooner or later in any event, or that it 
never would have been permitted to commence operations at 
all. . 
Accordingly, it is necessary to grant the motion for a new 
trial. At the new trial, if plaintiff is again permitted to adduce 
proof of lost future profits, defendants will be permitted to 
rebut that proof with evidence of the likely course of future 
community resistance and regulatory activities with respect to 
the remediation facility.

some upon the parties, however, the order for a new trial will be nisi titur. See Fai Nationwide M ay

in part).
