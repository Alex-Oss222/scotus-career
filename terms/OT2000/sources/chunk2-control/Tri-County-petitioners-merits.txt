QUESTION PRESENTED 
Gasperini v. Center for Humanities, Inc. , 518 U.S. 415 
(1996), holds that the Seventh Amendment does not preclude 
district courts from exercising substantial discretion in grant- 
ing new trials but does constrain appellate review of such 
orders. The question presented is whether, as some Circuits 
have recognized, Gasperini invalidates the contrary approach 
of other Circuits, including the D.C. Circuit here, which hold 
that the Seventh Amendment strictly circumscribes a district 
court’s discretion to grant a new trial and thereby expands the 
scope of appellate review.

LEGAL PROVISIONS INVOLVED 
This case involves the Seventh Amendment to the United 
States Constitution (U.S. CoNsT. Amend. VII), which pro- 
vides: 
In Suits at common law, where the value in controver- 
sy shall exceed twenty dollars, the right of trial by 
jury shall be preserved, and no fact tried by a jury, 
shall be otherwise re-examined in any Court of the 
United States, than according to the rules of the com- 
mon law. 
This case also involves Fed. R. Civ. P. 59(a), which 
provides in pertinent part: 
A new trial may be granted to all or any of the parties 
and on all or part of the issues (1) in an action in 
which there has been a trial by jury, for any of the 
reasons for which new trials have heretofore been 
granted in actions at law in the courts of the United 
States.... 
STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
I. INTRODUCTION. 
This appeal involves the award of damages for a procedur- 
al due process violation -- the District of Columbia’s tempo- 
rary suspension, without a prior hearing, of a permit to 
convert a warehouse in the District into a soil remediation 
facility. See Joint Appendix ("J.A.") 18-30 (Tri County 
Industries, Inc. v. District of Columbia, 104 F.3d 455, 460-62 
(D.C. Cir. 1997)). On remand from that appeal, the case was 
tried to a jury which awarded $5,000,000 in damages. Pet. 
3 
App. 2. Following the award, the district court, the Honor- 
able James Robertson, ordered a new trial because the damag- 
es award was grossly excessive; the permit holder had done 
absolutely nothing to mitigate damages; and he had disabled 
the jury from performing its job by excluding and striking evi- 
dence from its consideration. Pet. App. 12-14. A second 
jury, which considered a fuller evidentiary record in light of 
Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247, 266-67 (1978), as well as the 
rule that a party must mitigate its damages arising from an 
unlawful injury, awarded nominal damages of $100. J.A. 7. 
On appeal, citing Seventh Amendment concerns, the D.C. 
Circuit transformed the abuse of discretion standard of appel- 
late review into a “more searching inquiry" standard. Pet. 
App. 5 & 10. Under its revised standard, the court set aside 
the district court’s order granting the District a new trial and 
ordered reinstatement of the $5,000,000 damages award. 
In so ruling, the D.C. Circuit contravened Gasperini v. 
Center for Humanities, Inc. , 518 U.S. 415 (1996). Gasperini 
holds that district court judges retain substantial discretion 
under the Seventh Amendment to order new trials and that 
appellate courts must exercise restraint in applying the abuse 
of discretion standard of appellate review of new trial orders. 
The decision here turns Gasperini on its head.

In 1993, Tri County secured a building permit to convert

Ill. THE LITIGATION. 
A. Liability. 
On September 20, 1994, Tri County brought suit in the 
United States District Court for the District of Columbia 
claiming that Cross’s temporary suspension of its permit on 
September 20, 1993, without a prior hearing, violated the Due 
Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. J.A. 1, 11 & 13. 
The court (Robertson, J.) dismissed the complaint for failure 
to state a claim. J.A. 10-17 (Tri-County Industries, Inc. v. 
District of Columbia, 932 F. Supp. 4 (D.D.C. 1996)). 
7 
The D.C. Circuit reversed and remanded the case "to 
consider the extent of Tri County’s damages." J.A. 30. In its 
decision, the court observed, inter alia, that "Tri County took 
no action to challenge either the September 20 suspension, 
which all parties now agree had no legal basis at all, or the 
[September 22] stop work order, which is not challenged here 
and which all agree was a legally sufficient bar to Tri Coun- 
ty’s continuing work on the facility." J.A. 20; see also J.A. 
25. It also directed that, "[iJn calculating any damages, . . . 
the possibility of relief from the District’s Board of Appeals 
and Review, as well as the possible mooting effect of the stop 
work order, may be pertinent." J.A. 29-30.

B. History Supports The District’s Position. 
In Gasperini, this Court undertook an extensive examina- 
tion of history in reaching its conclusions. Here, we merely 
emphasize several aspects of the history of the Seventh 
Amendment that shed light on the relationship between the 
trial judge and the jury. This history supports the argument 
that, under the Seventh Amendment, a district court judge has 
broad discretion to order new jury trials for a variety of 
reasons, including his determination that the verdict is against 
the weight of the evidence, the damages award is excessive, 
and/or he unwisely excluded evidence from the jury’s consid- 
eration. An order granting a new jury trial does not deprive 
a prevailing litigant of its constitutional right to a jury trial, 
whether that litigant be a plaintiff or defendant, but only of a 
particular verdict.

however, the court, pursuant to its Circuit Rule 40(e), over-

E. The D.C. Circuit’s Approach Conflicts With 
Gasperini, History, And This Court’s Ante- 
cedent Jurisprudence. 
The D.C. Circuit's “more searching inquiry” standard for appellate review of district court decisions granting new trials 
conflicts with Gasperini, this Court's longstanding antecedent 
jurisprudence, and with history. The D.C. Circuit gave no 
deference to Judge Robertson's “‘unique opportunity to consider the evidence in the living courtroom context’" and he 
was not "give[n] the benefit of every doubt" in his decision to grant a new trial. Gasperini, 518 U.S. at 438, quoting Taylor 
v. Washington Terminal Co., 409 F.2d at 148, and Dagnello, 
289 F.2d at 806. Or, as the Seventh Circuit subsequently has stated, "[g]Jenerally, an abuse of discretion only occurs where 
no reasonable person could take the view adopted by the trial court." McClain v. Owens-Corning Fiberglas Corp., 139 
F.3d 1124, 1126 (7th Cir. 1998) (upholding order granting a 
new trial on damages) (internal quotation marks omitted). 
Instead, based on its misunderstanding that the Seventh Amendment acts as a restraint on trial, rather than appellate, 
judges, the D.C. Circuit brushed aside the reasons the trial 
judge, upon reflection, gave for granting a new trial. In 
effect, the D.C. Circuit assumed the role of the district court 
and substituted its judgment for that of Judge Robertson. 
First, because the jury had awarded only about half of 
what Tri County had requested, the D.C. Circuit “ques- 
tion{ed]" the district court’s considered judgment that the 
damages award was excessive. The "rule of thumb" applied 
by the D.C. Circuit seriously intruded into the district court’s 
analysis of the excessiveness of the award in this case based 
on the evidence it had heard and the serious flaws in that 
evidence it had identified.

CONCLUSION 
Tri County was not deprived of its Seventh Amendment 
right to a jury trial by the district court’s order granting a new 
trial. Instead, it received a jury trial in a proceeding that was 
fair to both sides, and the jury awarded it $100. 
The Court should reverse the decision of the D.C. Circuit 
setting aside the trial court’s new trial order, affirm that 
order, and remand for further proceedings consistent with its 
decision in this case.
