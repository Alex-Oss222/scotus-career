The conclusion that the Seventh Amendment permits 
district courts (and superior courts exercising original jurisdic- 
tion) to exercise substantial discretion to order new trials in 
cases tried before juries is reinforced by another consider- 
ation. The first Congress was well aware that successive jury 
trials in the same case were not uncommon on this side of the 
Atlantic. In Federalist No. 81, Alexander Hamilton wrote that 
in the New England states, as well as in Georgia, “an appeal 
from one jury to another is familiar both in language and 
practice, and is even a matter of course, until there have been 
two verdicts on one side." See Jacob E. Cooke, THE FEDER- 
ALIST 550 (Wesleyan University Press 1961) (emphasis 
added). See also Federalist No. 83, id. at 565-66 (describing, 
inter alia, the provision for jury trials in each of the states).’ 
In short, the first Congress plainly contemplated that 
federal courts exercising original jurisdiction would grant new 
trials for the reasons new trials were granted under the com- 
mon law. Just as plainly, this Congress was not disposed to 
permit appellate courts to reexamine factual matters. The 
conclusion follows, therefore, that the first Congress did not 
understand the contemporaneous Seventh Amendment as 
restricting the power of courts, when acting in a trial capacity, 
to reexamine facts in jury trials. Instead, as elsewhere in the 
Constitution, the framers of the Seventh Amendment con- 
structed a wise system of checks and balances. They imposed 
an important check on the factfinding function of the jury by 
authorizing, in accordance with the common law, trial court 
judges to order new trials whenever they were reasonably 
dissatisfied with an initial jury verdict. This balance was

Given this distinction, a new trial order should not be 
overturned unless the district court abused its discretion in the 
true sense of that concept: “where no reasonable person could 
take the view adopted by the trial court." McClain v. Owens- 
Corning Fiberglas Corp., 139 F.3d 1124, 1126 (7th Cir. 
1998) (internal quotation marks omitted). An appellate court 
does not sit, as Tri County suggests (Brief 24), to referee 
disputes between juries and trial judges. It should not reexam- 
ine the facts de novo, and it should not reweigh the evidence. 
Instead, to avoid running afoul of the Seventh Amendment, an 
appellate court should reverse only when the district court has 
made a clear legal error. An appellate court should bear in 
mind, moreover, that federal district court judges, burdened 
by crowded dockets, have no incentive to grant new trials and 
are very unlikely to conclude that they have unduly tipped the 
scales in favor of one party unless, upon careful examination 
of the entire record in the case, they are convinced that they 
have done so. 
Here, the district court did not, pursuant to Fed. R. Civ. 
P. 50(a), enter judgment as a matter of law in favor of the 
District in any respect. Instead, it merely granted a new jury 
trial because the evidence demonstrated that the jury’s verdict 
was grossly excessive in light of the evidence the jury consid- 
ered and because it concluded that it should have permitted the 
jury to consider additional evidence proffered by the District. 
In other words, the district court made factual or evidentiary 
judgments, and the D.C. Circuit should not have overturned 
those judgments unless no reasonable judge could have taken 
those views. That is not the case here. To the contrary, 
when the proper standard is applied, it is plain that the district 
court did not abuse its discretion and that its new trial order 
should be sustained on several independent grounds.
