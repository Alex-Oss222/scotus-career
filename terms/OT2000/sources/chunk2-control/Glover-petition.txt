JURISDICTION 
The judgment of the court of appeals was entered on July 15, 1999. A timely petition for 
rehearing was denied on October 7, 1999. (App. 8). This Court’s jurisdiction is invoked under 
28 U.S.C. § 1254(a). 
PERTINENT CONSTITUTIONAL AND STATUTORY PROVISIONS 
The relevant constitutional provisions are the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments to the 
United States Constitution. 
SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 
This case raises an issue of recurring importance — whether defense counsel’s error 
relating to the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines which causes the defendant to be sentenced to 
additional months of incarceration (in this case, between 6 and 21 months of additional 
imprisonment) constitutes ineffective assistance of counsel under the Sixth Amendment. Here, 
due to counsel’s ineffective performance, the district court made a clear error in calculating the 
offense level. Under the correct offense level, petitioner’s a of imprisonment should have 
been between 6 to 21 months lower than the 84-month sentence he actually received. 
Nevertheless, while conceding counsel’s deficient performance and the incorrect sentencing 
calculations, the court of appeals denied petitioner's motion under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 to correct 
his sentence. Applying the governing case law in the circuit, the Seventh Circuit held that an 
additional 6 to 21 months in prison was not “sufficiently significant” to satisfy the prejudice 
prong of the ineffective assistance of counsel standard. According to the Seventh Circuit, to be 
“significant prejudice,” a counsel’s error —_ lead to an incorrect sentence of more than two 
offense levels under the Sentencing Guidelines. 
The Seventh Circuit’s rule represents a marked and unwarranted departure from this 
Court’s established precedents relating to constitutionally ineffective assistance of counsel. In 
adding a a new “significance” threshhold, the Seventh Circuit misconstrues the prejudice 
requirement as set out in Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), and interpreted in 
Lockhart v. Fretwell, 506 U.S. 364 (1993). Nowhere in Strickland does the Court even suggest 
that additional months in prison due to counsel’s deficient performance would not constitute 
prejudice. Contrary to the Seventh Circuit’s reasoning, the Supreme Court’s decision in 
Lockhart also does not support the conclusion that defendant must show “significant prejudice.” 
Lockhart addressed the narrow circumstances where the habeas petitioner’s ineffectivenesss 
claim was based on the attorney’s failure to invoke at sentencing a court of appeals decision that 
was later overruled. The Lockhart Court refused to allow the habeas petitioner to obtain “a 
windfall;” that is a far cry from the instant case where Mr. Glover simply asks for the correct 
application of the Sentencing Guidelines. Moreover, the Seventh Circuit’s decision is contrary to 
this Court’s Sixth Amendment jurisprudence which has recognized that any term of 
imprisonment, even one day, requires the effective assistance of counsel. See Argersinger v. 
Hamlin, 407 U.S. 25 (1972) (rejecting argument that right to counsel exists only for felony 
offenses and hoiding that no person may be imprisoned for any period without presence of 
counsel at trial). 
Not surprisingly, the decision below also conflicts with other courts of appeals and 
district courts which have declined to adopt the Seventh Circuit’s “significant prejudice” 
requirement. The D.C., Third, and Eighth Circuits have not adopted the Seventh Circuit’s 
“significant prejudice” rule and have granted Section 2255 motions where the change due to 
counsel’s error is two offense levels or less. Furthermore, and even more striking, the Second 


=== PDF PAGE 7 ===
and Ninth Circuits have granted Section 2255 motions where there is no effect on the actual 
prison term of the petitioner. Even those circuit courts which favor a “significant prejudice” test 
— the Fourth, Fifth and Tenth Circuits -- have not adopted the Seventh Circuit’s rule that a two- 
offense level change due to counsel’s deficient performance is per se insufficient to constitute 
“prejudice” under Strickland. The Fifth Circuit has expressly recognized “an exception” for the 
very type of situation raised by Mr. Glover here -- “when a deficiency by counsel resulted in a 
specific, demonstrable enhancement in sentencing.”' Spriggs v. Collins, 993 F.2d 85, n.4 (Sth 
Cir. 1993). 
The Seventh Circuit’s “significant prejudice” rule also has led to inconsistent results in 
the lower courts. These courts have not been provided guidelines or standards for determining 
whether an increased sentence due to counsel’s error is “significant.” Instead, the lower courts 
are left to their c wn devices in attempting to evaluate if it is the number of months, number of 
offense levels, percentage of total sentence, or some other variable which is critical to showing 
sufficient prejudice. The seemingly arbitrary nature of the lower court decisions relating to what 
constitutes “significant prejudice” damages the public’s confidence in the court system and 
precludes the consistency in sentencing which was the goal of the Sentencing Guidelines. 
This Court’s review is necessary to establish uniformity on the issue of whether, and in 
what circumstances, additional months in prison due to counsel’s deficient performance 
constitutes constitutionally ineffective assistance. There already have been numerous cases

Given the frequently recurring nature of the problem of mistakes by counsel relating to 
Sentencing Guidelines calculations, it is especially important that this Court grant the writ in this

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
1. Conviction and Sentence 
Following a jury trial, defendant Paul Glover was found guilty of one count of a 
conspiracy in violation of the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act, 18 U.S.C. 
§ 1962(d); eleven counts of soliciting and receiving kickbacks in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1954; 
two counts of money laundering in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1956(a)(1)(B)(I); and four counts of 
tax evasion in violation of 26 U.S.C. § 7206. At the time of sentencing, it was determined that 
Mr. Glover’s total offense level was 28 and his criminal history category was level I, resulting in 
a guideline range of 78 to 97 months. He was sentenced to 84 months’ incarceration. Mr. 
Glover’s conviction and sentence were affirmed on direct appeal, where he was represented by 
the same counsel who represented him at trial. See United States v. Glover, 101 F.3d 1183 (7th 
Cir. 1996). ;

to vacate, set aside or correct his sentence. Mr. Glover contended that his sentencing range was 
incorrectly calculated because his money laundering offenses were not grouped with his 
conspiracy and kickback offenses. See U.S.S.G. § 301.2. He further contended that his counsel

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 
In Durrive v. United States, 4 F.3d 548 (7th Cir. 1993), the Seventh Circuit first 
announced that errors in sentencing calculations caused by counsel’s deficient performance must 
be “significant” to constitute constitutionally ineffective assistance of counsel. According to the 
Durrive court, only “Grave errors by judge and counsel” -- “[flor instance, an error that produces 
a large effect on the sentence” — could be deemed prejudicial for Strickland purposes. Id at 551. 
Subsequent panels within the Seventh Circuit, including in the instant case, have followed 
Durrive. See, e.g., Martin, 109 F.3d at 1177-78; Allen v. United States,, 175 F.3d 560, 563 (7th 
Cir. 1999). 
This Court should grant the writ in Mr. Glover’s case to review and reverse this Seventh 
Circuit rule requiring “significant prejudice” of more than two offense levels under the 
Sentencing Guidelines. The Seventh Circuit’s rule has no support in Strickland or subsequent 
Supreme Court jurisprudence. The “significant prejudice” rule also has not been widely 
followed in the circuit courts or trial courts outside of the Seventh Circuit. Even within the 
Seventh Circuit, three judges, including one of its original proponents, have strongly attacked the 
rule that additional months in prison does not constitute “significant” prejudice, Martin v. United 
States, 109 F.3d 1177, 1178 (7th Cir. 1997) (Judge Rovner, dissenting on denial of en banc 
review), and district courts have considered the reasoning underlying this rule to be 
“questionable,” Burgos v. United States, 968 F.Supp. 380, 384 (N.D. Ill. 1997), and “the source 
of considerable debate.” Davis v. United States, 1998 WL 142369 *3 n.5 (N.D. Ill. March 19, 
1998) (citing numerous cases). 
A. The Seventh Circuit’s “Significant Prejudice” Requirement Is Contrary to 
Supreme Court Precedent 
The Seventh Circuit has adopted a rule that an increased prison term due to a sentencing 
error caused by counsel’s deficient performance does not constitute prejudice for the purpose of 
the Strickland analysis unless the increase is significant. Unfortunately, the Seventh Circuit’s 
rule is contrary to Supreme Court precedent. 
The Supreme Court in Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 690 (1984), established a 
two-part test for reviewing claims of ineffective assistance of counsel. First, a defendant must 
demonstrate that his counsel’s performance fell below an objective standards of reasonableness. 
Second, a defendant must demonstrate that he suffered prejudice due to his counsel’s deficient 
performance. This second prong requires that the defendant “show that there is a reasonable 
probability ... that, but for counsel’s unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would 
have been different.” /d. at 694. 
Nowhere in Strickland does the Court state that the defendant must show “significant 
prejudice.” Nor is there any indication that the defendant must show a large increase in his 
prison sentence. Instead, Strickland makes clear that the test is whether counsel’s errors have 
adversely affected the proceeding’s outcome: “taking due account of the effect of the errors on 
the remaining findings, a court making the prejudice inquiry must ask if the defendant has met 
the burden of showing that the decision reached would reasonably likely have been different 
absent the errors.” Jd. at 696. Plainly, as the court of appeals itself assumed here, Mr. Glover 
can demonstrate that the sentence in his case “would reasonably likely have been different” had 
his counsel asserted the groupings issue and the dispositive Seventh Circuit case in his favor.

[A]n analysis focusing solely on mere outcome determination 
without attention to whether the result of the proceeding was

Durrive, 4 F.3d at 551 (quoting Lockhart, 506 U.S. at 369-70).2 The Seventh Circuit, however,

“result of the proceeding was fundamentally unfair or unreliable” in Mr. Glover's case—in fact, 
Glover received a sentence which was 6 to 21 months more than he should have received under 
the applicable law and regulations. There also can be no claim that Glover received “a windfall 
to which the law does not entitle him”—in fact, his counsel’s deficient performance caused 
Glover to receive an injury which he should not have received, i.e., a sentence longer than the 
Sentencing Guidelines properly allowed.

what is a “significant” increase in a defendant’s sentence. The inevitable result has been 
arbitrary and inconsistent lower court decisions.
