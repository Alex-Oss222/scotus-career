## Event

**Chen v. City of Houston** — Fifth Circuit No. 98-20440; 206 F.3d 502.

Submitted on the merits papers; no oral-argument date is established. Decided May 21, 2001. Review of the Fifth Circuit's affirmance of summary judgment for Houston. The accepted question is whether materially unequal citizen voting-age populations forbid municipal council districts based on total resident population. The separate racial-gerrymandering claim is outside that question.

Houston's 1997 plan had a maximum total-population deviation of 8.63 percent. The voters alleged materially greater disparity among citizen voting-age populations. The Court accepts the competing numerical consideration as real; it does not independently find exact demographic estimates or the feasibility of an alternative map.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

Merits decision after plenary review.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Affirmed on the accepted population-base question, 9-0.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** None.

Affirmed insofar as the accepted population-base claim is concerned. No redistricting, demographic hearing, new election or cancellation of an election is ordered. The separate racial claim is outside this review.

## Opinion Topology

Opinion of Stevens, joined in full by all eight other Justices.

No separate writing.

## Holdings

### Total population is a permissible measure

The Court holds that a municipality may apportion ordinary representative council districts by total resident population even when the districts have materially unequal citizen voting-age populations. Equal representation of all residents is a constitutionally permissible objective; the differing eligible electorates alone do not require replacement of that denominator or a new election.

**Authority:** The opinion of Stevens, joined in full by all eight other participating Justices, controls this rule, its explanation and its limits.

Representatives serve children and noncitizens as well as eligible voters. Counting those residents distributes representation and constituent demands on a coherent basis. Reynolds and Avery require equality in representative districting, but do not resolve every choice of population base. Burns permits a population choice in its particular setting; it does not command a citizen voting-age denominator here. The voters correctly identify differing numerical influence among eligible electorates. That difference does not establish that equal representation of residents is forbidden. No finding that both measures could or could not be equalized is needed to answer the categorical claim.

Voinovich's deviation inquiry follows selection of a permissible population measure. It cannot select citizen voting-age population as mandatory by applying the ten-percent threshold to that disputed measure. Nor does Houston's 8.63-percent total-population deviation create immunity from every constitutional claim. Department of Commerce permits constitutional review despite a range of lawful legislative choices; this judgment rests on the merits, not a political-question dismissal. Preclearance does not adjudicate the constitutional claim.

The Court neither requires total population in every setting nor decides whether a different proposed denominator would be lawful. Those alternatives and the separate racial claim are outside the accepted question. Intentional exclusion or a separately established dilution mechanism is not approved by this holding.

## Precedent Treatment

- Reynolds v. Sims and Avery v. Midland County: applied as the equality requirement for representative districts; neither becomes a command to count only eligible voters.
- Burns v. Richardson: its population-base permission is preserved, without converting permission there into a required denominator here.
- Voinovich v. Quilter: its deviation and justification inquiry remains effective within the legally relevant population measure; a deviation below ten percent is not categorical immunity.
- United States Department of Commerce v. Montana: applied to preserve judicial review; its interstate allocation holding does not displace intrastate equality.
- Easley v. Cromartie and Smallwood v. Cromartie: their racial-districting rule remains distinct and is not reconsidered in this population-base review.

## Law After Decision

Total resident population is an available constitutional base for these municipal representative districts. Future applications must still distinguish the population base from departures within it and from independently proved discrimination. No exclusive denominator for every setting is announced.

## Separate Writings

None.

## Procedure After Action

The mandate leaves the Fifth Circuit's judgment intact on the reviewed claim. No further factual or remedial work is directed on that claim.

## Source Notes

[Fifth Circuit opinion, 206 F.3d 502](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/206/206.F3d.502.98-20440.html), including its distinction between the two claims. The reported lower record supports the categorical population-base question; exact alternative-map findings and original merits filings are not supplied.

## Event

**Wharf (Holdings) Ltd. v. United International Holdings, Inc.** — No. 00-347.

Argued March 21, 2001; decided May 21, 2001. Review of the Tenth Circuit's judgment sustaining the civil jury judgment. The question is whether selling an oral option while secretly intending never to honor it constitutes deception in connection with a securities sale under section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5.

United supplied services for Wharf's cable-system bid. The jury found an actual option to acquire a ten-percent interest and a contemporaneous intention never to honor it, supported by internal memoranda. The option, rather than an unrealized later share purchase, is the security acquired. The lower court's state-law enforceability determination is an existing review premise.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

Merits decision after plenary review.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Affirmed on the reviewed securities issue, 9-0.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** None.

The Tenth Circuit's judgment is affirmed on the reviewed federal securities issue. The existing judgment is preserved within that scope. The Court awards no new damages, orders no share transfer or new trial, and does not reopen unreviewed state-law grounds. Federal compensation and state-law punitive relief retain their distinct legal sources.

## Opinion Topology

Opinion of Thomas, joined in full by all eight other Justices.

No separate writing.

## Holdings

### Deception at the sale of the option

The Court holds that a seller who obtains value by selling an actual option while concealing a contemporaneous intention never to honor it commits deception in connection with that security's sale. Oral form alone does not exclude the proved option; materiality, required culpability, legally sufficient reliance, causation and recoverable loss remain necessary to the private action.

**Authority:** The opinion of Thomas, joined in full by all eight other participating Justices, controls the rule and all qualifications below.

An option gives its purchaser a present right to choose a later purchase. A concealed decision to deny that choice misrepresents what the seller supplies now. The jury's option finding and contemporaneous memoranda distinguish this case from subsequent nonperformance. Kravitz supplies the distinction between a knowingly false securities undertaking and breach alone. O'Hagan requires an actual connection between deception and the transaction; direct deception of the option purchaser supplies it without revisiting source deception. Blue Chip Stamps requires a purchaser or seller, which the option acquisition supplies. Its evidentiary concerns do not authorize adding a writing requirement to the statute's coverage. Santa Fe excludes unfairness without deception, not the deliberate sham proved here. Central Bank's separate assistance theory is unnecessary because Wharf's own sale is the primary conduct.

The Tenth Circuit's conclusion that this agreement was enforceable under the then-applicable Colorado statute is not reopened. The Court does not decide treatment of an agreement unenforceable under state law, which is outside the accepted federal question, and does not make state enforceability an invariable additional element of section 10(b). A later breach, negligence or commercial unfairness alone proves neither initial deception nor the remaining elements. Unpresented foreign-transaction limits and the independent state-law punitive award are outside review.

## Precedent Treatment

- Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc. v. Kravitz: applied to distinguish a knowingly false securities undertaking from later breach; it supplies neither an option definition nor a universal duty to execute a transaction.
- United States v. O'Hagan: applied to require transaction-connected deception; the direct counterparty fraud here leaves its distinct source-deception dispute untouched.
- Blue Chip Stamps v. Manor Drug Stores: purchaser-seller standing is satisfied by the acquired option; no categorical writing requirement is added.
- Santa Fe Industries, Inc. v. Green: its requirement of deception beyond unfairness is preserved and satisfied by the verdict findings.
- Central Bank of Denver, N.A. v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A.: its confined affirmative-assistance action remains in force; no assistance theory is necessary for this seller's primary fraud.

## Law After Decision

Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5 reach this knowing sham option sale, whether the established option is oral or written. The ordinary private-action elements continue to separate securities fraud from contract nonperformance. Coverage of state-law-unenforceable agreements remains undecided.

## Separate Writings

None.

## Procedure After Action

The mandate leaves the reviewed securities determination in place. No damages recalculation or additional factual proceedings are directed.

## Source Notes

[Official report of the record and arguments, 532 U.S. 588](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-532/pdf/USREPORTS-532-588.pdf). The option and initial-intent findings are jury findings, not new appellate factfinding. The reported state-law enforceability determination defines the scope of review; original trial papers are not independently supplied.

## Event

**Appalachian Power Co. v. EPA** — D.C. Circuit Nos. 98-1512, 98-1536, 98-1537, 98-1538, 98-1540 and 98-1542; 208 F.3d 1015.

Submitted on the merits papers; no oral-argument date is established. Decided May 29, 2001. Review of the D.C. Circuit's judgment on consolidated petitions under Clean Air Act section 307(b). The questions concern reviewability of EPA's settled 1998 Periodic Monitoring Guidance and whether it may impose additional monitoring beyond the existing regulation without the applicable rulemaking.

EPA applied its acknowledged settled sufficiency-review directive through the Title V permit system. A statutory EPA objection prevents issuance of a state permit. The guidance required additional monitoring even where an applicable requirement already prescribed periodic testing; its concluding disclaimer did not withdraw that position.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

Merits decision after plenary review.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Affirmed; whole guidance vacatur sustained, 9-0.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** None.

The D.C. Circuit's judgment is affirmed, including immediate review and vacatur of the entire 1998 guidance. No individual permit or substitute federal monitoring requirement is ordered.

## Opinion Topology

Opinion of Scalia, joined in full by all eight other Justices.

No separate writing.

## Holdings

### 1. A completed directive with legal consequences is reviewable

The Court holds that this settled national monitoring directive is final agency action reviewable under Clean Air Act section 307(b). EPA has completed its position and implements it through the statutory permit-objection system, in which its objection prevents permit issuance; a disclaimer cannot remove those present legal consequences.

**Authority for all three holdings:** The opinion of Scalia, joined in full by all eight other participating Justices.

Bennett requires both completed agency action and legal consequences. Whitman applies that distinction to a settled Clean Air Act implementation framework before an individual sanction or plan determination. Both requirements are present here: EPA concedes its central position is settled and enforces that position through its permit authority. Expense, anticipated compliance or influence alone would not establish finality. Individual permit review does not displace the statutory route for this national action. The facial legal issue can be decided without choosing the monitoring terms of any particular permit and is ripe for that bounded review.

### 2. Guidance cannot remove the regulation's condition for added monitoring

The Court holds that section 70.6(a)(3)(i)(B) authorizes added periodic monitoring when the applicable requirement lacks periodic testing or instrumental or noninstrumental monitoring, including qualifying recordkeeping. It does not authorize universal sufficiency review that replaces already prescribed recurring monitoring merely because EPA considers it inadequate; that substantive enlargement requires the applicable rulemaking procedures.

Paragraph (A) incorporates applicable monitoring and testing, permitting streamlining only with at least equivalent compliance assurance. Paragraph (B) requires reliable, representative compliance data and terms, methods, units, averaging periods and other statistical conventions consistent with the applicable requirement; recordkeeping may suffice. Paragraph (C) requires necessary use and maintenance provisions and, where appropriate, installation provisions. Paragraph (c)(1)'s compliance-assurance requirement is expressly consistent with (a)(3). Reading assurance of compliance as unlimited authority to replace existing periodic monitoring would erase that structure and the absence trigger. Missing periodic testing, unspecified frequency and one-time startup tests remain genuine gaps that may be supplemented.

Auer and Thomas Jefferson require weight for responsible, fair and considered interpretations that fit the regulation. Christensen's regulatory-text holding forbids amendment through interpretation. EPA's expertise and practical compliance objective deserve consideration, but neither deletes this express condition. The 1992 explanation's contemplation of future rulemaking to repair deficient standards confirms that reading. The holding does not rest on the guidance label, forbid interpretive change generally, or impose notice and comment on every technical explanation. The regulation resolves the case without deciding the outer limit of Congress's delegation or the alternative challenge to the 1992 rule.

### 3. The whole intertwined guidance is set aside

The Court sustains vacatur of the entire guidance because its challenged and remaining directions are intertwined and the record gives substantial reason to doubt independent adoption of the remainder. This record-specific conclusion establishes no universal severability formula.

The supported integration finding defeats partial vacatur here. Existing emissions limits and monitoring requirements, the 1992 regulation, lawful gap-filling and independently authorized stricter state requirements remain effective. EPA may proceed through lawful future rulemaking; the Court prescribes no substitute rule, monitoring frequency or individual permit condition.

## Precedent Treatment

- Bennett v. Spear: applied through its separate completion and legal-consequence requirements; practical influence alone remains insufficient.
- Whitman v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.: applied to finality of a settled Clean Air Act framework and to preserving connected legal commands; its nondelegation holding does not decide this regulatory dispute.
- Auer v. Robbins and Thomas Jefferson University v. Shalala: preserved for fair and considered own-regulation interpretations within permissible text; neither authorizes amendment.
- Christensen v. Harris County: its regulatory-text holding supplies the limit on interpretive enlargement. Its distinct treatment of a nonbinding advisory letter is not converted into a categorical rule about all guidance.
- Solid Waste Agency of Northern Cook County v. United States Army Corps of Engineers: its authority inquiry is consistent with this result; it supplies no general rule invalidating informal administration.

## Law After Decision

National agency guidance may be reviewed when completed action has the required legal consequences under the governing review statute. EPA's existing monitoring regulation permits its specified gap-filling, but the 1998 guidance cannot supply a universal replacement-monitoring duty. The guidance is vacated as a whole; existing law and lawful future rulemaking remain available.

## Separate Writings

None.

## Procedure After Action

The mandate preserves whole-document vacatur. EPA and permitting authorities remain governed by the existing emissions and monitoring requirements. Particular permit applications and any later valid rule must be assessed on their own legal grounds and records.

## Source Notes

[D.C. Circuit opinion, 208 F.3d 1015](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/208/208.F3d.1015.98-1540.98-1542.98-1538.98-1537.98-1536.html), including its regulatory discussion and integration determination. The transcription's omitted graphical addendum supplies no ground used here; the body and notes establish the material regulatory conditions and permitting operation. No particular permit's complete record is supplied.

## Event

**Arkansas v. Sullivan** — No. 00-262.

Decided May 29, 2001, on Arkansas's petition for certiorari; no separate oral argument. The Arkansas Supreme Court upheld suppression in a pending prosecution on a federal pretext rationale and claimed authority to read the Federal Constitution more protectively than this Court. Review reaches that finally settled federal issue because it otherwise could evade review despite either eventual conviction or acquittal.

An officer stopped Sullivan for speeding and a windshield violation, recognized him from narcotics intelligence, observed a hatchet, and arrested him on traffic, documentation and weapon grounds. A search described as an inventory disclosed drugs. The complete policy and suppression record do not establish every separate custody, impoundment or inventory predicate on this review.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

Certiorari granted; reasoned summary merits disposition.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Certiorari granted; judgment vacated and remanded, 9-0 on merits.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** None.

The Arkansas Supreme Court's judgment is vacated and the case remanded for proceedings consistent with these federal rules and any actual preserved independent grounds. The Court directs neither admission of the evidence nor a particular outcome in the prosecution.

## Opinion Topology

Per curiam opinion joined by all nine Justices.

No separate writing.

## Holdings

### 1. Federal motive rules do not dispense with separate authority for each intrusion

The Court holds that an ordinary traffic stop supported by objective probable cause is not invalid solely because the officer also investigates another offense. State courts must follow this Court's controlling federal constitutional interpretation; greater protection must rest on an actual independent state-law ground, not a different reading of the same federal rule.

**Authority for all three holdings:** The per curiam opinion is joined by all nine participating Justices.

Whren supplies the ordinary stop rule while preserving independent limits on detention and search. Oregon v. Hass distinguishes a state's own protections from its duty to follow controlling federal law. The lower court's motive-only federal rationale and asserted power to disregard that hierarchy cannot stand. The reported federal basis, rather than an invented independent state ground, supports review; Quarles supplies the analogous otherwise-evading-review route. Correcting that legal error does not establish the validity of every subsequent act.

### 2. Added fine-only custody requires a supported enforcement function

The Court applies Atwater: added station transport and confinement for a known fine-only criminal offense must reasonably serve a supported actual enforcement function. Probable cause alone does not justify that added restraint; identification, appearance, safety, a continuing violation, evidence protection and necessary processing are nonexclusive possible functions, not presumed facts.

Atwater extends Ricci's actual-enforcement inquiry to this criminal setting. It requires neither the least restrictive means nor a citation whenever one is conceivable. Actual offense classifications and supported arrest grounds matter where relevant. This record does not conclusively establish that every asserted offense was fine-only, that the hatchet supplied a valid weapons predicate, that identification failed, or that processing required the restraint. Those matters remain for the proper court on the actual preserved record. The Court neither finds the custody unlawful nor treats investigative interest as an independent source of arrest authority.

### 3. Impoundment and inventory require their own lawful grounds

The Court holds that impoundment and a claimed inventory must independently satisfy their lawful authority, caretaking purpose and standardized-practice requirements. Investigative interest does not invalidate every otherwise lawful act, but a search that is an investigatory ruse fails the inventory exception itself; a policy label does not establish genuine compliance.

Bertine and Wells supply the inventory conditions that Whren preserves. Knowles keeps a search's justification distinct from the antecedent encounter and requires actual lawful custody for its search-incident rationale. Drugs found later cannot supply antecedent probable cause. The reported pretext finding does not necessarily decide every inventory condition under the correct rule. Remand must address actual preserved custody, impoundment, inventory and independent state-law grounds. The Court orders no new hearing, speculative reopening of settled facts, admission or suppression on a newly assumed ground, conviction, acquittal, or unsupported search-incident result.

## Precedent Treatment

- Whren v. United States: applied to reject a motive-only objection to the ordinary probable-cause stop while preserving independent detention and inventory limits.
- Ricci v. Village of Arlington Heights and Atwater v. City of Lago Vista: applied to require a supported enforcement function for added fine-only custody; neither becomes a universal citation duty or a final finding about Sullivan's custody.
- Colorado v. Bertine and Florida v. Wells: preserved as independent authority, caretaking and standardized-practice limits on inventories.
- Knowles v. Iowa: applied to keep search authority distinct and to reject automatic search consequences from a lawful stop.
- Oregon v. Hass: applied to the state court's federal-law duty while preserving genuine independent state protections.
- New York v. Quarles: supplies the analogous jurisdictional treatment of a finally settled federal issue otherwise likely to evade review.

## Law After Decision

The ordinary stop, added custody, impoundment and inventory remain distinct constitutional inquiries. A federal pretext theory cannot replace them, and probable cause for the stop cannot supply their missing predicates. The remand must apply the actual-function custody rule and genuine inventory requirements.

## Separate Writings

None.

## Procedure After Action

Certiorari is granted. The state court must reconsider the challenged suppression disposition under the separately stated standards on the actual preserved record. No new evidentiary hearing or final admissibility determination is mandated.

## Source Notes

[Official report of the proceedings, 532 U.S. 769](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-532/pdf/USREPORTS-532-769.pdf). Its reported federal rationale and record support the bounded legal correction. The complete operative inventory policy and suppression record would be needed for a final application not made here.

## Event

**Atkinson Trading Co. v. Shirley** — No. 00-454.

Argued March 27, 2001; decided May 29, 2001. Review of the Tenth Circuit's approval of the Navajo Nation's hotel occupancy tax after tribal administrative and judicial review and federal district-court proceedings. The question concerns nonmember guests at a nonmember hotel on non-Indian fee land within the reservation.

The hotel sits on non-Indian fee land; expansion of the reservation boundary did not change that title. The asserted tax falls on nonmember guest transactions. General tribal services and a federal Indian-trader license are offered as grounds for authority, but no tribal lease or specific treaty or statute authorizing this tax is identified.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

Merits decision after plenary review.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Reversed and remanded, 9-0.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** None.

The Tenth Circuit's judgment is reversed and the case remanded for lawful relief consistent with the absence of authority over the challenged transactions. No automatic refund or unrestricted injunction is awarded.

## Opinion Topology

Opinion of Stone-Zsela, joined in full by all eight other Justices.

Kennedy also filed a concurrence, joined by Souter and Thomas, stating an additional noncontrolling proposition while joining the Court opinion in full.

## Holdings

### 1. Tribal tax power over these nonmember fee-land transactions requires an applicable source or exception

The Court holds that Montana governs this occupancy tax on nonmember guest transactions at a nonmember hotel on non-Indian fee land. In the absence of superior federal authorization, the Tribe must establish a qualifying consensual relationship connected to the tax or the conduct's qualifying threat or direct effect on tribal political integrity, economic security, health or welfare; neither exception is established here.

**Authority for both holdings:** The opinion of Stone-Zsela, joined in full by all eight other participating Justices. The separate three-Justice view about other land settings is not part of this holding.

Merrion recognizes sovereign taxing power rather than merely a private owner's exclusion right, but its tribal-land lease setting does not supply unlimited authority over these fee-land transactions. Strate requires the actual source, meaningful relationship and separate tribal-effect inquiries; territorial balancing cannot replace them. The reservation boundary remains intact and does not decide every question of governing power.

The first exception requires a meaningful connection between the tax and commercial dealings, contracts, leases or similar arrangements with the Tribe or its members. General police, fire and medical benefits do not supply consent for all nonmember transactions. A federal trader license is not a tribal agreement authorizing this levy. Even considering the operator's business contacts, a connection in one area does not authorize an unrelated tax on guests. Those contacts fail here, so the Court need not decide whether operator contacts could ever justify a tax whose incidence falls on guests. Express tax consent or privity is not universally required, and no properly grounded fee for a particular service is adjudicated.

Under the second exception, ordinary revenue needs, service provision and business presence alone do not establish the required threat or direct tribal effect. The Court denies neither the value of services nor their real consequences; it adds no quantified threshold and does not reduce every limb of the exception to one formula. Taxes on members, qualifying tribal leases or transactions, and authority supplied by federal law are not invalidated.

### 2. The absence of tax authority controls relief without creating a waiver or refund action

The Court reverses approval of the challenged tax and returns the case for conforming lawful relief. Declaratory and prospective relief must fit the actual claims, lawful parties, immunity and ordinary equitable requirements; reversal does not itself create a refund cause of action or satisfy every injunction predicate.

Strate preserves the difference between governing power and amenability to suit. C & L enforces a clear, integrated contractual waiver involving arbitration and judicial confirmation; no such undertaking appears here. Commerce, location or receipt of services supplies neither unlimited taxing power nor an implied universal waiver. The returned court must give the no-authority ruling its proper effect under the applicable relief requirements, without inventing a monetary claim or a new tax arrangement.

## Precedent Treatment

- Montana v. United States: applied to this nonmember activity on non-Indian fee land, including both exceptions and their distinct conditions.
- Merrion v. Jicarilla Apache Tribe: its sovereign-taxing principle is preserved; its tribal-land lease relationship does not establish authority over the challenged fee-land guest transactions.
- Strate v. A-1 Contractors: applied to source, meaningful nexus, tribal effect and separate relief requirements; title is not converted into disestablishment or elimination of sovereign taxation.
- C & L Enterprises, Inc. v. Citizen Band Potawatomi Indian Tribe of Oklahoma: its specific integrated contractual waiver is preserved; location, commerce and services do not establish that waiver here.

## Law After Decision

The challenged occupancy tax exceeds tribal authority under the applicable fee-land rule. A relationship must actually connect to the levy, and the second exception requires its own qualifying tribal effect. Relief remains subject to its independent legal prerequisites. The Court announces no controlling all-land Montana rule.

## Separate Writings

**Kennedy, concurring, joined by Souter and Thomas.** The concurrence joins the Court's fee-land holding and remedy in full. It would begin with Montana's general nonmember principle even where the activity occurs on tribal or trust land. Land status remains highly relevant to whether a consensual relationship or qualifying tribal effect establishes an exception, but it does not displace the initial inquiry. Montana supplies the general principle; Strate's actual holding did not decide the opposite rule for every tribal-land setting. This additional view explains how the three Justices would approach a broader question; it neither adjudicates a different transaction nor commands a majority. No other Justice joins that additional proposition.

## Procedure After Action

The returned court must conform its judgment to the tax-authority holding while addressing actual parties, immunity and equitable prerequisites for the relief sought. Tribal exhaustion is complete. The Court orders no new tax arrangement.

## Source Notes

[Official report of the record and positions, 532 U.S. 645](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-532/pdf/USREPORTS-532-645.pdf). The reported land status, transaction incidence, license and services support the decision. No original ordinance or additional factual record is independently supplied.

## Event

Becker v. Montgomery, No. 00-6374. Argued April 16, 2001; decided May 29, 2001. On certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, which dismissed a timely civil appeal because the notice lacked a handwritten signature. The question is whether an otherwise sufficient timely notice may be signed promptly after the filer is notified of the omission, even after the appeal period expires.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No nonparticipation is reported.

## Public Action

The Court reverses and remands.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Reversed and remanded, 9-0. **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No Justice opposes the disposition. The Sixth Circuit must provide the prescribed prompt-correction opportunity, give effect to the corrected notice as the existing timely filing and restore ordinary appellate review. This judgment determines no underlying constitutional violation, exhaustion defense, pleading issue or damages.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joins | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Every other Justice; full coalition: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Entire rule, application, judgment and remedy |

## Holdings

### The required signature may be supplied through the rule's express cure

**Holding and operative rule:** An omitted signature on an otherwise sufficient, timely notice of appeal is cured by promptly supplying the signature after the omission is called to the filer's attention. Correction may occur after the appeal period because it completes the existing timely filing; a court may not dismiss for that omission without allowing the correction Civil Rule 11(a) provides.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court; the complete coalition is Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt the rule, explanation and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The notice must be signed, and its signature omission must receive the cure the same rule prescribes. Appellate Rule 1(a)(2) makes the district-court procedure applicable to this filing. Rule 3 distinguishes timely invocation from other procedural steps; its identifying requirements remain satisfied here. Smith v. Barry supplies the distinction between sufficient timely notice and defects in its form, while Rule 11 supplies the express correction absent from Smith's particular question.

Becker identified the appellant, judgment and appellate court within the required time. Docketing and briefing proceeded without warning of the omission. His prompt correction after notification therefore completes that notice; it does not institute a new late appeal. A filer may sign the paper already on file or furnish a signed duplicate. Requiring the signature while refusing the prescribed cure would detach two parts of the same governing rule.

The typed name on this paper form does not alone satisfy its signature requirement. Rule 3(c)(2)'s treatment of a pro se signer identifies the parties encompassed by that notice; it does not impose a different signature-correction deadline. Its spouse-and-minor-children provision applies only to persons who are parties and yields to a clearly contrary notice. No additional appellant or right of representation is decided here. The cure applies to litigants governed by the same rules, including those represented by counsel. It preserves both prompt action after notification and the original time and content requirements. Electronic execution under an authorized local rule is a different question outside this paper-signature dispute. The underlying exhaustion and pleading issues are outside the grant.

**Precedent treatment:** Smith v. Barry is applied to timely objective notice and extended to this signature setting only through Rule 11's express cure; no general power to excuse a missing timely notice is adopted.

## Precedent Treatment

- Smith v. Barry, 502 U.S. 244: applies the distinction between timely objective notice and informal defects; the present signature result rests on the specific cure provision and leaves mandatory time and identifying content intact.

## Law After Decision

A timely notice that supplies the required identification remains correctable for an omitted signature even after the appeal period, provided the filer acts promptly once notified. Rule 11 requires both authentication and its prescribed opportunity to cure. Smith remains controlling without alteration of its timing or content requirements. This is a new signature application, not a prisoner-only exception or an equitable extension of appellate jurisdiction.

## Separate Writings

No separate opinion was filed.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Sixth Circuit for recognition of the prompt signature correction and continued appellate proceedings on the reinstated timely appeal. Failure to act promptly after notification remains governed by Rule 11; it is not an established failure on this record.

## Source Notes

Contemporaneous Civil Rules 5 and 11 and Appellate Rules 1, 3 and 4 appear in the [2000 United States Code rules appendix](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2000-title28/html/USCODE-2000-title28-app-federalru-dup1.htm). The reported filing and appellate proceedings appear at 532 U.S. 757-768. The present decision concerns the omitted signature on the timely paper notice, not an electronic filing under a different authorized rule.

## Event

Booth v. Churner, No. 99-1964. Argued March 20, 2001; decided May 29, 2001. On certiorari to the Third Circuit, which affirmed dismissal without prejudice for failure to exhaust a prison grievance process. The question is whether an accessible process capable of responding to the complaint remains available under amended 42 U.S.C. section 1997e(a) when it cannot award the money damages sought.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No nonparticipation is reported.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the judgment.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Affirmed, 9-0. **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No Justice opposes the disposition. Dismissal without prejudice remains effective. Booth must complete the actually available grievance process before pursuing the covered damages action, subject to lawful lower-court consideration of completion and timing. No constitutional merits, damages amount or prospective compliance order is adjudicated.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joins | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Every other Justice; full coalition: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Complete availability rule, application and affirmance |

## Holdings

### A responsive administrative process remains available although it cannot award money

**Holding and operative rule:** A prisoner bringing a prison-conditions action governed by amended section 1997e(a) must exhaust an actually accessible administrative grievance process authorized to take responsive action concerning the complaint, even when that process cannot award the money damages demanded. Choosing damages alone does not remove the statutory condition.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer adopt the entire holding, explanation and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress required exhaustion of available administrative avenues before a covered action and removed the former qualifications concerning adequacy and effectiveness. The command therefore turns on a process capable of responding to the complaint, not on a match between administrative powers and every remedy a plaintiff requests in court. Investigation and corrective response remain possible here. They confirm the meaning of the enacted process requirement; practical usefulness alone would not create exhaustion.

McCarthy v. Madigan makes the statutory scheme decisive. Its damages-only result concerned a general BOP procedure where Congress imposed no exhaustion command. The new statute changes that premise for the actions it covers without overruling McCarthy's surviving no-mandate rule. McNeil v. United States reinforces that an enacted prior-process requirement cannot be displaced by a judicial assessment that compliance would be unnecessary in the particular litigation. A general futility exception based solely on unavailable compensation would restore the discarded adequacy qualification.

Booth did not complete the accessible review steps. His allegations do not establish that officials obstructed them or that the institution lacked all responsive authority. The Third Circuit's without-prejudice dismissal therefore stands. The accepted question does not decide whether excessive force generally falls within prison conditions, which the lower court separately addressed, or the constitutional merits. A nonexistent, wholly powerless or actually inaccessible process is outside the established record; this decision does not supply a comprehensive rule of unavailability or procedural default. Section 1997e(c)(2) permits dismissal before exhaustion of a claim facially frivolous, malicious, failing to state a claim, or seeking money from an immune defendant. That distinct permission does not entitle Booth to nonexhausted merits adjudication.

**Precedent treatment:** McCarthy's statutory-primacy principle is applied and its earlier absence-of-command damages holding distinguished; McNeil is applied to the force of Congress's expressly required administrative sequence.

## Precedent Treatment

- McCarthy v. Madigan, 503 U.S. 140: preserves statutory primacy and the former no-mandate damages-only holding; the PLRA supplies the command missing there, and this case construes its open availability term.
- McNeil v. United States, 508 U.S. 106: applies the principle that an express administrative precondition governs according to its terms; its distinct FTCA exceptions and timing rules are not transplanted into section 1997e.

## Law After Decision

For covered actions, absence of administrative power to award the requested damages does not make an accessible process unavailable if it can respond meaningfully to the complaint. The PLRA exhaustion command is given its own statutory scope; McCarthy continues to govern its surviving no-mandate setting. Section 1997e(c)(2) retains its separate permissive screening rule. No new constitutional merits rule, general procedural-default rule or limitations rule is adopted.

## Separate Writings

No separate opinion was filed.

## Procedure After Action

The federal dismissal without prejudice remains in place. A subsequent effort to pursue the covered action requires completion of the actually available administrative process and ordinary judicial consideration of any properly presented completion and timing questions. The decision makes no tolling ruling and awards no damages or injunction.

## Event

Buckhannon Board & Care Home, Inc. v. West Virginia Department of Health and Human Resources, No. 99-1848. Argued February 27, 2001; decided May 29, 2001. On certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, which affirmed denial of fees under the Fair Housing Amendments Act and Americans with Disabilities Act after the merits action became moot. The question is whether those statutes exclude prevailing-party status solely because a defendant voluntarily provides the requested relief before a merits judgment or court-approved consent decree.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No nonparticipation is reported.

## Public Action

The Court vacates the fee judgment and remands.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Vacated and remanded, 5-4. **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, who would affirm the fee denial. The lower courts must determine legal substantiality, actual enduring benefit and material causal contribution, then determine a reasonable fee if statutory eligibility is established. No fee is awarded here. The underlying mootness dismissal remains intact.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joins | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Other members of the full coalition: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Both holdings, vacatur and remand |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas | Judicially sanctioned material-alteration requirement; affirmance of fee denial |

## Holdings

### I. Proven litigation success may establish prevailing status without a decree

**Holding and operative rule:** Under the FHAA and ADA fee provisions, a plaintiff prevails when a legally substantial action materially causes the defendant to provide an enduring, concrete benefit sought in that action. The plaintiff bears the causal showing; timing alone, favorable judicial language, an unrelated policy change or a response to a groundless claim does not establish prevailing status.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, supported in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls this holding and Holding II. The four dissenters join neither rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The statutes reward litigation success through reasonable fees to a prevailing party. They do not expressly restrict success to a judgment or consent decree. A substantial suit may secure the concrete relief it seeks when the defendant voluntarily changes the challenged conduct. The absence of a decree does not undo that success, but the plaintiff must prove that the action materially contributed to the relief and that the benefit reflects a legally substantial claim.

Hewitt v. Helms rejects favorable language without personal relief; Rhodes v. Stewart rejects a declaration that benefits neither plaintiff. Neither decides this different case of concrete relief caused by litigation. Texas State Teachers Association v. Garland separates meaningful success from its degree, and Farrar v. Hobby identifies an enforceable nominal judgment as a sufficient route while keeping status distinct from amount. Those decisions do not make their judicial-relief examples exclusive.

The competing interpretation would allow unilateral delivery of the requested relief to defeat fee eligibility for the success the enactments make attainable. That consequence confirms the statutory reading; it is not an independent equitable source of fees. Concerns about weak suits and speculative causation require genuine proof, not categorical exclusion. Courts may examine contemporaneous communications, legislative records, testimony, admissions and the course of litigation. Neither temporal sequence alone nor a statistical study is required or sufficient. A defendant need not admit liability to reform voluntarily.

Here the categorical denial prevented the necessary findings. Repeal following suit does not itself establish causation, legal substance or benefit. Those factual predicates must be determined on remand. This holding governs the two fee provisions presented; a similar application under other prevailing-party language depends on that enactment's text and structure, not automatic extension. The moot merits action is not revived, and the former residency requirement's validity is not decided.

**Precedent treatment:** Hewitt and Rhodes are distinguished by the required concrete personal benefit; Garland's status/degree distinction and Farrar's status/amount distinction remain in force. None had foreclosed this catalyst route.

### II. Eligibility does not itself establish a fee award or amount

**Holding and operative rule:** If the plaintiffs prove the prevailing-party predicates on remand, the court must separately determine a reasonable statutory fee through an explained assessment of actual success, excluding unrelated unsuccessful work. Prevailing status does not guarantee a positive amount, and neither a mechanical damages ratio nor automatic compensation for all work supplies the answer.

**Controlling explanation:** Farrar v. Hobby requires an explained Hensley assessment when correcting an eligibility error leaves reasonableness unresolved. The same distinction governs here. Success produced through voluntary compliance must be proved and measured, rather than presumed from the suit's asserted importance or the resources spent. The court may reduce a requested award, including to zero where the explained circumstances justify it. No existing award is reinstated, and this Court fixes no amount. Findings under that governing standard are the ordinary remand work, not an unresolved legal entitlement.

**Precedent treatment:** Farrar and Hensley v. Eckerhart are applied to the separate reasonable-amount inquiry; their limits remain binding after catalyst eligibility is recognized.

## Precedent Treatment

- Hewitt v. Helms, 482 U.S. 755: distinguishes favorable legal language without actual personal relief; its open catalyst question is answered here only on the stated proof conditions.
- Rhodes v. Stewart, 488 U.S. 1: distinguishes declaratory relief that did not benefit the plaintiffs from an enduring concrete benefit caused by the suit.
- Texas State Teachers Association v. Garland Independent School District, 489 U.S. 782: applies its separation of prevailing status from degree of success; it does not make the judicial route considered there exclusive.
- Farrar v. Hobby, 506 U.S. 103: preserves enforceable nominal relief as a sufficient eligibility route and applies the separate explained amount inquiry; no categorical decree requirement or automatic zero-fee rule is imported.
- Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424: applies actual success, exclusion of unrelated unsuccessful work and explained reasonable-fee discretion.
- Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw, 528 U.S. 167: supplies no catalyst holding and is not extended to keep the moot merits claim alive for fees.

## Law After Decision

The FHAA and ADA now recognize a confined catalyst route to prevailing-party status: a legally substantial action must materially cause an enduring concrete benefit sought in the suit. Proof of those predicates and the separate reasonable-fee inquiry remain mandatory; voluntary relief, mootness or expenditure alone is insufficient. Prior judgments and consent decrees remain valid routes to prevailing status, while their absence is no categorical bar under the provisions decided. Farrar's reasonable-amount rule remains unchanged. The underlying merits controversy remains moot.

## Separate Writings

**O'Connor, dissenting, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** The dissent would require a judicially sanctioned material alteration of the parties' legal relationship and affirm the fee denial. An enforceable merits judgment or court-approved consent decree supplies that change; purely voluntary conduct does not, even if the lawsuit helped produce it. Judicial approval and enforceability distinguish those recognized routes from a private resolution.

The dissent reads the litigation term prevailing party through that requirement. Garland's material-alteration principle and Farrar's enforceable payment obligation support the threshold; the actual controlling Farrar amount rule remains binding once eligibility exists. Hewitt's rejection of favorable words without relief supplies no earlier catalyst holding. In the dissent's view, the majority's practical success does not establish the legally authorized success these fee provisions require.

Causation and legal-substance proceedings after mootness may prolong litigation and reward reform induced by the costs of defending a weak claim. Those considerations reinforce the dissent's statutory interpretation, rather than independently authorizing denial. The dissent recognizes the majority's answer that courts can take evidence and confine awards to substantial claims, but considers that answer insufficient to replace judicially sanctioned legal relief. It neither finds repeal unrelated to the suit nor holds the former residency law lawful. It need not classify every settlement device or interlocutory order to reject fees on this voluntary-change record. Affirmance would leave both the fee denial and moot merits dismissal in place.

## Procedure After Action

The Fourth Circuit must return the fee question for findings under the stated eligibility standard and, if appropriate, an explained reasonable-fee determination. The parties may litigate the actual causal and benefit evidence relevant to that collateral issue. The underlying mootness judgment is undisturbed; no liability finding, fee award or positive minimum follows from this decision.

## Source Notes

The fee provisions are 42 U.S.C. sections 3613(c)(2) and 12205. The underlying proceedings and statutory setting are reported at [532 U.S. 598-644](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-532/pdf/USREPORTS-532-598.pdf). The record establishes repeal during litigation, not that the action caused repeal or that any particular fee is reasonable. Those findings remain for the lower court.

## Event

Harris v. Garner, on review of 216 F.3d 970, Eleventh Circuit No. 98-8899. Decided May 29, 2001, following full-merits submission. The Eleventh Circuit, sitting en banc, held that confinement at commencement controls 42 U.S.C. section 1997e(e) and required dismissal without prejudice of the six released plaintiffs' claims barred solely by that provision. The question is whether release before judgment, including a supplemental pleading recording release, removes the physical-injury restriction from the existing action for mental or emotional injury suffered in custody.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at full-merits submission and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No nonparticipation is reported.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the en banc judgment on the temporal question.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Affirmed on the accepted temporal question, 5-4. **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. **Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, who would reverse the categorical foreclosure of supplementation and remand. The en banc direction to dismiss without prejudice the six released plaintiffs' claims barred solely by section 1997e(e) remains effective. Requests outside that coverage are not newly dismissed, and the other five plaintiffs receive no additional relief from the six plaintiffs' release.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joins | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Other members of the full coalition: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas | Filing-time rule, rejection of release-based cure and affirmance with the existing without-prejudice direction |
| Dissent | Stevens | Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Ordinary discretionary Rule 15(d) consideration after release; limited reversal and remand |

## Holdings

### Confinement at commencement controls the covered action despite later release

**Holding and operative rule:** Confinement when a federal civil action is brought determines section 1997e(e)'s application to claims for mental or emotional injury suffered in custody without the required prior showing of physical injury. Subsequent release and a supplemental pleading recording release do not cure that deficiency in the existing action; dismissal resting solely on it must remain without prejudice to a permissible post-release action and is no adjudication of the alleged misconduct.

**Authority:** Scalia's Opinion of the Court, supported in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. These five directly adopt the rule, its complete injury-specific scope and the remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The statute selects bringing the action as the relevant event. Its definition identifies who is a prisoner; it does not move that event to judgment. These six plaintiffs were confined when they commenced this action. Release changes their present status but not the legal consequence Congress attached to commencement.

Rule 15(d) permits supplementation on motion, reasonable notice and just terms, including where the original pleading was defective. It can record a later event; it cannot alter the statute's selected historical condition. Semtek preserves that boundary between procedural rules and substantive statutory consequences. McNeil v. United States likewise requires attention to an enacted prior condition rather than the amount of litigation that follows. Mathews v. Diaz allowed later-application supplementation under the benefits statute there; it does not establish that every commencement condition is curable. Its separately waived exhaustion issue supplies no waiver here.

Avoiding duplicated litigation is a substantial practical argument, but it supplies neither an ordinary release-based cure nor a special exception after unusual investment of judicial resources. The statutory trigger does not change when more work has been done. This conclusion assumes no improper litigant motive and does not determine the disputed procedural adequacy of the actual supplemental paper: even adequate supplementation recording release would not cure this defect.

The appellate court's without-prejudice direction respects the restriction's limited operation. The Court neither determines the alleged shakedown violations nor imposes a single bar on every monetary claim. General treatment of nominal, punitive, prospective or independent economic relief, the physical-injury threshold in other cases and the statute's constitutional validity are outside the accepted temporal question. Limitations, tolling and preclusion in a new post-release action require claims and procedural facts not presented here; no later action is declared timely or successful.

**Precedent treatment:** McNeil and Semtek are applied to preserve the enacted condition against procedural alteration; Mathews is distinguished by its particular application requirement and separate exhaustion waiver. Artuz and Randell do not already decide this temporal rule.

## Precedent Treatment

- McNeil v. United States, 508 U.S. 106: applies the force of an enacted precommencement condition and rejects litigation progress as a source of cure; the FTCA's separate administrative sequence and exceptions are not transplanted wholesale.
- Semtek International Inc. v. Lockheed Martin Corp.: applies the boundary preventing procedural rules from altering statutory substantive consequences.
- Mathews v. Diaz, 426 U.S. 67: distinguishes later satisfaction of its benefits-application condition from the statutory past event controlling this action; its separate exhaustion waiver establishes no waiver here.
- Artuz v. Bennett: preserves provision-specific examination of filing conditions and relief restrictions without classifying this provision as jurisdictional or resolving its temporal scope in advance.
- Randell v. Johnson: preserves its bounded loss-of-custody-review rule and supplies no release-alone exception to this express PLRA restriction.

## Law After Decision

For claims within section 1997e(e), the existing action's commencement during confinement fixes the temporal restriction; later release recorded in a supplemental pleading does not remove it. A dismissal based solely on that restriction does not adjudicate the underlying conduct and must not permanently bar an otherwise permissible post-release action. This new temporal holding supplies no universal classification of monetary remedies, physical-injury threshold, jurisdictional rule or timeliness decision for a later suit.

## Separate Writings

**Stevens, dissenting, joined by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.** The dissent accepts that the covered action was defective when filed during confinement, but would allow ordinary consideration of a supplemental pleading after release removes the personal disability. Rule 15(d) permits later-event supplementation even where the original pleading was defective, subject to proper motion, reasonable notice, just terms and the district court's discretion. The statute does not, in the dissent's view, foreclose that route once these plaintiffs are no longer confined.

Mathews v. Diaz illustrates later satisfaction of a personal application qualification; its distinct exhaustion waiver is not necessary to that comparison. McNeil protects a different interest: the administrative opportunity Congress required before an FTCA suit cannot be restored after a premature filing. Recording release would not eliminate such an administrative sequence here or create a substantive damages right. Semtek's rulemaking boundary remains respected because supplementation operates within the dissent's reading of the statute. Artuz likewise requires examination of the particular condition rather than reliance on a general label.

The dissent recognizes the majority's argument that commencement is a selected historical event, but considers dismissal and a second filing unnecessary where ordinary lawful supplementation can reflect the removed disability. It does not adopt a special requirement that the case be rare or that dismissal would manifestly waste resources. Litigation progress may inform ordinary discretion; it supplies neither a mandatory threshold nor proof that remand would be futile.

These Justices would reverse only the categorical foreclosure of supplementation for the six released plaintiffs and remand for the actual motion, notice, terms and permission questions, together with ordinary remaining claims and defenses. They would not deem the supplement valid, compel leave, establish physical injury, adjudicate the alleged misconduct or award damages. The separate disposition of the other five plaintiffs and questions outside the accepted injury-specific issue would remain undisturbed.

## Procedure After Action

The en banc judgment remains effective on the temporal issue, including its remand for dismissal without prejudice of the six released plaintiffs' claims barred solely by section 1997e(e). No new remand for discretionary release-based cure is ordered. An independently filed post-release case, if any, would require its own limitations, preclusion and merits analysis; this judgment supplies no outcome for it.

## Event

NLRB v. Kentucky River Community Care, Inc., No. 99-1815. Argued February 21, 2001; decided May 29, 2001. On certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, which refused enforcement of the Board's bargaining order insofar as the unit included six registered nurses. Review encompasses the burden of proving supervisory status, the meaning of independent judgment, and application of those standards to the six nurses.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No nonparticipation is reported.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the challenged supervisory-status ruling and remands for enforcement consistent with the Board's employee-status determination.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** The governing rules and enforcement disposition have the distinct coalitions below.

| Component | Disposition and remedy | Supporting | Other position |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supervisory-exclusion burden and governing functional criteria | Reject the Sixth Circuit's contrary allocation and construction; sustain the rules stated in Parts I and II, 9-0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | No opposition to these rules |

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joins | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I and II | Breyer | Every other Justice; full coalition: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Exclusion-proponent burden and functional supervisory criteria |

## Holdings

### I. The party asserting supervisory exclusion must establish it

**Holding and operative rule:** The party asserting that employees fall within the NLRA's supervisory exclusion bears the burden of establishing that status. In this derivative refusal-to-bargain proceeding, the employer's challenge to the certified unit does not convert supervisory status into an unfair-labor-practice element the General Counsel must disprove.

**Authority:** Part I of Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined at this level by all nine Justices: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The same unanimous coalition controls Part II. Part III has the distinct five-Justice coalition identified below.

**Controlling explanation:** Supervisory status excludes workers from the Act's otherwise applicable employee protection. The party invoking that exclusion must show the delegated authority that supports it. The allocation follows the issue asserted, not a categorical preference for one litigant: an employer, union or General Counsel asserting the exclusion must establish it. The employer here also possesses the relevant information about its delegation of authority.

Greenwich Collieries preserves the persuasion burden on the proponent of an order while distinguishing genuine exceptions and defenses. The General Counsel retains the burden of proving the elements of the unfair labor practice; the supervisory exception does not become such an element merely because the employer refused bargaining to obtain review of the representation decision. The Sixth Circuit's contrary allocation is therefore rejected. The Court adopts no new quantum of proof, presumption or general exception to the agency's proof obligations.

**Precedent treatment:** Greenwich Collieries is applied to preserve the distinction between the proponent's ordinary persuasion burden and proof of a statutory exclusion.

### II. Ordinary professional direction may be distinguished from independent supervisory authority

**Holding and operative rule:** The Board may treat ordinary professional or technical direction within employer-prescribed standards as insufficient to establish supervisory status unless the proponent proves authority in the employer's interest to perform a listed supervisory function using independent judgment beyond routine or clerical action. Any one function may suffice, even if rarely exercised; professional skill neither establishes supervision nor categorically prevents professional judgment from qualifying as independent judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Health Care & Retirement permits a functional distinction between skilled care and genuine supervisory authority. The Act protects professional employees whose ordinary work requires judgment while separately excluding supervisors. Treating every skilled instruction to an assistant as supervisory independence would disregard that distinction. Conversely, excluding all professional judgment would disregard the supervisory definition. The Board must examine the authority actually delegated.

The listed functions are hiring, transfer, suspension, layoff, recall, promotion, discharge, assignment, reward, discipline, responsible direction, grievance adjustment and effective recommendation of such action. Assignment or responsible direction can suffice without hiring, firing or disciplinary power. The question concerns possession of genuine authority, not merely how frequently it was exercised. Titles, credentials, highest on-site rank, patient-care responsibility and the absence of a manager from the premises do not themselves answer it; continuing off-site managerial authority is relevant but not automatically decisive.

Health Care & Retirement supplies a permissible construction, not a compulsory rule for every future Board or an occupational immunity. Holly Farms requires independent respect for the statutory boundary and review of the actual functional rationale. The Court reaffirms that framework rather than replacing it with either automatic professional inclusion or automatic exclusion.

**Precedent treatment:** Health Care & Retirement is reaffirmed in its complete functional scope; Holly Farms is applied to the statutory boundary and actual agency rationale.

### III. The supported employee classification must be sustained on this record

**Holding and operative rule:** When the Board applies the permissible functional distinction and its actual findings are supported on the whole record, a reviewing court must sustain that employee-status determination rather than replace it with its preferred classification. Here the employer did not establish the six nurses' required independent supervisory authority, and their inclusion in the unit and the existing bargaining order must be given effect.

**Authority:** Part III of Breyer's Opinion of the Court is joined by the other members of the five-Justice coalition: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas withhold assent from this application and remedy, while joining Parts I and II.

**Controlling explanation:** The director did more than invoke the nurses' professional duties. Prescribed staffing levels, voluntary and established call-list replacement procedures, inability to compel attendance under threat of discipline, independent managerial investigation of ordinary reports and lack of effective personnel recommendations supplied a discernible account of their actual authority. The director considered the conflicting building-supervisor memoranda and treated obtaining coverage as the demonstrated added function. Treatment plans, employer standards and management's continuing availability further informed that assessment.

The employer's evidence about interunit reassignment, reporting refusal and send-home authority was material, as was the nurses' on-site position. But it did not require the reviewing court to replace the agency's supported assessment of the delegation. The conclusion rests on the findings read together, not on nonexercise alone, lack of hiring power or a rule that professional judgment can never be independent. Describing some duties as medical services does not erase the preceding functional analysis.

Allentown requires the lawful announced standard to govern and the whole record to support the agency's finding; it does not authorize displacement of a fairly supported choice merely because evidence conflicts. Health Care & Retirement and Holly Farms permit reliance on the agency's discernible actual rationale, not reasons supplied in its place. Those conditions are met here. A new classification hearing is therefore unwarranted. The Sixth Circuit must give effect to the existing order concerning these nurses, resolving only genuinely preserved remaining enforcement matters. The separate political-subdivision and rehabilitation-counselor rulings remain outside this decision; no backpay or new bargaining terms are adjudicated.

**Precedent treatment:** Allentown's announced-standard and whole-record review is applied without reducing it to automatic deference; Health Care & Retirement and Holly Farms are applied to sustain the actual supported rationale.

## Precedent Treatment

- NLRB v. Health Care & Retirement Corp. of America, 511 U.S. 571: reaffirms the permissible distinction between ordinary skilled direction and genuine supervisory authority, all alternative statutory functions, and the relevance of possessed but rarely exercised authority. It is applied to sustain the supported agency determination, without an occupational exemption.
- Director, OWCP v. Greenwich Collieries, 512 U.S. 267: preserves the ordinary persuasion burden while permitting the supervisory-exclusion proponent to bear that distinct issue.
- Holly Farms Corp. v. NLRB, 517 U.S. 392: applies independent statutory-boundary review followed by assessment of the actual functional rationale and supported findings; it supplies no automatic deference or occupational entitlement.
- Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB: applies the announced lawful standard and whole-record evidentiary review, preserving respect for fairly conflicting views rather than allowing judicial replacement of a supported agency choice.

## Law After Decision

The functional Health Care & Retirement rule remains controlling without alteration: one genuine supervisory function exercised in the employer's interest with independent judgment can exclude a professional employee, but ordinary skilled direction within prescribed standards need not do so. The newly stated exclusion-proponent burden applies to that status question in representation and derivative enforcement review. All nine adopt those governing rules. Five also hold that this agency determination meets them and requires enforcement as to these nurses. The classification of workers under materially different delegations is ordinary application of the rule on their own records, not an occupational exception established here.

## Separate Writings

**Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas.** These Justices join Parts I and II. The exclusion proponent bears the burden, and Health Care & Retirement permits a functional distinction without excluding all professional judgment. Assignment or responsible direction can establish supervision, and actual authority may matter even when infrequently exercised.

They disagree with Part III's treatment of the existing findings. The competing memoranda and evidence of interunit reassignment, reporting refusal and sending employees home require a clear agency determination about possessed authority. Findings concerning routine coverage, lack of independently effective personnel consequences and nonexercise do not, in their view, adequately resolve that separate issue. Nor can the medical character of the work substitute for deciding whether the particular authority requires independent judgment.

Allentown requires the announced legal standard to govern the actual evidence. Health Care & Retirement now supplies a lawful functional route, but it does not make every agency use of professional-services language sufficient. These Justices regard the application as incomplete; they do not treat the permitted functional distinction itself as unlawful. ## Event

New Hampshire v. Maine, No. 130, Original. Merits decision in original jurisdiction, May 29, 2001. Argued April 16, 2001. Maine moves to dismiss New Hampshire’s complaint seeking a Piscataqua River boundary at the Maine shore. The question is whether that claim repudiates the interpretation New Hampshire successfully presented in the earlier marine-boundary litigation.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated at argument and decision. Souter took no part in consideration or decision; no reason is publicly stated.

## Public Action

The Court grants Maine’s motion and dismisses the complaint.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Maine's motion to dismiss is granted, 8–0. New Hampshire's complaint is dismissed on judicial-estoppel grounds.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.
**Not participating:** Souter.

No additional survey, title award, damages, injunction, or remand is ordered. The 1977 consent decree remains effective according to its actual terms.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | All other members of the eight-Justice coalition: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer | Dismissal | Entire opinion, including contextual estoppel, rejection of the asserted explanation, and limited remedy |

## Holdings

### Judicial estoppel protects an accepted adjudicative position

**Holding and operative rule.** A court may prevent a litigant from advancing a clearly inconsistent position after obtaining judicial acceptance of the earlier position when repudiation would confer an unfair advantage or impair the integrity of the earlier adjudication. Clear inconsistency, actual judicial acceptance, and resulting advantage or detriment guide this equitable inquiry without forming an exhaustive formula or inflexible prerequisites; a supported genuine mistake or material change in law, policy, or essential fact can affect its application.

**Authority.** The signed opinion by Kennedy, joined throughout by the other seven participating Justices, states the Court's rule and application. All eight directly adopt this holding; no narrower-ground aggregation is involved.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that judicial acceptance, rather than negotiation alone, gives the earlier position its force here. Davis v. Wakelee supplies the principle against obtaining relief on one position and later repudiating it for an inconsistent advantage. Cleveland v. Policy Management Systems Corp. requires attention to the actual contradiction and its explanation; different legal labels do not establish inconsistency by themselves. Here New Hampshire urged that the same 1740 boundary language meant the middle of the main navigable channel. The Court accepted that construction when rejecting the Special Master's geographical-middle approach and directing entry of the earlier consent decree. New Hampshire now uses the same language to claim the river to the Maine shore. The offshore and inland remedies differ, but those interpretations cannot both be the basis of the State's judicial demands.

The State's assertion of an insufficient earlier historical inquiry does not establish genuine mistake. The earlier materials examined historical and river-boundary authorities, and the new interpretation rests on materials available then. No intervening enactment or newly occurring essential fact explains the reversal. A State invoking this Court's adjudicative power is subject to the same protection of judicial integrity. That conclusion does not settle the effect of ordinary changes in government policy in another case. Dismissal bars this inconsistent claim without turning every compromise statement into a binding admission.

### Dismissal does not enlarge the earlier boundary decree

**Holding and operative rule.** New Hampshire's present shore-boundary complaint is barred by judicial estoppel and is dismissed. The 1977 decree remains effective within its existing marine-boundary scope; this dismissal supplies no new inland survey, independent title adjudication, or affirmative geographic decree.

**Authority.** The same eight-Justice Court opinion directly adopts the limited disposition.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Maine's motion can be resolved from the accepted earlier judicial position. New Hampshire v. Maine, 426 U.S. 363, supplies the acceptance of the States' interpretation; the decree at 434 U.S. 1 supplies its enacted geographic terms. The fact that those terms did not survey every inland segment does not permit repudiation of the interpretation that obtained relief. Conversely, preventing that repudiation cannot itself draw coordinates the earlier decree did not establish. Maine's separate claim- and issue-preclusion theories are therefore unnecessary to decision. A materially different boundary theory would require its own pleadings and record; no such claim is resolved here.

## Precedent Treatment

- Davis v. Wakelee: applied for the received rule against successfully asserting inconsistent judicial positions; its protection of adjudicative integrity remains in force.
- Cleveland v. Policy Management Systems Corp.: applied for examination of actual inconsistency and a supported explanation; it is neither displaced nor converted into an automatic estoppel rule.
- New Hampshire v. Maine, 426 U.S. 363, and the decree at 434 U.S. 1: given effect according to the accepted interpretation and the decree's marine-boundary scope; their geographic reach is not enlarged.

## Law After Decision

Judicial estoppel now expressly governs a State's repudiation of a clearly inconsistent interpretive premise accepted as a basis of this Court's relief, with contextual consideration of genuine mistake and material changed circumstances. The rule is distinct from claim and issue preclusion. The present complaint is dismissed; the earlier decree retains its existing scope, and no additional boundary line is established.

## Separate Writings

No separate opinion is filed.

## Procedure After Action

The original complaint is dismissed. No further proceeding, survey, deadline, or new decree is directed. The earlier consent decree continues within its terms.

## Event

PGA TOUR, Inc. v. Martin, No. 00-24. Merits decision, May 29, 2001. Argued January 17, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed the District of Oregon’s cart-accommodation injunction. The Court decides whether Title III covers Martin’s tournament access and whether his necessary cart fundamentally alters the competition.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the judgment sustaining the accommodation injunction.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Affirmed, 7–2. The Ninth Circuit judgment sustaining Martin's cart injunction remains effective.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

Martin may use the required cart while remaining subject to ordinary skill qualifications and other valid competition rules. No damages, automatic tour membership, or new trial is ordered.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | All other members of the seven-Justice coalition: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer | Affirmance | Coverage, both alteration routes, individual application, and remedy |
| Dissent | Thomas | Scalia | Would reverse | Service-recipient ground and independent fundamental-alteration ground |

## Holdings

### Competitors receive covered golf-course services

**Holding and operative rule.** A golf-course operator offering paid qualifying opportunities and tournament participation provides covered services and privileges to competitors as well as spectators under Title III of the Americans with Disabilities Act. An eligible entrant does not lose that recipient relationship because his play also contributes to the entertainment provided to others; employment status is unnecessary to this Title III holding.

**Authority.** The signed opinion by Stone-Zsela is joined throughout by the other members of the seven-Justice coalition identified in Opinion Topology. All seven directly join both holdings and the remedy.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the statutory golf-course category and the protection of goods, services, facilities, privileges, advantages, and accommodations encompass the opportunity the Tour offers here. Section 12181(7)(L) includes golf courses affecting commerce. Section 12182(a) protects an individual's full and equal enjoyment of the operator's offered services. Martin pays for access to a qualifying process and seeks access to the resulting competition. Providing athletic performance to spectators does not make those offered opportunities disappear.

The clients-or-customers language in section 12182(b)(1)(A)(iv) expressly applies to clauses (i) through (iii) of that subparagraph; it is not an enacted definition of every protected relationship throughout Title III. Even under the Tour's proposed recipient limitation, Martin receives the paid qualifying opportunity. The Court therefore need not decide a universal rule about workers, independent contractors, or every sporting relationship. Bragdon v. Abbott supplies individualized examination of the statutory conditions; Cleveland v. Policy Management Systems Corp. supplies the requirement to address those conditions rather than substitute a categorical label. Coverage leaves the separate accommodation requirements to be satisfied.

### Necessary modification and fundamental alteration

**Holding and operative rule.** Under section 12182(b)(2)(A)(ii), an operator must make a reasonable modification necessary for a disabled individual's access unless the operator demonstrates that the modification would fundamentally alter the offered service. In a competition, alteration can arise by changing an essential competitive feature, even uniformly, or by giving the particular competitor a material competitive advantage through an otherwise peripheral change; the supported findings establish neither effect from Martin's cart, so the modification is required.

**Authority.** The same seven Justices directly join this rule, its two distinct alteration routes, its individual application, and affirmance of the injunction.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that an existing uniform rule is evidence of how a competition operates, but its existence does not conclusively establish fundamental alteration. Otherwise the enacted duty to modify policies would have no work to do. The Tour's judgment deserves consideration; it does not replace the statutory defense. The court found after trial that shot-making is the essential competitive task, walking is not essential in this tournament setting, carts are used in related qualifying and tour settings, and Martin experiences greater fatigue even with a cart than other players ordinarily experience walking. These findings are reviewed for clear error and support the conclusion that a cart changes neither the essential task nor the competitive balance to his advantage.

Bragdon supplies objective, individual evaluation rather than unsupported generalization. Albertson's, Inc. v. Kirkingburg preserves a particular federal safety criterion; it grants no automatic exemption to a private tournament rule. Olmstead v. L.C. preserves genuine program limits, but its Title II resource defense does not replace Title III's enacted alteration inquiry. Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc. preserves individual substantial-limitation analysis; Martin's disability and need are established under that inquiry. The Court does not prescribe favorable outcomes, equal stamina, automatic membership, or waiver of essential skill qualifications. Martin must satisfy the ordinary qualifications and other valid rules. The operator bears its alteration defense after necessity and reasonableness are established; no auxiliary-aid undue-burden standard is imported.

## Precedent Treatment

- Bragdon v. Abbott: applied for objective individual statutory assessment; its direct-threat inquiry is not substituted for the distinct fundamental-alteration defense.
- Cleveland v. Policy Management Systems Corp.: applied for separate examination of statutory conditions rather than categorical labels.
- Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc.: preserved as the governing individualized disability rule; this case does not change it or decide disability from diagnosis alone.
- Albertson's, Inc. v. Kirkingburg: distinguished because its federal safety-standard holding does not exempt private sporting rules from Title III.
- Olmstead v. L.C.: used as a bounded comparison preserving genuine program limits; its Title II defense is not transplanted into Title III.
- Board of Trustees of the University of Alabama v. Garrett: concerns Title I enforcement against state employers and supplies no limit on this private Title III claim.

## Law After Decision

Title III reaches the Tour's offered qualifying and tournament opportunities for competitors. Necessary reasonable modification of competition rules is required unless the operator demonstrates an alteration of an essential feature or a material competitive advantage in the individual application. The cart injunction is sustained on the trial findings; normal skill qualification, genuine essential rules, and other valid conduct requirements remain enforceable. This is a new application of the enacted access and alteration rules, not a general entitlement to carts or tournament admission.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, dissenting, joined by Scalia.** The dissent would reverse because the professional tournament is the service provided to spectators, while competitors produce that service. Payment to enter a qualifying process does not, in its view, turn entry into a profession into consumption of the professional contest. It acknowledges the limited textual scope of the clients-or-customers clause and relies instead on the particular relationship and service identified by the statute. Alternatively, even assuming coverage, it would find fundamental alteration: a uniform walking rule is part of the contest being offered, and excusing one player changes that contest. Relying on the distinction between access to an existing service and a compelled different service reflected in its Olmstead position, the dissent rejects judicial identification of a sport's essence and comparative fatigue as the means of defining the offered competition. It disputes neither Martin's impairment nor the trial finding of substantial fatigue. Its grounds do not decide an employment cause or make every private rule immune from the ADA.

## Procedure After Action

The affirmed injunction remains operative. The Tour must permit Martin’s necessary cart use under the injunction, while he must meet ordinary qualification and other valid competition conditions. No additional appellate remand is directed.

[The official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-532/pdf/USREPORTS-532-661.pdf) supplies the argument date, proceedings below, medical and trial findings, and competition practices. [Title III’s enacted accommodation provision](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2000-title42/pdf/USCODE-2000-title42-chap126-subchapIII-sec12182.pdf) supplies the distinct modification obligation, alteration defense, and limited customer clause. Findings about Martin’s fatigue and advantage are individual trial findings, not universal statutory facts.

## Event

United States v. Napier, Sixth Circuit No. 00-5290, 233 F.3d 394. Merits decision, June 1, 2001. On review of the Sixth Circuit’s affirmance of firearm and ammunition convictions after a conditional guilty plea. Submitted on full merits materials; no separate Supreme Court docket or argument date is established. The accepted question is whether the Second Amendment permits the disability on these adjudicated civil domestic-violence orders without a criminal conviction.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated at submission and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms both convictions against the accepted Second Amendment challenge and rejects the collective-right rationale below.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Affirmed, 7–2, as to both the firearm and ammunition convictions against the accepted Second Amendment objection.

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas, who would vacate the appellate arms determination and remand for application of the governing framework.

The collective-right rationale below is rejected. The conditional plea and both convictions remain effective against this objection. No release, firearm return, restoration, new hearing, or remand is ordered; the distinct unpreserved evidentiary challenge is not reopened.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Other members of the full six-Justice coalition: Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer; Stone-Zsela as limited below | Affirms both convictions | Six full joins; Stone-Zsela joins personal coverage and the civil danger predicate, but not burden classification or intermediate scrutiny |

## Holdings

### Personal coverage and a civil danger predicate

**Holding and operative rule.** The Second Amendment protects Napier's ordinary pistol and its necessary ammunition without requiring organized-militia membership. A criminal conviction is not constitutionally indispensable to a preventive possession disability: a qualifying domestic-violence order supported by adjudicated domestic violence and risk of recurrence, entered after actual notice and an opportunity to participate, may justify disarmament while the order remains operative.

**Authority.** The six-Justice opinion of the Court by O'Connor directly adopts these propositions; Stone-Zsela also joins this portion. Scalia and Thomas accept personal coverage and the governing Wright framework but do not join the present justification holding.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that personal coverage and permissible regulation are distinct questions. Love v. Pepersack supplies personal invocation; United States v. Hale rejects affiliation as a prerequisite; United States v. Wright requires an actual justification for the burden imposed. The collective-only rationale below cannot support this judgment. Fraternal Order of Police v. United States upheld a conviction-based disability in an official-use controversy. Its reserved private-possession question prevents treating it as a completed answer here, but its reliance on adjudicated force-related conduct does not make criminal conviction the exclusive possible danger predicate.

Napier received notice and an opportunity to participate in the hearings, which he reportedly attended. The December 1996 and September 1998 orders found that domestic violence had occurred and might recur. His plea admitted knowing possession while both orders remained operative. These are adjudications of conduct and prospective danger, not the bare existence of a protective-order label. The later assault report supplies no additional adjudicated assault or conviction. Preventive protection need not await another completed offense. The holding does not authorize disarmament on an allegation alone or resolve ex parte and unrelated civil orders.

Section 922(g)(8) independently requires actual hearing notice and an opportunity to participate; restraint of harassment, stalking, or threats against an intimate partner or a child of that partner or the restrained person, or other conduct placing the partner in reasonable fear of bodily injury to the partner or child; and either a finding of a credible threat to the partner's or child's physical safety under (C)(i), or an express prohibition of using, attempting, or threatening physical force against the partner or child reasonably expected to cause bodily injury under (C)(ii). These alternatives remain distinct. Findings of violence and possible recurrence are not relabeled a statutory credible-threat finding.

### The admitted order-dependent restriction satisfies intermediate scrutiny

**Holding and operative rule.** A possession disability with these adjudicated domestic-violence and recurrence predicates, hearing protections, and dependence on an operative qualifying order is a substantial regulation under Wright. It satisfies intermediate scrutiny's requirements of an important objective, substantial fit, and a restriction that does not unnecessarily or effectively eliminate protected activity: the defined conduct-based disqualification substantially addresses armed recurrence and confines the exclusion to the adjudicated order's operation, so both convictions stand against this Second Amendment challenge.

**Authority.** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer directly join the entire six-Justice Court rationale. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on strict-scrutiny grounds and does not join the burden classification or intermediate-scrutiny application. No narrowest-ground inference is needed to identify the controlling six-Justice rule.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the restriction is serious and requires meaningful justification. Its classification rests on the concrete adjudicated conduct, domestic-danger findings, and order-dependent operation. Neither a safety label nor the fact that other people retain arms is sufficient. Wright supplies scrutiny responsive to the actual burden; the present defined restraint is substantial regulation rather than general abolition of meaningful ordinary defensive home possession. Its categorical protection against that general abolition remains intact.

The findings identify a person who committed domestic violence and might repeat it. Removing his access to a firearm and necessary ammunition substantially addresses the risk of armed recurrence. The government need not regard civil adjudication as categorically irrelevant until a criminal conviction occurs. This application extends beyond Fraternal Order's conviction and official-use setting on its own supported predicates; it does not import criminal safeguards into civil proceedings or excuse absence of adjudication. The statute's operation depends on the qualifying order remaining in force. No assumed short duration or unestablished modification program supplies the fit.

Wright also forbids unnecessary or effective elimination of protected activity. The bounded exclusion here preserves the underlying personal entitlement except while these adjudicated disqualification conditions operate. Its reach follows the established conduct-to-danger relationship and operative order, rather than withdrawal of protection by civil label alone. Disarmament during that operation addresses the identified armed-recurrence risk; the present conclusion does not turn every time-limited total prohibition into permissible regulation. A time limit by itself would not satisfy Wright, and arms remaining available to other people would not answer this individual's burden.

The prosecution arose from possession in a vehicle. The Court neither finds a separate home-possession event nor decides every order or threatened-conduct case. The plea's admissions and preserved legal question permit resolution without reopening the distinct unpreserved evidentiary challenge to the orders. Missing hearing transcripts do not establish a constitutional defect. No fixed civil proof standard, permissible maximum duration, or restoration procedure is adopted.

## Precedent Treatment

- Love v. Pepersack: applied for the personal arms guarantee, independent of organized-militia membership; its protection remains in force.
- United States v. Hale: applied for separation of personal coverage and permissible regulation.
- United States v. Wright: applied to the actual burden, with intermediate scrutiny for the defined substantial regulation; its strict-scrutiny category and prohibition on general abolition of ordinary defensive home possession remain intact.
- Fraternal Order of Police v. United States: its conviction-based, official-use holding remains intact; it is distinguished as leaving the present civil-order and private-possession application open. The new application does not treat a conviction as the exclusive permissible danger predicate.

## Law After Decision

The personal-right framework governs civil domestic-violence disarmament. On adjudicated violence and recurrence findings after notice and opportunity to participate, the defined order-dependent disability is permissible under the six-Justice intermediate-scrutiny holding. A criminal conviction is not invariably required. The Court does not approve mere allegations, all civil orders, ex parte process, or every duration; the ordinary statutory qualifications remain necessary. Stone-Zsela's stricter application and the dissent's demand for further justification do not alter the controlling six-Justice ground.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** He joins personal coverage and the rejection of an indispensable criminal-conviction requirement, but regards a complete firearm-and-ammunition possession disability as severe and applies strict scrutiny. Preventing armed domestic violence against a partner or child is compelling. These orders followed notice and meaningful opportunity to contest the evidence, established actual violence and a supported risk of recurrence, and remained operative when Napier knowingly possessed the weapon and ammunition. A public-carry limit would leave access in the domestic setting; repeating a command not to assault would not provide comparable protection against armed escalation. The government need not await another assault, prove inevitable recurrence, or show that the earlier violence already involved a firearm. Restricting necessary ammunition together with the firearm prevents evasion of the same restraint. The existing adjudication supplies the predicate; a separate criminal proceeding or freestanding second dangerousness adjudication is not required solely because the first was civil.

He distinguishes an evidence-backed threat finding from speculation, but does not decide the precise evidentiary boundary of a threat-only case because this record includes completed violence. The later assault allegation does no independent work, and absent hearing transcripts do not reopen the plea's unpreserved evidentiary issue. He allows the possibility of later lawful restoration. If modification or restoration is provided, it must be genuinely available and may require evidence that the danger sufficiently abated; eligibility is not entitlement to immediate firearm return. No particular restoration claim or waiting period is before the Court. When the qualifying order ends, this provision supplies no continuing prohibition, although an independent lawful disability may remain. A longer or permanent restraint requires its own legal basis and constitutional justification. These further remedial observations are his separate position, not additional holdings of the Court.

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** They would vacate the appellate arms determination and remand for actual application of Wright. They accept personal coverage and apply Wright as governing law while retaining their previously stated objections to its foundation. The admitted civil orders are relevant, but their existence alone does not complete the restriction-specific justification of a full possession prohibition. Fraternal Order's conviction safeguards and official-use record cannot be silently transferred to this private-possession application. They would require evaluation of the actual disability, its basis, and its operation, using the existing record or ordinary further proceedings if needed. They infer no defective hearing from unavailable transcripts and impose no categorical conviction prerequisite. Their disposition would not itself acquit Napier, release him, or return his weapons; consequential treatment of the conditional plea would remain below.

## Holdings

### Psychiatric claim: the statutory ground resolves this application

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 2254(d)(1) does not permit relief on this psychiatric claim: the state court's distinction between a defense-requested earlier competency report used in cross-examining a defense mental-condition expert and the compelled, uncounseled capital examination in Estelle v. Smith is neither contrary to nor an objectively unreasonable application of then-established federal law. The adverse psychiatric certificate disposition is affirmed without deciding a general direct-review admissibility or privilege-waiver rule.

**Authority.** Part I of the signed opinion by Souter is joined by all nine Justices. Its sufficient statutory ground controls; it makes no alternative finding about actual sentencing effect.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the statutory limitation must be applied to the actual examination setting. Williams v. Taylor supplies distinct contrary-to and unreasonable-application clauses and an objective inquiry. Estelle v. Smith protects against the compelled capital examination in that case; Buchanan v. Kentucky distinguishes a defense-initiated evaluation and responsive use. The Peebles report originated in defense counsel's request for a competency examination in an unrelated 1977 prosecution. Dr. Price reviewed it among fourteen reports while testifying for Penry about mental condition. Cross-examination elicited its dangerousness statement over objection, and the prosecutor repeated the statement in closing. Those uses present a substantial privilege concern, but they do not eliminate the materially different origin and use that supported the state court's distinction.

The applicable constitutional-law cutoff is the state merits decision of February 22, 1995; rehearing was denied June 7, 1995, and no later merits adjudication changing that cutoff is established. A preferred broader exclusion rule on direct review cannot replace the statutory standard. The Court adopts no universal subject-matter waiver, no rule admitting every report whenever mental condition is discussed, and no adverse finding based on absence of an objection. The statutory ground suffices; no general direct-review privilege decision or psychiatric harmlessness determination is necessary.

### The mitigation charge failed to provide an effective lawful verdict

**Holding and operative rule.** When supported capital mitigation can reduce culpability independently of the factual special issues, the jury must have an authorized, coherent way to give that judgment effect in a life verdict. An instruction allowing a negative answer for mitigation does not satisfy that requirement when the unchanged verdict form requires the jury to certify reasonable doubt about a factual proposition it may believe proved; on this complete charge and trial context there is a reasonable likelihood of unconstitutional restriction, and the state's acceptance was an objectively unreasonable application of the established rule.

**Authority.** Part II has seven direct joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Scalia and Thomas dissent from both the constitutional application and the unreasonable-application conclusion. No independent contrary-to holding is adopted on this component.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that admitting mitigation is insufficient when the verdict mechanism cannot lawfully express its effect. Lockett v. Ohio and Eddings v. Oklahoma protect relevant culpability mitigation. Penry v. Lynaugh requires an effective vehicle for the evidence at issue; Graham v. Collins and Johnson v. Texas establish before the 1995 state adjudication that this vehicle cannot require a false factual answer. Boyde v. California requires a reasonable likelihood of restriction in the complete charge and trial, not a mere possible misunderstanding.

The jury could believe intellectual impairment and childhood abuse reduced culpability while still finding deliberateness, future dangerousness, and lack of reasonable provocation. The supplement permitted a negative answer if life fit that culpability judgment. But the attached form still required a negative answer to certify that at least ten jurors had reasonable doubt about the factual issue. An affirmative answer required unanimity beyond reasonable doubt. The conflict therefore concerned the lawful meaning of the signed answer, not simply awareness that life was possible.

Each eventual juror received a largely similar supplemental instruction and a judicial explanation during selection; counsel also explained its operation, and the defense renewed the explanation at penalty closing. Those circumstances favor the State and must be considered. They instructed jurors how to answer negatively without reconciling the final factual certification. The interval and intervening trial stages do not prove forgetting, and the prosecution's emphasis on oath, evidence, and law is not a separately imposed mitigation prohibition. The final packet nevertheless retained the conflict. Its acceptance exceeded the reasonable range under the pre-1995 effective-vehicle rule. Neither favored weight, a prescribed fourth question, nor a categorical disability exemption is required. The form's ten-juror language is considered as part of the conflict; no separate unanimity claim is decided.

### A confined State-borne harmless-error rule governs the remaining effect inquiry

**Holding and operative rule.** For a preserved original-charge restriction of this kind on supported capital culpability mitigation, the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt, on the complete penalty record, that the constitutional defect did not contribute to the death sentence. If the State cannot make that showing, conditional sentence relief must allow a reasonable period for constitutionally adequate capital resentencing or a lawful noncapital sentence; guilt remains unaffected.

**Authority.** Part III has five direct joins: Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Stevens and Kennedy join the corrective return but reject this effect standard; Scalia and Thomas oppose the mitigation judgment and state a different effect position conditionally. This is a majority holding, not a synthesis of those separate positions under Marks.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court expressly extends Chapman's State-borne beyond-reasonable-doubt standard to this defined original-charge category. Calderon v. Coleman applied that allocation to commutation misinformation; it did not already settle every capital habeas error. Weeks v. Angelone left its response-error effect rule, burden, and uncertainty treatment unresolved. Neither holding had already classified the contradictory original-charge mechanism here. The present extension addresses reliable selection of death after an established denial of a lawful mitigation route and assigns uncertainty about the error's contribution to the State.

The State may rely on the entire penalty record, including testimony, instructions, and the clarifying explanations. The established error alone does not prove actual contribution or require automatic relief. Conversely, affirmative answers to the special issues do not establish what an unconflicted jury would have done with independent culpability mitigation. The reported excerpts do not permit a completed whole-record determination by this Court. The lower court must undertake that inquiry under the adopted standard.

The extension changes the present remedial rule for this bounded category; it does not become clearly established constitutional law at the 1995 section 2254(d)(1) cutoff, create a new retroactivity exception, or govern every habeas claim. The constitutional gateway has already been satisfied through the pre-1995 mitigation rule. No structural-error classification or presumption that the error actually contributed is adopted.

### Certificate review, corrective return, and the limit on immediate relief

**Holding and operative rule.** The Court may review the Fifth Circuit's judicial denial of a certificate of appealability under section 1254(1). The established mitigation violation and unreasonable application require reversal of that component of the certificate disposition and an issue-specific appellate return; they do not themselves constitute a present conditional writ or immediate sentence vacatur before the remaining effect inquiry.

**Authority.** Part I's reviewability framework has nine joins. Part II's mitigation certificate eligibility and corrective return have the seven direct joins identified above. Part III supplies the five-Justice standard binding the effect inquiry on remand.

**Controlling explanation.** Hohn v. United States permits review of the judicial certificate denial. Slack v. McDaniel keeps the substantial-showing threshold distinct from ultimate entitlement to relief and requires issue-specific treatment. The psychiatric statutory disposition remains adverse. The mitigation claim satisfies certification and the constitutional and section 2254(d)(1) questions are resolved in Penry's favor. Those conclusions bind the return; further proceedings are not an invitation to relitigate the decided gateway. But the certificate is not itself a writ, and the record does not establish actual harmlessness or harmfulness. The Court therefore returns the matter for the issue-specific appeal and full-record effect review. Conditional sentence correction follows if the State fails the adopted standard; immediate resentencing, release, or a fixed deadline is not ordered now.

## Event

Pollard v. E. I. du Pont de Nemours & Co., No. 00-763. Merits decision, June 4, 2001. Argued April 23, 2001. ## Holdings

### Reinstatement-related front pay is excluded from the added damages cap

**Holding and operative rule.** Front pay compensating employment loss between judgment and reinstatement, or serving in place of reinstatement when return is inappropriate, is available as appropriate affirmative equitable relief under Title VII section 706(g). Because section 1981a(b)(2) excludes relief authorized by section 706(g) from the new compensatory-damages category, section 1981a(b)(3)'s damages cap does not limit either form of that front pay; the classification establishes remedial authority, not automatic entitlement or a particular award.

**Authority.** The signed opinion by Thomas, joined throughout by the other seven participating Justices, directly adopts the statutory classification and remand. O'Connor does not participate.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the source of remedial authority determines whether the cap applies. Section 706(g)(1), codified at 42 U.S.C. section 2000e-5(g)(1), permits appropriate affirmative action, including but not limited to reinstatement, and other equitable relief. Compensation restoring the employment position while reinstatement is delayed, or replacing it when return is inappropriate, serves that retained remedial function. The 1991 Act added compensation through section 1981a(a)(1) while subsection (b)(2) expressly excluded all relief authorized under section 706(g), not just backpay.

Landgraf v. USI Film Products supplies the distinction between retained equitable remedies and the additional damages Congress created. It did not itself classify front pay; that question is decided here. The future-pecuniary-loss language in subsection (b)(3) governs damages within the new category. It does not override the adjacent exclusion or bring every monetary award for a postjudgment period within the cap. Nor does the text distinguish compensation bridging delayed reinstatement from compensation substituting for inappropriate reinstatement. Both can restore employment loss under section 706(g).

Pollard's established hostile environment and resulting inability to return support reconsideration under that authority, but do not fix the remedy's duration or amount. The lower court must determine whether reinstatement is delayed or inappropriate, the causally attributable earnings loss, a supported duration, mitigation, and avoidance of overlapping recovery. A label cannot convert emotional distress, separate consequential loss, or punitive damages into uncapped front pay. Those categories retain their statutory sources and limits. The Court therefore removes the erroneous cap premise and remands without reopening liability, backpay, or fees.

## Precedent Treatment

- Landgraf v. USI Film Products: applied for the preserved distinction between section 706(g) equitable relief and section 1981a's added damages; its temporal framework remains unchanged, and its earlier holding is not treated as already classifying front pay.
- Kolstad v. American Dental Association: its separate punitive-damages rules remain intact; they neither classify front pay nor permit overlap with equitable employment restoration.

## Law After Decision

Both front pay bridging eventual reinstatement and front pay properly substituting for inappropriate reinstatement fall outside section 1981a's added damages cap. Courts retain ordinary equitable responsibility to determine appropriateness, proof, causation, duration, mitigation, and duplication. Uncapped classification creates no unlimited or automatic award. Other compensation and punitive damages retain their own statutory limits. The Court adopts this classification without altering Landgraf's temporal framework or deciding general jury allocation.
