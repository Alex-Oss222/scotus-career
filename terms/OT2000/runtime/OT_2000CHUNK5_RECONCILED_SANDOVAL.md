## Adversarial and scope checks

The application path's strongest objection is that section 601 reaches only intentional discrimination and section 602 addresses agencies, so a valid implementing obligation does not establish congressional authorization of the private action. That is the ground of the Powell dissent O'Connor wrote and Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas joined. It cannot establish that Powell is distinguishable: Powell itself involved prospective claims against state officials and decided the same regulatory obligation. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer find the objection answered on Title VI's integrated ground; the four Powell dissenters keep it as their own view but find it insufficient to displace a one-year-old statutory holding, consistently with McClellan's contextual but nonautomatic inquiry.

The reconsideration path's strongest objection is statutory stare decisis: Congress may amend the statute, the prior ruling is directly on point, and neither the English-only policy nor proved rather than alleged effects changes the action question. No commitment takes that path; the four Powell dissenters treat that cost as decisive. Every commitment supports this Title VI route only, not a general cause of action for every valid regulation or a rule that prospective relief escapes action-creation requirements. None seeks a holding on Powell's separate section 1983 branch in this grant.

Administrative remedies, statutory prerequisites for agency sanctions and the established intentional-discrimination action remain distinct. No funding cutoff, private exhaustion requirement, damages entitlement, intent finding or invalidation of the effects regulations is supplied.

## Components and lawful alternatives

The question is whether to apply Powell's April 3, 2000 direct-action holding or expressly reconsider it. Powell already concerned prospective relief against state officials. A different agency's regulation, a different state policy or a finding rather than an allegation of effects therefore cannot defeat this action while purporting to follow Powell. The distinct section 1983 holding is not a claim supplied by this grant. Regulatory validity and the finding of prohibited effects remain assumed; neither becomes a constitutional intent finding.

Applying Powell requires affirmance on the granted question: the injunction is not vacated for want of a private action and remains subject to ordinary lawful modification. The best contrary argument is that section 601 reaches only intentional discrimination and section 602 addresses agencies, so a valid implementing obligation does not show congressional authorization of a private action and an agency's wording cannot create one. Powell considered and answered that argument for prospective relief. McClellan's contextual inquiry does not erase Powell's answer for Title VI. Franklin supplies relief only after identifying an action; Powell identifies the action and confines it to declaratory and prospective equitable relief, so the requested injunction does not create its own authority.

Reconsideration would require express displacement of Powell's direct-action branch. Its strongest objection is enhanced statutory stare decisis: Congress can change the statutory rule, and neither different regulated conduct nor a finding rather than an allegation of effects changes the action-creation question. No Justice takes that path. The four Justices who dissented from Powell's direct-action holding keep their published Powell objection as their own view, but no intervening amendment, decision or factual distinction supports reversing the same statutory judgment after one year, and the rule's youth is not a license to overrule a statutory precedent. No Justice seeks a new holding on Powell's separate section 1983 branch here.
