## Common disposition and operative propositions

All seven non-Stone participants would reverse the Ninth Circuit's recognition of this medical-necessity distribution exemption and remand. Section 841(a)(1), Schedule I's treatment of marijuana, and the statute's specifically authorized research route leave no medical-necessity exception authorizing the Cooperative's manufacture, distribution or related possession with intent. The Cooperative claims no authorization under that route. California's removal of its own prohibitions does not create federal authorization. The statutory conclusion concerns the challenged distribution operation; it does not adjudicate an individual patient's distinct possession prosecution, constitutional defense, or every necessity claim under all federal criminal statutes.

The same substantive exemption cannot be recreated through an injunction modification premised upon the court's independent judgment that the asserted medical benefits outweigh Congress's statutory restriction. Section 882(a) authorizes equitable relief; it does not on that account compel the broadest injunction in every circumstance. Lawful discretion concerning the need for relief and its form remains, but that discretion must operate consistently with the enacted scheme and cannot make the prohibited distribution substantively permissible.

The four lower-order criteria are preserved as terms of that order, not facts found true of all recipients: a serious medical condition; imminent harm without access to cannabis; need for cannabis to treat the condition or alleviate the condition or its symptoms; and no reasonable legal alternative for effective treatment or alleviation because the patient has tried all legal alternatives and they were ineffective or produced side effects the patient could not reasonably tolerate. Even actual satisfaction of those criteria would not supply the claimed statutory distribution authorization. The Court makes no universal finding that those criteria were, or could never be, satisfied.

## Enhanced alternative-path comparison

**Recognize a necessity defense.** Bailey's common-law discussion, extreme medical need and absence of reasonable legal alternatives provide the strongest path to a narrow defense. Its weakness is statutory: Congress's treatment of marijuana and the specified research mechanism address medical use rather than merely omitting an unrelated emergency topic. Rutherford likewise rejects turning a claimed therapeutic need into an exemption from an enacted medical-regulatory scheme. No Justice here treats the label necessity as enough to overcome those choices. General existence of a necessity defense in other federal crimes need not be decided.

**Preserve the requested exception through equity.** Hecht and Romero-Barcelo support discretion concerning the necessity and form of injunctions. Grupo Mexicano's dissenters have an additional jurisprudential reason to reject an artificially frozen view of equity. But an authorized court's discretion about relief is distinct from authority to approve conduct that the governing statute prohibits on the very ground Congress addressed. Meghrig's shared statutory-limit principle resolves this particular use of equity for all seven. Their agreement does not adopt Grupo Mexicano's disputed general-equity analysis as a universal statutory-injunction rule.

**Reject both grounds while preserving lawful remedial discretion.** This is the shared path. The statutory holding cannot be avoided by relabeling the same medical-distribution permission a limit on equitable relief. Other equitable considerations consistent with the statute are not categorically erased. No Justice requires an automatic injunction in all circumstances, the broadest order, or identical treatment of a separate constitutional defense. The proposed common opinion should state these distinctions rather than relying on a broad slogan that no necessity can ever matter.
