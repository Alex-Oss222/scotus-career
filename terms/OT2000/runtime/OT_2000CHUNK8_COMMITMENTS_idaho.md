## Reachability and scope

All eight Associates participate. The United States sues to establish its own trust title, with the Tribe intervening. The private officer-suit immunity dispute in Coeur d'Alene is not a bar to this suit or a resolution of its title. The historical findings are unchallenged on appeal; the question is the legal significance of the congressional instruments. No trial or further factual hearing is justified merely because the inference of congressional intent is disputed.

The decree covers the beds and banks of navigable waters within the reservation boundaries described by the March 3, 1891 and August 15, 1894 Acts, expressly excluding those waters and submerged lands claimed by Idaho to lie within Heyburn State Park. It recognizes United States trustee title and the Tribe's beneficial interest and exclusive use, occupancy, and quiet enjoyment, enjoins Idaho's adverse assertions within that estate, and denies Idaho's counterclaim. No present vote enlarges the decree to Heyburn, adopts a replacement coordinate description, or decides the excluded cross-appeal. Title and regulatory jurisdiction remain distinct. Atkinson decides neither this ownership nor present fishing, taxation, environmental, or nonmember regulation.

## Governing propositions and competing paths

**I1 — Two distinct clear showings.** Alaska requires clear inclusion of the navigable submerged estate in the reservation and clear intent to defeat the future State's otherwise presumptive title for an appropriate public purpose. The 1873 order's inclusion and congressional notice are conceded here. The contested second showing cannot be inferred from those concessions alone. Shively supplies the federal territorial power; Montana and Utah Division supply the strong state-title presumption and the requirement to identify effective retention rather than a useful general purpose.

**I2 — Timely enacted treatment retains the defined estate.** On this path the enacted 1886, 1888, and 1889 measures have different but cumulative legal work. The 1886 measure addresses cession outside the existing reservation; its ratification condition prevents the negotiated bargain itself from taking effect early. The 1888 railroad statute treats the existing reservation as a tribal estate, conditions the right of way on actual survey approval, consent, and compensation, and forbids the railroad to assist destruction of the Tribe's tenure. The 1889 measure authorizes purchase only of portions the Tribe consents to sell on just and equitable terms, with completion reserved to congressional ratification. Congress acted with notice that the defined reservation included the lake and river beds. Its enacted choice to obtain selected portions by consensual purchase preserves the remaining reservation pending that process, rather than allowing its navigable estate to pass to the State automatically in the interval. The admission instrument accepts the Indian-land disclaimer; that text corroborates retention of this already identified estate but does not independently reserve every bed beneath every Indian reservation.

The strongest objection is that recognizing present occupancy, requiring compensation for a railway, and authorizing negotiations are compatible with later state bed ownership. The ratification clauses expressly deny immediate effect to the agreements, and the 1890 Senate vote is not an enactment. I2 answers by relying on the enacted protection of the existing estate and consent-based cession process, not by treating the proposed bargains as already binding. Retention of this particular watery homeland preserves the subject of Congress's bargain and the uses established by the findings; geography and generalized dependence on fishing are not independent sufficient grounds. A later confirming enactment does not confiscate intervening state title because on I2 the relevant title never vested in Idaho.

**I3 — The enacted measures do not clearly defeat future state title.** On this path their definite legal effects concern railroad access, compensation, present tribal tenure, and negotiation authority. They neither consummate a permanent bed disposition before July 3, 1890 nor state the equivalent of Alaska's operative statehood exclusions. Continuing reservation occupancy and use can coexist with the State's presumptive bed title. The 1889 purchase condition makes the promised agreement contingent, rather than itself a title-preserving congressional segregation against admission. The general Indian-land disclaimer does not decide the antecedent question whether these particular beds had been retained against equal footing.

The strongest objection is that this reading makes Congress's consent-based cession program partially self-defeating and underreads the 1888 tenure-preservation terms. I3 accepts that the instruments have legal effect and that the water matters to the Tribe; it rejects only the further inference that those effects clearly withdraw future state bed title. Alaska's result rests on its identified statutory provisions, not an automatic equation between negotiation protection and title retention. No talismanic phrase is demanded, but the particular enactments must resolve the separate state-title consequence clearly.

**I4 — Later ratification, relation back, and aboriginal title.** A new postadmission reservation cannot itself divest a title already vested at admission. The 1891 ratification may confirm an earlier legally effective reservation, but its date cannot be moved by treating the June 1890 Senate vote as a completed law. The Tribe's distinct relation-back suggestion cites Wismer and Buttz; those decisions have not been separately examined in this model and no Justice is committed to adopting or rejecting that distinct doctrine on its merits. The narrower grant permits resolution of I2/I3 without making relation back a new holding. The preserved unextinguished aboriginal-title theory was not decided by either court below and may require first-instance consideration. If I2 is accepted, neither alternative needs decision. If I3 is accepted, the reservation ground fails but a final opposite title decree does not follow automatically; the unadjudicated alternative must receive lawful lower-court treatment. Any properly available relation-back argument remains subject to its own preservation, authority, and intervening-title limits on that remand.

## Proposition and remedy commitments for reconciliation

- **I1 governing standard and conceded inclusion:** All eight accept Alaska's two distinct showings as the governing rule. Scalia and Thomas preserve their earlier disagreement within its actual statutory scope; neither treats the 2000 implementing decree as new assent to the 1997 rationale.
- **I2 sufficient prestatehood congressional retention on this record:** Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer — five Associates support the same sufficient ground and affirmance. Their rule requires the specific known estate and enacted consent/tenure treatment; the generic disclaimer and later ratification are not independent sufficient holdings.
- **I3 insufficient retention:** Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas — three Associates support rejection of this reservation ground, with vacatur and remand rather than an unconditional award of Idaho title.
- **No new poststatehood divestiture; independent alternatives:** All eight preserve I4's distinction between confirmation and confiscation. Affirmance makes the preserved alternatives unnecessary to decide. The three I3 supporters require lawful lower-court treatment of the unreached aboriginal-title ground; they do not equate its preservation with success. No Justice adopts the separate relation-back theory on the presently inspected sources.
- **Judgment scope:** All eight keep Heyburn outside this review's decree. The five affirmers sustain the actual title, possession, injunction, and counterclaim consequences on the reservation ground. The three vacating Justices would undo reliance on that ground and return the remaining claims for adjudication; the Court should not invent a pending stay, automatic interim injunction, or fresh factual findings to describe that remand.

## Opinion form and separate-writing function

A signed plenary opinion can state the two-showing rule, explain its record-specific satisfaction, and affirm the defined trust-title decree. A substantive dissent can explain why the enacted access and negotiation obligations do not clearly accomplish state-title defeat and why the preserved alternative prevents an unconditional contrary decree. Scalia and Thomas may share that rationale with Kennedy without importing their different post-1953 Alaska statutory objection. No separate concurrence is needed merely to repeat the two distinct requirements or the Heyburn limit. No author or assignment has been selected.
