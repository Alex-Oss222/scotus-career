# Lackawanna County District Attorney v. Coss — independent non-Stone commitments

Event: merits, April 25, 2001, No. 99-1884. Stage: independent modeling; not a Court disposition. Eight Associate Justices participate. Authorized inputs: frozen April 24–25 neutral packet, preparation receipt and Coss entering-law slice. The latter contains the same substantive authorities as the Daniels slice, verified by a file-content comparison apart from the matter heading. No current-matter comparator or controlled position supplement was received. Research cutoff: April 25, 2001; same-date Daniels and Randell supply no earlier law.

## Components and alternatives

The federal petition was filed in September 1994 and challenges current confinement. Maleng permits that construction without treating the independently expired 1986 sentences as current custody. Garlotte's continuous aggregate is different. The former chapter 153 framework applies; neither the amended section 2254(d) gate nor the later factual presumption is silently substituted for it.

Three independent questions remain: whether the present-sentence vehicle permits review of these old predicates; whether the 1987 original state challenge failed to receive meaningful adjudication through no fault of Coss, with an unavailable or ineffective state process under the applicable exhaustion law; and whether an actually unconstitutional predicate adversely affected the current sentence. None answers the others. Appointment of counsel and a prosecutor's answer are not a merits ruling. Fourteen years without a ruling is material evidence of ineffective process but not a finding of faultlessness or abandonment. Removing criminal-history points settles those points; it does not alone disprove discretionary influence. Conversely, a reference to the criminal record does not itself establish a longer sentence.

Path A permits the current-sentence challenge under ordinary habeas requirements without an additional categorical expired-predicate bar. It relies on the express present-custody statute, Maleng's construction and the distinction between Custis's sentencing forum and this collateral proceeding. Its strongest objection is that enhancement could become a means of reopening every stale conviction. Its answer preserves actual exhaustion or statutory excuse, default, proof and present-sentence causation; no writ issues from past error alone.

Path B recognizes a narrower avenue when a properly pursued earlier constitutional challenge received no meaningful adjudication through no fault of the prisoner. A diligent claimant who could not obtain a decision differs from one who allowed usable review to lapse. Its strongest objection is that the statute's ineffective-process provision ordinarily excuses exhaustion rather than creates custody or a freestanding old-conviction jurisdiction. The answer accepts that distinction: present custody is the jurisdictional subject; the exception concerns the permissible predicate inquiry within that subject, and exhaustion remains separately required or excused. No freestanding writ against an expired judgment follows.

Path C extends the finality bar to expired counseled predicates even when ordinary review may have failed, preserving the exceptional complete deprivation of counsel and independently invalidated predicates. Its strongest objection is that an unexplained state failure to adjudicate could then insulate a potentially unconstitutional source of current custody. The answer treats present federal habeas as review of the operative current judgment, not a replacement forum for the expired judgment, and maintains the distinction between exhaustion excuses and authority to reopen. This requires an express extension of Custis; it is not an already controlling rule.

## Compatible scope and remand

Six Associates support vacatur and a bounded remand; Scalia and Thomas support reversal of the indirect-review permission. All eight agree on treating the petition as a challenge to present custody rather than a freestanding attack on an expired sentence, and on the separate need for an actual constitutional defect and adverse effect before relief. Agreement about present custody is not agreement about what predicate inquiry follows.

The six remanding Associates directly support the narrower permission: where the prisoner properly pursued the original challenge but, through no fault of his own, received no meaningful adjudication, the current-sentence court may examine the predicate subject to the applicable statutory requirements. Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg would also allow a broader channel subject to ordinary gates, but can join the limited sufficient proposition without making their broader view controlling. O'Connor, Kennedy and Breyer would not join an unqualified reopening rule.

The remand must determine the explanation and responsibility for state inaction and whether the applicable state corrective process is unavailable or ineffective; it may not require fictitious exhaustion of a genuinely ineffective process or presume every delay excuses default. If a lawful path to review exists, the court must address the preserved performance/prejudice issues under Strickland and the required effect on current custody. It must review actual findings under the historically applicable standards, not replace them with the Supreme Court's conjecture. Existing findings receive their proper treatment; remand is not permission to disregard them or an automatic new evidentiary hearing.

No present writ, declaration that the old trial was constitutionally invalid, ordered resentencing, fixed reduction or release follows. Relief, if ultimately warranted, must remove custody attributable to the established unconstitutional predicate while preserving independent lawful punishment. The precise delay history and sentencing evidence would be needed for a final application; the neutral packet supports the bounded channel ruling and remand without resolving those factual gaps. The stronger no-effect ground cannot presently be adopted merely from elimination of numerical points.
