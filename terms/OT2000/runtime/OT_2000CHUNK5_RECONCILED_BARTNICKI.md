## Common components and exact boundaries

**Accepted procedural and statutory predicates.** The two petitions concern the same interlocutory judgment. The certified issue assumes intentional unlawful interception, respondents' knowledge or reason to know, nonparticipation and lawful receipt. Those premises are not final findings resolving the disputed interception intent. The federal text reaches intentional disclosure or attempted disclosure with knowledge or reason to know that the communication was intercepted in violation of the relevant prohibition; it is not confined to the interceptor. The Pennsylvania disclosure rule presents the parallel constitutional application. Avoidance cannot rewrite either into an interceptor-only rule. The United States' intervention supplies no separate private damages claim.

**Protected activity and classification.** All eight Associates accept that professional status is not dispositive, that source-based neutrality can coexist with a direct speech burden, and that a general-law label does not finish constitutional review. The actual disclosures include Yocum's communications to school-board members and delivery to Vopper, Vopper's broadcasts and the other media disclosures. Delivering recorded words for communication is expression; Yocum is not excluded because he serves as an intermediary or lacks professional-media status. These conclusions do not themselves settle the justification dispute.

**The sufficient common holding.** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support the rule that the federal and Pennsylvania provisions cannot impose the requested disclosure damages on these defendants, who lawfully received an accurate recording without participating in its unlawful interception and disclosed this conversation of public importance concerning the contentious public-school bargaining dispute and apparent physical threats against participants. Knowledge or reason to know of the interception's illegality does not defeat the defense on those assumptions. This is an as-applied constitutional defense, not an Act-wide invalidation or a declaration that the statutory elements were not otherwise met.

**Explanation of deterrence.** The six recognize deterrence of unlawful interception as legitimate but find it insufficient to justify this direct burden on these nonparticipants' public-concern disclosures. The ordinary target is the person responsible for interception; suppressing a lawful recipient's speech to discourage a stranger requires a justification responsive to the actual speech burden. General incentive reasoning does not, by itself, establish adequate fit here. This conclusion does not hold that deterrence is never sufficient, that recipients cannot encourage wrongdoing indirectly, or that Congress must conduct an empirical study in every case. The four broader-protection Justices—Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg—do not treat a recorded violence statement as an indispensable trigger for all protected public-concern disclosure. O'Connor and Breyer require the actual unusual public interest and privacy balance; their judgments cannot establish the four's broader application standing alone.

**Explanation of the independent privacy interest.** All six affirmers acknowledge that later dissemination can inflict an injury beyond the original interception and can chill candid private conversation. That is an independent legitimate interest even if a prohibition has little incremental deterrent effect. Here the public-school negotiations, the participants' voluntary public roles and the apparent pressure involving physical harm create a strong public interest in the actual words. The settlement does not erase their relevance, and no finding of an ongoing danger at the publication moment or punishable true threat is required for this as-applied result. O'Connor and Breyer emphasize the unusually reduced legitimate privacy expectation in this particular conversation and the corresponding public interest. They do not say that public figures surrender all conversational privacy.

**Competing judgment.** Scalia and Thomas accept privacy's separate speech-enhancing function and find the restriction adequately fitted. It selects knowing disclosure of an unlawfully intercepted conversation, not a viewpoint, and may reduce the incentive to route unlawful interceptions through innocent recipients. They regard the lawful-source publication authorities as distinguishable because the speakers here kept their conversation private and the disclosure itself was proscribed. Their legislative-deference argument makes no adjudicated empirical finding about how many interceptions were deterred. They reject the constitutional defense, not every possible statutory defense, and do not establish liability automatically.

**Reservations.** The decision protects neither a participant in interception nor unlawful procurement or independently unlawful acquisition. Rice's actual purposeful-assistance holding remains. Lawful receipt is not a universal answer to every later-use restriction. The constitutional rules for trade secrets, domestic gossip, intimate personal material or other purely private information are not decided. The statutes' separate use provisions, an interceptor's own liability and valid independently binding duties remain outside this disclosure holding. No general press exemption, special immunity for newsgathering offenses, closed multifactor test or newly mandatory scrutiny tier follows.

## Precedent treatment available for the common opinion

- Daily Mail, Florida Star and Landmark supply strong protection for truthful public-significance publication lawfully obtained by the speaker. Their unlawful-source reservation is resolved only for the present nonparticipating lawful recipients and actual disclosures, not treated as already answered by those cases.
- Turner supplies content classification, meaningful scrutiny of an expressive burden and appropriate predictive deference. Its cable holding neither immunizes this statute from review nor imposes an empirical-study requirement.
- Rice distinguishes purposeful, materially effective assistance from ordinary publication or awareness of another's wrongdoing. It remains applicable to its actual predicates and does not establish universal recipient immunity.
- Sheets and Denius preserve the confined constitutional significance of objectively intimate information against official conduct. Their rules are not extended into private-publication liability or a finding that these negotiations are constitutionally intimate.
- Cohen and Branzburg preserve the absence of a categorical press privilege and liability for independently unlawful acts. They do not dispense with review of a direct publication burden.
- New York Times v. United States illustrates the distinct prior-restraint problem and does not itself settle subsequent damages. The present result must rest on the actual publication and justification analysis, not a transferred automatic prior-restraint rule.
- Harper & Row supplies the significance of choosing not to speak publicly; Katz supplies conversational privacy's importance in its governmental-search setting. Neither independently determines this private disclosure claim.
