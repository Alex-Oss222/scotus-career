## Adversarial comparison

**Affirm the Fourth Circuit.** When breach of an established general maritime duty of reasonable care causes death, general maritime law supplies a wrongful-death action unless an applicable statute precludes it. Moragne's removal of the death/nonfatal-injury anomaly supports this extension to negligence; the relevant congressional provisions preserve third-party actions rather than exclude this defendant's pleaded responsibility. Recognizing the action completes a recognized duty and a recognized death remedy. It does not create a new duty or override an exclusive statutory regime.

The strongest contrary argument is that the comprehensive maritime statutes make any judicially recognized death action an intrusion into Congress's allocation. That argument would carry weight if the actual defendant, claimant or geographic setting fell within a conflicting exclusive remedy. No such conflict is established here. Preserving third-party negligence claims and abolishing specified unseaworthiness actions are different legislative choices. Neither the nonseaman classification alone nor a failed state claim creates immunity from general maritime law.

**Reverse and restore categorical dismissal.** This path treats Moragne's facts as a substantive limit and Congress's neighboring schemes as occupying the field. Its strongest support is Miles's discipline against judicial supplementation of Congress's selected relief. Its decisive defect is the absence of the applicable exclusive scheme that makes that discipline operative. Yamaha and Dooley require classification by actual coverage. A maritime third party's negligence does not become nonactionable merely because the injury was fatal. No material coverage dispute in the supplied pleading posture requires an interim vacatur instead of deciding the categorical question.
