## 1. Questions, record and component boundaries

The lawful channel is merits review of the Seventh Circuit's completed affirmance of a Rule 12 dismissal. The granted issue is issue preclusion. The district court used claim preclusion; those grounds are analytically distinct. No independent jurisdictional defect, preservation bar to the granted issue or reason to dismiss the writ is established.

Adair alleges that the bank's attorneys knowingly overstated the value of his automobile in a preconfirmation proof of claim. The loan financed $16,483.22; the filed collateral valuation was $19,841.43. Notice and sufficient time to object are conceded for this review. He did not object before confirmation. The ensuing adversary proceeding ended with dismissal of the bankruptcy case, rather than an identified merits determination of valuation. A later return to Chapter 13 does not itself remove his capacity to sue on his statutory claim. Fraud is an allegation, not a finding. No contested valuation hearing or adjudicated factual valuation is identified in the supplied procedural account. That conclusion describes the record supporting the dismissal, not an inference that unrecovered papers do not exist.

The three components used below are:

- **A — Issue preclusion:** Does unopposed allowance and plan confirmation, with adequate notice and opportunity to object, by itself establish actual litigation of collateral value for a later FDCPA action?
- **B — Statutory effect and distinct defenses:** What remains binding under the Bankruptcy Code, and what does rejection of issue preclusion leave undecided about claim preclusion, FDCPA coverage and other defenses?
- **C — Judgment and remedy:** Whether to sustain dismissal or vacate and remand, and what the remand permits or requires.

## 2. Entering law and the supported competing paths

### A. Actual litigation and bankruptcy administration

Dodd v. Hood River County, November 19, 1998, Part I, written by Stevens and joined by every other Associate, applies the lawful Oregon preclusive effect of a fact actually and necessarily determined with a full and fair opportunity in proceedings binding the parties. Its profitable-use finding rested on competing evidence actually presented and ensuing adjudication. Failure to request every available evidentiary procedure did not undo that actual presentation. Its state-law and section 1738 setting supplies no automatic federal-bankruptcy rule, but each Justice's acceptance of the distinction between a litigated fact and the entire cause is directly relevant. Montana v. United States, as identified and quoted in the complete judgment below, supplies the ordinary actually-and-necessarily-determined principle. The federal bankruptcy judgment's effect requires federal analysis, not automatic importation of Oregon law.

The strongest affirmance path treats sections 502(a) and 1327(a), Rule 3001(f), the opportunity to object and actual plan confirmation as an integrated adjudication conclusively establishing secured value. The complaint cannot succeed on its stated theory without contradicting that value. The strongest objection is that this path turns an opportunity to contest into actual litigation and substitutes claim-finality cases for the particular element of issue preclusion. Deemed allowance and prima facie evidence are real consequences, but neither is synonymous with adversarial resolution of a factual issue.

The vacatur path holds that the supplied unopposed process does not satisfy actual litigation merely because an objection was available. Its strongest objection is the risk of strategic silence followed by collateral damages litigation that practically attacks the plan. That objection warrants preserving the plan's statutory force and every properly supported distinct defense. It does not itself supply the missing element of issue preclusion. The retained question is the defense's fit, not a general permission to contradict bankruptcy judgments.

Taylor v. Freeland & Kronz, April 21, 1992, enforces an enacted no-objection consequence: under section 522(l) and the applicable Rule 4003(b), the scheduled property becomes exempt after the prescribed 30-day objection period, measured from the end of the creditors' meeting or relevant amendment, whichever is later, absent a timely extension. Rule 9006(b)(3) permits enlargement only on the specified rule's terms. Lack of a colorable exemption is no exception. That rule remains binding and supplies no general bankruptcy license to equate every unopposed assertion with a litigated fact. The present action requests damages from attorneys, not allowance of a late exemption objection.

### B. Binding consequences and the source of preclusion law

Section 502(a) deems a proof of claim filed under section 501 allowed unless a party in interest objects; the express party-in-interest inclusion covers a creditor of a general partner of a partnership debtor in Chapter 7. Rule 3001(f) gives a conforming proof prima facie evidentiary force. Section 1327(a) binds debtor and creditor to plan provisions whether or not the creditor's claim is provided for and whether the creditor objected, accepted or rejected. Sections 1327(b) and (c) govern vesting and freedom from covered claims or interests, each subject to contrary plan or confirmation-order provision; subsection (c) concerns property vesting under (b) and a creditor provided for by the plan. None of these consequences is erased by the proposed decision. None establishes, without identification of what occurred, that a particular fact was contested and decided.

Semtek International Inc. v. Lockheed Martin Corp., February 27, 2001, is Scalia's unanimous opinion. Its federal common-law rule ordinarily incorporates rendering-state claim-preclusion law for federal diversity judgments on state-law claims unless a specifically demonstrated federal interest conflicts. Its Rule 41(b) holding rejects an automatic universal consequence from a rendering-court merits designation. This case concerns federal bankruptcy and issue preclusion; the rendering-state incorporation rule is not carried over. Semtek supports attention to the particular effect claimed, not an automatic answer here.

Heintz v. Jenkins, April 18, 1995, is Souter's unanimous opinion rejecting a categorical exemption for attorneys regularly collecting covered consumer debts through litigation. It leaves statutory definitions, activity-specific exceptions, conduct elements and defenses operative. The later statutory exception in section 1692e(11) for formal pleadings concerns the specified disclosure requirement; it is not an exception to all of section 1692e. Sections 1692e and 1692f(1) must be applied to the actual alleged representation or unauthorized collection. An unsuccessful collection claim is not by itself an FDCPA violation. The unpresented question whether an FDCPA claim may ever be predicated on a bankruptcy filing is not resolved by the lower opinion or this commitment.

Hartford Underwriters, May 30, 2000, is Thomas's unanimous opinion enforcing the actor and remedy designated in section 506(c), with genuinely separate remedies reserved on their own conditions. Pioneer, March 24, 1993, governs excusable neglect where Rule 9006(b)(1) permits it, attributes counsel's conduct and preserves (b)(2) prohibitions and (b)(3) limitations. Neither authorizes a new equitable exception or conclusively decides the present defense.

### C. Remedy and lawful limits

Vacatur removes the Seventh Circuit's issue-preclusion basis and returns the action for further proceedings. It does not find fraud, determine collateral value, award damages, alter a plan, extend a bankruptcy deadline or determine the full effect of the bankruptcy case's dismissal. The lower courts may address a distinct properly preserved and sufficiently supported defense, including claim preclusion, under the correct rule. No such defense is declared established. A blanket bankruptcy/FDCPA displacement rule, new litigation immunity, sanctions or revocation relief is not selected on an issue the parties did not present. The availability of sanctions and revocation procedures does not establish their predicates or their exclusivity. Reinstating consideration of the complaint is not a liability judgment.
