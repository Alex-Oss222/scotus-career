# OT_2000CHUNK5

# Rogers v. Tennessee

# SECTION II: STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

## A. STATUS

* **Approval Status:** Approved.
* **Current-Through Baseline:** Precedent effective through June 29, 2000, subject to any intervening decision effective before the stated event date.
* **Conditions:** Subject to intervening controlling precedent and material corrections to the record.

## B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse the Tennessee Supreme Court judgment insofar as it sustains the murder conviction by retroactively abolishing the year-and-a-day limitation, and remand to vacate that conviction and determine any lawful remaining disposition.
* **Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** Tennessee may abolish the limitation for later conduct. For Rogers, the undisputed interval cannot be cured by a new opportunity to prove that death occurred sooner. The mandate should not decide an unpresented lesser-offense judgment or compel release independently of other lawful custody; the state court must identify any authorized remaining disposition.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: A surviving offense limitation cannot be removed after the conduct merely because it is obsolete

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reach the preserved federal due-process question. Accept the Tennessee Supreme Court’s determination that the common-law limitation survived the statute. Separate the constitutional application date from whether medicine and policy justify prospective abolition.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether a defendant may be convicted of murder through a judicial change that removes a state-law temporal limitation acknowledged to have governed at the time of his act.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the conduct-time protection of United States v. Lanier and adopt its bounded extension to this acknowledged substantive state-law limitation. Stone extends the rule to the year-and-a-day doctrine. Carmell v. Texas reinforces the importance of retaining the earlier legal conditions but is not treated as literally controlling judicial action under the Ex Post Facto Clause.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Where the state’s highest court acknowledges that an offense-limiting rule remained in force when the defendant acted, its later abolition cannot sustain this conviction solely because the rule had become medically outdated or had been abolished elsewhere. Fair warning must come from the prior law’s reasonably apparent reach, not from reasons why the law should change.
* **Reach Within the Question:** The holding addresses the conceded survival and subsequent abolition shown here. It preserves ordinary judicial interpretation, foreseeable clarification and prospective common-law reform. It does not convert every procedural change into a new offense or demand personal reliance on a legal technicality.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The State has a strong reason to abandon an arbitrary interval when reliable proof can establish causation after a longer time. That reason supports lawmaking authority, not necessarily the effective date. Tennessee resolved the critical ambiguity against the State’s first theory: the legislature had not previously abolished the rule. The court therefore cannot substitute the statute’s general homicide language for its own conclusion about the complete law governing homicide. Rogers’s violence was plainly punishable, but notice of serious assault does not erase a surviving limit on murder liability. Nor can a rule’s infrequent practical use establish its absence. Common-law development is legitimate; once a court admits it is removing an existing substantive boundary, the protection against retrospective enlargement must have real content. Here the former rule and undisputed interval supply a concrete basis for relief without questioning the medical examiner’s causation account or giving the defendant a general immunity for his conduct. The conduct-time legal boundary is decisive; modern medicine explains why prospective change is sensible but does not establish prior notice. Bouie and Lanier's fair-warning principle therefore governs this acknowledged removal of an existing limit. The rule protects people against retrospective enlargement without freezing legitimate prospective common-law development.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The availability and form of a lesser-offense disposition are outside the presented federal question and are not resolved on this record. No broader criminal judgment is inferred.
