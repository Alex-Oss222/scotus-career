# Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Leatherman Tool Group, Inc.

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Leatherman Tool Group, Inc.
* **Citation or Docket:** 532 U.S. 424; No. 99-2035
* **October Term:** 2000.
* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS, review of the judgment identified below.
* **Simulated Event Date:** 2001-05-14 (historical decision date).
* **Question Presented:** Whether a court of appeals must independently review the constitutional excessiveness of a punitive-damages award, or may defer to the district court under an abuse-of-discretion standard, and whether the Seventh Amendment changes that allocation.
* **Question Granted:** The October 10, 2000 order expressly limited review to: “What is the standard of review of a trial court's ruling on a challenge to the constitutionality of a punitive damage award?” [531 U.S. 923](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep531/usrep531orders/usrep531orders.pdf).
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Ninth Circuit affirmed the district court’s refusal to reduce a $4.5 million punitive award under an abuse-of-discretion standard. In a separate published ruling, it set aside the injunction against copying the tool’s functional appearance. The punitive-damages judgment is the judgment presented here.
* **Relief Requested:** Cooper seeks vacatur and independent appellate examination of constitutional excessiveness. Leatherman seeks affirmance under deferential review. Neither the review-standard question nor an adverse ruling on that question itself fixes a replacement punitive amount.
* **Material Record:** Cooper prepared to market its ToolZall before production was complete. Employees modified a Leatherman Pocket Survival Tool, removed its trademark, and used photographs and a touched-up drawing as illustrations of Cooper’s product. The materials appeared at a national hardware show and in later marketing. Cooper ordered a promotional recall but some materials continued circulating. A jury found passing off, false advertising and unfair competition, awarded $50,000 compensation, and found the culpability necessary for $4.5 million punitive damages. It separately found infringement by the original tool but no resulting infringement damages; the Ninth Circuit subsequently held the appearance functional and copying lawful. The district judge sustained the punitive amount as proportional and needed to deter an enterprise of Cooper’s size. The appellate court accepted that evaluation while recognizing that the advertised photographs did not perceptibly promise superior features absent from the product delivered. These distinctions matter to culpability and harm: lawful product copying is not itself the adjudicated advertising wrong. [Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Leatherman Tool Group, Inc.](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-532/pdf/USREPORTS-532-424.pdf).
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Cooper preserved its federal excessiveness objection and challenges the appellate review actually supplied. The underlying compensation and the separate functional-design ruling are not reopened by selecting a constitutional review standard. A court must distinguish fact findings, state-law discretionary assessment, and the federal constitutional boundary. A challenge to whether Oregon may receive part of the award is not presented merely because the judgment directs 60 percent to a state compensation account.
* **Relevant Dates:** The governing provisions and conduct are evaluated in their historically applicable versions; the event date does not retroactively supply notice or alter the record.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** No separately listed companion requires an additional judgment in this module.
* **Participation Matter:** No case-specific recusal or nonparticipation is identified.

## B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** Precedent effective through June 29, 2000, subject to any intervening decision effective before the stated event date.
* **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** No additional same-term decision is relied upon.
* **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** Any earlier effective holding in TrafFix Devices, Inc. v. Marketing Displays, Inc. may affect a disputed functionality premise and must be considered before adjudication. The Ninth Circuit’s separate ruling establishes the present procedural posture.
* **Institutional or Procedural Law:** The Seventh Amendment protects jury-tried historical facts and restricts judicial reexamination. Federal new-trial practice and appellate review must be allocated by function. Review of a constitutional limitation can be independent without a new appellate trial of misconduct, causation or loss.

### Issue 1: Constitutional excessiveness and appellate review

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore establishes by five direct joins a binding substantive due-process limit on grossly excessive punitive awards, with qualified guideposts of reprehensibility, actual or supported potential harm, and comparable sanctions. Honda Motor Co. v. Oberg requires meaningful review of amount or an adequate functional substitute. TXO Production Corp. v. Alliance Resources has a majority-supported, record-specific procedural holding but no controlling numerical or potential-harm formula. United States v. Bajakajian independently requires de novo constitutional proportionality review for punitive forfeiture and clear-error review of underlying facts.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore preserves independent substantive protection despite adequate procedure, rejects mechanical ratios, and separates evidence of other conduct from punishment for that conduct. The forfeiture review rule supplies an analogy, not an already-decided rule for every punitive jury award. Ornelas v. United States similarly separates ultimate legal evaluation from historical findings, with no universal mixed-question rule.
* **Other Governing Law:** The Due Process Clause supplies the asserted constitutional limitation; the Seventh Amendment governs the civil jury allocation. State punitive entitlement, federal trial procedure, and constitutional excessiveness remain separate inquiries.
* **Material Other Authority:** Gasperini v. Center for Humanities, Inc. applies state compensatory limits through district-court new-trial review and requires deferential appellate assessment of that supported discretionary valuation, while independently correcting legal error. Its express treatment preserves the distinct protections of BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore and Honda Motor Co. v. Oberg rather than deciding punitive constitutional review.
* **Existing Rule's Reach:** A reviewing court must respect supported facts about conduct, injury and intent. Neither an enterprise’s wealth nor speculative market injury alone justifies a punitive sum. Constitutional review cannot punish copying that the separate judgment treats as lawful merely by relabeling it advertising culpability.
* **Open Point:** Whether the legal boundary under BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore requires independent appellate judgment when the punitive sum was set by a jury, and how that judgment coexists with the Seventh Amendment.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

Cooper argues that a constitutional maximum cannot vary with the district judge’s discretionary tolerance. Independent application gives the substantive guarantee operative force and keeps the court of appeals from approving punishment for lawful copying or conjectural profits. Its strongest application point is the mismatch between the limited advertising injury found and the district court’s broader assessment of competition and deterrence. Leatherman answers that the jury made a record-based judgment about deliberate deception and repeated conduct, the trial judge examined it, and Gasperini v. Center for Humanities, Inc. protects the trial court’s superior position. It argues that treating the amount as a legal conclusion strips the jury’s moral and factual assessment of ordinary protection. These arguments must confront distinct propositions: independent constitutional review does not necessarily authorize an appellate damages valuation, while adequate trial process does not extinguish a substantive due-process objection.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court could require independent constitutional review while retaining deference to supported historical findings, vacate the appellate punitive judgment, and leave application to the Ninth Circuit. It could instead hold that meaningful discretionary review adequately protects the constitutional boundary and affirm on the review issue, subject to an independently preserved legal misapplication. An application ruling setting or invalidating a particular amount would require examination of the actual record and permissible remedy beyond merely selecting the standard. The limited grant concerns the standard of review; overruling BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore's substantive rule lies outside that grant. Disagreement with it cannot silently erase its controlling force.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** A holding can address the appellate standard for constitutional punitive excessiveness without prescribing every state system of review, a numerical ceiling, or independent appellate valuation of compensation.

**Material Uncertainty:** The full trial record may affect the appraisal of threatened harm and the source of the jury’s punitive measure. The review-standard issue can be resolved without finding a particular amount lawful. No inference that every circulated promotional item generated a lost sale is warranted.
