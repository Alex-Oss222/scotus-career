### A. The five-Justice statutory commitment

For the previously admitted persons presented here, section 1231(a)(6) authorizes detention beyond the removal period only for a period reasonably necessary to accomplish removal. Once there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, continuing detention under this provision is unauthorized. Neither a bare final order nor general danger or flight concerns independently supplies an unlimited civil-commitment power after the removal purpose can no longer reasonably be accomplished.

The statute's removal setting, discretionary extension and express supervised-release alternative support this construction. The serious physical-liberty question reinforces it through constitutional avoidance. The five acknowledge the competing textual argument: Congress specified criminal and risk categories, permitted detention beyond the ordinary period, and supplied no express endpoint. Their answer is that these categories identify persons who may remain detained beyond the initial period while removal remains a genuine prospect; they do not clearly create lifelong preventive custody detached from that purpose. The word permitting detention does not itself confer unbounded discretion.

Foucha and Jackson supply the relationship between custody and a continuing lawful ground; National Center confines delegated discretion to the immigration scheme; Flores concerns temporary juvenile care and preserved individual challenges. Actual Hendricks and Seling require independent attention to punishment and custody authority, but neither supplies a general due-process holding approving the Kansas confinement or requiring a psychiatric diagnosis in every preventive regime. The five assume no proved punitive operation here and do not require a punishment showing to obtain statutory relief. They resolve the statutory claim without an independent constitutional invalidation, universal dangerousness burden, compulsory psychiatric model or ruling on every inadmissible person.

### A6. The implementation rule and its grounds

The five recognize six months as a presumptively reasonable removal-detention period. It is the six-month period in the removal process, not a new six months added after the ordinary 90-day period. It is a presumption, not an absolute statutory ceiling, an order releasing everyone at its expiration, or an immunity from other properly supported unlawful-custody claims during that interval.

After that period, the detainee must provide good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future. The government must then furnish evidence sufficient to rebut that showing. Passage of six months alone does not compel release; detention may continue until the absence of that significant likelihood has been determined. Conversely, an unanswered supported showing cannot be defeated by indefinite theoretical possibility, good faith alone, the removal order alone, or danger alone. As prior post-removal confinement lengthens, the reasonably foreseeable future must become correspondingly shorter. This preserves both the detainee's initial showing and the government's evidentiary response.

The court assesses supported attempts, foreign responses, negotiations, travel-document prospects, actual receiving arrangements and material detainee conduct. It gives appropriate leeway to executive immigration and foreign-policy expertise, administrative needs and supported assessments, without surrendering the legal-authority determination. No treaty is indispensable, no proof of permanent impossibility is required, and diplomatic uncertainty is not automatically failure. If removal remains reasonably foreseeable, risk of further crimes and flight may help justify detention within the reasonable removal interval. If it does not, the court must provide effective supervised-release relief under the statute, subject to the actual posture and independent lawful custody.

The historical implementation appears at 533 U.S. 699–701. The independent model already accepted the supported-showing and government-response structure, shrinking relevance of vague predictions, and effective relief. Its remaining objection was the lack of a justified interval. The already-validated former six-month removal period supplies a legal reference point; the current regulation's ordinary district-to-headquarters transition after 90 days and up to three additional months supplies an administrative reference point, with all its timing exceptions intact. Uniform judicial administration and a meaningful initial opportunity for executive arrangements justify using that interval as a rebuttable device. Neither reference establishes a statutory maximum or proves how long every deportation takes. The five's historical direct joins are strong additional evidence that this limited device answers, rather than contradicts, their individual concerns on unchanged material premises.

The historical majority also referred to an earlier congressional constitutional concern through a 1956 jurisdictional statement. That additional legislative-intent inference is **not adopted as a newly established neutral fact** here. The present implementation rests on the supplied statutory and regulatory references, the practical reason for a rebuttable rule, and the Justice-specific comparison. No unprovided legislative document, current private preference or mandatory administrative schedule supplies the number.

Zadvydas's time at liberty is not detention counted toward this presumption; his past extended detention and present status are part of the actual remand. The decision does not reset his case to a fictional first day of confinement or order a new six-month detention. Any proposed renewed custody must satisfy current lawful authority and the governing standards. No unpresented universal restart, tolling or aggregation rule is created.

### B. The contrary statutory commitment and the distinct constitutional positions

Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas read section 1231(a)(6) to permit continued discretionary detention of its covered persons without a judicial foreseeable-removal prerequisite. Its criminal, danger and noncompliance categories serve protective purposes as well as execution of removal. Foreign refusal, lack of an agreement or inability to predict a date therefore does not alone terminate statutory authority. They reject A's construction and the numerical presumption as conditions Congress did not enact. The same detention phrase's application to both inadmissible and removable categories is a substantial objection to implying different statutory meanings, although the present litigation does not adjudicate all initial-entry cases.

Scalia and Thomas reject the claimed general constitutional entitlement to supervised release into this country based on unforeseeable removal after a valid final removal order. Their precise description of the right, drawn from their Flores and Foucha positions, treats that adjudicated status as relevant; it does not falsely find that these persons never entered. Their historical Mezei analogy concerns the asserted substantive right to release, not a historical finding of nonadmission. They accept the enacted discretionary custody channel and the actual opportunity for release review. They do not adopt Kennedy's distinct affirmative procedural-liberty rationale or the model's K rule. The general challenge fails; a new indefinite remand to invent another constitutional theory is not their disposition. This does not authorize torture, punishment forbidden by governing law, sham exercises of authority or refusal to perform a legally required procedure. No such individual violation is found, and no new remedy for an unpresented violation is prescribed.

Kennedy also rejects the statutory limitation, but recognizes that both removable and inadmissible persons remain protected against arbitrary or capricious detention and unlawful punishment. Genuine danger or flight risk may support detention even when removal cannot be forecast. The current regulation provides meaningful means to contest continued custody, rather than a merely nominal promise of review; its existence does not establish compliant administration in either case. Denial of the prescribed process in an arbitrary or categorical manner can be challenged through habeas. He does not impose a new government burden, a universal hearing calendar or an automatic adversarial judicial release trial.

His historical dissent expressly left for a proper concrete case the severity of danger needed, the adequacy of review when an actual danger or flight finding lacks justification, and the burden's operation. Its express distinction between the general authority challenge and an improper application appears at 533 U.S. 723–725. The validated predecision material supplies the general procedural and less-restrictive-alternatives arguments; it establishes no concrete application defect compelling these new K remands. The old clear-and-convincing regulatory wording was replaced before decision. Neither the current alien-satisfaction language nor possible supervision is by itself adjudged unconstitutional. These are reservations on a resolved general challenge, not unfinished additional claims requiring an unsupported remand.

### Review machinery, statutory exceptions and supervision

The current regulation is the December 21 replacement. It requires the alien's satisfaction showing and the stated findings concerning unavailable or impracticable immediate removal, present and likely future nonviolence, community safety, compliance and no significant flight risk before discretionary release. It provides notice, records review, reasons, a possible district interview, headquarters review and a required personal interview when the stated nonrelease circumstances occur. A two-member panel must agree; a split adds a third member and permits a majority recommendation; the final official is not bound by the panel. Chosen assistance is at no government expense and subject to security and attendance limits; translation follows the stated discretionary determination. Current decisions have no administrative appeal, except completion of Board cases pending on December 21 under the transition rule.

Timing is qualified: the initial pre-expiration review can be waived by requesting additional preparation time; district responsibility can continue for the next three months; headquarters review follows the specified transfer points ordinarily or as soon thereafter as practicable. Subsequent review ordinarily begins approximately annually. Material-change requests may be made no more often than every three months, with a response approximately within 90 days. Shorter discretionary reviews, documented postponement for prompt proper removal or other good cause, unforeseen or emergent circumstances, and the distinct transition schedules remain. The rule is not an absolute six-month deadline or automatic annual release trial.

Travel-document prospects must be considered; ongoing efforts, forthcoming documents, impracticable or public-interest immediate-removal exceptions, and the rule dispensing with review when removal is ready to execute remain distinct. The rule also addresses cooperation, sponsorship or placement where ordered, conditions, discretionary employment authorization and withdrawal of approval before release. Revocation may rest on served release purposes, a condition violation, appropriate enforcement or commencement of removal, or conduct or another circumstance making release inappropriate. It requires reasons and a prompt informal response opportunity; subsequent review includes contested-fact evaluation and an interview ordinarily expected within approximately three months, followed by the stated periodic process. These regulatory provisions do not settle the statutory or constitutional limit of the custody they administer.

For A's five supporters, improved review does not cure detention after its statutory ground ends: considering travel prospects is not the same as requiring release when the requisite prospect fails. They need not invalidate the regulation facially to enforce A/A6. Kennedy finds the prescribed process material to rejecting the general challenge while preserving concrete adequacy questions. Scalia and Thomas likewise do not infer constitutional invalidity from duration or a government's failure to guarantee a removal date. None treats counsel's description of effective operation or statistical releases as a judicial finding about these detainees.

Section 1231(a)(1)(C) separately extends the removal period and permits detention upon the specified failure or refusal to make timely good-faith document applications, or conspiracy or action preventing removal. A foreign country's refusal does not prove those triggers. The government's allegations about Lithuanian applications, reporting appointments and citizenship possibilities, and Zadvydas's asserted renewed application, remain unresolved. Any reliance on that statute or a regulatory cooperation requirement needs its actual predicates; neither obstruction nor complete cooperation is inferred.

The ordinary 90-day period, its latest-of-three commencement rule and the mandatory initial detention categories remain distinct from discretionary paragraph (6). On lawful release, paragraph (3) requires supervision: periodic identification, necessary government-funded medical or psychiatric examination, sworn information and reasonable written conduct restrictions. A violation can support return to custody under actual lawful authority and process; no unlimited power to recommit on the old rationale is created. Release supplies neither a new admission or immigration status nor a waiver, asylum, cancellation or immunity from removal. A separate criminal, civil-commitment or specialized security regime requires its own established authority, predicates and protections. Terrorism and other special circumstances potentially justifying different preventive authority or heightened deference are not decided on these ordinary records.
