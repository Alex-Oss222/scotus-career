## Entering law and authority verification

### Controlling existing law and its limits

1. **Acker, June 21, 1999.** The operative standard is **Federal compensation taxation and occupational-license labels**, present operation effective Acker. Ginsburg's compensation-tax and source-discrimination portions, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas among others, examine actual operation rather than labels and reject the particular source-discrimination defense. They expressly leave the Article III Compensation Clause unresolved. Breyer's published disagreement concerns a direct local charge on performing federal functions; it does not create a general immunity from national income or payroll taxation. The distinct constitutional question here must be decided rather than treated as already resolved in Acker.
2. **Garvey Part I, May 14, 2001.** All six participating non-Stone Justices joined the rule that timely review of a later judgment permits review of a necessary antecedent ruling. An earlier lower-court mandate does not immunize its predicate from this Court's review. That is the ordinary review-channel rule; it did not involve the separate statutory Supreme Court affirmance adopted here.
3. **Semtek, February 27, 2001.** Scalia's unanimous opinion requires the particular judgment's legally established effect rather than an enlarged consequence inferred from a procedural label. It concerns the external preclusive effect of a diversity limitations judgment and does not decide Section 2109. Its source-and-scope discipline informs the comparison, without importing its state-law incorporation rule.
4. **Cleveland Indians, April 17, 2001.** Thomas's opinion, joined by Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, separately applies payroll-tax timing, rates and bases under an established regulatory interpretation. Scalia's concurrence treats the particular temporal problem as a statutory gap. Neither approach decides Article III protection, permits administrative convenience to override the Constitution, or supplies a claimant-specific amount here.
5. **Daniels, Coss and Randell, April 25–30, 2001.** Their different review channels and published positions supply relevant evidence concerning preservation of actual adjudications and the limits of review opportunities. They establish no Section 2109 rule. In particular, the fractured Coss judgment supplies no new general rule of access. The distinction between an actual judgment and an unprovided adjudication cannot itself nullify the expressly adopted statutory affirmance.

## Enhanced issue-specific comparison

### 1. Statutory affirmance, binding effect and later-judgment review

**Available paths.** The judges' strongest path gives Section 2109 the full interparty consequence of an equal-division affirmance: the liability question was resolved for this action even though no Supreme Court legal principle resulted. Garvey governs an unreviewed antecedent appellate ruling and cannot alone overcome this additional judgment. The government's strongest path distinguishes a conclusive final judgment in another suit from an interlocutory liability direction in an action still proceeding to damages. It preserves the earlier order's operation below while invoking this Court's authority to reconsider an antecedent pure-law premise on proper review of the later judgment.

The strongest objection to review is textual and institutional: Congress selected affirmance, and Pink separates binding party effect from precedent. The strongest objection to permanent insulation is that neither the statute nor Pink establishes that an affirmance makes an interlocutory direction immune from the reconsideration ordinarily possible within an unfinished action. Neither objection is answered by merely saying that the earlier Court lacked reasons.

**Scalia.** His Garvey Part I join supplies the current channel, while his Semtek opinion requires determining the effect actually attached to the particular disposition. His earlier Christianson join permits same-case reconsideration but demands a justification beyond ordinary disagreement. He permits the present review because the affirmed direction determined liability while leaving the action open for the later relief judgment, the Court has never collectively resolved the governing constitutional question, and the current review presents the continuing structural limit on Congress's taxation of judicial compensation. Taken together, those are the confined unusual circumstances warranting reconsideration here. They do not make every unreasoned affirmance provisional. His Daniels concurrence is a serious contrary analogy: a later vehicle does not automatically reopen an extant predicate. He distinguishes that separate expired judgment and statutory collateral route from this still-pending civil action. **Join barrier:** no opinion may reduce Section 2109 to an ordinary certiorari denial, infer a change in recusals, or announce that every later judgment permits reopening an earlier Supreme Court judgment. He will join express recognition of the earlier liability direction's binding force below and the confined same-action reconsideration holding.

**Kennedy.** His Garvey and Christianson joins support review power and restrained reconsideration. His current Semtek join insists on the particular judgment's actual scope. His Daniels join and Coss dissent provide the strongest resistance to treating renewed incentives as a new review opportunity. Here he distinguishes a reopened separate final conviction from ongoing appellate review of a still-unfinished pay action. The unusual combination identified for Scalia warrants first collective consideration of the constitutional liability premise without disturbing any separately final paid judgment. **Join barrier:** the Court must explain why this antecedent ruling remains susceptible to same-action reconsideration; no general constitutional-error exception to finality and no presumed absence of prior binding effect.

**Souter.** His Garvey and Semtek joins supply the same review-channel and judgment-scope premises. His Daniels dissent permits an expressly authorized current-judgment inquiry without pretending the older adjudication never existed; it also preserves ordinary procedural limits. He supports the confined reconsideration ground because the present action remains at the liability-to-damages stage and the earlier order supplied no collective legal resolution. Pink's party-binding rule is his strongest counterargument and must be acknowledged as operative while the earlier liability direction governed the remand. He does not read that rule, drawn from another controversy, as itself eliminating this Court's ordinary same-case power. **Join barrier:** neither absence of precedent nor general fairness alone suffices; the still-interlocutory predicate and proper later review are essential, and the power is exercised under the unusual circumstances specified here rather than made automatic.

**Thomas.** His Garvey join does not answer the additional statutory affirmance. His Acker removal position insists that one satisfied statutory condition cannot substitute for another, and his Daniels/Coss positions preserve the lawful effect of an outstanding adjudication despite a new vehicle. Applied here, Section 2109 supplies a prior Supreme Court affirmance, not merely an unanswered petition. Pink and Durant make that difference decisive for these parties. The government correctly observes that liability and damages are separate and that law of the case does not destroy the Court's power. Thomas nevertheless would not exercise reconsideration merely because no quorum previously permitted collective merits review: that is the very condition Congress addressed by prescribing an affirmance. No intervening controlling constitutional change, previously unknown fact or other independently established exceptional circumstance displaces liability. Acker expressly reserved the issue, and Garvey did not involve this intervening order. **Join barrier:** he will not join the proposed exceptional-reconsideration rule or its application. **Scope:** this is a nonjurisdictional refusal to reopen liability, not a bar to reviewing cure or relief. He can accept the Court's contrary threshold resolution as the institutional predicate for conditional merits joins, as his earlier Acker participation separately treated forum and independent merits components. He seeks no new quorum finding and attributes no 1996 vote.

**Ginsburg.** Her Garvey Part I and Semtek joins require lawful review of the present judgment while preserving the true reach of earlier adjudications. Her Randell separate join likewise rejects treating a remedial gap alone as permission to contradict an extant judgment. She permits review only because this is the same action's unfinished liability-to-remedy sequence and because the confined reconsideration circumstances distinguish a separate final judgment. She would expressly state Pink's binding-party rule before distinguishing its finality setting. **Join barrier:** no broad denial of finality for unexplained orders; no claim that Garvey itself decided Section 2109; no constitutional holding unnecessary to the actual compensation components.

**Breyer.** His Garvey and Semtek joins permit the current review while requiring an accurate account of the earlier judgment. His separate Daniels and Coss positions distinguish a practical first forum from repetitive litigation, but do not themselves establish the Section 2109 result. He treats the still-pending compensation action, absence of earlier collective legal determination and continuing constitutional importance together as sufficient unusual grounds for same-action reconsideration. The statutory objection remains substantial; the prior order must have operated as an affirmance below. **Join barrier:** he will not join the proposition that administrative difficulty alone overrides statutory judgment effect, or a rule permitting reopened final judgments merely because later review seems useful.

**Scope of the disagreement.** Five independently support the same confined reconsideration rule and its application; Thomas does not. The rule is a present extension of existing reconsideration principles to the specific interlocutory statutory-affirmance setting, not a quotation from Garvey, Christianson or Pink. The competing permanent-liability path remains legally serious. Neither branch invents an unentered event, a historical vote, or a reason for an earlier disqualification.

### 2. Medicare and the tenure-based exemption

**Available paths.** Evans directly protects an incumbent's compensation against a later tax and contains no general-tax exception. O'Malley, strictly read as a holding, concerns a later appointee and expressly reserves the extension to earlier appointees. That is the strongest path to affirmance. The contrary path adopts O'Malley's distinction between compensation protection and ordinary shared taxation, supported by Will's distinction between general income taxation and direct salary reduction. It rejects an appointment-date exemption for the broadly extended Medicare tax and expressly displaces the inconsistent Evans rule.

**Scalia.** His Acker joins recognize that a tax obligation measured by compensation is not automatically a charge on performing judicial duties, while preserving the independent constitutional question. His Cleveland Indians concurrence also keeps statutory characterization separate from the inference one might draw from the payment label. Here the broad Medicare extension imposes a shared tax, not a selective reduction of the compensation Congress has established for judges. He would reject the tenure-based tax exemption. Evans's unqualified language and O'Malley's appointment limit are the strongest counterargument; the answer must be a candid rejection of Evans to this extent, not an assertion that O'Malley already held the identical case. **Join barrier:** no rule validating every levy labeled a tax, no deference-based constitutional conclusion, and no general power to reduce nominal judicial salaries along with other salaries.

**Kennedy.** His Acker and Cleveland Indians joins support separately identifying the tax and the compensation entitlement. He accepts O'Malley's civic-obligation explanation, reinforced by Will's tax/direct-cut distinction, as the better constitutional rule for generally shared taxation. Protection from fiscal retaliation remains intact without preserving the particular tax regime at appointment. Evans's direct fit is the strongest contrary ground and requires express treatment. **Join barrier:** ordinary-tax approval must retain protection against discriminatory or disguised compensation reductions and must not suggest that general coverage validates a direct salary cut.

**Souter.** His Acker join withheld any answer to this exact constitutional reservation, and his Cleveland Indians join supplies no constitutional shortcut. He would resolve that reservation by distinguishing ordinary nondiscriminatory taxation from protected compensation reductions. The instability of a lifetime tax exemption keyed to the date of appointment reinforces the O'Malley/Will distinction, but does not itself override text. The adverse argument is the express in-tenure application in Evans; he requires its limited displacement, preserving its protection against indirect evasions that are genuine compensation reductions. **Join barrier:** no claim that the entire Evans opinion, including judicial independence and protection from indirect evasion, is repudiated.

**Thomas, conditionally.** His Acker joins accepted compensation-tax characterization while expressly preserving the constitutional issue, so they do not predetermine it. His Cleveland Indians opinion distinguishes a liability category from its operative temporal rules. Applying that discipline, he regards Article III's protection of compensation as compatible with the ordinary national tax burden recognized in O'Malley and distinguished in Will. The strongest objection is that the constitutional text has no exception for a new levy and Evans applies directly to incumbents. He rejects the inference that every ordinary tax is a diminution of constitutionally protected compensation; that reading would preserve a tax regime rather than a compensation entitlement. **Join barrier:** the Court must candidly displace the inconsistent Evans tax rule and preserve the ban on direct reductions; he will not join a holding that the earlier 1996 order had no binding party effect. This conditional ground does not change his preferred threshold disposition.

**Ginsburg.** Her Acker opinion separates the actual compensation levy from permission to perform federal functions and reserves the Compensation Clause. That framework supports examining this broad national tax on its own terms. She adopts the ordinary-tax distinction rather than making salary-source immunity the consequence of appointment timing. O'Malley's limited holding and Evans are substantial contrary authority, resolved by an express limited change in precedent. **Join barrier:** no automatic extension of Section 111 consent to Congress's constitutional power and no erasure of discrimination review.

**Breyer.** His Acker dissent identified a local charge on performing federal functions from the ordinance's cumulative operation, not a tax exemption for all judges. The described Medicare extension lacks that licensing operation and broadly extends a national program. O'Malley and Will permit treating the burden as ordinary taxation while maintaining meaningful independence protections. Evans's formal diminution argument is strongest but insufficient to turn participation in generally shared taxation into an institutional threat in these circumstances. **Join barrier:** this distinction must not declare his Acker direct-function concern irrelevant in a genuinely comparable case or require proof of hostile motive before a disguised compensation reduction can be invalidated.

**Common scope.** The six merits commitments support rejecting the Medicare liability theory for these incumbent judges. They expressly displace Evans insofar as it prohibits ordinary nondiscriminatory taxation of judicial compensation solely because the tax postdates appointment. O'Malley is followed and extended to that incumbent question; Will's direct-compensation protection remains. No new view of the Sixteenth Amendment's full scope, every tax classification or every later-appointed judge is necessary. Thomas's support is conditional on the Court's contrary threshold resolution.

### 3. Social Security transition and discrimination

**Available paths.** The government can characterize the scheme as a pension-based transition: an offset prevents additional contributions for workers already funding a pension, while a judge with a noncontributory retirement benefit has no existing contribution to offset. Acker cautions against treating every exemption or occupational difference as forbidden discrimination. The judges' competing path examines the whole transition: most existing employees can remain outside, mandatory participants with contributory pensions can use the offset, and the incumbent judges cannot avoid the new compulsory charge or obtain that protection. Their constitutional objection concerns the actual unequal burden, not an assertion that Congress disliked them.

**Scalia.** His Acker source-discrimination join rejected an invalid comparator inference; the mere existence of another occupation's separate licensing charge did not establish federal-source discrimination. That cautions against invalidating Social Security merely because judges have a different pension. Here, however, the combination of compulsory inclusion and unavailable protection places the affected incumbents under the new charge while the identified alternatives shield other incumbents. He would treat that operation as a selective compensation reduction, not ordinary taxation. **Counterargument and barrier:** equalizing benefits could rationally explain some distinctions, but it does not answer the newly unequal compulsory financial incidence on this record. He joins an as-applied rule tied to the combined transition, not a rule invalidating every pension classification or every tax exemption.

**Kennedy.** His Acker join requires the relevant comparator and actual tax operation; his Cleveland Indians join keeps separate statutory components separate. The Medicare expansion and Social Security transition cannot be merged. He concludes that the two transition protections described in the packet leave the incumbent judicial group bearing an unequal new charge, incompatible with the constitutional compensation safeguard. **Counterargument and barrier:** noncontributory retirement explains why no contribution can literally be offset, but does not itself justify denying both the election and effective protection afforded other incumbents. He requires that combined comparison and will not join an assumption of hostile intent or a general equality requirement among all retirement plans.

**Souter.** His Acker joins caution against reading a statutory label or a single exemption as conclusive. He nonetheless finds the described combined transition discriminatory in operation and therefore outside the ordinary-tax principle accepted for Medicare. Its structural effect supplies the constitutional objection without a finding concerning subjective legislative purpose. **Counterargument and barrier:** a pension-transition rationale has force and must be addressed; the holding is the unequal new burden on this incumbent group, not the proposition that different benefit structures can never justify different treatment. No missing legislative motive is supplied as a fact.

**Thomas, conditionally.** The Acker comparison and his Cleveland Indians separation of distinct tax rules support evaluating this transition as enacted rather than generalizing from the word payroll. Once the Court reaches liability, he treats the combination of forced entry and unavailable incumbent protection as a selective diminution. **Counterargument and barrier:** pension-based distinctions are not inherently unconstitutional, and unequal outcomes alone do not establish a universal constitutional tax-equality rule. The sufficient ground is the specified transition's direct operation on the protected compensation of these incumbents. His threshold view remains independently sufficient to preserve the existing liability judgment.

**Ginsburg.** Acker's actual-operation approach directly informs her analysis without deciding the separate constitutional question in advance. The relevant comparison is among the incumbents facing the new federal coverage transition, not between an abstract judge and every private taxpayer or between unrelated occupations alone. The combined exclusions and offset protections produce the prohibited burden. **Counterargument and barrier:** she accepts that noncontributory retirement is a relevant factual difference; she does not regard it as sufficient to explain why this group must bear the new deduction when the transition protects other incumbents through the two stated mechanisms. She joins only the as-applied conclusion and its stated comparison.

**Breyer.** His Acker separate writing insists on the whole provision's operation and the actual burden on federal judicial functions rather than the levy’s name. The national tax setting is different, but his concern with practical operation supports distinguishing the two expansions. He finds the Social Security transition's unequal compulsory financial burden incompatible with the compensation safeguard. **Counterargument and barrier:** an efficient integration of pension systems is a legitimate legislative aim, but it cannot alone justify this protected group's unequal reduction. He joins no finding about legislative hostility and no general prohibition on retirement coordination.

**Common scope.** All six merits positions invalidate the described Social Security transition as applied to the affected incumbent judges. That conclusion does not establish a tax exemption for every federal judge, invalidate all Social Security taxation, decide an unpresented later-appointee question, or require every retirement plan to use identical contributions. Thomas's constitutional join remains conditional. A broader tenure-based rule would also protect these judges, but none of these merits commitments adopts it; no broader rationale is counted toward the stated discrimination holding.

### 4. Subsequent raise, limitations and remedy

**Available cure paths.** The government's strongest argument compares actual post-raise compensation with the earlier protected level and invokes Will's later raise and limited pleaded recovery interval. The judges' stronger response distinguishes an increase in the legally owed compensation from payment of an existing shortfall: generally awarded new compensation does not authorize continuing to withhold an unconstitutional charge, and it does not retroactively satisfy deductions already unlawfully made. Will did not adjudicate such a recurring discriminatory tax cure.

**Scalia.** His Acker attention to the demand actually asserted and his Cleveland Indians insistence on the legal source of the payment rule support identifying the salary and deduction separately. Will protects an increase once effective. He rejects treating a lawful general increase as an unstated discharge of an unlawful deduction or accrued debt. **Join barrier:** no claim that Congress can never enact a properly structured compensatory measure; this record establishes only an unrelated increase, not such a measure.

**Kennedy.** His Cleveland Indians join applies distinct rate, base and temporal rules rather than netting unlike legal items. Will supplies the corresponding compensation-entitlement principle. The raise's amount alone does not eliminate the continuing discriminatory character of a deduction from compensation otherwise owed. **Join barrier:** the remedy must follow the actual surviving liability components and lawful claim periods, without presuming an exact loss.

**Souter.** His Acker and Cleveland Indians joins support legally separate treatment of the tax obligation and the compensation owed. The strongest cure objection, Will's recovery interval, establishes no rule allowing the government to use a generally owed increase to pay earlier unconstitutional deductions. He rejects that inference. **Join barrier:** preserve the possibility that a genuinely matched lawful payment or independently established satisfaction could matter; find none here without record support.

**Thomas.** On his preferred threshold path, liability is established for both taxes; the raise does not answer what those judgments already require. On the conditional merits path, the same cure rule applies only to Social Security. His Cleveland Indians opinion and Will support applying the actual entitlements and time rules, not a net-payment intuition. **Join barrier:** no remedy may silently convert his binding-liability position into agreement that all earlier liability was open; conditional and preferred paths remain distinct.

**Ginsburg.** Her Acker application of operative legal obligations and her Cleveland Indians join support the same separation. An ordinary salary increase belongs to the judges under the new salary law; it is not, without more, an appropriation satisfying prior liability or a permission for the continuing unconstitutional tax. **Join barrier:** avoid unnecessary general doctrine concerning every pension or benefit offset and do not determine claimant-specific recovery.

**Breyer.** His Acker operation-based position takes the government's net-pay argument seriously but rejects it because the practical comparison must include the compensation legally granted to the other judges as well as the ongoing discriminatory deduction. The rise in gross salary alone does not end that differential treatment. **Join barrier:** no suggestion that an appropriate targeted corrective measure could never cure an unlawful arrangement; the supplied general raise is not such a measure.

**Limitations and directions, independently accepted by all six.** The en banc judgment displaced the panel's conclusion that all Medicare claims were time-barred. Its continuing-claim treatment permits timely recurring deduction claims while excluding deductions more than six years before the relevant filing. The supplied review questions do not separately put the full validity of that limitations doctrine in issue. Accordingly, preserve its lawful operation for any liability component that survives; do not purport to announce a general new Supreme Court accrual rule or resurrect the superseded blanket Medicare bar. If Medicare liability is reversed, those compensation claims fail on the constitutional merits rather than through the former limitations holding. If liability is preserved on Thomas's threshold path, both components remain subject to the en banc method. In either event the lower court must determine individual timely amounts consistently with the Court's actual judgment. No numerical award, newly mandated evidentiary hearing, judicial tax exemption beyond the adjudicated class, or independent further filing condition is supplied.
