The statutory distinctions are indispensable:

- Section 2244(b)(1) commands dismissal of a claim in a second or successive application previously presented in a prior application.
- Section 2244(b)(2)(A) supplies a different exception for a new claim relying on a previously unavailable new constitutional rule made retroactive to collateral cases by the Supreme Court. Newness, previous unavailability, Supreme Court action and retroactive collateral operation are independent conditions.
- Section 2244(b)(2)(B) separately requires both previously undiscoverable facts despite due diligence and a clear-and-convincing whole-evidence showing that, but for constitutional error, no reasonable factfinder would have found guilt. No new factual-innocence gateway is presented here.
- Under (b)(3), a three-judge appellate panel gives advance permission only on a prima facie showing, must decide within thirty days, and its grant or denial is not appealable or subject to rehearing or certiorari. Under (b)(4), the district court must dismiss unless the applicant actually establishes the conditions, notwithstanding permission.

Felker directly preserves these distinctions. This proceeding reviews a later final judgment, not the initial authorization order, so (b)(3)(E) does not remove the present review channel. Stewart's previously presented, dismissed-as-premature and expressly preserved Ford claim is materially different; it creates no general exemption for a new legal argument after a completed first merits application. No unidentified state or federal procedural exception is inferred.

## Rule, error classification and source authority

Teague's relevant opinion by O'Connor, joined by Scalia and Kennedy among the present Associates, explained that a rule is new when its result was not dictated by precedent at finality. Its Parts IV and V originally had four Justices; this handoff does not invent a five-Justice original opinion. The actual entering-law Schiro and Caspari decisions leave the substantive nonretroactivity framework and its contemporary exceptions unchanged.

The relevant procedural exception requires both a serious contribution to accurate determination of guilt or innocence and a bedrock procedural element essential to fundamental fairness. It is not every accuracy improvement or every constitutional defect. Teague's substantive-protection exception does not protect the underlying homicide conduct; the present argument concerns criminal procedure. Stevens's Teague concurrence accepts Harlan's general approach but objects to narrowing fundamental fairness to factual accuracy. He also separates identification of a constitutional rule from entitlement to relief and recognizes the force of a controlling nonretroactivity decision. Those are his own positions, not additions to the Court's exception.

Sullivan, authored by Scalia and joined by every then-sitting Associate now participating, holds that vitiation of the jury's reasonable-doubt standard leaves no constitutionally valid verdict for harmless-error assessment. It expressly leaves collateral gates unresolved. Its Winship and jury-function grounds supply the strongest argument for the exceptional importance of the rule; its harmless-error holding is not itself an express retroactivity ruling. Breyer and Ginsburg were not members of that Court, and no historical Sullivan join is attributed to them.

Fiore's unanimous current-term opinion separates an established missing-element constitutional violation from actual statutory prerequisites. Artuz separates proper filing from claim-relief bars. Penry's current charge and effect rulings concern their defined capital categories and expressly do not create a new retroactivity exception. Duncan construes section 2244(d)(2), preserves actual independent alternatives and leaves general equity questions open; it does not authorize equitable dispensation from (b)(2)(A). Daniels and Coss concern expired predicates and different vehicles; their public disagreements inform individual judgment but create no majority exception to Tyler's successive restrictions.

## Competing lawful paths

**Path F — enforce the Supreme Court retroactivity condition and affirm.** Made retroactive requires Supreme Court holdings establishing retroactive operation, rather than a lower court's independent application or prediction of general principles. An express declaration in a single opinion is sufficient but not necessary: multiple holdings can perform the work when their necessary legal consequence is retroactivity for the rule at issue. Holdings that furnish strong arguments without compelling that conclusion do not suffice. Teague supplies a demanding category; Sullivan identifies structural harmless-error consequences. A further judgment whether this particular rule meets every part of the exceptional retroactivity category remains. The statutory condition therefore fails even assuming Cage supplies the previously unavailable new rule on which Tyler relies. Affirmance sustains denial on that ground without approving the exact charge, the district court's section 2254(d) analysis or a categorical denial of retroactivity in an appropriate vehicle.

**Strongest objection to F:** No constitutionally valid jury verdict under the governing burden of proof can be a fundamentally fair and sufficiently reliable conviction; requiring an additional opinion elevates form over the substance of the Court's holdings and makes eligibility depend on an accidental separate vehicle. **F response:** A necessary entailment is enough, so no magic wording or solitary-opinion form is imposed. But structural error answers whether a valid actual verdict permits effect review; retroactivity separately asks whether a new procedural rule changes a bedrock element of criminal adjudication with the required accuracy significance. Important constitutional procedure does not automatically satisfy every collateral condition. The legislature's allocation to Supreme Court holdings would do little work if any persuasive lower-court classification were sufficient.

**Path P — existing holdings establish the condition; reverse and remand.** The conjunction of Teague's fundamental fairness and accuracy requirements with Sullivan's treatment of the reasonable-doubt defect makes the relevant rule retroactive without needing a single opinion using that precise formula. A constitutionally inadequate governing standard for every jury finding denies both the indispensable safeguard against wrongful conviction and the essential lawful factfinding process; this is more than a separable instructional error. The Court has supplied the legal premises and their necessary relation. The lower court's reliance on no individual retroactivity decision is too restrictive. Reverse the successive-gate rejection and remand for the remaining complete-charge and section 2254(d) issues and any genuinely preserved independent prerequisite. Passage through this gate issues no writ, acquittal, release or automatic retrial.

**Strongest objection to P:** Sullivan reserved collateral gates and did not find the separate Teague conditions; the conjunction still requires a new legal classification. **P response:** The reservation preserved an unpresented question, not a holding that the rule lacks necessary implications. Unlike the varied collection of structural defects, vitiation of the reasonable-doubt standard governs every guilt finding and disables the jury's constitutionally indispensable function. The conclusion rests on that specific substantive character, not on the word structural. This path must identify both exceptional requirements rather than treat any one general pronouncement about fairness as retroactivity.

**Alternative request to make the rule retroactive now:** That request is a distinct live question, not proof the preexisting condition was satisfied. A Justice finding existing holdings sufficient need not issue a new prospective declaration. A Justice finding the condition unsatisfied must separately decide whether a new declaration would be necessary to resolving a lawfully reviewable current question, or merely advise a future applicant. No opinion about a hypothetical future petition should be called a holding. The positions below preserve that disagreement without inventing an original petition or treating the final-judgment review channel as repeal of the successive limitation.
