## 2000-11-27 — Wasson v. Sonoma County Junior College; Ninth Circuit No. 98-15967; 203 F.3d 659; no separate Supreme Court docket supplied.

**Stone-Zsela, dissenting.** The dissent would recognize an employee's direct claim when attributed expression protected by the public-employment rules substantially motivates an adverse employment action, subject to the employer's proof that it would have acted for an independent lawful reason. The First Amendment constrains the punitive use of expression against the person officials target; mistaken identity does not remove that person's injury or justify suppressing protected criticism. Actual authorship would therefore be unnecessary. Pickering–Connick would determine protection and legitimate employment interests, and Mt. Healthy would govern causation and the same-decision defense. Erroneous attribution or an imperfect investigation alone would prove neither liability nor the other elements. Waters's limited protection for genuine reliance on a reasonable account of disciplinable speech would remain; it would not protect hostility to protected criticism. Letters, notices, chronology, testimony, and competent circumstantial evidence could establish the actual ground under ordinary Crawford-El procedure, without a statistical requirement.

The dissent would also replace Harlow's defense insofar as lack of sufficiently similar precedent defeats damages after an underlying violation is established. An official would instead have to prove actual and objectively reasonable reliance on identified binding precedent or enacted authority directly permitting the challenged conduct. Legal silence, custom, or general supervisory power would not suffice. In the dissent's view, §1983's remedy and the protections for reasonable factual mistakes within the substantive inquiry make that narrower reliance defense adequate. Elder's independent consideration of relevant law and ordinary procedural protections would survive, as would independent absolute immunity. This would change existing immunity doctrine, including positions Stone-Zsela previously accepted. He would reverse and remand to determine protected status, adverse action, motive, causation, any supported reliance defense, and compensation for proved injury. No damages judgment would follow now, and prospective relief would require an actual continuing threat rather than a presumed revival of the expired notice.

## 2000-11-28 — City of Indianapolis v. Edmond, No. 99-1030; 531 U.S. 32.

**Thomas, dissenting, Part I, joined by Scalia.** The dissent would reverse because this program genuinely performs systematic license and sobriety checks that provide an independent roadway justification. Under the dissent's reading of Whren and the checkpoint precedents, the added narcotics objective and exterior dog use do not invalidate a stop whose actual operation stays within that legitimate scope. The dissent answers the concern about nominal checks by requiring real, independently supportable roadway functions and lawful execution; it does not approve a drug-only checkpoint merely because it efficiently detects crime. It would return the case for proceedings under that objective mixed-function rule rather than approve every execution detail.

**Thomas, dissenting, Part II, joined by no other Justice.** Thomas would apply the restrained scope and objective reasonableness analysis of the checkpoint cases to these brief systematic automobile seizures. He rejects a primary-purpose threshold transferred from suspicionless searches of bodies, homes, or businesses. He also questions whether indiscriminate stops accord with the Fourth Amendment's original understanding, but does not seek to overrule Sitz or Martinez-Fuerte here because that issue was not developed for decision. Scalia joins neither this additional critique nor that reservation.

## 2000-12-11 — Daggett v. Commission on Governmental Ethics & Election Practices; consolidated First Circuit Nos. 99-2243, 99-2274, 00-1061, and 00-1066; 205 F.3d 445; no separate Supreme Court docket supplied.

**Kennedy, dissenting, Part I, joined by Scalia and Thomas.** The dissent accepts Russell's and Shrink Missouri's contribution standard and recognizes that the influence evidence justifies some prophylaxis. It finds the State's operational answer inadequate for this amount because projected funding losses are substantial and concentrated in affected competitive races. Average expenses and a special election with different donor competition do not sufficiently establish replacement capacity. Recruiting more donors is a relevant possibility, not proof that campaigns can replace large shares of their funding. The protected association is support for the chosen candidate; public enrollment, self-financing, or expression through another speaker does not answer loss of that route. The dissent relies on the scale and concentration of the burden, not every static loss, an inflation floor, completed-bribe requirement, or unlimited-giving rule.

**Kennedy, dissenting, Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, and Thomas.** The dissent applies the expressive-burden rationale these Justices joined in Day to both candidate-financing and independent-expenditure triggers. The State attaches an adverse public-funding consequence to outsiders' protected activity, supplying a reason to stop below the trigger. Caps shorten the burden and the favorable-expenditure deduction improves fit, but neither removes that legal consequence. Voluntary initial grants are legitimate; making their program attractive does not justify burdening nonparticipants' expression or merely offsetting an opponent's advantage. The State has not shown why its anti-dependence interest requires grants measured by another speaker's activity. This objection does not erase the contribution/expenditure distinction or invalidate every public grant or adjustment.

The dissenters seek component-specific declaratory and prospective relief, implemented on remand under 1 MRSA §71(8): preserve provisions and applications that can operate independently, subject to the construction rule's plain-meaning qualification. They would neither rewrite a trigger or ceiling nor invalidate unrelated reporting, reclaim initial grants, reopen elections, or impose retrospective liability. Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas need not reach the additional whole-system theory after their proposed targeted relief. O'Connor joins the Court's rejection of that additional ground while continuing to seek invalidation of matching.

## 2000-12-11 — Green Tree Financial Corp.-Alabama v. Randolph, No. 99-1235; 531 U.S. 79.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** Stone-Zsela joins the entire Court opinion and would require Randolph to identify a concrete likelihood of required costs preventing effective statutory vindication. Her sworn resources, applicable schedules, contracts, forum correspondence, and other competent evidence may substantiate that objection; neither statistical research nor prior payment is necessary. Through ordinary focused procedure, the party seeking compulsion must disclose materially relevant cost and allocation information within its control. Contractual silence cannot become an impossible demand for concealed facts. He would compel arbitration if the obstacle is unproved or lawfully removed, otherwise withhold enforcement to the necessary extent, while preserving unresolved independent grounds. His initial likelihood and disclosure formulation is not attributed to the other remand Justices or treated as a complete majority rule.

**Ginsburg, concurring, joined by Stevens and Souter.** These Justices distinguish whether an arbitral forum is capable of deciding a category of federal claims from whether this claimant can afford to use it. A burden imposed on a party claiming that Congress excluded a statutory category from arbitration does not itself establish the same burden for financial access. They would resolve the concrete forum, allocation, lender practice, and affordability questions through the focused opportunity, with substantiation required, without announcing a comprehensive substitute burden rule. Contract silence proves neither prohibitive cost nor accessibility, and a nonbinding assurance is no enforceable allocation. Breyer joins the Court's inquiry and access ground but not this broader burden critique.

**O'Connor, dissenting from the cost disposition, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas.** These Justices join finality and reject both silence-based invalidity and indifference to a genuine statutory-access barrier. They would require the resisting party to demonstrate a likelihood of prohibitive expense and reverse the cost ruling because no likely forum, charges, or amount chargeable to Randolph was established. Exact advance billing is unnecessary, but a conjectural risk should not mandate cost-development litigation. They would preserve ordinary properly supported procedural grounds and the unresolved class-action alternative on remand, without ordering unconditional arbitration or deciding statutory merits. Their disagreement is with the required new cost opportunity on this showing, not the possibility that a demonstrated cost barrier can defeat enforcement.

## 2000-12-12 — Bush v. Gore, No. 00-949; review of Florida Supreme Court No. SC00-2431.

**O'Connor, joined by Kennedy, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They join reviewability, the supported equality defect, rejection of separate Article II and due-process reversal, the section 5 distinction, and corrective vacatur. Their disagreement concerns state law: the December 11 majority's notes 17 and 22, read with its comprehensive-code account and the relation between certification and contest, establish December 12 finality. Palm Beach's recognition that §5 is not itself a federal prohibition does not erase a State's choice to meet that date. Correction must therefore stay within that state limit. They do not find that correction has already become impossible, infer current progress, authorize an extension, or award electoral votes. The supervising court must apply the retained deadline to the actual feasible process rather than receive an unsupported terminal factual conclusion from this Court.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting from the judgment.** They agree on reviewability, unequal treatment, absence of a separate established due-process violation, and §5's limited federal operation. Article II supplies an independent reason to reverse the challenged directions. First, de novo treatment of discretionary board decisions combined with automatic inclusion of late contest tallies removes the remaining legislative allocation among certification, executive discretion, and judicial contest. Palm Beach permits some late amendments; it does not abolish every residual limit. Second, the legal-vote construction conflates failure to tabulate a properly marked vote with failure to mark in the prescribed manner. The statutory instruction and machine provisions and the Secretary's delegated interpretation matter; §101.5614(5) does not furnish a general answer for these ballots.

They recognize §102.168's rejected-legal-vote ground, change-or-place-in-doubt language, and investigative authority, but find this use materially displaces the particular institutional and substantive limits. The pre-election existence of an intent provision does not decide its scope. Their disposition reverses the affected directions and remands for conformity with the pre-existing allocation and voting requirements, without invalidating the entire contest statute or deciding individual ballots. An equality protocol alone cannot cure unauthorized relief. Only if the Court rejects this Article II ground do they accept equality correction, confined to the state-law December 12 limit. They adopt neither an unsupported present impossibility finding nor a blanket termination of every lawful state action. The two rejected contest claims remain undisturbed.

**Stevens, joined by Ginsburg, dissenting from corrective vacatur.** They join bounded review, rejection of independent Article II and due-process reversal, the federal-calendar rule, and refusal to impose a terminal state-law construction. They would affirm. A common clear-intent rule can produce different results from different physical evidence, and board disagreement, changing evidentiary judgments, or incomplete implementation detail does not itself establish that this judicial decree authorizes unconstitutional treatment. Spencer's and Burton's accounts warrant focused examination of disputed treatment through ordinary objections and judicial correction; they do not demonstrate that such correction is unavailable or that the operative statewide command is constitutionally defective.

Harper and Reynolds protect equal participation and weight, but do not demand mechanical uniformity for different evidence. Burdick permits neutral administration, and the actual Shaw rule requires an identified deprivation. The legislature's contest and corrective provisions and the state majority's substantial explanation support authoritative interpretation under Palm Beach; Yoshina prevents treating disagreement alone as fundamental unfairness. They require neither partisan bad faith nor proof that a winner changes. Their disagreement concerns the established defect and its attribution to the decree. If a constitutional defect is found, they accept a lawful corrective opportunity; that conditional position does not make them supporters of vacatur. They authorize no count beyond binding state law and make no feasibility or winner finding.

## 2001-01-09 — Solid Waste Agency of Northern Cook County v. U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, No. 99-1178.

Souter dissents, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer. They read the defined waters term, the aquatic-protection objective and section 404(g)(1)'s state-administration structure to authorize this supported habitat-water application. Riverside Bayview leaves isolated waters open; its adjacency facts do not establish an exhaustive limit. Sweet Home and PUD No. 1 support applying broad protective language according to its actual conditions. The agency's interpretation reinforces their textual conclusion without turning every preamble into a source of binding authority or disregarding section 101(b)'s state role.

They would also sustain this application under Home Builders' complete independent externality route. The regulated subject is discharge of dredged or fill material into the claimed waters. Filling removes aquatic habitat used by a shared mobile population; loss in one State can diminish the resource available elsewhere, while another State cannot restore that out-of-state habitat through ordinary territorial authority. The regional production and conversion evidence supports substantial class-wide importance, and the agency's aquatic-use and interstate-bird findings, together with the breeding-colony material, connect the site functionally to that mechanism. Direct permit review of habitat filling reasonably fits the resulting spillover. These are supported inferences and bounded predictive judgments, not new measurements of population loss.

Their strongest contrary concern is that the regional report does not describe every pond or quantify this fill's effect. They find the combined functional evidence sufficient without requiring a separate substantial effect for each discharge, a site percentage, a specified scientific method or proof of failed interstate agreements. The estimate of up to 50 percent of United States migratory-waterfowl production is not a percentage attributable to this site or to Illinois. Neither bird presence nor conservation's national importance alone is sufficient. They identify no independent necessary-regime predicate, and Holland's treaty holding is not a Commerce holding. Their preserved view that actual habitat-altering construction may itself be economic conduct does not replace their full join to this externality ground. They would affirm jurisdiction without reopening the abandoned permit-merits challenge or awarding a permit.

## 2001-01-10 — Lopez v. Davis, No. 99-7504.

Stone-Zsela dissents, joined by Stevens and Kennedy. Congress's nonviolent-conviction criterion and successful-treatment condition make a genuine opportunity for consideration part of the rehabilitative scheme. Permission to deny a reduction does not make that adjacent eligibility language idle. A firearm sentencing circumstance may strongly indicate danger, but it is not the conviction Congress specified and does not establish that treatment and individual circumstances can never matter.

The record establishes treatment admission eligibility, not completion. The Bureau could still deny a reduction on lawful individual grounds. No particular reduction, immediate release, universal constitutional hearing right or liberty entitlement would follow.

## 2001-01-17 — Denius v. Dunlap; Seventh Circuit No. 99-1402; 209 F.3d 944; no separate Supreme Court docket supplied.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part, concurring in the judgment in part, and dissenting in part.** He joins the legal-consultation and intimate-information holdings and their equitable remedies. He agrees that Button and Trainmen protect independent legal assistance, that the actual written condition governs, and that objectively intimate medical and financial content requires a justified and suitably confined governmental demand. These grounds do not constitutionalize ordinary financial secrecy or dispense with proof of coverage and injury.

He would replace the broader clearly-established-law defense with a narrow defense requiring defendants to prove genuine, objectively reasonable reliance on identified binding precedent or enacted authority directly authorizing the demand. Legal silence and routine supervisory power would not suffice. Justified factual judgment belongs in the constitutional merits analysis; uncertainty in precedent should not independently defeat compensation for a proved deprivation. Authoritative legal permission would protect actual, reasonable reliance without immunizing conduct merely because no earlier decision used the same authorization form. This changes the defense preserved by Elder, Crawford-El and Wasson; it does not claim the new acquisition rule was established in 1996.

He would therefore reverse attorney damages immunity, affirm the medical damages remand on his distinct defense rule, and vacate financial damages immunity insofar as it forecloses a qualifying intimate-information claim solely for lack of a previously specific decision. The record identifies no binding permission for the unrestricted demand. Remand would permit a properly supported reliance defense and adjudication of actual scope, participation, justification, protected content, causation and injury. His position awards no damages or reinstatement and does not affect independently required live-equitable-relief conditions.

**Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Thomas.** They join the attorney and financial damages-immunity holding. They reject the First Amendment extension because Button and Trainmen protect litigation and expressive association rather than a general constitutional guarantee covering individual civil legal confidences. The absence of an identified constitutional source is not cured by the demand's poor justification or by treating every evidentiary privilege as protected activity. They accept the employment injury and the distinction between immunity and equitable relief.

They also reject extending substantive due process from Sheets's actual public-dissemination holding to compulsory access or authorization. Their position, reflected in the Sheets dissent and Doe concurrence, requires a demonstrated constitutional source for the new acquisition guarantee; intimate subject matter and state confidentiality expectations do not alone supply one. They accept Sheets as binding within its decided scope and do not treat information held by a third party as necessarily public.

Independently, they would restore medical damages immunity. Pesce and Schaill upheld justified disclosures in different settings, and Anderson v. Romero distinguished other interests while granting immunity. Those sources did not sufficiently establish the asserted application to an unexecuted authorization with disputed medical coverage. They would reject the three constitutional equitable claims on the grounds stated and retain damages immunity for each category. Their position makes no finding that the demand was wise or that a state-law remedy displaces a proved federal right.

## 2001-01-17 — District of Columbia v. Tri County Industries, Inc., No. 99-1953.

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, dissenting from dismissal.** He would reach the question and affirm under ordinary abuse-of-discretion review. Williams permits review of a federal question expressly decided below; the court of appeals expressly applied its more-searching-review formulation drawn from binding circuit precedent. The parties' conflicting preservation accounts and the unexplained rehearing denial establish no waiver finding. The existing first-trial record permits review of each stated new-trial ground without new facts; affirmance leaves the separate second-trial challenges unnecessary to decide.

Gasperini preserves trial-court discretion while permitting independent review of legal error, and forbids a fresh appellate valuation. Its particular state-law compensation measure does not govern this federal civil-rights action. A judge may reconsider an earlier discretionary exclusion; the Chief Justice does not treat the initial ruling's permissibility as a bar to doing so. The reconsidered proof must nevertheless have a lawful relevance and evidentiary foundation.

Here the judge repeatedly sought a lawful mechanism for terminating the permitted operation and found the District's proffer inadequate. He struck the October 15 and December 6 letters and health-and-safety testimony and excluded Siess's expert proffer, while submitting the fine and appeal defenses to the jury. The July order relies on that same unsupported prospect of community resistance and later closure. Community opposition is not itself lawful regulation. The permit retained testing and inspection conditions; the judge assumed compliance at the approved site, and counsel's contrary suggestions and the excluded proffer supplied no adequate foundation. No community or misrepresentation proceeding had occurred. The order did not rely on occupancy review, and the Chief Justice does not decide whether section 3203.1 or Woodley Park could supply a lawful mechanism on a different record.

The order's excessiveness reason particularly, and its speculation reason partly, depend on the same deficient closure premise. Its remaining doubts about inexperience, the failed Virginia operation and falling treatment prices concern disputed valuation evidence the jury was instructed to weigh, including new-business uncertainty and competition. The award was less than half the profit projection. On this record those doubts do not justify displacing the jury's supported assessment.

The categorical premise that future profits cannot be recovered without seeking permit reinstatement is also legal error. Mitigation was for the jury on proof the District bore the burden to supply, taking account of the available route, cost, delay and likely utility. The order did not expressly rest on the October letter as an unanswered invitation to be heard; insofar as failure to seek reinstatement supplies its mitigation ground, the same categorical error remains. The District had an opportunity at the first trial to challenge the expert's premises. Later-trial evidence cannot justify the earlier order. Competent testimony and its factual premises, contemporaneous business evidence, mitigation and causation matter; no separate statistical study is required.

He would preserve the first verdict and the appellate interest amendment without another trial. His reasoning establishes neither automatic lost-profit recovery for permit suspensions nor a new damages formula, and it does not decide the deference owed to a different, properly supported order or materially different record.

## 2001-02-20 — Brentwood Academy v. Tennessee Secondary School Athletic Association, No. 99-901.

Kennedy dissents, joined by Scalia and Thomas. American Manufacturers requires actual governmental responsibility for the challenged choice. Public employment and numerical predominance do not alone establish that administrators act in a governmental capacity when they voluntarily govern an association representing public and private members. Although every voting governor was a public-school administrator when the penalties issued, the rules permit private-school administrators and guarantee no permanent governmental majority. The nonvoting state representatives, independent gate-receipt financing and retirement eligibility without a state-paid employer contribution do not supply that missing responsibility.

The dissent accepts that Lebron states a sufficient, nonexclusive structural rule and that private incorporation is not conclusive. It nevertheless finds no comparable actual governmental control, state compulsion, exclusive public function or adequately established official character for this enforcement. Tarkanian's single-State distinction is relevant but does not itself convert an association into government. The Court's strongest argument is that schools can exercise their responsibilities collectively without an external command. The dissent regards the inference of official capacity as unproved by this voluntary structure and rejects a rule that treats the officials' associational choices as governmental merely because they predominate. It gives effect to the actual 1996 textual amendment while recognizing unchanged operation. It would affirm the attribution ruling and decide neither recruiting-rule constitutionality nor the appropriate relief.

## 2001-02-20 — Illinois v. McArthur, No. 99-1132.

**Souter concurs and joins the Court opinion in full.** Exigency and permissible scope depend on the risk at the time of the proposed action. In his assessment, while McArthur was inside with immediate access to the hidden drugs, the actual destruction risk could have justified prompt entry and search. Once McArthur stepped outside and police could control access, the same immediate necessity for a greater warrantless evidentiary intrusion was absent. Temporary control of reentry while pursuing a warrant respects the preference for a magistrate's authorization and affords a reasonable opportunity to secure it. Police need not let an occupant return simply to recreate the emergency that would support a greater intrusion. This analysis distinguishes the risk and available response at each point; it does not find that a greater search occurred, infer an unreported occupant or turn the later discoveries into the earlier justification. The conditional greater-intrusion view belongs to his concurrence and is not an additional Court holding.

**Stone-Zsela dissents.** Control of access to a home is a seizure requiring an adequate warrant exception. The wife's contemporaneous observation of concealment strengthens probable cause and bears on risk, but hiding an existing stash does not itself show imminent destruction. McArthur could see his wife confer with police and leave with an officer; those facts support anticipation of a search, but add no particularized urgency beyond that awareness and the disposable character of drugs. Treating that combination as sufficient would authorize a similar restraint in nearly every residential drug investigation. An actual destruction attempt is not invariably required, but the inference must concern imminent loss rather than probable cause alone.

The two-hour duration and accompanied entries reduce the burden but cannot supply the missing exception. Segura does not validate every initial seizure because a later warrant produces evidence. Welsh gives the modest offenses real weight without misclassifying them as nonjailable. Contextual reasonableness evaluates a demonstrated exception; it cannot replace the warrant boundary. Stone-Zsela would affirm suppression for the search resulting from the unsupported restraint, subject to any independently preserved exclusionary-rule issue actually left unresolved. He would not invalidate the later warrant on an unpresented ground or dismiss independently supported charges.

**Stevens dissents.** He first would dismiss the writ as improvidently granted because these slight possession offenses make an unsuitable occasion to define the limits of restraints on residential possession. Because the Court decides the merits, he would affirm. Welsh requires serious attention to offense gravity, and his understanding of Segura gives deprivation of home possession independent significance despite an eventual warrant. Even accepting probable cause, diligent warrant pursuit, accompanied entries and the comparatively limited restraint, he concludes that the substantial interest in private use of the home outweighs this modest preservation interest. His position concerns these facts; it neither denies that the offenses permit imprisonment nor categorically prohibits every misdemeanor preservation measure. He does not demand an observed destruction attempt in every case.

## 2001-02-20 — Urofsky v. Gilmore; Fourth Circuit No. 98-1481, 216 F.3d 401; no separate Supreme Court docket supplied.

**Stone-Zsela concurs in Parts I and II and dissents from the academic disposition.** He recognizes an individual interest in bona fide independent university teaching and research, distinguished by its scholarly function from the State's prescribed message. Boring left that question open; Sweezy and Keyishian support protection against ideological control. Pickering and NTEU then require an adequately justified fit, and he concludes that this record already establishes the violation.

The removed indecency-law assignment, planned Victorian-poetry study, website interference and curtailed inquiry establish concrete activity and burden. The amended definition still reaches protected material studied for scholarly purposes. No denied request or statistical showing of abstract harm is needed to challenge the compulsory condition. The State's efficiency and harassment interests are legitimate, but content-based discretionary permission and public identification of research are insufficiently connected to misuse or unwanted exposure of colleagues. The narrower definition, academic delegation and successful permissions do not remove that burden or justify public approval records. Ordinary professional, curriculum, budget, resource-use and anti-harassment rules remain available.

He would reverse the rejection of the individual academic claim and direct a declaration and injunction against enforcing the prior-approval and associated public-disclosure condition on the plaintiffs' bona fide independent teaching and research. The lower court should identify covered scholarly activities and frame workable terms, not reopen whether the established defect warrants relief. He would not restore the agency-wide employee decree, award damages against the official-capacity defendant, grant a personal-use right or decide a hypothetical refused project. Independent disputes over scholarly versus prescribed institutional activity retain their own facts.

**O'Connor concurs in Part I and dissents from vacatur of the academic disposition.** She assumes a qualifying scholarly interest and applies the case-specific Pickering balance reflected in her NTEU position. She does not adopt the Court's affirmative personal-interest rule or Scalia and Thomas's antecedent-entitlement rejection. Boring reserves university inquiry while preserving institutional control, and Waters recognizes genuine employer operational concerns.

The narrowed dominant-theme and lascivious-content requirements, separate research and other-undertaking approval branches, internal administration, prompt permissions and blanket approvals support the actual condition. The record also shows genuine misuse concerns. Preventive regulation need not await actionable harassment or completed injuries. Written and publicly available approvals impose a real accountability burden, but that burden does not outweigh these interests on this record. Administrative delegation is relevant without becoming a statutory universal peer-review requirement; successful approvals are relevant without guaranteeing all future requests. She would affirm the faculty disposition without denying every personal academic interest, sustaining a future discriminatory refusal or adopting a universal rule permitting advance restrictions.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurs in Part I and dissents from the academic vacatur on two independently sufficient grounds.** First, scholarly value and delegated discretion do not establish the claimed personal entitlement to these publicly supplied research means. Boring's university reservation is not itself an affirmative access right; neither its limited assigned-production rule nor every duty-related statement is mechanically controlling. Keyishian's independent restrictions on orthodoxy and association survive, while Sweezy's separate rationales establish no general faculty authority over public resources. The present limited equipment condition does not itself prescribe an academic conclusion or association.

Second, even assuming a personal interest, the condition survives the employment balance. Their NTEU position gives weight to preventive and administrable restrictions; Waters preserves genuine permissible employer purposes without a free-standing investigative right. The narrower covered category, reported misuse and institutional approval of required research or other undertakings provide adequate protection and accommodation. The written public-approval burden does not demand completed misconduct or expert prediction before prevention is permissible. They would affirm on either ground, preserve independent constitutional prohibitions and decide no future refused project. O'Connor's agreement in result supplies no join to their antecedent-interest ground or to this entire writing.

## 2001-04-25 — Daniels v. United States, No. 99-9136; historical record citation, 532 U.S. 374.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins the common available-review holding, counsel exception and affirmance, and additionally adopts an affirmative faultless-loss exception as part of his governing access rule. Section 2255 review should remain available when meaningful original review was unavailable or frustrated through no fault of the prisoner, subject to applicable procedure, proof of constitutional defect and adverse effect on present custody. This is his present legal position, not merely a suggestion that an exception might be recognized later.

The distinction is between meaningful first review and repetitive litigation. Custis's forum rule and reservation do not establish that every completed conviction had an effective first opportunity, while Maleng's custody requirement must still be respected. A later enhancement creates a new incentive but does not make an earlier available remedy unavailable. Conversely, administrative inconvenience should not insulate a demonstrably unlawful current sentence from every authorized challenge. He does not equate every attorney failure with the prisoner's deliberate choice. Daniels identifies no complete counsel deprivation or external obstruction to meaningful first review, so even this affirmative exception does not allow his claims. Stone's exception has no controlling majority and is not borrowed from a decision in Coss. He orders no fresh sentencing or predicate invalidation.

**Scalia, concurring.** He joins the actual available-review rule and finality reasoning, but adds a statutory objection: section 2255's remedy for an unlawful federal sentence does not itself authorize adjudicating an outstanding state predicate's non-Gideon constitutional defect. Custis supports distinguishing an operative conviction from a collateral objection, although its actual sentencing holding did not already decide every expired-custody setting. A possible constitutional duty to provide review would concern the rendering jurisdiction and would not identify section 2255 as the remedy. He does not affirmatively endorse a section 2255 faultless-review exception. The Court's statement of what it has not decided is a scope description, not his agreement that such an exception exists. He orders no alternative forum remedy and decides no absent constitutional claim.

**Souter, dissenting, joined by Stevens and Ginsburg.** Section 2255 expressly authorizes review of the current federal sentence when constitutional error allegedly makes that sentence unlawful. Custis's restriction at the sentencing proceeding supplies no sufficient statutory basis for a new categorical exclusion from this express collateral remedy. Custis's available original-custody setting and reservation make the difference particularly important. The dissent's reading permits examination of an allegedly unlawful enhancement without treating Daniels as still in custody under the old state judgments.

Finality and stale evidence matter through applicable procedure and lawful proof rules; they do not justify the additional categorical bar. Parke preserves regularity and proof, Garlotte separates custody from remaining conditions, and Fiore and Glover distinguish an actual constitutional wrong and its causal effect from access requirements. The dissent preserves applicable defaults and does not automatically import all section 2254 exhaustion requirements into section 2255. It would reverse and remand for authorized consideration, not find either plea invalid, guarantee a hearing or order a lower sentence. The remaining qualifying predicates and the actual effect on lawful federal punishment would still require adjudication.

**Breyer, dissenting.** He also would reverse the categorical denial and remand, but on a distinct statutory and practical ground. Custis should be reconsidered: the sentencing scheme is better read to permit properly raised constitutional challenges to predicates at sentencing under lawful proof rules. His earlier opinion in United States v. Paleo supplies that approach. A prohibition at the original federal sentencing then generates restrictive or increasingly complex collateral exceptions; section 2255 is an awkward substitute for deciding the predicate issue when the enhancement is imposed. His disposition agreement with Souter does not make him an automatic join in every aspect of Souter's construction of section 2255. He proposes reconsideration, not an accomplished overruling, and establishes no plea defect, excuse of default, hearing entitlement or sentence reduction.

## 2001-05-21 — Bartnicki v. Vopper / United States v. Vopper, Nos. 99-1687 and 99-1728; 532 U.S. 514.

**Breyer, concurring, joined by O'Connor.** They join the Court's opinion in full because it resolves this application without making truthful publication automatically superior to every privacy interest. The statutes protect private speech as well as privacy: people may speak less freely when an illegal interception can be disseminated without consequence. The competing public-speech interest is therefore assessed against a real constitutional and practical value, rather than against a mere dislike of publicity.

The recipients acted lawfully in obtaining the recording and did not cause its interception. The speakers were directing a public-school labor dispute, and the recorded discussion concerned apparent physical harm against board members. Those circumstances create an unusually strong public interest and reduce the legitimate claim to secrecy for this particular discussion. Their Turner, Rice, Sheets, Denius and Sterling merits positions support actual review, lawful responsibility boundaries and serious protection of genuine confidentiality. They preserve legislative latitude to protect private conversation and to prevent the distinct harm of dissemination in materially different settings. **Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** They would reverse the constitutional ruling and summary-judgment direction in both dockets, then return the claims for ordinary adjudication of statutory elements and preserved defenses. Dissemination enlarges the intrusion and undermines the privacy that permits candid conversation.

The source criterion does not select a viewpoint or message. Under the content-neutral framework, the dissent finds adequate justification in preventing the additional disclosure injury, reinforced by the reasonable legislative judgment that limiting downstream dissemination can reduce the reward for interception. They would not require lawmakers to accept a route around the prohibition through recipients who did not themselves conduct the tap. That argument is a judgment about permissible prevention, not a finding of a quantified deterrent effect or respondent participation.

They distinguish Daily Mail, Florida Star and Landmark because the information here remained private until unlawful interception and the knowing disclosure itself is prohibited. Cohen and Branzburg preserve application of valid general law to the press; Rice supplies no universal requirement that a discloser must have assisted the antecedent crime before any publication restriction can be justified. Their objections to constitutional extensions in Sheets, Denius and Sterling do not deny legislative authority to protect conversation. They accept the need for First Amendment review and the significance of lawful receipt and public relevance, but find those considerations insufficient to invalidate this application. Reversal would not establish intentional interception, the remaining statutory elements, damages or any criminal threat.

## 2001-06-18 — Saucier v. Katz; No. 99-1977; judgment below, 194 F.3d 962.

**Souter, announcing the judgment.** Souter considers the merits and immunity inquiries legally distinct: an officer may perceive the facts correctly yet reasonably misunderstand the particular constitutional limit. Harlow and Anderson preserve that legal-error protection; Graham's allowance for reasonable factual perceptions does not itself decide it. General prohibition of excessive force is insufficient by itself, but a materially identical precedent is not indispensable. Hunter and Elder require the proper adverse factual assumptions and the full relevant conduct-date law. Souter would have ordinary lower-court immunity adjudication first determine whether those assumed facts establish a constitutional violation and, if so, whether the particular right was sufficiently established, so that the constitutional rule can develop. He recognizes the limits of the granted review here and assumes a possible violation; he does not require decision of a constitutional issue outside review or an advisory ruling. That sequencing view is his own, not a rule adopted by the judgment majority. Unlike the dissenters, he leaves the first distinct application to the lower court and directs neither immunity nor its denial. Unlike the other concurring Justices, he retains the distinct legal-error rationale in this force setting.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in the judgment.** The Chief Justice accepts the exact existing-law remand while proposing a different personal defense. He would displace Harlow and Anderson only insofar as they protect an official merely because the violated right was insufficiently clearly established, preserving their compatible particularized analysis and lawful summary-judgment requirements. First, the complete constitutional rule would account for urgency, reasonably perceived danger, reasonable factual mistakes and operational discretion. If a violation were established, the official would bear the burden of identifying binding precedent or enacted authority directly authorizing the conduct, genuine reliance on it and a reasonable basis for treating it as valid and applicable. An instruction accurately conveying that authority could implement reliance. Legal silence, lack of a materially identical case, department custom, an unsupported supervisor's assurance and general job authority would not suffice.

In his view, legitimate protection against unexpected legal change concerns affirmative authoritative permission. The existing defense's predictability and litigation-control benefits do not justify withholding an otherwise available remedy from the first claimant establishing a particular application of settled protection. Graham already accounts for the officer's practical circumstances; those same factual allowances should not excuse wrongful force again. On Katz's supported account, an unnecessary shove of an already controlled, unresisting demonstrator violates the force standard without requiring physical injury, while necessary proportionate security removal remains lawful. Actual necessity, resistance, perceptions and participation remain disputed. The record identifies no binding precedent or enactment directly authorizing the alleged unnecessary shove; general security authority is insufficient.

His proposed defense would govern this pending case, with a fair opportunity for bounded submissions on genuinely new reliance elements. An expectation of immunity from unclear law would not preserve the displaced defense; final judgments and ordinary preservation and preclusion rules would remain. The proposal concerns this personal legal-uncertainty defense and materially equivalent claims, not function-specific absolute immunity, sovereign immunity, proper defendants, causation, injury or the availability of a cause of action. Failure of the proposed defense would establish neither a constitutional violation, damages, institutional liability nor a new cause of action; each independent requirement remains. These are separate views: the Court's mandate imposes no reliance burden, transition rule or additional submissions requirement.

**Ginsburg, joined by Stevens and Breyer, concurring in the judgment.** These Justices join the exact vacatur and open application remand while maintaining that Graham already tests the practical choices and reasonable mistakes available to an officer using force. They reject a second overlapping reasonableness shelter for force found objectively unreasonable under that complete standard. That objection is confined to this excessive-force setting; they retain ordinary Harlow protection elsewhere and do not endorse the Chief Justice's general actual-reliance replacement.

The supported unnecessary shared shove of a controlled, unresisting person cannot be dismissed by treating every degree of force as justified by the initial need for security removal. Johnson and Sterling prevent reappraisal of the participation evidence on this appeal. Plakas permits early merits judgment on a properly supported no-violation account, but its immediate armed attack without a contrary material account is absent here. The continuous movement into the van is relevant context, not a factual answer to the alleged unnecessary force. Their separate analysis does not alter their vote for the mandate: neither immunity nor denial, automatic trial, liability or damages is directed.

**Kennedy, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, dissenting.** These Justices would reverse and direct personal damages immunity while assuming a possible constitutional violation. Harlow and Anderson protect reasonable legal error independently of Graham's merits inquiry. Accepting the plaintiff-favorable two-officer account, they regard the carrying and van entry as a continuous urgent removal from a protected event; the particular boundary of this brief nondeadly force was not sufficiently apparent in September 1994. They recognize the strongest contrary point: a controlled, unresisting person's forceful van entry may lack additional demonstrated need. They nevertheless treat this as uncertainty over the degree of force during removal, not a legally unmistakable separate assault after the seizure ended.

Their position does not depend on deciding that Parker acted alone, finding resistance, declaring the force lawful or making injury a prerequisite. His rule covers the officers' own connected warrant undertaking, not unrelated official conduct or an officer who never accepted tribal process. It waives no state immunity and decides no search violation, compensable injury, personal participation or immunity merits.

## 2001-06-25 — New York Times Co. v. Tasini, No. 00-201; OT2000, chunk 8.

Justice Stevens, joined by Justice Breyer, dissents. They would recognize the demonstrated archival uses as revisions only when both conditions are met: each article expressly identifies its source collective work, and substantially the rest of that collective work is readily accessible to the reader of the individual file at the same time. The publisher's editorial selection and issue association must remain available. A detached article with only a source label does not qualify.

In their view the files corresponding to an issue can constitute its revision, and aggregation of those revisions into a searchable archive does not destroy that status. The library and microform analogies demonstrate why a reader's selection of one item need not change the character of the collection. GPO's images add page context, but their conclusion does not depend on images alone. They regard the accessible editorial contents and express issue association as sufficient; the Court instead requires a perceptible collective presentation these interfaces lack. Neither side permits alteration of the contribution itself or use in an unrelated anthology merely because its former source is identified.

The dissent preserves the author's ownership and the compiler's limited privilege under §§103(b) and 201(c). Stewart's different 1909 Act dispute does not erase the modern statutory separation; Campbell and Quality King likewise require giving the actual statutory permission its full effect. Archive completeness and clearance costs reinforce their construction rather than create a free-standing public-benefit exemption or prove inevitable deletion.

On §201(d), they reject complete nontransferability: a publisher may farm out printing or distribution of its own collective work or qualifying revision. That does not permit assignment authorizing the contribution's inclusion in a wholly different collective work. Because the Court and the court of appeals did not decide transferability, they decline further resolution. They would reverse the §201(c) construction, vacate the dependent liability disposition and return for consistent statutory proceedings, without reopening the unpressed contract defenses or guaranteeing final publisher judgment on every other preserved issue. They order no fixed license, universal archive remedy or revision of unpresented agreements.

## 2001-06-25 — United States v. United Foods, Inc., No. 00-276; OT2000, chunk 8.

Justice Stevens concurs and joins the Court's opinion in full. Compelling payment differs from forcing a person to speak, and not every governmental use of money impairs the same liberty. But requiring an independent producer to finance competitors' preferred advertising still burdens protected choice. The assertion that a useful campaign benefits everyone does not supply the sufficient independent function that justified Glickman's collective regulation or Southworth's broad educational program. He finds the compulsory-subsidy ground sufficient and declines the new operational government-speech inquiry. He decides nothing about a materially different health, safety or artistic purpose and does not announce that every compulsory expression payment is forbidden.

Justice Thomas concurs separately as well as delivering the Court's opinion. He would not reduce the protection of compelled financing because the message is commercial. His Glickman position rejects Central Hudson's discounted framework as the governing answer to that burden. The Court's sufficient compulsory-subsidy rule resolves this case without adopting his broader standard. His observation that failure under a lesser test would also establish failure under a stronger one does not adopt that lesser test, find its elements empirically unsatisfied or supply an additional Court holding. He preserves Southworth's separately justified neutral educational program and does not prohibit every regulatory fee or tax supporting actual government speech. No other Justice joins this stronger-framework concurrence.

Justice Breyer, joined by Justices O'Connor and Ginsburg, dissents. They accept that compelled financial support can burden expression and that actual Glickman supplies no general economic-regulation immunity. They would nevertheless sustain this assessment under independent commercial-speech scrutiny: government must show a substantial interest, direct advancement and reasonable fit; no issue of unlawful or misleading commercial content is presented.

They would reach that alternative because it concerns the same assessment, constitutional controversy and relief, the government denies waiver, and United Foods addresses its merits. This exercise of discretion does not establish automatic consideration of every reply argument or new evidentiary admission. Government speech's conceded nonpresentation is separate and need not be decided on their sufficient ground.

National agricultural-market maintenance and development, together with the supported collective-action problem, supply substantial interests. The actual promotion serves those interests, and the legislative and program record and reported experience support its practical capacity to advance them. The reported failures of voluntary campaigns support compulsory contributions. Brand advertising seeks a producer's share and need not perform the same market-wide function, so an automatic credit is unnecessary. The exemption at or below 500,000 pounds can avoid disproportionate collection and administration; the reply's numerical illustration is arithmetic, not a verified cost study.

National promotion need not return benefits to each region in proportion to a producer's distribution. Exempt beneficiaries, elastic supply, new entry and possible intermediary capture do not establish that market expansion fails to serve the public objective. Reasonable fit does not require the least restrictive method, identical treatment of every commodity, guaranteed profit for United Foods or a prescribed empirical study. The reported effectiveness submission supports their assessment of the existing record; they adopt no numerical coefficient, new methodology finding, evidentiary-admission order or concession by the producer.

These three reject a conclusive subsidy bar where the government has independently justified the commercial burden. They would reverse and remand for lawful conforming proceedings, without a fixed assessment award or a new evidentiary inquiry. Accounting and other lawful implementation remain; they make no finding that an official authored, adopted or controlled the message.

## 2001-06-27 — Adair v. Sherman; Seventh Circuit No. 99-1736, 230 F.3d 890; district-court No. 98 C 3946; OT2000, chunk 8.

All nine Justices join the Court's complete reasoning and disposition. No separate opinion or additional published ground is filed.

## 2001-06-28 — Lorillard Tobacco Co. v. Reilly and Altadis U.S.A. Inc. v. Reilly, Nos. 00-596 and 00-597; OT2000, chunk 8.

**Kennedy, joined by Scalia, concurring in part and in the judgment.** They join preemption, the sufficient advertising failures, access and nonreach, but withhold Part II-A. The product-and-medium-specific causal showing remains doubtful and unnecessary to decide because the actual outdoor burden fails fit even on the State's strongest supported account. Edenfield and Rubin require justification of the precise restriction; neither accepting ordinary commercial-speech analysis nor joining a fit conclusion establishes an affirmative finding at every prong. They do not find that advertising never affects youth, deny the existence of evidence or adopt Thomas's entire stronger theory. Their sufficient fit ground supports reversal without another tailoring trial.

**Thomas, concurring in part and in the judgment.** Thomas expressly accepts the identified outdoor-fit and two height failures on the condition that the ordinary test is applied, while refusing to endorse reduced protection for lawful truthful commercial expression. His independent ground demands strict scrutiny for suppression operating through the message's effect on willing adult listeners. His positions in 44 Liquormart, Greater New Orleans and United Foods distinguish failure even under a weaker test from approval of that test. The serious child-protection interest does not permit broad suppression of lawful adult information without the required fit; he can assume the State's strongest supported preventive case and still reject these means. He withholds affirmative outdoor advancement and does not turn contested map attribution or disputed characterizations into new factual findings. He joins expression-independent access scrutiny because it does not discount speech as commercial. His stronger rationale does not become the Court's framework, a sampling holding or a decision about derivative inseverability.

**Stevens, joined by Ginsburg and Breyer, and by Souter in the preemption and outdoor-remand portions, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** The four read the federal clause in its warning-and-information setting. Congress's concern about diverse cigarette messages and traditional local placement authority support preserving these location rules, despite their health purpose and the broader 1969 wording. Cipollone does not exempt every positive command, but neither its predicate-duty method nor Medtronic and Geier establish an exclusive federal spatial policy. They would reach cigarette speech as well as the noncigarette claims. They accept the supported outdoor advancement proposition but would vacate for trial on fit: disputed incremental coverage, statewide variation, traffic and quality of permissible sites, actual alternative media and differences between retailers and manufacturers prevent final judgment about practical adult channels. USSTC's own-advertising claim belongs in that inquiry. Greater New Orleans involved structural defects stronger evidence could not cure; it does not forbid this application-sensitive trial. They predict no winner and do not reopen unappealed relief.

Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer alone would sustain the height rule as a limited indoor adjunct to valid staff-mediated physical sales and display. They acknowledge doubt if it is treated as a direct speech restraint, and adopt no affirmative ordinary-test advancement or fit finding. They also concur in access affirmance because the requirement regulates physical acquisition, without joining the Court's separate assumed-speech analysis. Assisted inspection preserves the real sensory information important to cigar purchasers. Souter instead joins the Court's independent height failures and assumed-speech access ground. All four accept the distinct sampling and inseverability nonreach. Their alternative cigarette speech positions do not add controlling constitutional grounds to the preemption disposition.

## 2001-06-28 — Palazzolo v. Rhode Island, No. 99-2047; OT2000, chunk 8.

**Stevens, concurrence in part and dissent in part.** Stevens agrees that the existing fixed-restriction challenge is final and joins the bounded acquisition and complete-loss holdings. He would nevertheless affirm because he reads this compensation claim as seeking redress for a restriction imposed before the present owner took title. Danforth and Dow distinguish the owner who suffered the taking from a later title holder. Ripeness for review and the time of injury are different questions; repeating a denial under an unchanged restriction does not itself transfer the same property interest again. The owner's asserted 1986 date does not resolve that objection. Stevens makes no neutral finding that a taking occurred at enactment and finds no assignment. He accepts Dodd's requirement of a combined inquiry and therefore does not say that acquisition notice alone reduces all acquired property to a discretionary opportunity for a hearing. A genuinely new post-acquisition deprivation would involve a different supported injury theory. His disagreement is the present owner's compensable injury, not finality or a universal notice bar.

**Ginsburg, joined by Souter and Breyer, dissenting.** They would affirm because the additional upland sites and their access remain materially unresolved aspects of actual permitted use on the same parcel. Suitum concerned an already definitive agency answer; it did not treat uncertainty about on-parcel development as merely a question about saleable rights elsewhere. The State's statement that it would permit a valuable home establishes a floor, not a ceiling, and the trial testimony supplies possible additional sites without establishing issued permission. Their approach requires neither endless resubmission of the same rejected design nor an exact maximum valuation. It requires a sufficiently settled regulatory burden for the whole-parcel challenge actually litigated. If the merits were properly reached, they agree that acquisition alone does not bar the owner's own claim, meaningful retained use defeats total deprivation and a preserved partial-taking claim receives Penn Central's combined inquiry. Those conditional views supply no vote for this remand, no new findings about additional uses and no automatic resolution of title, claim ownership or preservation.

## 2001-06-28 — Tyler v. Cain, No. 00-5961; OT2000, chunk 8.

**Scalia, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** The dissent joins the multiple-holdings construction and the distinction between authorization and actual verification. It rejects the Court's conclusion that the relevant holdings leave no further retroactivity judgment. Sullivan classified a conceded verdict-wide error for harmless-error purposes and expressly reserved collateral gates. Teague's demanding procedural exception requires a rule-specific classification; the seriousness of the defect and its structural treatment do not show that this Court has already made that distinct determination. The majority's reasoning may support making the rule retroactive, but, in the dissent's view, it does not identify existing holdings that necessarily did so.

Their position does not require a single opinion, ritual wording or an express formula. Several holdings can suffice when the consequence is strictly necessary, rather than a persuasive application of general principles. Nor does their gate-based affirmance approve Tyler's charge, declare Cage nonretroactive in every proper proceeding, reopen accepted newness or previous unavailability, or find an additional claim bar from missing earlier-application materials. They would decline an unnecessary new declaration in this proceeding because it would not supply the preexisting Supreme Court action they regard as required for this application. They promise no future vehicle and decide no unpresented constitutional attack on the successive scheme.

## 2001-06-28 — Zadvydas v. Davis and Ashcroft v. Ma, Nos. 99-7791 and 00-38; OT2000, chunk 8.

**Stone-Zsela, concurrence in part and in the judgments.** Stone agrees with the removal-purpose limit, retained habeas review, both vacaturs and effective supervised relief when authority ends. He does not join a numerical presumption. Once the removal period has ended and the detainee presents concrete evidence of repeated refusals or comparable obstacles, he would require the government promptly to identify facts supporting a reasonably foreseeable removal prospect. The detainee must have a meaningful opportunity to answer that account. Duration, progress, receiving-country decisions, travel-document history and actual obstruction bear on individualized necessity; ongoing negotiations or theoretical possibility alone are insufficient.

His objection does not characterize the Court's rebuttable rule as an absolute safe harbor. He declines to make a calendar presumption part of his rule because custody may lose its purpose before six months, while genuinely imminent removal can justify custody after the ordinary period. He adopts neither automatic day-ninety-one release nor a mandatory six-month ceiling. Danger may inform custody within a live removal process but cannot preserve that source after its purpose fails. Other preventive detention requires its own authority and safeguards. His different trigger is noncontrolling; the Court's five directly adopt Part III.

**Kennedy, dissenting, Part I, joined by Scalia and Thomas.** The dissenters read paragraph (6) to authorize discretionary detention of the covered criminal and risk categories without a judicial foreseeable-removal condition. Congress expressly permitted custody beyond the ordinary period and selected categories serving protective as well as removal purposes, yet supplied no endpoint. Foreign refusal, absence of an agreement or inability to forecast a removal date therefore does not alone terminate authority. The common detention phrase also reaches inadmissible and removable categories, which weighs against implying different meanings, although these cases do not adjudicate every initial-entry setting. They reject both the statutory limitation and the numerical implementation, while applying the governing habeas channel and preserving actual regulatory requirements. Their dispositions would affirm the Fifth Circuit against this general challenge and reverse and remand the Ninth Circuit; neither automatically orders renewed custody.

**Kennedy, dissenting, Part II, for himself.** Kennedy recognizes protection against arbitrary or capricious detention and unlawful punishment for both removable and inadmissible people. Genuine danger or flight may sustain detention even if removal cannot be forecast. The replacement regulation supplies meaningful ways to contest continued custody: notice, reasons, records and interview review, headquarters responsibility and later review. Those requirements matter without establishing compliance in either case. Denial of prescribed process in an arbitrary or categorical manner is subject to habeas challenge.

He would reject this presented general duration challenge, without a new affirmative-current-justification holding or two new constitutional merits remands. A proper concrete case may require deciding the severity of danger needed, whether an actual danger or flight finding is unjustified, the adequacy of its review, or the operation of the burden. Neither the present alien-satisfaction language nor the availability of supervision alone proves such a defect. He imposes no universal hearing calendar, new government burden or automatic adversarial release trial. The distinction between general authority and an unlawful particular application preserves those questions without inventing unfinished claims in these proceedings.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, dissenting.** They reject the claimed general constitutional entitlement to supervised release into this country merely because removal after a valid final order is not foreseeable. Their Flores and Foucha positions make the precisely asserted right, adjudicated status and authorized custody channel material. Their use of Mezei as an analogy concerns that asserted right; it is not a finding that these people were never admitted. They accept the enacted discretionary channel and the opportunity for release review but do not adopt Kennedy's separate procedural-liberty explanation or a new affirmative-current-justification rule. Their general-challenge disposition supplies no indefinite remand to develop another theory. It does not authorize torture, unlawful punishment, sham authority or denial of a legally required procedure; no such particular violation is found, and no unpresented remedy is prescribed.
