### Board of Trustees of the University of Alabama v. Garrett — 2001-02-21

Authority: [controlling holdings](../records/Board_of_Trustees_of_University_of_Alabama_v_Garrett_merits_2001-02-21.md#holdings); [precedent treatment](../records/Board_of_Trustees_of_University_of_Alabama_v_Garrett_merits_2001-02-21.md#precedent-treatment).

#### I. Authorization and the valid employment duty

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 12202 unmistakably authorizes private federal Title I actions against States, and Title I's employment obligations validly regulate covered state employment under the Commerce Clause. Authorization and a valid duty do not establish liability or entitlement to any award.

**Authority:** Part I of Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's opinion has the direct joins of all nine Justices and controls. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas join it while dissenting from Parts III–IV and the judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 12202 expressly removes State immunity for violations of the chapter and makes legal and equitable remedies available on the same terms as against other public or private entities. Section 12117 supplies the employment-enforcement channels. These provisions resolve authorization without establishing consent, constitutional power, liability or an award.

Title I regulates hiring, retention and the other terms of covered paid employment, an economic relationship participating in actual labor markets. That connection satisfies Lopez before aggregate effects are considered; generalized productivity or social costs do not supply the constitutional basis. Garcia and EEOC v. Wyoming, applied in Kimel, sustain direct federal regulation of covered state employment. Whether a State may be made to answer a private action for breach of that valid duty, and whether Title I also enforces the Fourteenth Amendment, are distinct questions that Parts II and III decide.

#### II. Section 5 does not independently sustain Title I at the full scope presented

**Holding and operative rule:** Title I's general employment and accommodation requirements, although valid Article I legislation, are not independently established as congruent and proportional preventive enforcement of equal protection against state employers at the full statutory scope presented. The record of disability exclusion supports legislative concern, but not every statutory failure to accommodate or adverse decision is a constitutional wrong. The Court does not announce that disability discrimination cannot support Section 5 legislation, that accommodations can never be constitutionally relevant, or that all ADA titles receive the same analysis.

**Authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, directly supplies five votes for this holding. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissent from Part II. The holding does not decide the judgment, which rests on Part III.

**Controlling explanation:** Boerne permits prevention extending beyond conduct that a court would independently hold unconstitutional, but requires congruence and proportionality between the constitutional injury and the means. As in Florida Prepaid, Congress need not establish a fixed numerical count of prior violations, make formal findings in a prescribed format, confine a preventive measure to proven violators, or require each plaintiff to prove a constitutional violation. Cleburne, as applied in Heller and Romer, supplies the baseline: it forbids irrational disability prejudice and requires a rational relationship to the actual disadvantage, while preserving relevant generalizations and conceivable legitimate explanations, without heightened disability scrutiny or a constitutional entitlement to accommodation.

Congress received accounts of categorical exclusions, stereotyped assumptions, harassment and rejection in State departments and universities, alongside evidence concerning local employers, private conduct and other public services. That record supports legislative concern that official exclusion may reflect stereotyping or indifference as well as overt hostility. The approximately 600 state-actor submissions are neither adjudicated violations nor a count of State-employment violations, and state antidiscrimination statutes do not by their existence establish compliance.

The defect is fit. Title I's general requirements impose ordinary statutory liability across all covered state employment: reasonable accommodation of known limitations up to undue hardship, qualification and screening standards that must be shown job-related and consistent with business necessity, and liability for each adverse decision within the statute. Constitutional rationality permits many decisions those requirements forbid. Qualified-worker limits, essential-function rules and the undue-hardship and business-necessity defenses narrow that gap but do not establish that the full requirements are a congruent and proportional response to unconstitutional state-employment discrimination. The partial dissent's preventive inference from the official-employment accounts and the safeguards supports concern about the problem, not fit for every general requirement at the scope enacted. The conclusion rests on fit at the full statutory scope, not on a presumption of State compliance, the existence of state laws, the absence of adjudicated violations or an evidentiary quota. It leaves Part I's valid duty and Part III's Article I ground untouched, and a differently supported or fitted measure remains within Section 5.

#### III. Article I sustains the expressly authorized Title I action

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress validly regulates covered state employment and unmistakably authorizes the Title I action; immunity does not bar it. Under Florida Prepaid's Article I enforcement rule, which Kimel applies to employment, a State may be subjected to the expressly authorized private Title I action because the employment obligation and its ordinary enforcement measure fall within the Commerce and Necessary and Proper Clauses. This ground alone sustains the judgment; amenability establishes neither liability nor entitlement to a particular award.

**Authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Parts III and IV, joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, directly supply five votes for this holding and for the affirmance that rests on it. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from Parts III–IV and the judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Florida Prepaid permits expressly authorized private enforcement of a valid Article I duty against a State; Kimel applies that principle to employment. Part I establishes the valid employment duty and the unmistakable authorization. An ordinary action enforcing that duty is an appropriate enforcement measure, not compelled State administration of a federal program. The rule depends on a valid enumerated-power obligation, an appropriate enforcement measure and unmistakable authorization. It does not depend on showing that the same duty also enforces the Fourteenth Amendment, so Part II leaves it intact.

The States' distinction between valid employment regulation and private suit seeks reconsideration of Florida Prepaid's and Kimel's enforcement rule and of Union Gas's retained conclusion; the Court adheres to them. Requiring an individual constitutional violation as an added entry condition would remove the statutory protection that Congress validly enacted. Because Part II holds that Section 5 does not sustain Title I at the scope presented, this ground alone decides the judgment. Part IV affirms on it and returns the actions for ordinary adjudication of coverage, qualification, discrimination, accommodation, defenses, causation and remedies.

**Statutory limits and work remaining:** Covered-employer status requires an industry affecting commerce and ordinarily at least 15 employees for each working day in at least 20 calendar weeks of the current or preceding year; the original two-year transition used 25 employees. Agents are included; the United States, wholly owned federal corporations, Indian tribes and bona fide private membership clubs other than labor organizations that are exempt from taxation under 26 U.S.C. section 501(c) are excluded from this employer definition. Actual coverage remains to be proved.

Disability retains impairment substantially limiting a major life activity, record-of-impairment and regarded-as branches. A qualified worker must perform essential functions with or without reasonable accommodation. Employer judgment receives consideration; a job description prepared before advertising or interviews is evidence, not a conclusive veto. Accommodation examples are permissive and include reassignment to a vacant position. Section 12112(b)(5)(A) concerns known limitations and allows the employer to demonstrate undue hardship: significant difficulty or expense assessed by cost, facility and entity resources, workforce, operational and organizational factors. Section 12112(b)(6) separately addresses screening criteria shown job-related for the position and consistent with business necessity; section 12113(a) separately requires that performance cannot be accomplished by reasonable accommodation. Direct threat concerns a significant risk to the health or safety of others that reasonable accommodation cannot eliminate. No general reasonableness exception or Title II fundamental-alteration defense replaces those provisions.

No medical-inquiry, foreign-workplace, religious-entity, food-handling or drug/alcohol defense is established or adjudicated here. The Court creates no exception or holding about those distinct provisions. Title II, Rehabilitation Act waiver, individual liability and award-specific limitations lie outside this Title I abrogation question; no Title II dismissal or abandonment is ordered. Qualification, discrimination, causation and relief are ordinary remaining merits issues, not unresolved aspects of the constitutional enforcement holdings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Florida Prepaid, June 23, 1999: applies Part III's express Article I enforcement rule, which alone sustains this judgment, and Part II's Section 5 holding: a statute enforcing ordinary federal liability against States without differentiating the constitutional wrong needs an adequately supported preventive relationship to that wrong, without a numerical count, formal findings, confinement to proven violators or an individual constitutional tort; neither ground requires consent.
- Kimel, January 11, 2000: applies its Article I employment-enforcement holding; its reservation of the ADEA's Section 5 validity remains untouched.
- Lopez: applies the identifiable commerce connection before aggregation; no general social-cost theory or automatic jurisdictional-word test is adopted.
- Garcia and EEOC v. Wyoming: apply their support for valid federal regulation of state employment.
- Boerne: applies congruence and proportionality, informed by the rational-basis baseline and Title I's actual duties, to reject Section 5 support for Title I's general requirements at the full scope presented. It preserves preventive authority and the prohibition on constitutional redefinition, with no mechanical evidentiary quota.
- Cleburne, Heller and Romer: apply actual rationality review as the constitutional baseline against which Title I's duties are measured, without heightened disability scrutiny or a constitutional guarantee of accommodation.
- Olmstead: distinguishes its statutory integration holding and express nondecision of immunity and Section 5; its Title II defense is not imported.
- Union Gas and Seminole Tribe: preserve the constitutional conclusion extended in Florida Prepaid and the distinction between authorization and power; no categorical Article I prohibition displaces current law.
- College Savings Bank: preserves the rejection of consent inferred from ordinary State activity; these actions rest on legislative authority.


### Shafer v. South Carolina — 2001-03-20

Authority: [controlling holdings](../records/Shafer_v_South_Carolina_merits_2001-03-20.md#holdings), [precedent treatment](../records/Shafer_v_South_Carolina_merits_2001-03-20.md#precedent-treatment), and [current-law effect](../records/Shafer_v_South_Carolina_merits_2001-03-20.md#law-after-decision).

#### The jury's actual sentencing alternatives govern the information right

**Holding and operative rule:** When the State places future dangerousness in issue and a capital jury, after finding the required statutory aggravator, can choose only death or life without ordinary parole, due process requires an effective opportunity to explain that ineligibility through accurate defense presentation or an adequate instruction. A lesser sentence available only to the judge if the jury finds no aggravator does not defeat that protection at the later jury sentencing choice; the rule governs this staged procedure and materially equivalent choices.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies seven direct votes for this and the following two holdings. No combination of separate rationales is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The sentencing alternatives matter when the jury exercises the choice between death and continued life. Under section 16-3-20, a jury that has not found an aggravator performs a factual task and cannot recommend a sentence. If no aggravator is found, the judge becomes the sentencer. Once the aggravator is found, the jury has no thirty-year option. The State's reliance on the statute's complete list of possible punishments therefore mistakes a conditional judicial alternative for an alternative available at the decision Simmons protects.

Simmons supplies the right to answer a State future-danger case with the legally accurate noncapital alternative. Gardner supplies the opportunity to answer material capital-sentencing information, and Skipper protects a defendant's response to the State's future-danger premise. The protection cannot depend on whether the same two sentencing choices follow a preliminary factual determination. Ramdass likewise requires attention to actual legal consequences and a truthful account of any genuinely conditional consequence. Here life without ordinary parole is the actual alternative after the aggravator finding, not an anticipated conviction or uncertain future event.

The preliminary aggravator inquiry itself does not involve the sentencing discretion addressed by this rule. The right still requires a State future-danger case and a legally accurate ineligibility premise. The Court rejects the categorical structural bar without finding that the dangerousness predicate was satisfied in this trial.

#### The combined presentation was inadequate if future dangerousness was in issue

**Holding and operative rule:** If the prosecution put future dangerousness in issue, the repeated until-death definitions and permitted natural-life defense argument did not provide an effective informational opportunity in this case when considered together with the refusal of the requested statutory parole explanation and the supplemental direction excluding parole eligibility or ineligibility after the jury's questions. This determination concerns the legal adequacy of the complete presentation, requires no particular words, and makes no finding about any juror's actual belief.

**Authority:** The seven-Justice Court opinion identified above controls this complete, conditional adequacy determination.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court considers what counsel was allowed to say as well as what the judge said. This was not merely an undefined reference to life imprisonment: the judge repeatedly described confinement until death, and counsel argued that Shafer would spend his natural life in prison and never go home. Those explanations support the State's argument that the substance of ineligibility was conveyed. Simmons, however, requires an effective opportunity to use the accurate legal answer, not simply the presence of accurate duration language somewhere in the presentation.

After refusing the requested express statutory explanation, the court answered the parole inquiries by repeating the duration definition while placing both parole eligibility and ineligibility outside the jury's consideration. Taken as a whole, the presentation did not make ordinary-parole ineligibility available as the answer to a future-danger case. The questions referred generally to a murder conviction and do not establish what the jurors thought about a life sentence. Their generality nevertheless does not answer the legal question the combined presentation left unavailable at the jury's actual death-or-life choice.

The conclusion rests on that combination, not the refusal alone, the supplemental direction alone, or a requirement to use the word parole. Accurate effective defense presentation or an equivalent adequate judicial explanation remains sufficient. Ramos permits truthful release-related information; it does not require a guarantee against clemency or later legislation, and neither does this decision. The State remains free to present truthful evidence of danger within prison. Whether the State raised future dangerousness and whether an established violation affected death selection remain separate inquiries.

#### The remand returns dangerousness and error effect, not informational adequacy

**Holding and operative rule:** The state court must determine on the trial record whether the prosecution's evidence or argument placed future dangerousness in issue; if it did, informational inadequacy is settled and the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the selection of death. If the State fails that burden, a new lawful penalty proceeding is required if it seeks death, or a lawful noncapital sentence may be imposed; if future dangerousness was not in issue, this Simmons due-process claim alone establishes no violation.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices join the Court's remand and its governing standard in full.

**Controlling explanation:** The South Carolina Supreme Court's categorical view of the sentencing scheme prevented it from deciding whether the prosecution raised future danger. The evidence, the purpose for which it was offered, the prosecutor's argument and the jury's questions must be assessed together. No prescribed phrase is necessary to raise future dangerousness, and circumstantial or qualitative proof cannot be disregarded merely because the prosecutor did not use that phrase. The jury's questions alone establish neither the predicate nor its absence. The state court must perform that record-based inquiry rather than return to the rejected structural bar.

If the predicate exists, the Court's second holding establishes the informational error. Chapman supplies the direct-review burden: the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to death selection. Simmons applies that burden to this kind of error and requires a lawful new penalty proceeding if the State cannot carry it and continues to seek death. Simmons's finding of harmfulness on its own record does not resolve the effect of this different presentation. The error is not structural and neither harmlessness nor harmfulness is found here.

Ramdass's distinct federal habeas conditions do not govern this direct review. The remaining predicate and effect determinations are ordinary work on remand under settled rules, not an invitation to decide adequacy again. No immediate new hearing, sentence substitution or sentence vacatur follows from the present judgment. Guilt and the separate sentences are unaffected.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The broader instruction right when the State has not raised future danger is not decided because the federal question resolved here is the conditional Simmons protection. No independently sufficient Eighth Amendment instruction ground is adopted. The actual dangerousness predicate and, if necessary, error effect await state adjudication on the existing record; these are application and remedial work under the holdings, not gaps in the legal adequacy rule.

- **Simmons v. South Carolina, 512 U.S. 154 (1994):** Applied to the actual death-or-life choice in a staged sentencing procedure and to the complete instructional presentation. Its effective-opportunity rule, alternative means of explanation and direct-review remedy remain controlling. Its own harmfulness finding is confined to its record.
- **Gardner v. Florida, 430 U.S. 349 (1977):** Its opportunity to answer material capital-sentencing information supplies the due-process foundation; no new disclosure rule independent of the Simmons predicates is adopted.
- **Skipper v. South Carolina, 476 U.S. 1 (1986):** Its protection for rebutting a State future-danger case supports accurate parole information; its distinct mitigation protection remains intact.
- **California v. Ramos, 463 U.S. 992 (1983):** Distinguished to preserve truthful release-related information without treating hypothetical clemency as ordinary parole or requiring a guarantee against future legal change.
- **Ramdass v. Angelone, 530 U.S. 156 (2000):** Its attention to actual and conditional legal consequences is applied. Its separate habeas requirements and unresolved factual predicates do not control this direct-review remedy or establish error effect here.
- **Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967):** Applied without changing the State's burden to establish beyond a reasonable doubt that a constitutional error did not contribute to the death verdict.

Effective March 20, 2001, Simmons governs South Carolina's staged capital procedure and materially equivalent jury choices: an earlier conditional judicial sentence does not eliminate the information right when the jury actually chooses death or life without ordinary parole. The State-raised-danger trigger and accurate legal ineligibility premise remain necessary, and effective defense presentation or adequate instruction remains sufficient without a required script.

The combined presentation in this case fails that requirement if the danger predicate is established. Adequacy is settled for the remand. The state court must decide danger and, if present, apply Chapman's State-burden inquiry before determining whether a new capital penalty proceeding or a lawful noncapital sentence is required. This application does not overrule a precedent, impose automatic reversal or settle a broader instruction right without the danger predicate.


### Board of Trustees of the University of Alabama v. Garrett — 2001-02-21

**Justice Breyer, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** The four join Parts I, III and IV and the judgment and dissent from Part II. Given the sufficient Article I ground, they would not have decided Section 5; because the Court decides it, they would hold that Section 5 independently sustains Title I. Boerne permits fitted prevention beyond conduct that is independently unconstitutional. Investigation and the official-employment accounts support the inference that exclusions and apparently neutral barriers conceal or perpetuate irrational official treatment, and Title I's qualification, essential-function, known-limitations, accommodation, screening and hardship rules connect the means to that risk. Acknowledging some constitutionally permissible generalization does not invalidate fitted prophylaxis. The opinion adopts no heightened disability scrutiny or constitutional duty to provide every accommodation, finds no liability or entitlement to compensation, and makes no Title II disposition.

**Justice O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** The four join Parts I and II and dissent from Parts III–IV and the judgment. The dissent accepts section 12202's express authorization and Congress's authority to regulate employment, but distinguishes that authority from power to compel a nonconsenting State to defend a private damages action. It would reconsider Florida Prepaid's and Kimel's Article I enforcement rule and Union Gas's retained conclusion to the necessary extent. Because Part II holds that Section 5 does not sustain Title I at the scope presented, the judgment rests entirely on the rule the dissent would reconsider. It would reverse only the presented Title I immunity rejection and remand.

### Shafer v. South Carolina — 2001-03-20

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by no other Justice.** He would affirm because due process does not authorize this additional constitutional parole-information requirement. His Simmons objection distinguishes Gardner's unavailable adverse sentencing material and Skipper's excluded rebuttal evidence from a general duty to explain parole law; Ramos preserves substantial state control of truthful sentencing information. He accepts the logical relation between Simmons and this actual death-or-life choice but would confine that precedent to its facts. Sound instructional policy does not itself establish a constitutional command. No affirmative State representation that ordinary release was available establishes the distinct misleading-assertion problem. He decides neither adequate compliance under the Court's rule nor harmlessness and does not join Thomas's dissent.

**Thomas, dissenting, joined by no other Justice.** Assuming Simmons applies, he finds its substance satisfied by the repeated judicial until-death definitions, the natural-life verdict description and counsel's permitted argument that Shafer would remain in prison and never go home. The questions about someone convicted of murder are compatible with inquiry about the judge's options if no aggravator is found; they do not necessarily contradict the jury's explained life alternative. In his view the supplemental response confined the jury to its own task without affirmatively suggesting ordinary release. Simmons permits substance to be conveyed without preferred words, and greater precision does not itself justify constitutional intervention. He would affirm for adequate opportunity even assuming future danger, not because danger was absent or error harmless. His disagreement with Simmons remains a reservation, not a second independent ground here. He makes no finding about jurors' actual beliefs and does not join Scalia's dissent.
