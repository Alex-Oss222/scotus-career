#### Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp.

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-1525.
**Decided:** February 21, 1995.

For the other questions in this decision, see Federal Courts — Preserved claims and disavowed legal arguments (The Court may consider this included legal argument despite its earlier disavowal); Federal Courts — Relief after reversal of governmental-actor status (Governmental status requires a remand, not an immediate display order).

##### Special federal creation and permanent majority appointment make Amtrak governmental

**Holding:** When the Federal Government creates a corporation by special law to advance governmental objectives and permanently retains authority to appoint a majority of its governing directors, the corporation is governmental for First Amendment purposes notwithstanding a statutory declaration that it is not a governmental agency. Amtrak satisfies those combined conditions; ordinary operating discretion in its corporate officers does not require a separate external federal command for each challenged act.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's constitutional-status portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight Justices.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- San Francisco Arts & Athletics, Inc. v. United States Olympic Committee, 483 U.S. 522 (1987): distinguishes federal charter and assistance to a private institution from special creation coupled with permanent governmental majority-appointment authority.
- Edmonson v. Leesville Concrete Co., 500 U.S. 614 (1991), and Georgia v. McCollum, 505 U.S. 42 (1992): preserve attention to actual governmental legal machinery in their jury-selection settings; neither makes billboard advertising an exclusively governmental function or supplies a general rule for all publicly benefited corporations.
- Jackson v. Metropolitan Edison Co., 419 U.S. 345 (1974), and Blum v. Yaretsky, 457 U.S. 991 (1982): distinguish attribution of a private entity's particular act from identifying an institution that is itself governmental under the combined rule.
- Hess v. Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp., decided November 14, 1994: keeps the distinct inquiry into an entity's state sovereign immunity separate; absence of immunity does not imply constitutional privacy.
- American National Red Cross v. S. G., 505 U.S. 247 (1992): preserves its express charter-jurisdiction rule, which supplies no constitutional actor classification.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Federal charter, subsidy, regulation, governmental property, public benefit, a single appointment, temporary investment, or merely the current ownership of shares is not independently sufficient under this rule. Permanent authority to appoint the governing majority, governmental objectives, and special governmental creation must be present together. The Court decides no universal agency status, sovereign immunity, Treasury obligation, federal tort-liability rule, or charter jurisdiction. The 1994 recodification from former 45 U.S.C. §541 to 49 U.S.C. §24301(a) does not itself adjudicate constitutional status. The statutory board includes the Secretary of Transportation; Amtrak's president; three Presidentially appointed, Senate-confirmed directors representing the statutory labor, state-executive, and business categories; two Presidential selections from commuter-authority nominees under the statute; and two preferred-shareholder selections. The governmental preferred shareholder supplies those last two selections on this record. Neither the entire board's consideration of this particular refusal nor a new direct federal order to reject it is found.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Second Circuit is reversed and remanded, 8–1, to adjudicate the unreviewed First Amendment and forum questions with Amtrak treated as governmental and reconsider associated state-law dispositions under their own rules. The display injunction is not automatically reinstated; no final display, damages, contract or TDI termination relief is awarded.

#### Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-329.
**Decided:** June 29, 1995.

##### Religious viewpoint in the private publication program

**Holding:** The University may not exclude an otherwise eligible private student publication from its generally available printing benefit because its editorial viewpoint is religious. The rule applies to this privately edited, independently attributed publication program on its otherwise lawful eligibility, payment and administrative terms; it does not require funding every religious activity or every subject.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas; five Justices adopt the program-specific viewpoint rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Recognition, facilities, distribution and money remain distinct; not every public expenditure creates a speech forum. The publication remains independently edited and attributed under required disclaimers and approved vendor-payment terms.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators’ Association: preserves limited-forum definition while forbidding viewpoint discrimination.
- Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches; Widmar v. Vincent: apply private religious-viewpoint equality without assuming that facility access decides every subsidy.
- Regan v. Taxation With Representation: distinguishes definition of a government financing program from discriminatory exclusion within this opened private-publication arrangement.

##### Permissible printing support on the actual program terms

**Holding:** Providing this neutral printing benefit to the otherwise eligible private religious publication does not itself violate the Establishment Clause. Its secular student-expression purpose, broad neutral availability, independent editorial attribution and controlled vendor payments matter together; neutrality alone is not a universal authorization of devotional financing or governmental sponsorship of worship.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas join this concrete program conclusion. Lemon remains governing law; no new universal framework is adopted.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The actual purpose, effect and entanglement inquiry remains; neutral availability alone neither authorizes unrestricted devotional financing nor governmental worship. Compelled-fee challenges, unrestricted religious aid and particular awards are outside review.

The denial concerned religious activity, defined by the Guidelines as primarily promoting or manifesting belief in or about deity or ultimate reality, rather than an established finding of religious-organization ineligibility. The organization rule separately concerns devotion as organizational purpose. Political restrictions separately exclude principal-purpose lobbying and candidate electioneering while permitting incidental or insubstantial advocacy, nonpartisan study/research and ideological views; those permissions are not exceptions to the religious-activity rule. Permitted ability-to-perform membership limits likewise remain distinct. Abandoned free-exercise/state individual-rights claims and unchallenged equal-protection intent findings are not reopened.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Lemon v. Kurtzman: retains the existing purpose, effect and entanglement inquiry; no universal replacement is adopted.
- Witters v. Washington Department of Services for the Blind; Mueller v. Allen: apply neutral-benefit and private-direction distinctions within the actual program, not neutrality alone.
- Board of Education of Kiryas Joel v. Grumet: distinguishes denominational governmental authority and retains religious-neutrality limits.
- Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills: preserves the actual antecedent-law remand; supplies no religious-aid merits holding here.
- Lee v. Weisman: preserves limits on governmental religious participation and coercion; this private printing benefit compels no student worship.
- Church of Scientology v. Clearwater: distinguishes RFRA burdens and exemptions from constitutional financing authority.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse and remand for removal of the religious-viewpoint exclusion under otherwise lawful eligibility, payment and administrative terms. Examine any properly available independent Virginia funding defense consistently with the federal viewpoint holding, without declaring applicability, validity or revival of forfeited defenses. No full payment, damages, immunity, fee or compelled-fee refund is determined.

**Current-force modification:** Effective June 23, 1997, Lemon remains the governing Establishment Clause test as modified in Agostini v. Felton, Holding I. Purpose, effects and entanglement inform a single inquiry with three routes: controlling specific prohibition or distinct doctrine, established permissible relationship, and residual attribution and function. Clear cases apply that test; its former independent prongs do not remain a separate or fallback method. Rosenberger's actual controlled vendor-funded printing benefit remains permissible on its program terms. It concerns financing, not merely facility access; it neither decided Agostini's public-teaching case nor authorized every devotional expenditure.

**Later-authority backlinks:** Agostini v. Felton; Chancellor, Board of Education of the City of New York v. Felton, Nos. 96-552 and 96-553, June 23, 1997, Holdings I–II; First Amendment — Public instruction on religious-school premises.

#### Board of Regents of the University of Wisconsin System v. Southworth, 529 U.S. 217 (2000)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 98-1189; Seventh Circuit Nos. 97-3510 and 97-3548.

**Decided:** March 22, 2000.

##### Whether a broad independent student-expression program may use a general compulsory fee without ideological refunds

**Holding:** A public university may require a generally applicable student activity fee to support a broad program of independent extracurricular student expression serving its educational mission when eligibility, allocation and administration are objective and viewpoint neutral. Within that program, a student's disagreement with a recipient's political, ideological or religious viewpoint does not require an ideological opt-out or pro rata refund.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Ginsburg; six direct rationale votes. Stevens, Souter and Breyer concur in the judgment without joining the framework.

**Material application:** Independent student control, broad educational opportunity and stipulated viewpoint-neutral administration justify the ordinary program together. Absence of attribution, majority approval or enrollment alone is insufficient. Optional contributions and refunds remain permissible choices.

##### What viewpoint neutrality requires within the actual student-expression program

**Holding:** The protection for a compulsory independent-expression program requires viewpoint-neutral eligibility, allocation and administration; neither an educational label nor popular approval permits selection by viewpoint. Reasonable neutral limits on program scope, financial accountability, performance and fraud prevention remain available; the rule does not require equal grants or funding every activity.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Ginsburg; six direct rationale votes. Stevens, Souter and Breyer concur in the judgment without joining the framework.

**Limits and questions not reached:** This rule does not impose forum neutrality on every curricular choice, faculty instruction or governmental message, and it confers no immunity from other constitutional requirements. Neutral access to religious editorial expression does not itself authorize governmental worship or devotional instruction.

##### Whether the binding funding referendum is validated by the ordinary-allocation stipulation

**Holding:** A university cannot distribute access to its compulsory private-expression program according to majority approval of a group's viewpoint. The binding referendum is outside the ordinary-allocation neutrality stipulation, so the Seventh Circuit's general compulsory-subsidy judgment must be reversed and the case remanded only to determine the referendum's actual operation and appropriate relief.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Ginsburg; six direct rationale votes. Stevens, Souter and Breyer concur in the judgment without joining the framework.

**Material application:** The referendum may allocate, terminate or veto a group's support and is treated as binding. Its actual operation, not an assumed neutral final override, must be examined. The Court holds neither that every ballot is unconstitutional nor that popularity substitutes for neutrality.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The operative remand does not reopen a late partisan-policy challenge foreclosed by the reiterated ordinary-allocation stipulation, and no written restriction is assumed rescinded, amended or universally valid. The separate limits on GSSF lobbying or primarily political groups are not exceptions to a different religious-activity provision. The decision does not adjudicate every outside expenditure or every written exclusion. Off-campus location alone supplies no categorical constitutional disposition of an otherwise eligible student expressive activity; the actual program relationship and proper claim remain necessary.

No Establishment, free-exercise, RFRA or state claim is revived or decided. Religious editorial viewpoint is not identical to governmental worship or devotional instruction. No actual devotional purchase is established here, and the ruling does not authorize one. The two reported WISPIRG sums are not treated as the same accounting measure or used to calculate relief. Unchallenged program components remain outside the judgment.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the general compelled-subsidy liability judgment and remand only for the referendum and relief its actual operation warrants. Do not restore the previously removed all-students injunction or detailed administrative scheme. No fixed refund, damages, particular appropriation, universal opt-in mechanism or revived late partisan-policy challenge follows.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Abood v. Detroit Board of Education; Keller v. State Bar of California:** Their protection against compelled nongermane ideological support remains effective in exclusive-representation and integrated-bar settings. Their representative-duty inquiry does not itself decide this different educational expression program.
- **Lehnert v. Ferris Faculty Association:** Its union chargeability framework and divided applications remain intact; the statutory-duty separate account is not converted into a universal Court rule for universities.
- **Glickman v. Wileman Brothers & Elliott, Inc., June 25, 1997:** Its specified permission for germane nonideological promotion within collective agricultural regulation is distinguished, not expanded into a prior approval of ideological student subsidies. Its recognition that financing can burden expression remains controlling at that scope.
- **Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia, June 29, 1995:** Its private-publication viewpoint equality and controlled printing-benefit holdings remain intact. The compulsory-fee question it reserved is resolved here for the defined broad educational expression program, with separate safeguards for payers and recipients.
- **Gibson v. Florida Bar, December 4, 1991:** Its advance-protection, chargeability-burden and impartial-review requirements remain for established compulsory nongermane charges. It does not itself establish a violation in the ordinary program upheld here, so those procedures are not imposed on that program.
- **Air Line Pilots Association v. Miller, May 26, 1998:** Its distinction between offering impartial arbitration and requiring nonconsenting objectors to exhaust it remains unchanged. No new mandatory arbitration mechanism is created.
- **Agostini v. Felton, June 23, 1997; Jackson v. Benson, March 16, 1999; Strout v. Albanese, February 28, 2000:** Their distinct rules concerning the actual governmental religious function remain effective. This private-expression decision does not replace Agostini's unified Lemon inquiry, authorize a devotional purchase or treat religious identity alone as governmental religious work.

### Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp., No. 93-1525

Merits decision, February 21, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 7, 1994; decided February 21, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit, reviewing 12 F.3d 388 (1993), which reversed the district judgment, 811 F. Supp. 993, on the absence of governmental action and directed dismissal. The question is whether Amtrak is a governmental actor for First Amendment purposes despite its statutory disclaimer. Michael Lebron challenged Amtrak's refusal to display his political photomontage on the Spectacular billboard at Penn Station. The First Amendment claim remained before the lower courts although he expressly disavowed treating Amtrak itself as a governmental entity and relied instead on attributing the challenged conduct to government.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated at argument and decision. No Justice is noted as not participating.

#### Judgment

The Second Circuit judgment is reversed and the case remanded, 8–1, for adjudication of the First Amendment claim treating Amtrak as governmental. The Court does not decide whether rejection of the advertisement violated the First Amendment or order its display.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The Court may consider this included legal argument despite its earlier disavowal

**Controlling proposition:** The Court elects to consider Amtrak's governmental-entity status despite Lebron's earlier express disavowal of that argument because it supplies a legal basis for the same preserved First Amendment claim, falls within the governmental-actor question presented, and can be resolved on the established institutional record without a new factual theory or additional findings. This bounded exercise of review creates no obligation to consider every abandoned argument and does not dispense with limits on distinct claims, questions, requested relief, factual stipulations, or missing material facts.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's reachability portion, joined by Justices Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Seven Justices adopt the stated discretionary-review rationale; Chief Justice Stone-Zsela reaches the question but does not join this portion, and Justice O'Connor declines the argument as a prudential matter.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the earlier litigation choice deserves consideration but does not require refusal of this legal argument. Yee separates a new supporting argument from a distinct claim and independently enforces the question presented. Lebron's injury, constitutional claim, rejected advertisement, and requested relief remain the same. The question before the Court expressly concerns governmental status despite the statutory disclaimer, and the established corporate structure permits its resolution without new factual findings. Bray withheld a separate statutory claim outside the granted dispute and lacking a necessary finding; those defects are absent here. Deliberate disavowal is more substantial than mere omission, but the Court exercises its authority to decide this included legal basis on the present record. The appellate reliance on Amtrak's private status makes that premise relevant without establishing a rule that any passing discussion preserves every argument. No actual adversarial prejudice or broader concession is found, and the Court imposes no burden on the respondent to prove prejudice.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Yee v. City of Escondido, 503 U.S. 519 (1992): applies its distinction between a supporting argument and a new claim, together with its independent question-presented limit; the present decision separately addresses deliberate disavowal in this setting.
- Bray v. Alexandria Women's Health Clinic, 506 U.S. 263 (1993): distinguishes the unadjudicated separate hindrance theory, outside the granted questions and lacking a necessary finding; its scope limits remain operative.
- Bankers Life & Casualty Co. v. Crenshaw, 486 U.S. 71 (1988): applies, through Yee, the allowance of supporting arguments for a preserved federal claim, without erasing this Court's discretion over deliberately excluded grounds.
- Kamen v. Kemper Financial Services, Inc., 500 U.S. 90 (1991): preserves the distinction between legal arguments and the claim to which they relate; no unrelated claim is introduced.
- United States v. Mezzanatto, decided January 18, 1995: distinguishes the actual, limited evidentiary agreement there from this decision whether to consider an included argument; its waiver rule does not make every litigation choice an irrevocable surrender of an entire constitutional claim.

##### Special federal creation and permanent majority appointment make Amtrak governmental

**Controlling proposition:** When the Federal Government creates a corporation by special law to advance governmental objectives and permanently retains authority to appoint a majority of its governing directors, the corporation is governmental for First Amendment purposes notwithstanding a statutory declaration that it is not a governmental agency. Amtrak satisfies those combined conditions; ordinary operating discretion in its corporate officers does not require a separate external federal command for each challenged act.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's constitutional-status portion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Eight Justices directly support the same permanent-majority-appointment rule and its application; Justice O'Connor's conditional view of a question she declines to reach is not a join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress cannot settle constitutional responsibility simply by choosing a corporate label. Amtrak was specially created to pursue national transportation objectives, and the statutory arrangement permanently retains governmental authority over selection of its governing majority. The Secretary's position, the five other direct Presidential selections, and the preferred shareholder's governmental selections must not be mistaken for nine direct Presidential appointments. The different routes nevertheless establish the required enduring majority authority; the corporate president's selection within the institution does not defeat it. Congress's nonagency classification keeps its statutory operation, but cannot place an institution created and governed in this manner beyond the Constitution. San Francisco Arts & Athletics distinguishes a federally chartered and assisted private corporation; charter, subsidy, or regulation alone is not the ground here. Nor does delegation of everyday decisions to corporate officers make this institution private. An external federal order to reject Lebron's work is therefore unnecessary. The Court decides governmental status, leaving the advertising policy's constitutionality and the suitable remedy for later adjudication.

**Precedent treatment:**

- San Francisco Arts & Athletics, Inc. v. United States Olympic Committee, 483 U.S. 522 (1987): distinguishes federal charter and assistance to a private institution from special creation coupled with permanent governmental majority-appointment authority.
- Edmonson v. Leesville Concrete Co., 500 U.S. 614 (1991), and Georgia v. McCollum, 505 U.S. 42 (1992): preserve attention to actual governmental legal machinery in their jury-selection settings; neither makes billboard advertising an exclusively governmental function or supplies a general rule for all publicly benefited corporations.
- Jackson v. Metropolitan Edison Co., 419 U.S. 345 (1974), and Blum v. Yaretsky, 457 U.S. 991 (1982): distinguish attribution of a private entity's particular act from identifying an institution that is itself governmental under the combined rule.
- Hess v. Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp., decided November 14, 1994: keeps the distinct inquiry into an entity's state sovereign immunity separate; absence of immunity does not imply constitutional privacy.
- American National Red Cross v. S. G., 505 U.S. 247 (1992): preserves its express charter-jurisdiction rule, which supplies no constitutional actor classification.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Federal charter, subsidy, regulation, governmental property, public benefit, a single appointment, temporary investment, or merely the current ownership of shares is not independently sufficient under this rule. Permanent authority to appoint the governing majority, governmental objectives, and special governmental creation must be present together. The Court decides no universal agency status, sovereign immunity, Treasury obligation, federal tort-liability rule, or charter jurisdiction. The 1994 recodification from former 45 U.S.C. §541 to 49 U.S.C. §24301(a) does not itself adjudicate constitutional status. The statutory board includes the Secretary of Transportation; Amtrak's president; three Presidentially appointed, Senate-confirmed directors representing the statutory labor, state-executive, and business categories; two Presidential selections from commuter-authority nominees under the statute; and two preferred-shareholder selections. The governmental preferred shareholder supplies those last two selections on this record. Neither the entire board's consideration of this particular refusal nor a new direct federal order to reject it is found.

##### Governmental status requires a remand, not an immediate display order

**Controlling proposition:** Reversal of the Second Circuit's governmental-action ground restores the First Amendment claim for adjudication with Amtrak treated as governmental, but does not establish that this rejection was unconstitutional or that Lebron is entitled to mandatory display. The lower courts must address the unreviewed speech and forum questions, lawful relief, and the related disposition of the contract claim and TDI counterclaim under their own governing rules; the district display injunction is not automatically reinstated.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's remand portion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Eight Justices adopt the same threshold reversal and limited remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that governmental responsibility identifies the institution's constitutional duties without deciding whether the challenged restriction violates them. The Court of Appeals stopped at attribution and did not adjudicate the First Amendment merits. The district court's contrary decision and mandatory-display order therefore cannot be restored merely by correcting that threshold. The character of the advertising forum, the grounds and operation of the speech restriction, and the conditions for equitable relief remain separate questions. Young preserves the distinction between the existence of substantial federal jurisdiction and the legal defeat of a federal claim; restoration of the claim accordingly requires reconsideration of associated state-law dispositions without prescribing their answers. Tome illustrates only the limited principle that correcting one ground does not automatically establish ultimate relief; its hearsay and criminal-error rules do not govern this civil remand. No damages amount, contract entitlement, forum classification, or validity of a substitute advertising policy is determined here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Young v. Northern Illinois Conference of United Methodist Church, decided January 17, 1995: applies the distinction between statutory jurisdiction and legal defeat on the federal merits without extending its ministerial-selection defense.
- Tome v. United States, decided January 10, 1995: uses only its separation of threshold legal error from automatic ultimate relief by analogy; no evidentiary or criminal harmless-error standard is transferred.

**Remedial limits:** The Court awards no display, damages, final contractual relief, or adjudication of TDI's requested declaration permitting termination. The Second Circuit's dismissal directions must be reconsidered consistently with the restored federal claim and ordinary law governing the separate state-law issues. The lower courts may not treat this actor-status ruling as a merits judgment approving the advertisement or invalidating every political-advertising policy.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Yee and Bray preserve the distinction between a legal supporting argument, a separate claim, and the actual question presented; the Court exercises bounded discretion over this deliberately disavowed argument. San Francisco Arts & Athletics, Jackson, and Blum retain their private-actor scope. Edmonson and McCollum concern governmental jury machinery. Hess addresses immunity and Red Cross charter jurisdiction, neither of which settles constitutional actor status. Young and Tome support only the stated separation of thresholds, merits, and relief. No earlier holding is overruled.

Effective February 21, 1995, special federal creation for governmental objectives combined with permanent federal authority to appoint a governing majority establishes First Amendment governmental status despite a statutory nonagency label. That rule governs Amtrak on this record without requiring an external governmental command for the particular refusal. The decision also permits consideration of this included legal argument despite its earlier disavowal on the stated claim, question, and record conditions, without a universal rule requiring review of abandoned arguments. The advertising merits and final remedies remain unresolved.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice O'Connor would affirm. Lebron preserved his First Amendment claim, but deliberately excluded the theory that Amtrak itself was a governmental entity in his presentations below. The express governmental-actor question here permits consideration of that theory; it does not require the Court to disregard the litigant's earlier choice. Her objection is prudential. She does not claim that the Court lacks jurisdiction, that the entire constitutional claim was waived, or that entity status falls outside the question presented. Nor does she find an additional concession or actual reliance prejudice on a record that establishes neither.

She would decide the conduct-attribution theory on the private-entity premise on which it was litigated. Jackson and Blum require a supported governmental connection to a private actor's particular choice, while her Edmonson dissent distinguishes such choice from governmental assistance. The reported funding, property, stockholding, and board relationships do not establish an external governmental direction causing this refusal or an exclusively governmental advertising function. The political subject of the artwork does not itself supply the missing connection. On that limited path she would leave the appellate rejection of the federal claim and disposition of the unresolved state-law matters in place. She does not convert the failure of the federal theory into a universal jurisdictional dismissal rule.

O'Connor recognizes that the permanent public governance would support the Court's structural conclusion if that alternative were reached. That conditional agreement supplies no vote for a holding she declines to adjudicate and no judgment vote for reversal. Even an affirmative actor ruling, she explains, would not itself establish the right to the district court's mandatory-display injunction; forum, speech, and equitable questions would still require decision.

### Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia, No. 94-329

Merits decision, June 29, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the Fourth Circuit and requires removal of the religious-viewpoint exclusion from the generally available printing benefit.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the University’s decision to support independent student expression does not make each publication its own speech. Perry permits definition of a limited forum and its subjects, but not discrimination against a religious perspective on otherwise included subjects. Lamb’s Chapel and Widmar supply religious-viewpoint equality in genuinely opened private-expression channels. Here the broad publication program leaves editing with the students, requires independence disclaimers and pays approved outside printing expenses. The denial treats primarily religious belief or manifestation as a forbidden activity while allowing comparable student perspectives. That operation excludes viewpoint rather than neutrally defining a government message. The Court does not erase the distinction between recognition, facilities, distribution and money, or equate every expenditure with a speech forum. Nor does it find the organization ineligible under a separate organizational rule. The religious-viewpoint exclusion must be removed; other lawful program terms remain. Actual payment amounts and properly available independent defenses remain subject to the federal viewpoint holding.

- Regan v. Taxation With Representation: distinguishes definition of a government financing program from discriminatory exclusion within this opened private-publication arrangement.

##### Permissible printing support on the actual program terms

**Controlling proposition:** Providing this neutral printing benefit to the otherwise eligible private religious publication does not itself violate the Establishment Clause. Its secular student-expression purpose, broad neutral availability, independent editorial attribution and controlled vendor payments matter together; neutrality alone is not a universal authorization of devotional financing or governmental sponsorship of worship.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas join this concrete program conclusion. Lemon remains governing law; no new universal framework is adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the publication’s religious message remains private under the actual safeguards of this program. The fund serves broad student expression rather than government worship, and officials approve printing expenses without adopting the students’ editorial choices. Required disclaimers and independent organizations separate the private message from the University. Vendor payments provide the defined printing service rather than unrestricted grants to churches. Witters and Mueller supply the relevance of neutral benefits and private direction, without making beneficiary neutrality conclusive in every setting. Under the existing purpose, effect and entanglement inquiry, this arrangement does not become governmental religious speech merely because one publication includes devotion and proselytizing. Kiryas Joel’s denominational allocation of governmental power is different; its neutrality limits and retention of Lemon remain. The contrary direct-financing objection gives too little weight to the concrete private-expression structure. Zobrest’s actual procedural remand supplies no aid-merits approval. Compelled-fee refund claims, unrestricted religious support and any particular monetary award remain outside this decision.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Lemon v. Kurtzman: retains the existing purpose, effect and entanglement inquiry; no universal replacement is adopted.
- Witters v. Washington Department of Services for the Blind; Mueller v. Allen: apply neutral-benefit and private-direction distinctions within the actual program, not neutrality alone.
- Board of Education of Kiryas Joel v. Grumet: distinguishes denominational governmental authority and retains religious-neutrality limits.
- Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills: preserves the actual antecedent-law remand; supplies no religious-aid merits holding here.
- Lee v. Weisman: preserves limits on governmental religious participation and coercion; this private printing benefit compels no student worship.
- Church of Scientology v. Clearwater: distinguishes RFRA burdens and exemptions from constitutional financing authority.

The denial concerned religious activity, defined by the Guidelines as primarily promoting or manifesting belief in or about deity or ultimate reality, rather than an established finding of religious-organization ineligibility. The organization rule separately concerns devotion as organizational purpose. Political restrictions separately exclude principal-purpose lobbying and candidate electioneering while permitting incidental or insubstantial advocacy, nonpartisan study/research and ideological views; those permissions are not exceptions to the religious-activity rule. Permitted ability-to-perform membership limits likewise remain distinct. Abandoned free-exercise/state individual-rights claims and unchallenged equal-protection intent findings are not reopened.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Lemon remains governing; Zobrest is procedural rather than an aid-merits holding. No denominational political power, compulsory worship or unrestricted religious subsidy is authorized.

Otherwise eligible private student publications receive viewpoint-neutral access to this defined printing benefit. Independent expression and the program’s safeguards do constitutional work; this is no rule that every public expenditure creates a forum or every formally neutral religious subsidy is lawful.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O’Connor concurs and joins the Court in full at its program-specific level. Organizational independence and disclaimers, approved vendor payments, a vigorous competing forum and the availability of a properly presented objection by students who oppose compelled fees provide safeguards; the last is a possible challenge, not an existing opt-out or refund award. Neutrality alone is insufficient. Thomas also concurs, joining the Court while explaining his own view that equal participation in broadly available civic benefits should not turn solely on a financial-versus-in-kind classification; that broader historical theory is not adopted by the Court. Souter, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer, dissents. Mandatory fees pay for devotional production, and routing money to the printer does not turn that support into mere facility access. Ball and Nyquist distinguish direct support of religious activity; the beneficiary’s own editorial choice is not an independent third party’s selection under Witters and Mueller. The dissent does not exclude all religious viewpoints or invalidate every neutral private religious benefit.

#### Source Notes

Fourth Circuit, 18 F.3d 269; Student Activities Fund Guidelines, petition pp. 61a–67a; respondent’s opposition, p. 20 n. 11, and reproduced appendix passages, Records and Briefs collection, No. 94-329. The asserted Virginia funding defense is distinct from abandoned affirmative state-rights claims. Its applicability and available remedies remain unresolved; no payment amount is guaranteed.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Fourth Circuit is reversed and the case remanded. The University shall remove the religious-viewpoint exclusion and apply otherwise lawful eligibility, payment and administrative terms. Any properly available independent Virginia funding defense must be examined consistently with the federal viewpoint holding; no applicability, validity or revival of forfeited defenses is declared. No full requested amount, damages, immunity, fee or compelled-fee refund determination is made.

**End of entry: Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia, merits decision, June 29, 1995.**

### Board of Regents of the University of Wisconsin System v. Southworth, No. 98-1189

Merits decision, March 22, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 9, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, 151 F.3d 717, decided August 10, 1998, Nos. 97-3510 and 97-3548.

The question is whether a public university violates the First Amendment by requiring students to pay an activity fee supporting organizations engaged in political, ideological and religious expression. The live dispute concerns speech and association and the objectors' allocable fees. The general fee power, nonallocable services, ordinary student government, nonideological organizations, nonobjectors' money, the student newspaper and Distinguished Lecture Series are outside the challenge.

In 1995–96 a full-time Madison student paid a mandatory $165.75 segregated fee each semester; nonpayment prevented receipt of grades and graduation. About 80 percent supported unchallenged nonallocable services. The challenged allocation program supported registered student organizations and qualifying services through student-government processes subject to University approval. The ordinary grant and General Student Services Fund routes were stipulated to operate without viewpoint discrimination or University advocacy of a particular view. Student organizations controlled their own messages, which included lobbying and other political advocacy; the University disclaimed their expression. Most funding reimbursed expenses. WISPIRG received lump-sum funding, and all uses of that money are not established.

The stipulation expressly excludes the referendum. Student government treated referendum outcomes as binding, permitting allocation, termination or veto of a group's funding. The record contains a WISPIRG funding vote and an October 1996 termination vote, without establishing a constitutional finding about each ballot's operation. The lower court had already confined relief to objectors' allocable fees and removed the district court's all-students injunction and prescribed detailed administrative mechanism. It nevertheless sustained the general compelled-subsidy liability rule challenged here.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|
| General compelled-subsidy liability for the stipulated ordinary program | Reversed, 9–0 in judgment | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | The general prohibition against using objectors' allocable fees for that program cannot stand. |
| Binding referendum | Remanded, 9–0 in judgment | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Determine its actual operation and any relief warranted by a defect. |

No damages, fixed refund, specific WISPIRG appropriation, universal opt-in procedure or order to fund every recipient is entered. The district court's already vacated all-students reach and detailed administrative design are not restored.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Ginsburg | Reversal of general compelled-subsidy liability; referendum-only remand | Entire opinion, including the educational private-expression framework, neutrality requirements, referendum treatment and all limits |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stevens | Souter, Breyer | Agrees with reversal and referendum remand | Actual program's sufficient justification; does not join the complete necessity framework |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The genuine educational private-expression program may be supported by a general fee

**Holding and operative rule:** A public university may require a generally applicable student activity fee to support a broad program of independent extracurricular student expression serving its educational mission when eligibility, allocation and administration are objective and viewpoint neutral. Within that program, a student's disagreement with a recipient's political, ideological or religious viewpoint does not require an ideological opt-out or pro rata refund.

**Authority:** Justice Thomas's opinion of the Court, with the six-Justice coalition identified above, directly controls this holding and its limits. Stevens, Souter and Breyer support the judgment on their separate ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Compelled financing implicates expression even without personal attribution. Abood and Keller protect against compulsory ideological support outside the representative duties that justify union and integrated-bar charges; Lehnert applies those distinct union principles. Here the common educational function is maintaining opportunities for independent and competing student expression. It does not appoint one organization to speak for every payer. Requiring courts to sort student ideas by agreement with an official educational orthodoxy would defeat that function.

Glickman recognizes expressive burdens while permitting a specified common expense within collective agricultural regulation. Its nonideological product-promotion holding does not itself authorize student ideological subsidies; this different program supplies its own justification. Rosenberger protects viewpoint equality in its controlled private-publication benefit and reserved the compulsory-fee question now presented. Independent student control, broad educational opportunity and viewpoint-neutral administration together answer that question here. Lack of attribution, common benefit, majority approval or enrollment alone would not suffice.

The ordinary SGAF/GSSF allocation and approval process is stipulated neutral, and the University does not advocate a particular viewpoint through it. Students need not adopt recipients' views, join their groups or surrender their own expression. The fee therefore supports a forum, not governmental endorsement of each message. The University may choose optional contributions or refunds, but the Constitution does not require ideological exemptions on these facts. No finding that opt-outs are impossible or every subsidy indispensable is made.

##### Neutrality governs the actual program, not every university expenditure

**Holding and operative rule:** The protection for a compulsory independent-expression program requires viewpoint-neutral eligibility, allocation and administration; neither an educational label nor popular approval permits selection by viewpoint. Reasonable neutral limits on program scope, financial accountability, performance and fraud prevention remain available; the rule does not require equal grants or funding every activity.

**Authority:** The same six Justices join Justice Thomas's opinion on this holding and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Rosenberger distinguishes independently attributed student expression from official advocacy and forbids viewpoint exclusion within the benefit actually opened. A neutral program may define the work it supports and require an account of its performance; those conditions cannot conceal approval or rejection of ideology. The decision addresses the actual broad student-expression program and establishes no constitutional requirement that curricular choices, faculty instruction or governmental speech follow the same forum rule. It does not immunize those activities from other constitutional review.

This case establishes no particular devotional purchase. Agostini's unified Lemon inquiry, Jackson's funded-function rule and Strout's integrated-tuition application remain distinct from protected religious editorial viewpoint in Rosenberger. Neutrality alone does not authorize governmental worship or devotional instruction as such. The Court neither decides nor revives an Establishment, free-exercise or RFRA claim, and does not conclusively approve a particular external payment without its facts and proper claim.

##### The referendum requires separate examination

**Holding and operative rule:** A university cannot distribute access to its compulsory private-expression program according to majority approval of a group's viewpoint. The binding referendum is outside the ordinary-allocation neutrality stipulation, so the Seventh Circuit's general compulsory-subsidy judgment must be reversed and the case remanded only to determine the referendum's actual operation and appropriate relief.

**Authority:** The same six Justices join Justice Thomas's complete referendum rule. Stevens, Souter and Breyer independently support the referendum remand without adopting the complete necessity framework.

**Controlling explanation:** The referendum can allocate, terminate or veto funding, and the student government treats its result as binding. Ordinary committee neutrality does not establish referendum neutrality. Minority expression cannot depend on majority ideological consent. Yet the record does not decide every ballot's operation or establish that every possible voting arrangement is unconstitutional. The Court assumes no neutral final override and orders examination of the existing procedure.

The lower court already removed the district court's all-students reach and prescribed administrative scheme. Neither is restored. The late partisan-policy challenge is not reopened by a general reference to unresolved questions; the operative remand concerns the referendum alone and permits only relief its actual defect warrants. No damages, fixed refund, specified WISPIRG payment, universal opt-in procedure or order to fund every recipient is entered.

##### Limits and questions not reached

The operative remand does not reopen a late partisan-policy challenge foreclosed by the reiterated ordinary-allocation stipulation, and no written restriction is assumed rescinded, amended or universally valid. The separate limits on GSSF lobbying or primarily political groups are not exceptions to a different religious-activity provision. The decision does not adjudicate every outside expenditure or every written exclusion. Off-campus location alone supplies no categorical constitutional disposition of an otherwise eligible student expressive activity; the actual program relationship and proper claim remain necessary.

No Establishment, free-exercise, RFRA or state claim is revived or decided. Religious editorial viewpoint is not identical to governmental worship or devotional instruction. No actual devotional purchase is established here, and the ruling does not authorize one. The two reported WISPIRG sums are not treated as the same accounting measure or used to calculate relief. Unchallenged program components remain outside the judgment.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Abood v. Detroit Board of Education; Keller v. State Bar of California:** Their protection against compelled nongermane ideological support remains effective in exclusive-representation and integrated-bar settings. Their representative-duty inquiry does not itself decide this different educational expression program.
- **Lehnert v. Ferris Faculty Association:** Its union chargeability framework and divided applications remain intact; the statutory-duty separate account is not converted into a universal Court rule for universities.
- **Glickman v. Wileman Brothers & Elliott, Inc., June 25, 1997:** Its specified permission for germane nonideological promotion within collective agricultural regulation is distinguished, not expanded into a prior approval of ideological student subsidies. Its recognition that financing can burden expression remains controlling at that scope.
- **Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia, June 29, 1995:** Its private-publication viewpoint equality and controlled printing-benefit holdings remain intact. The compulsory-fee question it reserved is resolved here for the defined broad educational expression program, with separate safeguards for payers and recipients.
- **Gibson v. Florida Bar, December 4, 1991:** Its advance-protection, chargeability-burden and impartial-review requirements remain for established compulsory nongermane charges. It does not itself establish a violation in the ordinary program upheld here, so those procedures are not imposed on that program.
- **Air Line Pilots Association v. Miller, May 26, 1998:** Its distinction between offering impartial arbitration and requiring nonconsenting objectors to exhaust it remains unchanged. No new mandatory arbitration mechanism is created.
- **Agostini v. Felton, June 23, 1997; Jackson v. Benson, March 16, 1999; Strout v. Albanese, February 28, 2000:** Their distinct rules concerning the actual governmental religious function remain effective. This private-expression decision does not replace Agostini's unified Lemon inquiry, authorize a devotional purchase or treat religious identity alone as governmental religious work.

#### Law After Decision

Effective March 22, 2000, a genuine broad educational program for independent extracurricular student expression may be financed through generally applicable student fees without ideological opt-outs when its eligibility, allocations and administration are objective and viewpoint neutral. This is a justification for the defined program, not a conclusion that compulsory financing is nonexpressive or that every useful subsidy is permissible. The protection extends to political, ideological and religious viewpoints within the program's lawful scope. Neutral accountability and program limits remain available; popularity cannot replace viewpoint neutrality. The ordinary-allocation stipulation does not validate the separate referendum. The earlier compelled-representation, private-publication and religious-function rules retain their actual fields.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Stevens, concurring in the judgment, joined by Justices Souter and Breyer.** They support reversal and referendum remand. The fee reaches an independent distribution program advancing no single ideology and supporting many competing speakers. That actual program, its educational purpose, the attenuated payer-message connection and stipulated neutral administration defeat the claimed ideological exemption. The recipients' political advocacy remains real; the conclusion does not depend on classifying the money as nonspeech, enrollment as waiver, taxation as immunity, or lack of attribution alone.

They would decide that the actual stipulated neutrality and program structure suffice, without declaring a newly necessary viewpoint-neutrality framework for every compulsory university-expression arrangement. They preserve Rosenberger's existing viewpoint-access rule and create no academic-autonomy immunity. They agree that the referendum falls outside the stipulation and requires further proceedings. The same unpresented religious-function and remedial limits apply. They do not join the Court's opinion; agreement with its judgment does not adopt its complete constitutional framework.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review of the general compelled-subsidy claim is complete. The case returns to the Seventh Circuit for proceedings concerning only the referendum's actual operation and appropriate relief consistent with the opinion. The general liability rule against the stipulated ordinary program is reversed. No revived policy challenge or religious claim, mandated administrative design, new evidentiary hearing, filing deadline or monetary award follows.
