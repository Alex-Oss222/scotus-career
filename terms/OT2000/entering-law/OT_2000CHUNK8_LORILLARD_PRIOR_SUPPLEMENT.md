#### Medtronic, Inc. v. Lohr; Lohr v. Medtronic, Inc., 518 U.S. 470 (1996)

**Docket or dockets:** Nos. 95-754 and 95-886.
**Decided:** June 26, 1996.

##### What federal predicate is required and whether substantial-equivalence clearance supplies design approval

**Holding:** Section 360k(a) requires an applicable federal requirement and a state requirement respecting a human-use device that differs from or adds to it and concerns safety, effectiveness or another matter included in the federal requirement, subject to subsection (b). This lead's substantial-equivalence clearance does not establish substantive federal approval of its design and therefore does not preempt the restored negligent-design or design-based strict-liability claims on that asserted ground.

**Proposition-level authority:** Breyer’s Part I, unanimous.

**Material application:** The 1982 marketing letter and §807.97 separate clearance from substantive safety approval. Class III status, grandfathering and manufacturing conformance to the original design do not establish PMA or a substantive FDA design choice.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No design defect, breach, causation, actual PMA case or hypothetical device-specific standard is decided. Regulatory overlap alone supplies no independent actual conflict.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Design restoration affirmed in No. 95-754, 9–0; state-law elements remain necessary.

##### Whether a genuinely parallel state conduct duty becomes additional because it supplies damages

**Holding:** Section 360k(a) does not expressly preempt an independently available state duty that genuinely requires the same conduct as the applicable federal duty merely because state law adds damages. Requiring negligence or an unreasonable hazard in addition to violation of that same conduct obligation can narrow recovery without making the substantive duty different; a claim requiring different warnings or production conduct is not parallel merely because its pleading invokes federal law.

**Proposition-level authority:** Breyer’s Part II, unanimous.

**Material application:** Actual operation controls: equal conduct plus narrower recovery elements differs from liability despite federal compliance or a requirement of additional warning/production conduct. The appellate court reached the reply-stage contention and assumed, without deciding, Florida recognition and adequate pleading.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No private FDCA enforcement action is created. Florida recognition, pleading, violation, causation and relief remain undecided; independently established actual conflict remains a separate question.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The categorical parallel-duty barrier in No. 95-886 is reversed and remanded, 9–0; no exemption must first be sought for an otherwise nonpreempted equal duty.

##### Whether the asserted generic manufacturing and warning predicates preempt the presented duties

**Holding:** The general manufacturing and prescription-information requirements invoked here do not establish a federal counterpart that makes the presented ordinary manufacturing and warning duties different or additional within section 360k(a). The challenged preemption barriers are therefore reversed for negligent manufacture, failure to warn and corresponding strict-liability theories, without exempting all tort law or all claims involving generally applicable regulations.

**Proposition-level authority:** Breyer’s Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; six Justices.

**Material application:** Part 820 and §801.109 impose genuine obligations but their legal force and shared subject do not establish the specific substantive federal choice these duties would alter. Master records and original-design conformance do not substantively approve safety; label submission is not approval of precise contents.

**Limits and questions not reached:** General framing does not mean no federal command, and no universal manufacturer- or model-specificity prerequisite applies. Ambiguous duties may be identified in ordinary proceedings; no mandatory preliminary hearing in all products cases.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Manufacturing/warning and corresponding strict-liability barriers reversed, 6–3. O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas dissent as to genuinely divergent duties, while joining the equal-duty remand.

**Independent alternative holding:** Within the statutory comparison, the FDA reasonably interprets section 360k through section 808.1(d) to require specific counterpart regulations or other specific applicable requirements before divergent state requirements are displaced. Giving that formal construction substantial weight independently supports survival of these manufacturing and warning claims, while preserving its general-applicability, equal-duty and divergent substantive-application qualifications.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** Breyer’s Part V, joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; five direct votes. Stone-Zsela does not join. The FDA’s formal §808.1 interpretation receives weight within actual delegation and enacted ambiguity, not authority to contradict Congress, universal deference to litigating positions or free-standing preemptive power. Section 808.1(d)(6)(ii) still reaches a generally worded rule whose actual application imposes a divergent specific-device duty.

##### Whether common-law damages obligations can be requirements under §360k(a)

**Holding:** A state obligation enforced through common-law damages can constitute a requirement under section 360k(a); common law has no categorical exemption from the express clause. Inclusion alone does not establish preemption: the applicable federal obligation, actual difference or addition, subject connection and statutory exceptions must still be satisfied.

**Proposition-level authority:** Breyer’s Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas; five direct votes. Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg reserve this general question.

**Material application:** Judicial damages duties can change conduct as effectively as enacted commands. Inclusion does not itself preempt any surviving Lohr claim or supply a categorical preemption outcome.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The following operative qualifications govern the statutory comparison and remand; they do not enlarge the separately stated proposition coalitions.

- **Separate state-requirement exemption:** Section 360k(b) permits the Secretary, on a State or political subdivision's application, after notice and opportunity for an oral hearing, to exempt a requirement by regulation on prescribed conditions if it is more stringent than the otherwise applicable federal requirement, or if compelling local conditions require it and compliance would not violate an applicable federal requirement. The no-federal-violation condition belongs to the second alternative. An exemption does not remove federal obligations. No pertinent exemption is established; an equal, nonpreempted duty does not first require one.
- **Section 808.1's complete scope:** Subsection (b) includes force-of-law court decisions. Subsection (d)(1) preserves general requirements whose purpose reaches other products as well as devices, including general electrical codes and the Uniform Commercial Code's fitness warranty, and unfair-trade rules not limited to devices. Subsection (d)(2) preserves equal or substantially identical requirements. Subsection (d)(6)(ii) preserves general adulteration/misbranding prohibitions but displaces an actual divergent substantive device requirement; interpretation and enforcement, rather than literal wording alone, control that comparison.
- **Other section 808.1 qualifications:** Professional-practice permits, licensing, registration, certification and related approval remain subject to conflicting federal restrictions under sections 520(e) or (g), which prevail. State procurement specifications and Medicaid or similar payment criteria are preserved. General enforcement, inspections, registration and licensing remain subject to conflicting federal records, reporting and good-manufacturing-practice rules under sections 519 and 520(f), which prevail. Administrative delegations and related matters, measures solely raising revenue or charging fees, and the identified atomic-energy and radiation measures remain outside preemption on their stated terms; the last category lasts until applicable specific FDA requirements intervene. Subsection (e) assigns the FDA a determination role through prescribed procedures subject to judicial review, without establishing an exhaustion bar here. Subsections (c) and (f) preserve applicable federal duties whether or not a state requirement is exempt or preempted. No unestablished factual exception is applied.
- **Original manufacturing regime:** Part 820 applies to finished human-use devices and defines a finished device to include an accessory suitable for use whether packaged or labeled. It is not generally directed at manufacturers of components or parts; blood and blood components have Part 606's separate treatment. The everyday description of this lead as a component does not settle that classification. Additional critical-device duties retain their own applicability: the definition addresses an implant or life-supporting device whose failure when properly used can reasonably be expected to cause significant injury, with identification by the Commissioner after advisory consultation. Class III status alone is not a substitute finding. Where simultaneous compliance with applicable regulations is impossible, the specific device regulation supersedes the general regulation. No exemption, variance or violation is found here.
- **Real manufacturing obligations:** The original provisions require appropriate quality assurance, trained personnel, suitable premises and equipment, controlled component acceptance and handling, written manufacturing specifications and processing controls, inspection and failure investigation, labeling integrity, protective packaging, distribution controls and records. Section 820.100 requires manufacturing conformance to the original design or approved changes, with controlled and documented specification and process changes; it does not itself approve the design's safety. Critical-device additions, including component sampling, operation verification, labeling, distribution and history records, depend on their actual applicability. The Court decides no breach of any particular provision.
- **Distinct manufacturing exemption or variance:** Section 360j(f)(2) permits a subject person to petition in the prescribed form with required information. An exemption requires a determination that compliance is unnecessary to assure safety, effectiveness and statutory compliance. A variance requires a determination that the proposed replacement methods, facilities and controls for manufacture, packing, storage and installation are sufficient to assure safety, effectiveness and compliance with the Act; an approval order must impose conditions necessary to assure those ends. Optional advisory referral requires recommendations within sixty days; the Secretary must approve or deny within sixty days after submission or, if referred, after expiration of the committee's sixty-day period, whichever is later. An informal hearing is available after the order. These provisions differ from a State's section 360k(b) exemption, and no granted exception is inferred.
- **Prescription information:** Section 801.109 conditionally exempts a device unsafe without licensed-practitioner supervision from consumer adequate-directions requirements. The device must be in the possession of a person, or that person's agents or employees, regularly and lawfully engaged in its manufacture, transportation, storage or wholesale or retail distribution, or in the possession of a practitioner licensed by law to use or order it; it must be sold only to or on the prescription or other order of such a practitioner for professional use. Subsection (b)'s prescription caution and method-of-use label requirements exclude surgical instruments. Subsection (c) requires dispensing-package information about indications, effects, routes, methods, frequency and duration, relevant hazards, contraindications, side effects and precautions for intended and advertised uses. That information may be omitted only when commonly known to licensed practitioners; on written request stating reasonable grounds, the Commissioner will offer an opinion on proposed omission. No omission qualification is found here.
- **Separate labeling exceptions:** Section 801.109(d) independently requires adequate use information on manufacturer, packer or distributor labeling that furnishes or purports to furnish use information, whether or not within the dispensing package. Its exception covers a reminder piece calling attention only to the device name without indications or other use information. Subsection (e)'s issuance-or-revision-date duty excludes labels and cartons. These exceptions do not merge with subsection (c)'s permissive omission or subsection (b)'s surgical-instrument exception. Section 807.87(e)'s submission of proposed labels, labeling and advertisements is not substantive approval of their contents.
- **Clearance, classification and approval:** Section 360(k)'s report ordinarily precedes intended interstate commercial introduction by at least ninety days. Classification, substantial equivalence and premarket approval remain distinct. Section 360e(a) requires approval for a class III device subject to a subsection (b) regulation or classified under section 360c(f), unless exempt under section 360j(g). Section 360e(b) supplies the rulemaking route for pre-1976 devices and their substantially equivalent types; no such approval is found for this lead. PMA's distinct evaluation and application requirements do not become clearance requirements by inference. The Court decides neither a different approval pathway nor a particular investigational exemption.
- **Temporal limits:** The 1994 Code and April 1, 1996 CFR are text sources, not findings that every amendment applied to the 1982 clearance, 1987 implantation or 1990 injury. The 1990 codification requires the same intended use and a Secretary's order finding either the same technological characteristics or, for different characteristics, submitted information—including clinical data if the Secretary deems it necessary—demonstrating that the device is as safe and effective as a legally marketed device and raises no different questions of safety and efficacy than the predicate. Different technological characteristics means a significant change in materials, design, energy source or other device features. A predicate removed from the market at the Secretary's initiative or judicially determined adulterated or misbranded cannot establish substantial equivalence. That later text is not backdated; the lower court assumed that it described earlier practice. Later submission amendments and design-validation language create no earlier approval or breach. The original Part 820 regime governs the comparison, and no later quality-system revision enters the decision. An actual conduct-date duty remains to be identified for liability.
- **Savings and federal enforcement:** Section 360h(d) preserves liability despite compliance with an order under that section; it is not an unrestricted saving clause for every device action. When damages for economic loss enforce such liability, the value of a remedy supplied under the order must be taken into account. No such order or offset is adjudicated. Section 337 reserves direct enforcement to the United States except for its State food-enforcement provision. A State may bring proceedings in its own name and jurisdiction to civilly enforce, or restrain violations of, sections 341 and 343(b), (c), (d), (e), (f), (g), (h), (i), (k), (q) and (r), only for food located in the State. Proceedings may not begin until thirty days after notice to the Secretary, or until ninety days after notice if the Secretary begins an informal or formal enforcement action concerning the food within the first thirty days. Proceedings are barred while the Secretary diligently prosecutes a court proceeding concerning the food or after settlement of that proceeding or the related informal or formal enforcement action; the State may intervene as of right in the described court proceeding. This exception creates no private device action. No unpresented standalone enforcement defense or later implied-preemption rule is decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Ordinary proceedings retain the separate design, manufacture and warning strict-liability predicates and derivative consortium claim. The unappealed warranty dismissal remains. No compliance finding, liability, damages, mandatory pleading amendment, trial or agency referral.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc., June 24, 1992:** Its actual covered common-law warning proposition supports identifying the duty imposed by liability. Its distinct statutory clauses and category-specific surviving grounds remain intact; no whole amended-Act opinion or blanket tort exemption is imported. Part IV applies the inclusion principle to section 360k through its own text.
- **CSX Transportation, Inc. v. Easterwood, April 21, 1993:** Supplies the requirement of the actual applicable federal predicate and subject comparison. Its railroad-specific substantial-subsumption language, warning predicates, local-hazard qualification and speed holding are not substituted for the Medical Device Amendments.
- **Freightliner Corp. v. Myrick, April 18, 1995:** Applies the distinction between an operative federal requirement and regulatory absence or overlap, and preserves independently established actual-conflict analysis. Its reserved common-law question under another statute and its motor-vehicle saving clause do not decide this clause.
- **New York State Conference of Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans v. Travelers Insurance Co., April 26, 1995:** Supports examining the connection required by the particular statute instead of unlimited displacement from indirect effects. Its ERISA holding becomes no general exemption for state health or tort law.
- **MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. AT&T, June 17, 1994:** Requires independent adherence to statutory limits before agency deference. The FDA may not dispense with Congress's comparison or create unlimited preemption authority.
- **Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837; Babbitt v. Sweet Home Chapter of Communities for a Great Oregon, June 29, 1995:** Preserve reasonable agency interpretation within enacted ambiguity and actual delegated responsibility. Part V applies that bounded principle to the formal FDA construction without adopting every agency view or enlarging the statutory exemption power.
- **Smiley v. Citibank (South Dakota), N.A., June 3, 1996:** Preserves statutory construction before reasonable delegated interpretation, and distinguishes substantive agency responsibility from free-standing power to preempt. Its specific banking permission and qualified use of interpretation for antecedent transactions do not supply substantive approval or retroactive duties for this device.

### Medtronic, Inc. v. Lohr; Lohr v. Medtronic, Inc., Nos. 95-754 and 95-886

Decision, June 26, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 23, 1996; decided June 26, 1996. On writs of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, 56 F.3d 1335, reviewing dismissal of a Florida action removed on diversity grounds.

The question is whether the Medical Device Amendments displace the Lohrs' negligent-design, negligent-manufacture, failure-to-warn and strict-liability claims concerning a pacemaker lead cleared through substantial equivalence. The Court separately considers what counts as a state requirement, whether a relevant federal requirement applies, whether the state obligation differs from it or adds to it, and whether a genuinely equal duty becomes different because state law supplies damages.

The Model 4011 lead received substantial-equivalence clearance in 1982, without premarket approval. The FDA's November 30 letter permitted marketing subject to general controls and did not endorse safety. Lora Lohr received the pacemaker in 1987; failure in December 1990 required emergency surgery. Her physician identified a likely lead defect. The alleged design, manufacture and warning failures, and their causal consequences, have not been adjudicated.

The Eleventh Circuit restored negligent design and strict liability based on dangerous design, while sustaining preemption of negligent manufacture, failure to warn and strict-liability contentions based on manufacture or labeling. Medtronic challenges the restoration in No. 95-754; the Lohrs challenge the adverse preemption rulings in No. 95-886. Michael Lohr's consortium claim follows the underlying claims. The separate warranty dismissal was not appealed.

#### Holdings

##### I. Substantial-equivalence clearance does not supply the asserted design approval

**Operative rule:** Section 360k(a) requires an applicable federal requirement and a state requirement respecting a human-use device that differs from or adds to it and concerns safety, effectiveness or another matter included in the federal requirement, subject to subsection (b). This lead's substantial-equivalence clearance does not establish substantive federal approval of its design and therefore does not preempt the restored negligent-design or design-based strict-liability claims on that asserted ground.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Breyer's opinion, joined by all nine Justices named in the topology.

**Controlling explanation:** Permission to market is not the substantive safety determination Medtronic's design argument requires. Section 510(k), codified at section 360(k), requires advance notification and information about classification and compliance with applicable standards or approval requirements. It does not itself declare the notified design safe. Section 807.97 expressly separates a substantial-equivalence determination from official approval. The 1982 letter likewise did not endorse safety.

The separate premarket-approval system examines safety and effectiveness under its own statutory predicates. Class III status does not establish that this device underwent that process. Nor does grandfathering supply a federal judgment selecting its design. Part 820's requirement that manufacture conform to an original design or authorized changes ensures manufacturing conformance; it does not itself make that design the FDA's substantive choice.

Easterwood requires the actual federal predicate, not regulatory overlap. Freightliner likewise rejects inventing a protected federal choice where the asserted operative standard is absent. Their distinct statutory language remains intact. Here, the presence of real federal controls does not fill the missing design comparator.

The Court therefore affirms the design restoration. It decides no defect, breach or causal issue, no claim involving an actual premarket approval, and no hypothetical device-specific standard. No independently established actual conflict arises merely from this clearance. The surviving claims remain subject to their state-law elements.

##### II. An equal conduct duty is not made additional merely by a damages remedy

**Operative rule:** Section 360k(a) does not expressly preempt an independently available state duty that genuinely requires the same conduct as the applicable federal duty merely because state law adds damages. Requiring negligence or an unreasonable hazard in addition to violation of that same conduct obligation can narrow recovery without making the substantive duty different; a claim requiring different warnings or production conduct is not parallel merely because its pleading invokes federal law.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress required a difference or addition in the obligation that the State imposes. A remedy for violating the same obligation does not supply that difference. The lower court therefore erred in treating the prospect of damages as sufficient to bar an equal-duty theory. Additional state-law conditions for recovery, such as negligence or an unreasonable hazard, do not broaden the conduct requirement when a violation of the same federal obligation remains necessary.

Section 808.1(d)(2) expressly preserves equal or substantially identical requirements. The comparison concerns how the duty actually operates, not identical words alone. A theory permitting liability despite satisfaction of the identified federal conduct obligation, or demanding additional warning content or changed production conduct, cannot establish equality by calling itself parallel. Cipollone's claim-specific approach similarly requires attention to the duty supporting liability rather than the pleading label.

The Eleventh Circuit reached this argument despite its first appearance in reply, so no forfeiture bars review. It assumed Florida recognition and adequate pleading without deciding them. Those questions, actual violation, causal connection and available relief remain for ordinary proceedings.

This holding creates no private action directly enforcing the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. Section 337's enforcement allocation and any independently presented question under it remain distinct from the express-preemption issue decided here. Freightliner also preserves inquiry into an independently established actual conflict; no such conflict is proved merely by regulatory overlap or an additional remedy. The categorical parallel-duty barrier is reversed.

##### III. The asserted manufacturing and warning rules do not displace these claims

**Operative rule:** The general manufacturing and prescription-information requirements invoked here do not establish a federal counterpart that makes the presented ordinary manufacturing and warning duties different or additional within section 360k(a). The challenged preemption barriers are therefore reversed for negligent manufacture, failure to warn and corresponding strict-liability theories, without exempting all tort law or all claims involving generally applicable regulations.

**Authority:** Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** Part 820 and section 801.109 impose enforceable obligations. Describing them as general does not make them advice or establish that no federal law governs this lead. But legal force and common subject matter do not by themselves identify the substantive federal choice that these state duties would alter.

The manufacturing provisions govern quality assurance, component acceptance, production controls, inspection, records, labeling and related operations. They leave substantial choices to manufacturers; maintaining a device master record and conforming production to the original design do not establish substantive FDA approval of that design. The warning rules require practitioner information with their own qualifications. Submission of proposed labels under section 807.87 does not establish substantive approval of their precise contents.

The contrary argument would treat these applicable commands as necessarily sufficient to displace any further state assessment of manufacture or warning. It omits the required comparison of actual obligations. Neither the negligence label nor the strict-liability label resolves that comparison. Freightliner supplies the need to identify the operative federal choice; Travelers reinforces examination of the particular statutory connection rather than unlimited effects from shared subject matter.

The result is confined to the asserted federal predicates and the duties presented. A generally framed rule can contain a precise applicable command; the Court imposes no universal condition that a regulation name one manufacturer or model. An actually ambiguous pleaded duty may require identification in ordinary proceedings, but every products case need not begin with a new evidentiary hearing. Regulatory compliance, liability and a different approval pathway remain undecided.

##### IV. A common-law damages duty can be a preemptible requirement

**Operative rule:** A state obligation enforced through common-law damages can constitute a requirement under section 360k(a); common law has no categorical exemption from the express clause. Inclusion alone does not establish preemption: the applicable federal obligation, actual difference or addition, subject connection and statutory exceptions must still be satisfied.

**Authority:** Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Breyer. These are five direct votes on this proposition despite their different positions on Part III.

**Controlling explanation:** A damages judgment can require a manufacturer to change its conduct as effectively as an enacted command. The statutory word requirement does not cease to apply because a court enforces the obligation. Cipollone's covered common-law warning duties illustrate that distinction, while its different statutory clauses and separate surviving categories caution against treating inclusion as universal displacement.

Section 808.1(b) also recognizes requirements having legal force through court decisions. That provision is consistent with the enacted term. It does not eliminate the rest of the statutory comparison or turn every state tort rule into a forbidden device requirement.

Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg would leave the general question open because these claims survive on narrower grounds. The Court decides the limited inclusion question but no hypothetical future application. Part IV does not itself preempt a surviving Lohr claim, establish a new balancing test, or make the dissenters' manufacturing/warning application the Court's rule. The direct majority for inclusion and the direct majority rejecting preemption on this record answer different questions.

##### V. The FDA's formal interpretation independently supports the bounded manufacturing and warning result

**Operative rule:** Within the statutory comparison, the FDA reasonably interprets section 360k through section 808.1(d) to require specific counterpart regulations or other specific applicable requirements before divergent state requirements are displaced. Giving that formal construction substantial weight independently supports survival of these manufacturing and warning claims, while preserving its general-applicability, equal-duty and divergent substantive-application qualifications.

**Authority:** Part V, joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. This independent ground has five direct supporters; Stone-Zsela does not join it.

**Controlling explanation:** The FDA administers the device scheme, exercises the express exemption responsibility in section 360k(b), and has section 371(a)'s authority to issue regulations for efficient enforcement. Those responsibilities support consideration of its formal interpretation of the relation between federal and state requirements. MCI first requires adherence to statutory boundaries; Sweet Home and Smiley then give force to reasonable interpretation within actual delegated responsibility. No agency receives authority to contradict Congress.

Section 808.1 reads the federal and state requirements together. Its specificity provision identifies a meaningful counterpart; its examples preserve general requirements reaching products beyond devices and genuinely equal obligations. These terms reasonably support the conclusion that the asserted generic controls do not displace the ordinary duties here. They are read with subsection (d)(6)(ii), which permits preemption when a generally worded adulteration or misbranding prohibition actually imposes a divergent substantive requirement on a specific device. Labels alone therefore protect neither side.

The objection that this construction adds an impermissible condition gives insufficient weight to the ambiguity in the required federal/state comparison and the agency's responsible administration of that boundary. But the construction is not a universal individual-model requirement. Nor does it establish automatic deference to every litigating position or agency pronouncement about preemption. Smiley assumed without deciding that courts always independently determine statutory preemption; its substantive banking interpretation supplies no free-standing FDA power to preempt. The Court decides this reasonable formal construction and application, not every possible device regulation or state duty.

A variance requires a determination that the proposed replacement methods, facilities and controls for manufacture, packing, storage and installation are sufficient to assure safety, effectiveness and compliance with the Act; an approval order must impose conditions necessary to assure those ends. Optional advisory referral requires recommendations within sixty days; the Secretary must approve or deny within sixty days after submission or, if referred, after expiration of the committee's sixty-day period, whichever is later. An informal hearing is available after the order. These provisions differ from a State's section 360k(b) exemption, and no granted exception is inferred.
- **Prescription information:** Section 801.109 conditionally exempts a device unsafe without licensed-practitioner supervision from consumer adequate-directions requirements. The device must be in the possession of a person, or that person's agents or employees, regularly and lawfully engaged in its manufacture, transportation, storage or wholesale or retail distribution, or in the possession of a practitioner licensed by law to use or order it; it must be sold only to or on the prescription or other order of such a practitioner for professional use. Subsection (b)'s prescription caution and method-of-use label requirements exclude surgical instruments. Subsection (c) requires dispensing-package information about indications, effects, routes, methods, frequency and duration, relevant hazards, contraindications, side effects and precautions for intended and advertised uses. That information may be omitted only when commonly known to licensed practitioners; on written request stating reasonable grounds, the Commissioner will offer an opinion on proposed omission. No omission qualification is found here.
- **Separate labeling exceptions:** Section 801.109(d) independently requires adequate use information on manufacturer, packer or distributor labeling that furnishes or purports to furnish use information, whether or not within the dispensing package. Its exception covers a reminder piece calling attention only to the device name without indications or other use information. Subsection (e)'s issuance-or-revision-date duty excludes labels and cartons. These exceptions do not merge with subsection (c)'s permissive omission or subsection (b)'s surgical-instrument exception. Section 807.87(e)'s submission of proposed labels, labeling and advertisements is not substantive approval of their contents.
- **Clearance, classification and approval:** Section 360(k)'s report ordinarily precedes intended interstate commercial introduction by at least ninety days. Classification, substantial equivalence and premarket approval remain distinct. Section 360e(a) requires approval for a class III device subject to a subsection (b) regulation or classified under section 360c(f), unless exempt under section 360j(g). Section 360e(b) supplies the rulemaking route for pre-1976 devices and their substantially equivalent types; no such approval is found for this lead. PMA's distinct evaluation and application requirements do not become clearance requirements by inference. The Court decides neither a different approval pathway nor a particular investigational exemption.
- **Temporal limits:** The 1994 Code and April 1, 1996 CFR are text sources, not findings that every amendment applied to the 1982 clearance, 1987 implantation or 1990 injury. The 1990 codification requires the same intended use and a Secretary's order finding either the same technological characteristics or, for different characteristics, submitted information—including clinical data if the Secretary deems it necessary—demonstrating that the device is as safe and effective as a legally marketed device and raises no different questions of safety and efficacy than the predicate. Different technological characteristics means a significant change in materials, design, energy source or other device features. A predicate removed from the market at the Secretary's initiative or judicially determined adulterated or misbranded cannot establish substantial equivalence. That later text is not backdated; the lower court assumed that it described earlier practice. Later submission amendments and design-validation language create no earlier approval or breach. The original Part 820 regime governs the comparison, and no later quality-system revision enters the decision. An actual conduct-date duty remains to be identified for liability.
- **Savings and federal enforcement:** Section 360h(d) preserves liability despite compliance with an order under that section; it is not an unrestricted saving clause for every device action. When damages for economic loss enforce such liability, the value of a remedy supplied under the order must be taken into account. No such order or offset is adjudicated. Section 337 reserves direct enforcement to the United States except for its State food-enforcement provision. A State may bring proceedings in its own name and jurisdiction to civilly enforce, or restrain violations of, sections 341 and 343(b), (c), (d), (e), (f), (g), (h), (i), (k), (q) and (r), only for food located in the State. Proceedings may not begin until thirty days after notice to the Secretary, or until ninety days after notice if the Secretary begins an informal or formal enforcement action concerning the food within the first thirty days. Proceedings are barred while the Secretary diligently prosecutes a court proceeding concerning the food or after settlement of that proceeding or the related informal or formal enforcement action; the State may intervene as of right in the described court proceeding. This exception creates no private device action. No unpresented standalone enforcement defense or later implied-preemption rule is decided.

#### Law After Decision

Section 360k(a) can reach common-law damages duties, but it does not categorically displace them. Courts must identify the actual state obligation, an applicable federal requirement, the difference or addition and the required subject connection, preserving statutory exceptions. A separate remedy and additional elements that narrow recovery do not themselves make an otherwise equal conduct duty different. State-law recognition and all liability elements remain necessary.

The design claims here survive because substantial-equivalence clearance did not establish the asserted substantive design approval. The manufacturing and warning claims survive the asserted generic federal predicates on the six-Justice comparison in Part III and the independent five-Justice formal-regulation ground in Part V. General regulatory form alone neither guarantees survival nor establishes displacement. The decision creates no private FDCA action, no universal model-specificity test, no all-tort exemption and no rule for an actual PMA approval or another future device pathway. Independently established actual conflict remains distinct.

### Geier v. American Honda Motor Co., No. 98-1811

Merits decision, May 22, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 7, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, which affirmed dismissal of the District of Columbia tort action. The questions concern the Safety Act's express and common-law saving provisions and actual conflict between the asserted no-airbag duty and FMVSS 208's operative restraint transition.

In 1992 Geier was injured when her 1987 Honda Accord struck a tree. The car had manual seat belts and no airbag or other passive restraint. The presented theory alleges that the car should have had a driver's airbag despite federal compliance. No trial finding establishes defect, causation, injury prevention or damages.

**Statutory setting.** Former 15 U.S.C. §1392(d), when a federal motor-vehicle standard is in effect, bars a nonidentical state standard addressing the same aspect of performance. It permits enforcement of identical standards and higher performance requirements for vehicles or equipment procured for a federal, state or local government's own use. Former §1397(k) separately provides that compliance with a federal safety standard does not exempt a person from common-law liability. Neither permission is an exception silently transferred to the other provision; no procurement dispute is decided.

**Restraint options.** S4.1.2.1 provides automatic frontal/angular protection at front outboard positions, conforming Type 1 or Type 2 belts at center-front and rear positions, and either automatic lateral/rollover protection or the prescribed front-outboard belt arrangement with the additional testing. S4.1.2.2 permits the narrower head-on automatic system, with the prescribed belts, perpendicular-impact testing both with automatic protection and the Type 1 belt, and front-outboard warning requirements. S4.1.2.3 permits the lap-and-shoulder-belt/warning arrangement: except for convertibles and open-body vehicles, front outboard positions require either a conforming Type 2 assembly with a nondetachable shoulder belt or the Type 1 alternative meeting the prescribed perpendicular-impact requirement. Center-front and other positions retain their separate belt/warning requirements. Convertibles and open-body vehicles have distinct all-position Type 1/Type 2 belt and front-position warning requirements. The automatic amendment covers passenger-car front outboard positions; other seats and vehicle classes retain their applicable requirements. None of these alternatives is an airbag mandate.

**Phase-in and credits.** The successive September-to-August production periods beginning September 1, 1986 require automatic protection for 10%, 25% and 40%, respectively, subject to the rule's conditions. The original 1984 rule uses the preceding three-year production averages: September 1983–August 1986, September 1984–August 1987 and September 1985–August 1988. The manufacture-date 1986 provisions permit those averages or the manufacturer's annual production for the respective phase-in year. Nonbelt automatic driver protection with automatic right-front protection receives 1.5 credit; the right-front technology need not match the driver's. The 1986 provision separately allows one credit for qualifying nonbelt driver protection with a manual Type 2 right-front belt.

Qualifying production from September 1985 through August 1986 may count toward the first year. The second-year carry covers qualifying production through August 1987 not already counted toward the first; the third covers qualifying production through August 1988 not counted toward either earlier year. Cars produced by more than one manufacturer are attributed only once: imports to the importer and domestic cars to a participating marketer, subject to a qualifying written contract assigning the car to one manufacturer and Part 585 reporting. The 1986 due-care provision requires the manufacturer to establish that, despite due care, it had no reason to know of nonconformity; it addresses regulatory noncompliance, not general tort immunity. The 1984 preamble had contemplated later action on that subject. No Honda production, credit, attribution or diligence finding is made.

**Full implementation and belt-law condition.** The September 1, 1989 all-new-passenger-car automatic-protection requirement is subject to S4.1.5. No later than April 1, 1989, the Secretary must determine that qualifying enacted belt-use laws cover at least two-thirds of the combined population of the 50 States and District of Columbia, using the most recent Census estimates. Cars manufactured on or after that determination may use any of the three options. The condition is neither automatic nor a count of two-thirds of the States.

Qualifying laws require proper belt use by front-seat occupants while the car moves forward, medical-only waivers, at least a $25 penalty including allowable costs for each violator, permission to mitigate an injured violator's damages, a compliance-encouragement program, and effectiveness by September 1, 1989. Existing state permission to mitigate can suffice; reduction of damages is not mandatory. Preamble discussion of evaluation reports and waivers for substantially complying pre-August 1, 1984 laws does not replace that operative list. No finding establishes that the determination occurred.

The agency sought safety, development of alternative technologies, consumer acceptance, innovation and a gradual transition attentive to cost and production problems. It encouraged nonbelt systems and recognized airbag benefits, without prescribing a numerical technology mix or a minimum number of airbag-free cars. Its own explanation also relied on potential liability for deficient systems, competition and product pride. The legal effect of those purposes, read together, is the dispute before the Court.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Holdings

##### I. The express clause does not independently extinguish this saved common-law claim

**Holding and operative rule:** Former §1392(d), read with §1397(k), does not expressly displace this common-law no-airbag claim merely because the car complied with FMVSS 208. The common-law saving rule defeats categorical compliance immunity without declaring that damages duties can never be state safety standards.

**Authority:** Part I of Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court gives effect to both provisions. Section 1392(d) addresses nonidentical standards governing the same aspect of performance and preserves identical-standard enforcement and higher government-procurement requirements. Section 1397(k) expressly refuses to make compliance an exemption from common-law liability. Reading the first as categorically eliminating this action would deprive the second of its operative protection. Cipollone requires attention to the actual enacted clause and the duty necessary for liability; Medtronic likewise distinguishes the existence of a damages duty from whether the particular federal provision displaces it. Neither supplies a universal exemption for tort law. The Court decides the interaction of this Act's provisions, leaving their separately stated qualifications intact. The failure of this express defense does not itself determine the independent conflict defense.

##### II. Preserved common-law claims remain subject to an independently established actual conflict

**Holding and operative rule:** The presence of the express clause and compliance saving provision does not foreclose an independently demonstrated conflict between a state common-law duty and an operative federal command or supported regulatory objective. Federal compliance, overlapping regulation, agency preference or regulatory silence alone does not establish that conflict.

**Authority:** Part II of Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Myrick rejects the inference that an express clause necessarily exhausts every conflict inquiry, while requiring a demonstrated federal premise. Its invalidated standard and regulatory absence did not establish a policy against the omitted equipment. Gade likewise supplies no automatic displacement from overlap and no Court-wide physical-impossibility-only rule. The saving clause preserves liability notwithstanding compliance; it does not require enforcement of a duty that actually defeats governing federal law. Equally, describing a manufacturer's choice as federally permitted cannot establish immunity from the liability Congress saved. The inquiry must identify the valid federal method, the actual duty alleged and their concrete incompatibility. The possibility of installing an airbag while satisfying the regulation does not alone defeat every conflict argument, but a policy preference for uniformity does not alone sustain one. The present operative program therefore requires its own examination.

##### III. The general no-airbag duty defeats this deliberately chosen transition

**Holding and operative rule:** The presented general duty to install a driver's airbag is preempted because it would displace FMVSS 208's deliberately retained restraint alternatives and phased transition, which were operative means of promoting safety through development and public acceptance. This conflict sustains dismissal only of that general theory; compliance does not immunize every federally permitted design, and nonconflicting federal-minimum, noncompliance, defective-installation, performance, risk-specific and genuinely model-specific duties remain available when otherwise supported.

**Authority:** Part III of Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Breyer; five direct joins. The four partial joiners dissent from this holding and the judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court finds the affirmative regulatory choice absent in Myrick. The alternatives, staged percentages and differentiated incentives are means of developing restraint systems and gaining acceptance during a gradual transition. A categorical duty demanding airbags in the covered vehicles would remove the relevant nonairbag choices and accelerate the transition the agency deliberately structured. The conflict does not depend on a fixed numerical mix, a minimum number of cars without airbags, or a command that this Accord lack one.

The saving text and the agency's own recognition of deficient-system liability are substantial objections. They preserve real legal work: federal compliance alone is no defense to an independently deficient system or another duty compatible with the program. But such liability differs from the general technology mandate presented here. The safety objective, airbag benefits and encouragement of nonbelt protection do not erase the contemporaneously chosen method of obtaining those benefits through alternatives and transition. The Court relies on that method, not on every policy compromise or a later litigation assertion.

Shanklin illustrates the need to identify the operative federal selection rather than presume that every minimum standard is a ceiling; its different crossing regime supplies no automatic answer. Mining and Public Lands Council preserve actual statutory authority and temporal limits, which remain necessary here. Later safety evidence cannot retroactively alter the regulation governing this vehicle. The retained claim categories do not establish a defect, a preserved alternative pleading or recovery. Their actual duties, proof and relation to the federal program require their own analysis. On the general no-airbag theory before the Court, the demonstrated conflict warrants affirmance.

##### Whether the CDA's indecency and patently-offensive restrictions satisfy the First Amendment

**Holding:** A content-based criminal restriction on expression through a general-purpose communications network is facially invalid when uncertain and substantially overbroad categories suppress substantial protected adult speech and the Government fails to establish narrow tailoring in light of practical, materially less restrictive means of limiting minors' access. On this Internet record, §223(a)(1)(B)'s indecency prohibition and §223(d)(1)'s patently-offensive transmission and display prohibitions, together with their corresponding facility-permitting offenses, fail that requirement.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; eight direct votes. O'Connor joins parts of the judgment only.

**Material application:** Uncertain criminal categories reach substantial lawful adult discussion; scienter does not supply a clear content boundary. The record's age-verification costs, anonymity loss and exclusion of lawful adults leave the statutory defenses inadequate, while user filtering and precise treatment of unprotected material provide materially less restrictive approaches.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Vagueness contributes to the First Amendment holding; no separate Fifth Amendment ruling is made. The decision establishes no uniform classification of all Internet services and does not invalidate every age screen or every future control.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Sable, Butler and Bolger preserve adult speech despite child-protection interests. Ginsberg's confined commercial rule for material obscene for minors is distinguished. Pacifica retains its broadcast setting; ACT/Pacifica preserves independent protection of adult access. Turner's content-neutral carriage setting is distinguished. Denver Area's compulsory-segregation holding supplies an adult-access analogy, without generalizing its fractured permission judgments. Broadrick supplies substantial overbreadth.

The Court affirms the clarified preliminary injunction against that indecency branch and its corresponding §223(a)(2) liability, and against §223(d)(1)–(2); the general severability provision does not authorize creating a new minor-only offense or inserting a value exception absent from the enacted text.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; eight direct votes. O'Connor joins parts of the judgment only.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No minor-only offense or serious-value exception is judicially added. The Court reserves a blanket prohibition for seventeen-year-olds, parental approval, extraterritorial reach and future effective nonexclusionary age assurance. Independently lawful obscenity, child sexual-abuse material, threats, fraud, unlawful solicitation and direct harmful-contact laws retain their own predicates.

### Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union, No. 96-511

Merits decision, June 26, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 19, 1997; decided June 26, 1997. On direct appeal from a three-judge District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania under §561 of the Telecommunications Act of 1996. The question is whether the CDA's indecency and patently-offensive restrictions are facially invalid under the First Amendment because they are content based, vague, substantially overbroad and insufficiently tailored to protecting minors.

The Act was enacted February 8, 1996. The district court's preliminary-injunction order was dated June 11 and entered June 12, 1996; its June 28 clarification preserved June 12 as the appeal-clock entry date. The plaintiffs demonstrated present speech restrictions and practical compliance burdens. The record describes diverse lawful Internet expression and age-verification costs and barriers, especially for noncommercial and small speakers.

Section 223(a)(1)(B) reaches knowing creation or solicitation and initiation of transmission by a telecommunications device of an obscene or indecent communication, knowing its recipient is under eighteen, regardless of who placed the call or initiated the communication. The enacted definition of telecommunications device excludes an interactive computer service. Section (d)(1)(A) separately reaches knowing use of an interactive computer service to send to specified persons under eighteen; (d)(1)(B) reaches display available to a person under eighteen. Their content category covers contextual depictions or descriptions of sexual or excretory activities or organs in terms patently offensive under contemporary community standards. The corresponding facility offenses require knowing permission and intent that the facility be used for the prohibited activity. Violations carry fines, imprisonment of up to two years, or both.

Section (e)(1) protects mere access or connection to a facility, system or network outside the provider's control, including incidental functions that do not create content. Section (e)(2) denies that defense to a conspirator with an entity actively creating or knowingly distributing prohibited communications, or one knowingly advertising them; the conspirator need not personally create content. Section (e)(3) separately denies it for access to an offending network the provider owns or controls. Employer liability under (e)(4) requires within-scope employee or agent conduct plus knowing authorization or ratification, or reckless disregard. Section (e)(5) provides alternative defenses: good-faith reasonable, effective and appropriate measures under the circumstances to restrict minors' access, or restricted access requiring a verified credit card, debit account, adult access code or adult personal identification number. Under (e)(6), the FCC may describe suitable measures; it may not approve, sanction, compel or enforce their use or endorse particular products. Their use is evidence of good faith. Interactive services do not thereby become common or telecommunications carriers.

Section (f)(1) protects lawful good-faith implementation of defenses or access restrictions against suit. Section (f)(2) bars inconsistent state or local liability for the identified commercial, library and higher-education activities, while allowing complementary regulation confined to intrastate services without inconsistent interstate duties and preserving regulation of conduct outside this section. Section (g) preserves other federal laws. Section (h)'s device definition adds no broadcast-licensee or cable-operator obligations under other indecency provisions; its access-software definition covers content-neutral filtering, selection and transmission tools that do not create or provide the communication's content. These distinct savings provisions supply no absent narrow content definition.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Authority for both holdings:** The eight-Justice opinion of the Court identified above directly adopts the following propositions, explanations, limits and remedy. O'Connor's agreement with part of the judgment does not join this opinion.

##### I. The challenged content restrictions violate the First Amendment

**Holding and operative rule:** A content-based criminal restriction on expression through a general-purpose communications network is facially invalid when uncertain and substantially overbroad categories suppress substantial protected adult speech and the Government fails to establish narrow tailoring in light of practical, materially less restrictive means of limiting minors' access. On this Internet record, §223(a)(1)(B)'s indecency prohibition and §223(d)(1)'s patently-offensive transmission and display prohibitions, together with their corresponding facility-permitting offenses, fail that requirement.

**Controlling explanation:** Sable recognizes the compelling interest in protecting children but forbids reducing adults to children's speech when less restrictive means are available. Ginsberg sustained a confined commercial restriction on material obscene for minors, including a value limitation absent here. Pacifica's scarcity, intrusiveness and broadcast setting do not describe this medium; ACT/Pacifica requires independent protection of adult access even there. Turner concerns content-neutral carriage, not this direct selection of prohibited content. Denver Area's compulsory-blocking holding supplies the practical adult-access analogy, without extending its fractured permission judgments into a universal rule.

The criminal categories reach serious discussion of health, sexuality, art, politics and personal experience. Uncertainty about content and community standards chills speech; scienter cannot tell a speaker which protected discussion is criminal. Knowledge of a minor or reference to specified recipients does not prevent the prohibition from reaching conversations also intended for adults. Other communication channels do not cure exclusion from this one.

The defenses do not remove those burdens. Many small and noncommercial speakers lack feasible age verification; costs, loss of anonymity and exclusion of lawful adults persist. Proposed tagging does not establish the effective protection the defense requires on the present record. User-controlled filtering and precise regulation of unprotected material offer materially less restrictive approaches. The Government has not justified this broad criminal suppression in light of those alternatives. The Court does not find every age screen invalid or all future controls ineffective. Vagueness contributes to the First Amendment holding; no independent Fifth Amendment holding is needed.

##### II. Textual severance preserves obscenity law; judicial rewriting cannot save the remaining restrictions

**Holding and operative rule:** The discrete words imposing indecency liability in §223(a)(1)(B) are severable from its obscenity prohibition. The Court affirms the clarified preliminary injunction against that indecency branch and its corresponding §223(a)(2) liability, and against §223(d)(1)–(2); the general severability provision does not authorize creating a new minor-only offense or inserting a value exception absent from the enacted text.

**Controlling explanation:** The word choice permits removal of the indecency branch without disabling the distinct obscenity rule. The proposed further construction would instead change the offenses' substantive coverage. Knowledge and audience limits do not provide the missing narrow content definition, and a serious-value or harmful-to-minors exception cannot be supplied by judicial legislation. The statutory defenses preserve their own terms but do not establish workable protection for the record's diverse speakers. The challenge's varied activities and substantial protected reach make a selective judicial reconstruction inappropriate. O'Connor would retain knowing transmissions solely to minors; the Court does not adopt her conclusion that minors' protected-speech overbreadth is insubstantial or her proposed application severance.

The ruling does not hold that Congress can never regulate protected Internet indecency, decide the proposed blanket ban on such messages to seventeen-year-olds or the role of parental approval, or resolve the separate question of extraterritorial efficacy and scope. It does not require one constitutional classification for every Internet service. Independently lawful obscenity, child sexual-abuse material, true-threat, fraud, unlawful-solicitation and direct-harmful-contact provisions remain governed by their own predicates; they do not save these overbroad restrictions. A future precisely drawn rule using effective, nonexclusionary age assurance remains open.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Sable, Butler and Bolger preserve adult access despite the compelling interest in protecting minors; child protection does not alone justify suppressing adult expression.
- Ginsberg's commercial restriction on material obscene for minors is distinguished because the CDA reaches broader protected categories without its value limitation.
- Pacifica retains its contextual broadcast force and supplies no reduced Internet protection.
- ACT/Pacifica's independent review of content-selected restrictions and adult access remains intact; the Internet record is separately examined.
- Turner retains the distinction between content-neutral carriage and content discrimination, with independent scrutiny of the evidentiary fit.
- Denver Area's compulsory-segregation holding is applied by analogy to practical adult-access burdens; its separate permission judgments create no universal Internet rule.
- Broadrick supplies the substantial-overbreadth framework; the enacted breadth cannot be repaired by an unavailable narrowing construction. No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

The challenged indecency and patently-offensive restrictions cannot be enforced at the clarified scope. Content-based criminal regulation of this medium must protect lawful adult expression and satisfy narrow tailoring on the actual record. Obscenity and other independently unprotected conduct retain their own legal treatment. Future, precisely drawn regulation and genuinely effective nonexclusionary age controls remain open; no general Internet-service classification or freestanding Fifth Amendment rule is adopted.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, concurring in the judgment in part and dissenting in part.** Butler, Sable and Bolger protect adult access; Ginsberg permits genuine restrictions confined to minors. The Internet presently lacks generally usable identity gates, so the display offense and mixed adult/minor conversations cannot be saved by treating the network as divided into adult and child zones. She agrees with the injunction to that extent and with preservation of independent obscenity enforcement.

She would retain the transmission provisions only where the initiating adult knows all recipients are minors, with corresponding facility liability confined to those underlying communications. She construes §223(d)(1)(A) to require knowledge of minority, relying on its wording and DeBartolo's constitutional-avoidance principle. Such a communication has no other adult audience whose access is lost. Brockett's application-severability principle and the statutory severability provision support preserving that application. Although the CDA's content categories are broader than Ginsberg's obscenity-for-minors rule, she finds no showing under Broadrick that the additional protected minor speech is substantial relative to the provisions' legitimate sweep. She does not deny minors all First Amendment protection or foreclose individual vagueness challenges. Her view relies on current technology, not predicted improvements. No other Justice joins her separate writing.
