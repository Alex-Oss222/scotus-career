### Presented questions, subsidiary arguments and exceptional Supreme Court review

**Governing question:** When an issue outside the stated certiorari question may be considered.

**Current rule:** Rule 14.1(a) creates a strong prudential presumption against deciding questions neither stated in the petition nor fairly included, subject to exceptional circumstances. A distinct intervention denial is not fairly included merely because reversing it would permit reaching a settlement-vacatur question; discussion in the petition’s body and merits briefing does not alone suffice. A consistently asserted Teague defense may be subsidiary where it limits application of the very constitutional rule and habeas relief challenged by the petition. Geographic tailoring is fairly included in a challenge to exclusion from designated speech zones. An exceptional extension requires its own supported basis; adversarial briefing, preservation, logical priority or usefulness alone does not enlarge the questions. The duty to notice jurisdictional defects remains separate. A respondent may defend the existing favorable judgment on a record-supported alternative rejected below when doing so neither enlarges its own rights nor reduces the adversary’s rights under that judgment. The Court may also elect to consider a deliberately disavowed legal argument supporting the same preserved claim when it falls within the question presented and can be resolved on the established record without a new factual theory or additional findings. That discretion imposes no duty to consider abandoned grounds and does not erase limits on distinct claims, relief, factual stipulations or missing facts.

### Article III power before disputed statutory merits

**Governing question:** Whether a court may assume disputed constitutional jurisdiction to resolve contested federal statutory liability.

**Current rule:** A federal court may not assume disputed Article III power merely to decide a contested statutory merits question. Statutory enforcement-and-penalty jurisdictional wording does not automatically make every liability element jurisdictional; substantial federal claims remain distinct from success on their merits. A court may dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction before resolving subject-matter jurisdiction when personal jurisdiction is straightforward and presents no complex state-law question while subject-matter jurisdiction is difficult. Ordinarily an easily resolved subject-matter question should come first. The choice must respect statutory remand commands and review bars, comity and relevant forum interests, and may decide no contested merits; an established absence of subject-matter jurisdiction retains its statutory consequence.

**Authority by component:**

- Citizens for a Better Environment v. Steel Co. — 1. Constitutional power before this disputed statutory merits question, 1997-06-02; Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five, with O'Connor and Kennedy reserving other legitimate exceptional sequences

- Ruhrgas AG v. Marathon Oil Co. — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, “A court may choose a straightforward nonmerits jurisdictional ground”, 1999-05-17; Scalia, all nine Justices.

**Present operation effective:** Ruhrgas, May 17, 1999, for bounded nonmerits sequencing in removed and originally filed cases; Steel Co.’s merits prohibition retains its June 2, 1997 date.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule prescribes no exhaustive hierarchy among thresholds and authorizes no assumed jurisdiction over disputed merits. Norton and Avrech retain their own controlled-merits and jurisdictional settings. A particular statutory priority, such as Price-Anderson coverage before tribal exhaustion, remains operative.

**Holdings navigation:** Citizens for a Better Environment v. Steel Co. (1997-06-02) — Constitutional jurisdiction before a disputed EPCRA merits question. [Ruhrgas AG v. Marathon Oil Co. — A court may choose a straightforward nonmerits jurisdictional ground](HOLDINGS.md#a-court-may-choose-a-straightforward-nonmerits-jurisdictional-ground-1).

### Federal detention credit, initial computation and conditional release

**Governing question:** Who initially computes credit under 18 U.S.C. §3585(b), when the computation occurs, how time credited to another sentence is treated, and where conditional release ends and official detention begins for credit eligibility.

**Current rule:** The Attorney General through the Bureau of Prisons must initially calculate §3585(b) credit after the federal sentence commences; a district court may not calculate that credit at sentencing. Time already credited against another sentence when the federal term begins cannot also receive federal credit merely because the federal sentencing hearing occurred first. Administrative review and proper subsequent judicial review remain available. Under the federal Bail Reform Act, a conditional-release order is not official detention under §3585(b), however severe its restrictions; no conditions-based credit inquiry converts that release into detention. Qualifying uncredited detention resulting from the offense sentenced or another charge for which arrest occurred after that offense retains the statutory credit command.

**Authority by component:**
- Actor, timing and review: United States v. Wilson, March 24, 1992, “BOP makes the initial credit computation after commencement.”
- Commencement-based no-double-credit construction: the same record, “Credit already applied to another sentence cannot be counted again.” Thomas's opinion for Stone, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter controls both components.

- Official detention and federal conditional release: Official detention and federal conditional release, 1995-06-05. Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven directly adopt the status rule and its application without an independently sufficient administrative-deference holding.

**Present operation effective:** Koray, June 5, 1995, for the federal conditional-release boundary; Wilson, March 24, 1992, for initial computation and the no-double-credit rule.

**Open question:** State custody and other unaddressed forms of official detention remain unresolved. Koray does not universally require physical BOP custody for every creditable day and establishes no independent administrative-deference or bail-choice notice rule.

**Holdings navigation:** United States v. Wilson, 503 U.S. 329 (1992) — Detention credit; Official detention and federal conditional release.

### Discretionary waiver of entry-related immigration fraud

**Governing question:** Eligibility for former INA §241(a)(1)(H) relief and permissible discretionary consideration of entry-related fraud.

**Current rule:** Eligibility under former §241(a)(1)(H) does not compel a waiver or prohibit considering an ongoing pattern of immigration fraud merely because acts relate to entry. Eligibility requires covered willful or innocent entry fraud, the specified relationship as spouse, parent, son or daughter of a citizen or lawful permanent resident, possession of an immigrant visa or equivalent document, and admissibility at entry apart from the qualifying fraud and the labor-certification or documentation grounds in §§1182(a)(5)(A) and (7)(A) that directly resulted from it. The exclusion for assistance in Nazi persecution or participation in genocide remains. A waiver actually granted also reaches entry-inadmissibility grounds directly resulting from the fraud. Discretion must remain individualized: qualifying fraud cannot automatically defeat every eligible application. Permissible factors remain subject to binding rules, rational administration and adequate explanation of a departure from genuinely settled policy; pretext and a litigation explanation for an unexplained change do not suffice.

**Authority by component:**

- Eligibility/discretion separation, statutory conditions and continuing fraud: INS v. Yueh-Shaio Yang — INS v. Yueh-Shaio Yang — II. Eligibility does not exclude the continuing fraud pattern from discretion; administration: INS v. Yueh-Shaio Yang — III. Permissible factors remain subject to reasoned and consistent administration. Souter for all nine Justices, November 13, 1996.

**Present operation effective:** Yang, November 13, 1996, for the continuing-fraud construction.

**Open question:** Whether the minimum fraud necessary for eligibility alone could justify a particular individualized denial remains unresolved. The Court does not reweigh the agency balance or adopt a reopening rule from Doherty’s noncontrolling opinions.

#### Reno v. Koray

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-790.
**Decided:** June 5, 1995.

##### Official detention and federal conditional release

**Holding:** Time under a federal Bail Reform Act conditional-release order is not official detention under § 3585(b), even when the restrictions are severe, so Koray’s release period requires no conditions-based credit inquiry. This rule concerns the presented federal release/detention distinction, preserves qualifying uncredited detention and judicial review, and does not decide state custody or require physical BOP control for every creditable day.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven directly adopt the status rule and its application without an independently sufficient administrative-deference holding.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Mandatory credit remains for qualifying detention resulting from the offense sentenced or another charge for which arrest occurred after that offense, if not credited against another sentence. BOP initial computation and exhausted judicial review remain. State custody, a universal physical-BOP-control requirement and the unpresented bail-choice notice question are not decided. Administrative practice does not supply an independent deference holding.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- United States v. Wilson, March 24, 1992: applies BOP initial computation, no duplicate credit and subsequent lawful review; Wilson did not itself define official detention.
- Thomas Jefferson University v. Shalala, June 24, 1994: distinguishes qualified controlling weight for an agency’s own-regulation interpretation from a program statement construing a statute; its own-regulation rule remains intact.
- MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co., June 17, 1994: preserves the statutory-boundary requirement; administrative practice does not independently rewrite the credit provision.
- Crandon v. United States, 494 U.S. 152: applies ordinary text and context before lenity; the Court finds no residual ambiguity in the presented federal-release category.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the Third Circuit's conditions-inquiry judgment and remand consistently with exclusion of this federal conditional-release period from section 3585(b) credit. No conditions-based credit inquiry, exact-day award, resentencing, immediate release or equality adjudication follows.
