#### Babbitt v. Sweet Home Chapter of Communities for a Great Oregon

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-859.
**Decided:** June 29, 1995.

##### Statutory authority for habitat-caused injury

**Holding:** The ESA permits the Secretary to define harm to include significant habitat modification or degradation that actually kills or injures protected wildlife by significantly impairing essential behavioral patterns, including breeding, feeding, or sheltering. Habitat change without the required animal injury is outside this clause.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part I; Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer join this statutory proposition. Seven votes make it controlling independently of Part II's deference rationale.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 9's individual-animal protection remains distinct from section 7's jeopardy and critical-habitat duties; environmental purpose alone supplies no authority.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. AT&T: followed as requiring enacted authority; its rejection of removing a mandatory filing requirement does not foreclose this different statutory definition.
- PUD No. 1 v. Washington Department of Ecology: consistent with authority grounded in the particular statute, rather than environmental purpose alone.

**Independent alternative holding:** Where this ESA definition leaves room concerning indirect injury, the Secretary's actual-injury construction is reasonable and receives Chevron deference. This rationale neither dispenses with statutory authority nor establishes that the regulation is the only permissible construction.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part II; Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer join. Six votes independently control this rationale; Stone does not join it.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The six-Justice deference rationale is independently sufficient and does not make the regulation the only permissible construction or create a criminal knowledge requirement.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council: applied to a reasonable construction within statutory ambiguity.
- MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. AT&T: followed; deference does not authorize displacement of an unambiguous statutory command.
- Thomas Jefferson University v. Shalala: distinguished; own-regulation interpretation does not establish the statutory delegation here.

##### Actual injury and ordinary causal limits

**Holding:** The habitat clause requires actual death or injury proximately caused, with ordinary foreseeability, by significant habitat modification through significant impairment of essential behavior; bare but-for connection or speculative future harm is insufficient. An omission can be considered only where an independently existing legal duty supplies the obligation, and this decision creates no general duty to maintain habitat.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part III; Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer join the common rule. O'Connor's concrete reproduction example and Palila reservations are her concurrence, not additional majority applications.

**Limits and questions not reached:** A carcass is not indispensable evidence: appropriate proof may establish injury or support prevention of injury that will actually occur. Speculative population effects are insufficient. Specific causal applications and the separate civil/criminal scienter requirements remain open; O'Connor's reproduction example and Palila reservations are not additional majority applications.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Brown v. Gardner: distinguished; its benefits provision does not decide ESA proximate causation or civil and criminal scienter.

##### Distinct statutory protections and conditional permissions

**Holding:** Facial validity preserves the separate triggers of endangered-species section 9, threatened-species rules under section 4(d), federal-agency section 7, and statutory exceptions and conditional permits under section 10. Neither a no-jeopardy opinion nor otherwise lawful land use alone exempts a taking.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part IV; Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer join. The statutory qualifications below are integral limits, not new adjudications of applications.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The integral statutory qualifications below remain distinct. The Court does not construe or apply section 6(g)(2); its exact terms and application require the official provision and applicable State-agreement record, absent from the positively reviewed appendix.

Section 3(19) defines take through harass, harm, pursue, hunt, shoot, wound, kill, trap, capture, collect, and attempts at that conduct. Under 50 C.F.R. §17.3, harm means an act actually killing or injuring wildlife; it may include significant habitat modification or degradation where it actually kills or injures by significantly impairing essential behavioral patterns, including breeding, feeding, or sheltering.

Section 9(a)(1) concerns listed endangered fish or wildlife and persons subject to United States jurisdiction, subject to sections 6(g)(2) and 10. Its taking prohibitions cover the United States and territorial sea and the high seas. Section 9(a)(1)(G) separately prohibits violation of regulations lawfully issued for endangered or threatened species. Section 4(d) requires necessary and advisable conservation regulations for threatened species and permits extension of section 9 prohibitions; 50 C.F.R. §17.31 supplies a separate regulatory route. For taking resident fish or wildlife, section 4(d) regulations apply in a State having a section 6(c) cooperative agreement only to the extent the State also adopts them. The Court does not construe the separate section 6(g)(2) exception or decide its application; its exact terms and any application require the official section 6(g)(2) text and applicable State-agreement record, which are not supplied in the positively reviewed appendix.

Section 7's no-jeopardy and critical-habitat obligations govern federal agency action; they do not substitute for section 9's actual-taking inquiry. Section 7(o)(1) protects activity necessary to an agency action exempted under section 7(h). Section 7(o)(2) protects a taking compliant with the terms and conditions of a written section 7(b)(4) incidental-take statement. A no-jeopardy opinion alone is not a blanket exemption.

Section 10(a)(1)(A) permits scientific or propagation-and-survival permissions. Section 10(a)(1)(B) permits taking incidental to, and not the purpose of, otherwise lawful activity. Section 10(a)(2)(A) requires a conservation plan stating likely impacts; minimization and mitigation steps and funding; alternatives considered and reasons rejected; and other necessary and appropriate measures. After public comment, section 10(a)(2)(B) requires findings of incidental taking, minimization and mitigation to the maximum extent practicable, adequate funding, no appreciable reduction in likelihood of survival and recovery in the wild, satisfaction of other required measures, and receipt of required assurances. Upon those findings the Secretary shall issue the permit with necessary or appropriate terms, conditions, and reporting requirements; section 10(a)(2)(C) requires revocation for noncompliance. These requirements do not grant blanket permission for unpermitted land use.

Section 10(j)'s experimental-population treatment is separate: released populations, including solely their offspring, qualify only while wholly geographically separate from nonexperimental populations of the same species. Release outside the current range requires a finding that conservation is furthered, identification by regulation before release, and a best-available-information determination of essentiality. Members are treated as threatened; a nonessential population is treated as proposed for listing for section 7 other than section 7(a)(1), except within the National Wildlife Refuge or National Park System. Critical habitat may not be designated for a nonessential population. Earlier authorized geographically separate releases are subject to the statutory regulatory determination. No experimental-population application is decided here.

Distinct section 11 civil and criminal channels retain their own conditions, including applicable notice and hearing and the civil good-faith protection for action believed necessary to protect a person from bodily harm. This facial decision establishes no new mens rea test or universal knowledge-of-illegality requirement.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Tennessee Valley Authority v. Hill: relied on for the Act's operative protection, subject to the distinct permissions and exemptions Congress enacted.
- PUD No. 1 v. Washington Department of Ecology: consistent with giving each environmental provision its own statutory trigger.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the D.C. Circuit's facial invalidation and remand. The regulation remains valid, but particular enforcement requires applicable injury, causation and enforcement predicates. No logging license, damages, fine, particular injunction, conviction or actual permit application is adjudicated.

#### PUD No. 1 of Jefferson County v. Washington Department of Ecology, 511 U.S. 700

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-1911.

**Decided:** 1994-05-31.

##### A discharge triggers certification; necessary conditions can govern the activity

**Holding:** When an activity requiring a federal license or permit may result in a discharge into navigable waters, §401(a)(1) triggers certification, and §401(d) permits limitations and monitoring on the applicant's activity as necessary to assure compliance with its listed federal requirements and other appropriate State-law requirements stated in the certification. Conditioning authority is not confined to the particular triggering discharge, but it remains tied to those lawful compliance requirements; EPA's reasonable activity-compliance interpretation reinforces this reading without creating authority to contradict the statute.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; seven direct joins to this proposition and its explanation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The conceded construction and tailrace discharges supply the trigger, not a ruling that every diversion is a discharge. Section 401(d) reaches applicable §301 and §302 effluent and other limitations, §306 performance standards, §307 prohibitions, effluent and pretreatment standards, and other appropriate State-law requirements set out in the certificate. Conditions and monitoring must assure compliance and become federal-license conditions. Section 401(a)(1) requires certification from the originating State or appropriate interstate agency, or EPA where that entity lacks authority; requires public-notice procedures for every application and public-hearing procedures to the extent deemed appropriate for particular applications; provides that certification is waived if the responsible certifying authority fails or refuses to act within a reasonable period no longer than one year after receiving the request; and forbids licensing before certification or waiver or after denial. Where no applicable §301(b) or §302 effluent or other limitation and no §306 or §307 standard exists, the State certifies that circumstance; that certification does not satisfy §511(c). No notice, waiver, denial or other procedural breach is found here.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837: applies deference to a permissible agency construction within the statute's delegation, not against its limits.
- Arkansas v. Oklahoma, 503 U.S. 91: uses its respect for a permissible water-quality interpretation without transplanting its distinct §402 proof and review rules.
- NLRB v. Health Care & Retirement Corp. of America, May 23, 1994: preserves scheme-specific permissible interpretation, not automatic approval of every agency position.
- City of Chicago v. Environmental Defense Fund, Inc., May 2, 1994, and John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank, December 13, 1993: preserve the requirement that agency interpretation stay within the particular statutory boundary.

##### Section 301 supplies one route incorporating water-quality standards

**Holding:** Section 401(d)'s incorporation of §301 includes §301(b)(1)(C)'s more stringent limitations necessary to meet applicable water-quality standards, including standards adopted under §303. That statutory route independently permits certification conditions necessary to secure compliance with those standards, subject to the actual discharge trigger and the lawful scope of the standards themselves.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; seven direct joins to this proposition and its explanation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Arkansas v. Oklahoma, 503 U.S. 91: preserves the statutory role of applicable water-quality standards; its distinct permitting setting informs rather than substitutes for the §401/§301 incorporation analysis.

**Independent alternative holding:** Section 401(d)'s separate authorization of any other appropriate requirement of State law includes limitations necessary to comply with state water-quality standards adopted pursuant to §303. This is an independently sufficient incorporation route for those standards, not a holding that every State-law requirement or preferred project policy becomes a federal-license condition.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; seven direct joins to this proposition and its explanation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Arkansas v. Oklahoma, 503 U.S. 91: supports recognition of lawful water-quality standards while preserving the distinct statutory source and limits of the present conditioning authority.

##### Designated uses are enforceable components alongside criteria

**Holding:** A §303(c)(2)(A) water-quality standard contains both designated uses and criteria based on those uses; satisfying numerical criteria does not invariably satisfy the standard if the activity would defeat a protected use. A necessary minimum flow may protect the approved fish use here without an asserted numerical-criteria violation, subject to the distinct statutory toxic-criteria rules and the lawful attainability, use-removal and procedural qualifications; no unconditional duty to achieve every aspirational use is imposed.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; seven direct joins to this proposition and its explanation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Criteria may be numerical or narrative and must rest on sound science, cover enough parameters to protect uses and protect the most sensitive of multiple uses. Section 131.11(b)'s numerical guidance says should, not a universal shall. Section 303(c)(2)(B) separately requires, during review or adoption, numerical criteria for listed toxic pollutants with published §304(a) criteria whose presence or discharge could reasonably interfere with designated uses, as necessary to support those uses; where numerical criteria are unavailable, biological-monitoring or assessment criteria consistent with §304(a)(8) are required. Existing numerical, biological and other permit controls are not delayed. Use removal under §131.10(g) concerns nonexisting uses or subcategories and requires proved infeasibility from natural pollutant levels; natural ephemeral, intermittent or low flows unless enough effluent can compensate consistently with state conservation requirements; irremediable human conditions or greater harm from correction; hydrologic modification that cannot feasibly be restored or operated to attain the use; natural physical conditions unrelated to water quality precluding aquatic-life protection; or controls beyond §§301(b) and 306 causing substantial and widespread economic and social impact. Existing uses cannot be removed unless a more protective use is added, nor can uses attainable through statutory controls and cost-effective reasonable nonpoint practices be removed. Notice and a hearing opportunity are required before uses or subcategories are added or removed. Omission or removal of §101(a)(2) uses, or establishment of less-stringent subcategories, requires attainability analysis; designation including those uses does not. Seasonal criteria cannot defeat a more protective use in another season. No qualifying removal or infeasibility is established.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Arkansas v. Oklahoma, 503 U.S. 91: preserves application of actual water-quality standards; the present uses-and-criteria rule rests on §303 and the eligible regulations, without changing Arkansas's separate evidentiary rules.

**Independent alternative holding:** Within the certification authority established above, §131.12(a)(1)'s requirement to maintain and protect existing instream uses and the water quality necessary for them, together with Washington's existing-beneficial-use protection, supplies an additional sufficient ground for this preservation condition. Permission to lower higher-quality water under separate qualified procedures does not authorize destruction of existing uses, and no finding of an outstanding-resource designation or universal dam-operation duty is made.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; seven direct joins to this proposition and its explanation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Under §131.12(a)(2), lowering water quality exceeding fish, shellfish, wildlife and recreational needs requires a State finding, after full intergovernmental coordination and public participation through continuing planning, that lowering is necessary for important area economic or social development. Existing uses must still be fully protected, the highest applicable point-source requirements met and all cost-effective reasonable nonpoint practices applied. Outstanding-resource waters receive separate protection under (a)(3), and thermal impairment remains governed consistently with §316 under (a)(4); neither situation is found here. Washington's existing-beneficial-use rule independently bars degradation interfering with or injuring those uses. Its separate rule combining interference or injury with long-term irreparable environmental harm is not merged into that protection. The State's preservation finding, not an enhancement or optimal-fishery goal, supports the condition.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Arkansas v. Oklahoma, 503 U.S. 91: leaves statutory water-quality protection operative; this additional existing-use application arises from the eligible antidegradation requirements, not from a new §402 rule.

##### Water-allocation savings do not categorically exclude necessary flow protection

**Holding:** Sections 101(g) and 510(2) preserve state water-allocation authority, established quantity rights and jurisdiction, but do not exempt a user's activity from otherwise lawful water-quality conditions merely because sufficient quantity is necessary to protect an aquatic use. This certification does not allocate ownership or grant the separately required water-right permit, and §510(1)'s protection of stricter pollution regulation remains subject to its express prohibition on less-stringent requirements where federal requirements are in effect.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; seven direct joins to this proposition and its explanation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- First Iowa Hydro-Electric Cooperative v. FPC, 328 U.S. 152: preserves the federal licensing structure without displacing Congress's separate express §401 role or resolving ownership of water here.

##### This authorized certification condition survives the present licensing objection

**Holding:** Congress expressly makes lawful §401 conditions conditions of a later federal license, so the Federal Power Act does not invalidate this authorized water-quality condition merely because the project also requires FERC approval. No federal license or conflicting federal flow term yet exists; the Court preserves available review of a later license and decides no actual future conflict, grants no license, and orders no construction.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; seven direct joins to this proposition and its explanation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- First Iowa Hydro-Electric Cooperative v. FPC, 328 U.S. 152, and California v. FERC, 495 U.S. 490: distinguish conflicting independent state licensing demands from the certification role Congress expressly preserves; their federal-licensing principles remain intact.
- Escondido Mutual Water Co. v. La Jolla Band of Mission Indians, 466 U.S. 765: preserves available judicial review in the federal licensing process; no future license dispute is decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Washington Supreme Court is affirmed, 7–2, sustaining seasonal residual flows of 100–200 cubic feet per second for the bypassed 1.2-mile reach. This preserves the existing fish use without a new optimal-flow calculation. The certificate grants neither the separate water-right permit required before construction nor a federal license; no future conflict with a FERC condition is adjudicated.

#### MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co., 512 U.S. 218

**Docket or dockets:** Nos. 93-356 and 93-521.

**Decided:** 1994-06-17.

**Companion or consolidated matters:** United States et al. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co. et al., No. 93-521, is consolidated with No. 93-356; the same rule invalidation applies to both.

##### The modification power does not permit this replacement of mandatory filing

**Holding:** Under 47 U.S.C. §203(b)(2), the Commission may, in its discretion and for good cause shown, modify tariff requirements in particular instances or by general order applicable to special circumstances or conditions, but may not replace §203(a)'s mandatory filing obligation throughout the nondominant long-distance sector with an optional system or lengthen the statutory notice period beyond 120 days. The challenged permissive-filing rule exceeds that delegation and is invalid; Chevron does not require deference to a construction outside this statutory boundary.

**Proposition-level authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg. Five of eight participating Justices directly adopt the structural modification ground, its application and its stated limits; no aggregation of separate rationales is necessary.

**Material application:** The November 1992 rule makes filing optional, not forbidden, for nondominant long-distance carriers; AT&T alone remains classified as dominant in this record. A modification can have consequential effects. Courts must examine the obligation changed, the scope of the modification clause and whether the agency adjusts the obligation's operation or removes its essential operation for the regulated class. This inquiry does not turn on a numerical market-share threshold or automatically invalidate a class-wide adjustment. Changes to filing form, contents, timing or place remain possible under the statutory conditions; timing changes cannot extend the 120-day notice ceiling. The Court does not categorically foreclose deferral or waiver in limited circumstances, and decides no particular proposed waiver. Good cause alone cannot authorize replacement of mandatory filing throughout this sector.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide whether the challenged order independently fails the special-circumstances-or-conditions requirement; no separate alternative ground commands a majority. It prescribes no tariff rates or replacement program, decides no damages or retrospective nonfiling consequences, resolves no general rule for individual nonenforcement decisions, and adopts no independently sufficient ratification or prohibition theory from §§226 or 332.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984): applies the inquiry into statutory meaning before deference; no new exception based on economic importance and no replacement of Chevron is adopted.
- National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp., March 25, 1992: preserves contextual interpretation within an actual delegation and the requirement of a discernible agency ground; the agency's competitive-policy rationale is examined, not replaced by a judicial rationale.
- Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala, June 7, 1993: preserves permissible interpretive change within a statutory system; the defect here is the rule's statutory reach, not the fact that the Commission changed its policy.
- Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB, January 27, 1992: preserves the principle that agency expertise operates within binding statutory limits; its distinct nonemployee-access rule is not imported into tariff regulation.
- NLRB v. Health Care & Retirement Corp. of America, May 23, 1994: distinguishes a permissible functional construction preserving the statute's operative distinctions from removal of the filing obligation here; its supervisory-status holding remains intact.
- PUD No. 1 of Jefferson County v. Washington Department of Ecology, May 31, 1994: distinguishes broad conditioning authority grounded in that Act's operative compliance language; no general rule against consequential agency action follows.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The D.C. Circuit's judgment setting aside the permissive-detariffing rule is affirmed in Nos. 93-356 and 93-521. No tariff rate, replacement program, damages or retrospective monetary consequence of earlier nonfiling is decided, and no new direction is given in the distinct complaint proceeding.

#### FDA v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp., 529 U.S. 120 (2000)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 98-1152.

**Decided:** March 21, 2000.

##### Which FDCA version governs any tobacco authority after the 1997 Act

**Holding:** Public Law 105-115, section 422, leaves the question of tobacco authority unchanged and requires any such authority to be exercised under the FDCA as it stood on November 20, 1997. The Act's new mission and regulatory procedures therefore supply neither a new tobacco delegation nor a means to avoid a restriction of that earlier regime.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's opinion of the Court, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five direct votes.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 501's generally applicable ninety-day effective date after November 21, 1997 yields to express provisions and excludes sections 111, 121, 125 and 307. It does not override section 422. Section 406's new mission and later classification, review and evidentiary procedures do not enter the earlier tobacco regime; preexisting qualified scientific-evidence and reclassification flexibility remains.

##### Whether the connected statutory scheme authorizes the asserted customary-tobacco drug/device regime

**Holding:** Read with its operative safety and marketing requirements and Congress's distinct tobacco enactments, the FDCA does not authorize the asserted drug/device regime for tobacco as customarily marketed. The FDA cannot obtain that power by construing broad coverage terms to include a continuing-market accommodation that the connected statutory requirements do not permit; the challenged rule is invalid on that ground.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone-Zsela's opinion of the Court, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five direct votes.

**Material application:** Intended-use evidence is assumed for this inquiry. The chemical action of nicotine does not decide the delivery component's classification, and therapeutic advertising is not the sole possible intended use. Combination-product assignment of responsibility under section 353(g) does not waive independent drug, misbranding, safety, approval or cessation commands. The Court rejects the claimed continuing-market accommodation under this particular scheme; it adopts no magnitude-based major-questions rule or categorical bar to reasoned agency reinterpretation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The following distinctions limit the statutory reasoning; they are not new enforcement findings:

- **Directions and warnings:** Section 352(f)(1) requires an exemption by regulation when adequate directions are unnecessary for public-health protection. That directions exemption does not waive section 352(f)(2)'s separate necessary warnings concerning pathological conditions, childhood use, unsafe dosage, method or duration. Section 352(j) concerns danger under labeled dosage, manner, frequency or duration; existing tobacco warnings do not automatically establish FDCA compliance.
- **Classification and approval:** Device classes depend on their statutory control and information criteria, including the qualified low-risk route; risk alone does not compel an immediate judicial ban. The earlier scientific-evidence and reclassification procedures remain. No particular classification, new-drug application, grandfather status or approval is adjudicated. Section 321(p)'s pre-June 25, 1938 exception requires the drug's prior-Act status and unchanged labeling representations; the Court does not find those conditions satisfied or unsatisfied here.
- **Restricted devices:** Section 360j(e) permits practitioner-authorization or other sale, distribution or use conditions upon the required determination that harmful potential or necessary collateral measures prevent reasonable assurance of safety and effectiveness otherwise. Training or facility limits require their own safe-use determination; lack of specified board eligibility or certification alone cannot exclude a user. Required label statements remain. None of those permissions is a general exemption from another command.
- **Ban proceedings:** Section 360f permits rulemaking on findings of substantial deception or unreasonable and substantial risk. When the Secretary has determined that labeling can correct the problem and provided written notice specifying the change and time allowed, failure to make that change is separately required. Immediate effect additionally requires unreasonable, direct and substantial danger and advance manufacturer notice, followed by prompt notice, an informal-hearing opportunity and expeditious affirmance, modification or revocation.
- **Cessation and recall:** Section 360h(e)(1) requires immediate cessation of distribution and notification and cessation-of-use instructions to health professionals and user facilities after a reasonable-probability finding that a human-use device would cause serious adverse health consequences or death. An informal-hearing opportunity must be provided within ten days of the order, and inadequate grounds require vacatur. Recall requires a further determination, timetable and progress reports; it cannot reach devices held by individuals, and the greater-risk exception concerns recall from user facilities. Those recall limits do not make the initial cessation command elective. Individual risk-notice duties remain. The Court makes none of the required enforcement determinations itself.
- **Other enactments and State law:** The cigarette statutes' labeling and advertising limits and the smokeless-tobacco statute's distinct package/advertisement rules retain their own scopes, including the latter's outdoor-billboard exception. Section 360k requires a relevant federal device requirement and a different or additional State duty in the specified subject relationship. Its exemption procedure requires an application, regulation, notice and oral-hearing opportunity; greater stringency is one route, compelling local conditions without violating federal requirements another. Medtronic therefore prevents treating coverage alone as universal displacement of State youth-access laws. No particular preemption or exemption is decided.

The Court decides no advertising merits, constitutional objection, therapeutic-claim product, biological-license question, particular new-drug refusal ground, international coordination requirement or later classification procedure. It does not pronounce every risky device unmarketable, require therapeutic claims for every drug, create a general prohibition on agency change, or require Congress to use particular words in every delegation. Congress may confer tobacco authority expressly.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm invalidation of the August 28, 1996 rule for want of the asserted authority. Only genuinely necessary implementation or severability proceedings remain permissible. No replacement rule, tobacco ban, recall, damages or retrospective liability is ordered; the separately vacated advertising construction remains unadopted.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.:** Applies statutory construction before deference; agency reasonableness cannot supply a delegation absent from the enacted scheme.
- **United States v. Haggar Apparel Co.:** Preserves the distinction between genuine delegated ambiguity, regulatory validity and application; formal rulemaking alone does not establish authority.
- **MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co.:** Applies its requirement that a bounded agency power preserve an operative statutory obligation. It creates no universal magnitude-based exception to delegation.
- **Babbitt v. Sweet Home Chapter of Communities for a Great Oregon:** Preserves broad statutory coverage within actual delegated language and concrete limits; it does not authorize disregard of a connected command.
- **AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Board:** Preserves separate analysis of general implementing power, particular statutory constraints and unresolved applications.
- **Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala:** Preserves considered interpretive change within the statute; the FDA's former position is not an automatic legal bar.
- **National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp.:** Preserves recognition of the agency's actual discernible ground without supplying missing findings or a judicial replacement policy.
- **Adams Fruit Co. v. Barrett:** Preserves the requirement of an actual congressional delegation; an agency cannot create its own authority.
- **United States v. Bacto-Unidisk:** Its broad protective reading of the FDCA remains, subject to the entire enacted scheme rather than isolated definitions.
- **United States v. Rutherford:** Preserves statutory safety and effectiveness requirements against a judicially created exemption; it is not a present tobacco ban or an adjudication of an application.
- **Medtronic, Inc. v. Lohr:** Preserves the actual federal/state counterpart and subject conditions for device preemption; federal coverage does not automatically eliminate every State rule.
- **United States v. Locke:** Its particular national-vessel and local-waterway allocation remains distinct; overlap alone supplies no general preemption rule here.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone concurs in the judgment and joins Parts I, III, and IV. He independently reads the enacted definition, the conservation structure, and the permission provisions to authorize significant habitat modification that actually injures protected animals by impairing essential behavior. Harm has work beyond direct hunting and capture. Ecological expertise and reasoned regulatory narrowing have substantial persuasive weight, but the statute supplies authority. He does not join Part II's Chevron ground. His position retains actual injury, proximate causation, and the statute's distinct exceptions and permit conditions; it creates no unrestricted habitat power. This separate statutory reading does not announce a universal abolition of deference.

Justice O'Connor concurs and joins all four Parts. She insists on actual injury to identifiable protected animals and a foreseeable, proximate consequence, rather than hypothetical descendants or speculative population effects. Making currently living animals unable to reproduce can injure them by impairing an essential physical function. That differs from descendants never coming into existence, reduced prospects for future population growth alone, or regeneration of habitat not presently sustaining animals. She requires neither a carcass nor a single proof method, and does not equate every decline in reproductive success with actual injury. Her reservations about Palila's habitat-regeneration application concern proof of actual injury and remoteness; erroneous particular applications do not invalidate every lawful operation of the rule. She adopts no new general omission duty or criminal knowledge rule.

Justice Scalia dissents, joined by Justice Thomas. They would affirm invalidation of the challenged habitat clause, retaining other ESA protections. They read the active verbs and their wildlife object to confine take to conduct directed against animals; the separate habitat provisions confirm that distinction. The incidental-take provisions can address unintended injury arising from directed activities without necessarily authorizing regulation of indirect land-use effects. They accept that the majority states causal limits, but regard those limits as insufficient to establish delegated authority for this habitat rule. Potential omission and population effects and criminal fair-notice concerns reinforce their objection. They announce neither a constitutional property immunity nor a universal knowledge-of-illegality rule, and do not invalidate every part of the harm definition.

## Published noncontrolling positions; source: terms/OT1993/output/OT_1993CHUNK7.md

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia concurs, joined by Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg, while joining the Court's opinion in full. They would also hold that the general order independently fails §203(b)(2)'s special-circumstances-or-conditions requirement. Even assuming the modification power could reach the change, a substitute filing regime covering every long-distance carrier except AT&T and approximately forty percent of customers has the ordinary reach of sector regulation. Calling the category nondominant does not, in their view, establish the limiting condition for this order. They do not adopt a numerical cutoff or find that each nondominant carrier has market power; breadth is evidence of the order's regulatory character. They preserve general orders addressing genuine qualifying conditions and do not determine whether a smaller or differently conditioned waiver would qualify. The four Justices regard this statutory objection as sufficient by itself. It is not an alternative holding of the Court.

Justice Stevens dissents, joined by Blackmun and Souter. They would reverse the statutory-authority judgment and remand because the express power to modify any requirement, including by a general order for special conditions, reasonably accommodates the difference between dominant and competitive carriers. Nondominance can be special by character rather than by numerical rarity. The agency's actual competition rationale matters: mandatory advance public filing may burden competition without delivering the same protection against market power. The rule preserves voluntary filing, just-and-reasonable rates, nondiscrimination, complaint enforcement and the possibility of restoring filing obligations if conditions change. These features persuade the dissent that the Commission adjusted the statutory system rather than abandoned it. The 120-day ceiling limits an increased notice burden; it does not confine every relaxation to an insignificant detail. Boston & Maine supports contextual delegation, and the permissible constructions sustained in Health Care & Retirement and PUD No. 1 show why operative text and safeguards matter. Lechmere still requires respect for established statutory limits, but it does not resolve the scope of this express modification clause. The dissent rejects both the Court's modification ground and the concurrence's independent special-condition objection. Good cause, actual qualifying conditions and the agency's own reasons remain necessary; the dissent would not authorize elimination of §§201–202, sustain compulsory nonfiling, or adjudicate the separate complaint's monetary remedy.

## Published noncontrolling positions; source: terms/OT1999/output/OT_1999CHUNK4.md

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Stevens, dissenting, joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.** They would reverse the categorical authority judgment and remand. The structure-and-function definition reaches nicotine deliberately supplied for pharmacological effects, and the delivery-component definition can reach the apparatus used to supply it. Therapeutic advertising is not the only evidence of intended use. The earlier regulatory architecture at least permits that construction within the delegation recognized by Chevron, Sweet Home and Haggar.

They accept that restricted-device authority cannot erase misbranding, applicable approval or cessation duties. Their sufficient disagreement is that an obligation to remove a covered product, if established under the statute's own conditions, limits the FDA's permissible methods rather than proving that the product is outside the protective statute. They do not make section 360h(e)(1) elective merely because other remedies exist, and do not adopt a universal exception for withdrawal or substitute risks. The precise provision must permit consideration of those consequences.

The tobacco-specific statutes address their actual labeling, advertising, reporting and access subjects; they do not establish the categorical FDCA exclusion necessary to sustain the judgment. MCI's insistence on mandatory limits does not erase different, broad coverage language. Section 422 reserves the authority question and requires the earlier regime, supplying neither new power nor a new exemption. No new mission, classification shortcut or later review flexibility is used.

Their remand would address the actual combination-product method, particular restrictions and unresolved constitutional objections under ordinary procedures. It would not unconditionally reinstate the full rule, guarantee a lawful continuing tobacco market, order a ban or recall, or supply new agency findings. The statutory constraints remain enforceable at their own scope.
