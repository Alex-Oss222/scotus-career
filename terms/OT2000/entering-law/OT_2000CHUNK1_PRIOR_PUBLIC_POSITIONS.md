### Russell v. Burris and Citizens for Clean Government v. Russell, Nos. 98-397 and 98-399

Merits decision, April 28, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 22, 1999. On writs of certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, 146 F.3d 563. Review concerns Arkansas's candidate contribution ceilings, the annual ordinary-PAC receipt ceiling and the qualified small-donor PAC allowance. The distinct independent-expenditure-committee limit and local-authorizing provision are outside review.

#### Judgment

The judgment is affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded for conforming declaratory and injunctive proceedings.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Contribution limits require actual anticorruption fit; two candidate categories fail here

**Holding and operative rule:** Candidate contribution limits must be closely drawn to a sufficiently important interest in preventing corruption or its appearance while preserving meaningful political association. On this record the $300 per-person/per-election ceilings for Governor, Lieutenant Governor, Secretary of State, Treasurer of State, Auditor of State, Attorney General and Commissioner of State Lands, and the $100 ceilings for Arkansas Supreme Court Justice and Court of Appeals Judge, fail that requirement.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part I is unanimous. Her Parts II–IV control the three holdings that follow through the five-Justice coalition identified above.

**Controlling explanation:** Buckley distinguishes contributions from expenditures and allows prophylaxis without a completed bribe; it does not permit equality of political influence as an independent justification. Neither a dollar figure nor an inflation comparison establishes a permanent constitutional boundary. The statewide campaign scale and fundraising burden make the legislative examples inadequate to justify the enumerated $300 limits. The two appellate judicial offices face a distinct combined burden: large electoral areas, no personal solicitation or acceptance, and committee fundraising confined to 180 days before the primary through 45 days after the last contested election. The supported findings establish inadequate resources for effective advocacy under that combination. Suster recognizes judicial impartiality but does not ignore the practical restriction or decide a contribution case through expenditure rules. The Court invalidates neither the solicitation canon nor every judicial limit. Arkansas Supreme Court races belong to the statutory $100 category despite their statewide geography. Successful campaigns, self-financing and a small gift's relative share are relevant circumstances, not automatic answers.

##### The residual $100 restriction survives the tried categorical challenge

**Holding and operative rule:** The record does not establish the asserted categorical invalidity of the $100 per-person/per-election ceiling for covered offices outside the seven-office $300 list and the two named appellate judicial offices. This residual class includes other covered judicial offices; rejection of the blanket challenge does not decide a genuinely different office-specific application without its own presented grounds and record.

**Controlling explanation:** The trial evidence included perceived influence from concentrated contributions, the Maddox fundraiser and tobacco-related giving, even though actual improper access or undue influence was not found. Candidate agreement with donors is not itself corruption. The actual special-election experience, reported $24,000 and $40,000 Senate fundraising and resetting of limits for each election support the State's assessment in the demonstrated legislative setting. Against that stand professional fundraisers' contrary testimony, sharp fundraising changes and effects on up to eighty percent of earlier legislative contributions. The Court finds the practical evidence sufficient for this tried challenge without requiring campaign impossibility or equating hypothetical donor counts with actual recruitment. Kentucky Right to Life requires analysis of the actual contribution channel, not automatic invalidation from the amount. The two appellate offices' cumulative burden cannot be imputed to every judge merely because solicitation restrictions are shared. Nor are legislative successes findings about every other office. The decision rejects the grounds offered for blanket invalidation; it orders no universal office survey and invents no waiver or absent distinct claim.

##### The ordinary-PAC receipt limit survives on this operational record

**Holding and operative rule:** The $200 annual limit from one person to any one ordinary PAC survives the presented challenge where the tried operational record preserves effective collective advocacy and the restriction is fitted to the actual candidate-conduit channel. It is a per-recipient limit, not one aggregate pool across all committees, and supplies no general approval for materially different recipient functions or demonstrated pooling burdens.

**Controlling explanation:** Day protects meaningful political pooling and requires a real fit inquiry. Here years of operation and robust candidate-directed PAC activity support the lower findings of no appreciable impairment in the tried case. Ordinary Arkansas PACs lack the federal multicandidate qualifications of at least fifty contributors and contributions to at least five candidates, so the candidate-transfer channel gives the circumvention concern concrete work. Kentucky Right to Life's aggregate-pool problem is distinct. Annual duration, recipient function, actual operation and amount must be considered together. Supporting other committees does not alone cure a burden on the chosen association, and aggregate success is not an automatic safe harbor. The combination established here nevertheless supports the restriction. California Medical permits genuine conduit regulation without making its fractured lowest-protection rationale a universal rule. A different operational burden or independent recipient function remains open.

##### The exact small-donor qualification supports a different candidate allowance

**Holding and operative rule:** Arkansas may permit a qualifying registered small-donor PAC to give $2,500 per candidate per election while ordinary sources face lower ceilings, where that committee receives contributions exclusively from individuals and no more than $25 cumulatively from any individual during a calendar year. The financial distinction is justified by the anticorruption interest and fit of the dispersed-donor structure; it is neither a viewpoint preference nor a rule that any small-donor label suffices.

**Controlling explanation:** Annual individual aggregation disperses financing and attenuates concentrated donor control; ordinary PACs may receive larger individual amounts and entity contributions. Those differences support differentiated treatment under the asserted compelling anticorruption interest and the required fit of this exact classification; the ruling does not rest on rational-basis review. A committee's $2,500 transfer can still create recipient-end influence, the principal contrary argument. The Court acknowledges that risk but finds this precise qualification sufficient to distinguish the financing channels without requiring identical caps. It does not find small-donor committees incapable of corruption, authorize unlimited candidate giving or rely merely on the possibility of forming a different association. California Medical recognizes that financing structures can matter; Day preserves actual association rather than nominal permission. The $25 condition is annual cumulative intake, not a repeatable allowance per gift. The $2,500 figure governs outgoing candidate contributions, not committee receipts. Every qualification remains operative; sham or materially different structures gain no adjudicated protection.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Buckley v. Valeo preserves contribution/expenditure distinctions, sufficient interest and close fit without a permanent dollar floor.
- Day v. Holahan protects actual pooling and requires a supported circumvention fit; the Court distinguishes its burden record rather than overruling it.
- Kentucky Right to Life preserves separate direct-contribution, aggregate-pool and sourcewide-receipt analyses and their actual claim limits.
- California Medical Association v. FEC supports actual candidate-conduit regulation and attention to financing structures; its fractured lowest-protection rationale is not a universal rule.
- Colorado Republican and Clifton preserve genuine independence and require actual candidate connections; a committee label bypasses neither protection.
- Suster preserves judicial impartiality while distinguishing expenditures from contributions and requiring examination of the actual restraint.
- Reform Party and Brower reject resource-parity entitlements while leaving practical prohibitory burdens reviewable.

#### Law After Decision

The seven enumerated $300 limits and two named appellate-office $100 applications remain unenforceable. The residual $100 categorical challenge and challenges to the ordinary-PAC receipt cap and exact small-donor differential fail on this record. The residual category includes other covered judicial offices without new findings about every such race. Contribution and expenditure rules, meaningful association and fact-specific future claims retain their distinct requirements. No precedent is overruled.

#### Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela agrees with the two invalidated candidate categories but would invalidate the remaining $100 and ordinary-PAC $200 limits as well. Buckley permits prevention of corruption and its appearance, but the severe restrictions on effective pooling require a demonstrated connection to the actual corruption or circumvention risk. He finds that fit lacking and treats the PAC receipt burden as arising before any contribution to a candidate. He proposes no permanent dollar minimum. On the small-donor allowance he would vacate and remand rather than validate or invalidate it now: actual donor aggregation, control, earmarking, operation and safeguards, together with any consequent severability issue, must be examined. Diffuse financing can justify distinction, but neither its label nor nominal inequality decides the question.

Justice Kennedy, joined by Justices Scalia and Thomas, would affirm all invalidations. Their Day and Kentucky Right to Life grounds distinguish three defects: the residual candidate cap's substantial fundraising burden is not sufficiently answered by appearance evidence and limited successful campaigns; the annual chosen-PAC receipt restraint reaches beyond the identified conduit problem; and a committee can induce candidate obligation through a $2,500 transfer despite diffuse intake. They preserve tailored conduit and earmark regulation and do not demand completed bribery, expenditure scrutiny for candidate cash, identical source ceilings or a permanent dollar floor.

#### Source Notes

The challenged provisions and record appear in [Russell v. Burris, 146 F.3d 563](https://ecf.ca8.uscourts.gov/opndir/98/06/973922P.pdf) and [978 F. Supp. 1211](https://openjurist.org/978/fsupp/1211). The trial's judicial exception names Supreme Court Justice and Court of Appeals Judge specifically; its legislative and PAC findings are not converted into findings about every possible office or committee.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The lower courts must conform declaratory and injunctive relief to the component judgments and implement actual continuing statutory relationships under the existing severability framework. The Court selects no substitute ceiling, orders no refund or damages, and directs no retrospective enforcement or automatic new trial. The separate $500 independent-expenditure-committee restriction and local-authorizing provision receive no merits disposition.

**End of entry: Russell v. Burris and Citizens for Clean Government v. Russell, Merits decision, April 28, 1999.**

### Nixon v. Shrink Missouri Government PAC, No. 98-963

Merits decision, January 24, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit; argued October 5, 1999. The challenge concerns Missouri's $1,075 statewide/state-auditor contribution ceiling. The PAC gave Fredman's committee $1,025 in 1997 and $50 in 1998 and wished to give more; Fredman asserted that larger gifts were necessary for effective campaigning. The District Court sustained the ceiling on cross-motions for summary judgment. The Eighth Circuit enjoined enforcement pending appeal, 151 F.3d 763, and then reversed the merits judgment, 161 F.3d 519. The question is whether the State has justified the presented ceiling under the contribution rule established in Russell.

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, who would affirm the presented invalidation.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice O'Connor delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those six Justices adopt the holding, its explanation and limits, and the bounded mandate. Justice Kennedy dissents, joined in full by Justices Scalia and Thomas; their dissent would affirm the presented invalidation on its record-specific close-fit ground.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The presented indexed ceiling satisfies the existing contribution standard on this record

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Russell, government must justify a direct candidate-contribution ceiling by an objectively grounded, sufficiently important corruption or appearance interest and a close fit that preserves meaningful association and effective campaign advocacy. Missouri meets that burden for the presented $1,075 statewide/state-auditor ceiling, indexed biennially from the January 1, 1995 $1,000 base and rounded to the nearest $25; this record-specific ruling establishes no dollar floor, safe harbor, other-office approval or immunity from a future materially different suppression challenge.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Missouri's combined risk and operational evidence justifies this ceiling under Russell. Goode's account, the reported $20,000 bank contribution and related official banking choice, $40,000 brewery and $20,000 bank gifts in the auditor contest, a $420,000 PAC contribution and reported sponsorship-for-kickback accusations ground a concern about official obligation and its appearance. Accusations remain accusations, and the property-related conspiracy plea proves its actual offense only. The Court does not find completed exchanges in every episode, require a proved bribe, or treat ordinary donor agreement, access or resource inequality as sufficient. Proposition A's 74-percent approval supplies context, not constitutional authority.

The District Court's affirmative findings of effective campaign capacity and substantial fundraising, the direct candidate channel, amount and indexing support the practical fit. The contrary statewide figures are serious: combined spending fell from $21.599 million to $9.337 million, primary spending from $14.249 million to $1.625 million, contested primaries from ten to one, and candidates from thirty-two to eleven. Those changes support the burden objection but do not establish this ceiling as the cause of each decline or displace the supported capacity finding for the presented application. The record supplies no finding of an alternative cause, and no decline is treated as harmless. The figure that 97.62 percent of contributors gave no more than $2,000 measures neither contributed dollars nor the effect of a $1,075 ceiling.

Missouri's affirmative combined showing meets its burden; the test is not whether challengers prove literal campaign impossibility. Russell invalidated materially lower statewide restrictions on their actual record while rejecting a universal monetary boundary. Buckley's contribution/expenditure distinction remains, but a generic appeal to deference or nominal dollar comparison cannot replace Russell's fit inquiry. The Court therefore reverses the presented invalidation, leaving materially different burdens and unchallenged tiers unresolved.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Russell v. Burris (April 28, 1999): applies its sufficient-interest, close-fit and meaningful-association rule and its government burden. Its invalidation of the named statewide $300 and appellate-office $100 ceilings remains effective; neither those outcomes nor its sustained residual/PAC applications establish a monetary floor or automatic analogy to this category.
- Buckley v. Valeo: retains the distinction between candidate contributions and independent expenditures, but does not replace Russell with a presumption of validity or a fixed federal-dollar benchmark.
- Day v. Holahan (May 15, 1995): preserves meaningful pooling and actual circumvention fit for intermediary receipt restrictions; other associations or aggregate political success do not automatically cure a burden on the chosen association.
- Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. FEC (June 26, 1996): preserves genuinely independent party expression and its actual-connection inquiry; it does not immunize direct candidate cash or decide this ceiling.

#### Law After Decision

Russell remains the governing test, with government carrying the burden of objective justification and close fit preserving meaningful political association and effective advocacy. The presented $1,075 ceiling satisfies that test on the combined risk and practical-capacity record despite substantial contrary spending and competition evidence. Neither a nominal comparison, a general presumption of validity, the contributor-count statistic nor absence of a completed bribe controls. No contribution ceiling receives a permanent safe harbor, and no other office tier or materially different practical-suppression record is decided.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Kennedy, dissenting, joined by Justices Scalia and Thomas.**

The dissent accepts Russell's close-fit standard and the sufficiently important anticorruption interest, but would affirm invalidation of this presented statewide ceiling. The large-gift episodes support some prophylaxis without adequately justifying this amount. The severe statewide contraction in funding, contested primaries and candidates, together with campaign scale, requires a stronger operational answer than general findings of campaign capacity, indexing or examples of successful financing. The contributor-count statistic does not establish the amount of funding lost, and a ceiling's being higher than Arkansas's invalid limits does not establish its fit.

The dissent does not treat the comparative trends as conclusive proof of causation, demand an exact optimal amount or completed bribe, dismiss the corruption evidence, or adopt expenditure-level strict scrutiny for all direct contributions. Kennedy's Russell separate opinion, joined by Scalia and Thomas, preserves tailored conduit and earmark regulation and meaningful association while rejecting inadequately fitted burdens. That ground supports invalidating this limit on this record without overruling Buckley, ordering a replacement ceiling or recognizing unlimited candidate giving.

#### Source Notes

The official report at [528 U.S. 377](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-528/pdf/USREPORTS-528-377.pdf) supplies the dates, lower-court posture, statutory amount and indexing, evidentiary recitations and comparative campaign figures. Pages 383–384 distinguish the injunction pending appeal from the later merits judgment; they do not establish a separate permanent injunction. Published descriptions of gifts, accusations and a property-related conspiracy plea retain their respective evidentiary status. The campaign figures rest on published recitations of the datasets and Appendix; they do not themselves establish why every decline occurred.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Court reverses the Eighth Circuit's constitutional invalidation of the presented $1,075 statewide/state-auditor ceiling. It vacates its injunction against enforcement of that ceiling, insofar as the pending-appeal restraint remains operative, and remands for judgment for the state officials on this presented challenge. No unchallenged office tier, retrospective enforcement liability, refund, damages or replacement amount is decided; a future challenge on materially different evidence of practical suppression remains open. The remand requires no new factual study or replacement contribution amount. Conditional vacatur does not determine that the pending-appeal restraint remains in force.
