### Gasperini v. Center for Humanities, Inc., No. 95-719

Decision, June 24, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 16, 1996; decided June 24, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit's September 12, 1995 judgment, 66 F.3d 427, in a diversity action in the Southern District of New York.

The Center conceded liability for 300 lost original photographic transparencies. A June 1994 jury awarded Gasperini $450,000, reflecting $1,500 per transparency. The district judge denied the Rule 59 new-trial motion without explanation. Applying New York's material-deviation standard, the Second Circuit ordered a new trial unless Gasperini accepted $100,000. No acceptance is established.

The questions are whether the state compensation standard governs, how Rule 59 operates with it, and what appellate review the Seventh Amendment permits. The appellate order is conditional, not an unconditional replacement judgment. The separate ruling allowing industry-standard evidence with other proof does not make $1,500 per transparency conclusive and is not overturned here.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens would affirm the existing conditional new-trial/$100,000 order; Scalia and Thomas would reverse that order and restore the district judgment against the reviewed excessiveness challenge.

The district judge must apply the state standard through Rule 59; appellate review is for abuse of discretion within the limits below. The Court selects neither $450,000 nor $100,000, orders no automatic new trial, and makes no independent findings about uniqueness or earning prospects.

#### Holdings

##### I. New York's compensation standard applies through the federal new-trial function

**Operative rule:** In this diversity action governed by New York damages law, the federal district court must assess excessiveness under CPLR section 5501(c)'s material-deviation measure through Rule 59. The state compensation constraint applies without importing the State's appellate hierarchy, displacing federal new-trial procedure, creating a fixed damages cap or authorizing federal additur.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven Justices adopt this rule and explanation. Stevens does not join Part II's constitutional allocation or remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Erie and Guaranty Trust require respect for the State's substantive recovery limits in diversity. Allowing materially different compensation merely because federal jurisdiction is available would invite forum selection for a larger award and unequal administration of the same state claim. This is more than a preference for a particular reviewing court.

Hanna first requires asking whether the Federal Rule actually governs the disputed point. Rule 59 permits new trials for reasons historically recognized in federal actions at law. Excessiveness is such a reason; that text does not prescribe an immutable substantive degree of excessiveness independent of the law governing compensation. Applying New York's measure therefore preserves the federal motion and trial function rather than overriding a conflicting Rule.

Section 5501(c) directs the Appellate Division, in its specified review of a money judgment following an itemized verdict, to find excessiveness or inadequacy when the award materially deviates from reasonable compensation; the context is a request for a new trial unless a different award is stipulated. Although the enacted direction names the appellate court, Inya, Cochetti and Shurgan establish trial application of the same measure under New York law. Those decisions preserve sparingly exercised trial discretion. They do not compel one remedy for every claimed deviation or establish a photographic price schedule.

The contrary view treats the measure as a standard of judicial reassessment already occupied by Rule 59. But the traditional federal ground and the State's compensation constraint can perform their separate work. Browning-Ferris recognizes state damages confines alongside federal new-trial procedure; its reserved appellate constitutional question requires the separate analysis in Part II. Byrd preserves federal allocation of functions. The Court does not transplant every outcome-affecting state practice, the State's reviewing hierarchy or its power of conditional increases.

##### II. The district judge applies that measure; appellate review must remain genuinely deferential

**Operative rule:** The district judge must exercise the federal new-trial function using the state measure, while the court of appeals reviews that determination for abuse of discretion, independently correcting legal error and deferring to the district judge's supported case-specific assessment. The Seventh Amendment permits that review of trial-court discretion but does not permit the appellate court to substitute its own first-instance valuation of the jury's compensatory award; this judgment must be vacated for district reconsideration under the proper allocation.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer; six Justices adopt the rule, application and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Reexamination Clause protects jury-tried facts while preserving the traditional judicial power to grant a new trial. The district judge's trial experience and recognized new-trial responsibility place the state-standard assessment there. Byrd requires preserving the federal division of functions even when state procedure affects outcomes.

Appellate examination of whether that authority was lawfully exercised can coexist with the protected jury function when it genuinely reviews discretion. It must correct an erroneous legal measure, but may not use the abuse label to perform a fresh valuation from the cold record. This resolves, within that limit, the constitutional appellate question Browning-Ferris expressly reserved. The contrary historical objection properly demands more than a change of labels; the required restraint preserves the distinction between reviewing the district court's exercise of new-trial power and independently retrying compensation.

The district denial supplies no demonstrated application of New York's measure. That absence is not a finding that the judge actually used a particular wrong formula. The Second Circuit's first operative valuation cannot substitute for the assigned district assessment. Its use of favorable inferences and a conditional remittitur does not remove that allocation problem. Another district consideration therefore remains necessary.

On remand the judge must examine the record under the state standard, informed by pertinent New York decisions, and select lawful relief. Comparable cases are guidance, not fixed prices. Neither the jury's $450,000 nor the appellate $100,000 is declared correct. Donovan's accepted-remittitur bar does not apply because no acceptance is established. If the district court orders remittitur, Gasperini must have the choice of accepting the reduced award or a new jury trial; the court may not simply substitute a lower judgment. This differs from the additur barred by Dimick. No automatic retrial or imported state conditional increase follows.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64, and Guaranty Trust Co. v. York, 326 U.S. 99:** Apply respect for substantive state recovery limits in diversity without making every outcome-affecting procedural rule binding.
- **Hanna v. Plumer, 380 U.S. 460:** Applies the Federal Rule coverage inquiry before an unguided choice of law. Rule 59's traditional new-trial ground coexists here with the state compensation measure.
- **Byrd v. Blue Ridge Rural Electric Cooperative, Inc., 356 U.S. 525:** Preserves federal institutional allocation rather than requiring reproduction of the state reviewing hierarchy.
- **Browning-Ferris Industries of Vermont, Inc. v. Kelco Disposal, Inc., 492 U.S. 257:** Applies its distinction between state damages constraints and federal new-trial procedure. Its express reservation of the Seventh Amendment appellate question is resolved here only by the bounded abuse-of-discretion rule.
- **Donovan v. Penn Shipping Co., 429 U.S. 648:** Preserves federal procedural control and the bar on appealing an accepted remittitur. The absence of acceptance here distinguishes that bar.
- **Dimick v. Schiedt, 293 U.S. 474:** Preserves traditional remittitur and the prohibition on federal additur. State conditional increases do not alter that distinction.
- **Honda Motor Co. v. Oberg, June 24, 1994, and BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore, May 20, 1996:** Preserve their distinct punitive-damages protections. Neither supplies a compensatory formula, mandatory number of reviewing courts or answer to this federal reexamination question.
- **Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc., April 23, 1996:** Preserves function-specific jury allocation and its reservation of subsidiary appellate review; patent construction does not dictate this compensation review.
- **Thompson v. Keohane, November 29, 1995; Ornelas v. United States, May 28, 1996; and Koon v. United States, June 13, 1996:** Retain their particular legal/factual review standards. No universal independent review of mixed issues or separate Seventh Amendment holding is inferred from them.

No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

New York's material-deviation measure governs compensation review in this diversity action through the district judge's Rule 59 authority. The court of appeals may correct legal error and review for abuse of discretion, but must respect the district judge's case-specific assessment and may not make its own first operative valuation.

The state substantive constraint and federal allocation both retain force. There is no universal rule importing state appellate procedure, no automatic damages cap, and no change to federal review of an independently presented constitutional limit on damages. The state measure does not itself decide which amount is reasonable here or compel a particular form of relief.

#### Separate Writings

Justice Stevens joins Part I but dissents from Part II and the judgment. He agrees that the New York compensation constraint applies and that Rule 59 does not displace it. He would affirm the Second Circuit's existing order because, in his view, it enforced a legal outer boundary while drawing reasonable factual inferences for Gasperini. A court can correct compensation beyond the legal limit without impermissibly retrying a fact.

Stevens rejects a Seventh Amendment requirement of the Court's particular appellate deference. His common-law account preserves the essentials of jury protection without fixing every historical institutional form; practical respect for the district judge does not create a constitutional bar to this legal correction. The district court already had an opportunity to review the award, and he identifies no permissible different result requiring another pass. His affirmance would retain Gasperini's choice between a new trial and accepting $100,000, not impose an unconditional replacement judgment. He does not convert Markman into a general appellate-review holding or BMW into compensatory-damages law.

Justice Scalia dissents, joined by Justice Thomas. Their first independent objection is that Rule 59 governs the federal degree of judicial excessiveness review through its reference to historical federal practice. Under Hanna, Erie policies cannot displace a Federal Rule that governs the point. They acknowledge the State's trial-court application of section 5501(c), but classify that measure as judicial reassessment rather than a substantive cap. Genuine state damages elements, categories and actual statutory ceilings remain binding; the dissent does not insist upon a single verbal federal excessiveness formula.

Their second independent objection is historical: appellate correction of legal error differs from reassessing compensation facts or evidentiary weight. Neither an abuse-of-discretion label nor a conditional new-trial option makes prohibited appellate factual reexamination permissible. Browning-Ferris expressly reserved that constitutional issue; Honda's punitive procedure and the other modern allocations do not decide it.

Scalia and Thomas retain traditional trial-court new-trial power and conditional remittitur, distinguish additur, and preserve ordinary correction of genuine legal insufficiency. They would reverse the appellate conditional-remittitur order and restore the district judgment against this excessiveness challenge, without computing a different award. No independent reversible industry-evidence error is established.

#### Source Notes

The [Second Circuit opinion, 66 F.3d 427](https://static.case.law/f3d/66/html/0427-01.html), supplies the verdict, unexplained district ruling, conditional appellate order, separate evidence ruling and the material-deviation language in the itemized-verdict context. Its assessments of uniqueness and earning prospects are the appellate analysis under review, not findings newly made by this Court.

[Inya v. Ide Hyundai, 209 A.D.2d 1015](https://static.case.law/ad2d/209/html/1015-01.html), [Cochetti v. Gralow, 192 A.D.2d 974](https://static.case.law/ad2d/192/html/0974-01.html), and [Shurgan v. Tedesco, 179 A.D.2d 805](https://static.case.law/ad2d/179/html/0805-02.html), establish state trial application of the measure with sparingly exercised discretion. Their personal-injury amounts are not valuation evidence for these transparencies, and their state remedies do not authorize federal additur.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Court of Appeals with directions for district reconsideration of the Rule 59 motion under New York's material-deviation standard. The district court must perform that assessment and select any legally available relief; a later appeal is subject to the bounded abuse-of-discretion rule.

The Court neither directs a new jury trial now nor reinstates the $450,000 as substantively correct. It does not approve the $100,000 limit, find how many transparencies were unique, or infer acceptance of remittitur. The distinct industry-evidence ruling remains undisturbed. No new liability issue, waiver, damages tariff or further Supreme Court event is prescribed.

### Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp., No. 93-1525

Merits decision, February 21, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 7, 1994; decided February 21, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit, reviewing 12 F.3d 388 (1993), which reversed the district judgment, 811 F. Supp. 993, on the absence of governmental action and directed dismissal. The question is whether Amtrak is a governmental actor for First Amendment purposes despite its statutory disclaimer. Michael Lebron challenged Amtrak's refusal to display his political photomontage on the Spectacular billboard at Penn Station. The First Amendment claim remained before the lower courts although he expressly disavowed treating Amtrak itself as a governmental entity and relied instead on attributing the challenged conduct to government.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated at argument and decision. No Justice is noted as not participating.

#### Judgment

The Second Circuit judgment is reversed and the case remanded, 8–1, for adjudication of the First Amendment claim treating Amtrak as governmental. The Court does not decide whether rejection of the advertisement violated the First Amendment or order its display.

| Second Circuit judgment rejecting governmental action and dismissing the complaint | Reversed and remanded, 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | O'Connor | Consider the unaddressed First Amendment merits and lawful relief with Amtrak treated as governmental; reconsider the associated state-law dispositions consistently with that restoration of the federal claim. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The Court may consider this included legal argument despite its earlier disavowal

**Controlling proposition:** The Court elects to consider Amtrak's governmental-entity status despite Lebron's earlier express disavowal of that argument because it supplies a legal basis for the same preserved First Amendment claim, falls within the governmental-actor question presented, and can be resolved on the established institutional record without a new factual theory or additional findings. This bounded exercise of review creates no obligation to consider every abandoned argument and does not dispense with limits on distinct claims, questions, requested relief, factual stipulations, or missing material facts.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's reachability portion, joined by Justices Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Seven Justices adopt the stated discretionary-review rationale; Chief Justice Stone-Zsela reaches the question but does not join this portion, and Justice O'Connor declines the argument as a prudential matter.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the earlier litigation choice deserves consideration but does not require refusal of this legal argument. Yee separates a new supporting argument from a distinct claim and independently enforces the question presented. Lebron's injury, constitutional claim, rejected advertisement, and requested relief remain the same. The question before the Court expressly concerns governmental status despite the statutory disclaimer, and the established corporate structure permits its resolution without new factual findings. Bray withheld a separate statutory claim outside the granted dispute and lacking a necessary finding; those defects are absent here. Deliberate disavowal is more substantial than mere omission, but the Court exercises its authority to decide this included legal basis on the present record. The appellate reliance on Amtrak's private status makes that premise relevant without establishing a rule that any passing discussion preserves every argument. No actual adversarial prejudice or broader concession is found, and the Court imposes no burden on the respondent to prove prejudice.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Yee v. City of Escondido, 503 U.S. 519 (1992): applies its distinction between a supporting argument and a new claim, together with its independent question-presented limit; the present decision separately addresses deliberate disavowal in this setting.
- Bray v. Alexandria Women's Health Clinic, 506 U.S. 263 (1993): distinguishes the unadjudicated separate hindrance theory, outside the granted questions and lacking a necessary finding; its scope limits remain operative.
- Bankers Life & Casualty Co. v. Crenshaw, 486 U.S. 71 (1988): applies, through Yee, the allowance of supporting arguments for a preserved federal claim, without erasing this Court's discretion over deliberately excluded grounds.
- Kamen v. Kemper Financial Services, Inc., 500 U.S. 90 (1991): preserves the distinction between legal arguments and the claim to which they relate; no unrelated claim is introduced.
- United States v. Mezzanatto, decided January 18, 1995: distinguishes the actual, limited evidentiary agreement there from this decision whether to consider an included argument; its waiver rule does not make every litigation choice an irrevocable surrender of an entire constitutional claim.

##### Special federal creation and permanent majority appointment make Amtrak governmental

**Controlling proposition:** When the Federal Government creates a corporation by special law to advance governmental objectives and permanently retains authority to appoint a majority of its governing directors, the corporation is governmental for First Amendment purposes notwithstanding a statutory declaration that it is not a governmental agency. Amtrak satisfies those combined conditions; ordinary operating discretion in its corporate officers does not require a separate external federal command for each challenged act.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's constitutional-status portion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Eight Justices directly support the same permanent-majority-appointment rule and its application; Justice O'Connor's conditional view of a question she declines to reach is not a join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress cannot settle constitutional responsibility simply by choosing a corporate label. Amtrak was specially created to pursue national transportation objectives, and the statutory arrangement permanently retains governmental authority over selection of its governing majority. The Secretary's position, the five other direct Presidential selections, and the preferred shareholder's governmental selections must not be mistaken for nine direct Presidential appointments. The different routes nevertheless establish the required enduring majority authority; the corporate president's selection within the institution does not defeat it. Congress's nonagency classification keeps its statutory operation, but cannot place an institution created and governed in this manner beyond the Constitution. San Francisco Arts & Athletics distinguishes a federally chartered and assisted private corporation; charter, subsidy, or regulation alone is not the ground here. Nor does delegation of everyday decisions to corporate officers make this institution private. An external federal order to reject Lebron's work is therefore unnecessary. The Court decides governmental status, leaving the advertising policy's constitutionality and the suitable remedy for later adjudication.

**Precedent treatment:**

- San Francisco Arts & Athletics, Inc. v. United States Olympic Committee, 483 U.S. 522 (1987): distinguishes federal charter and assistance to a private institution from special creation coupled with permanent governmental majority-appointment authority.
- Edmonson v. Leesville Concrete Co., 500 U.S. 614 (1991), and Georgia v. McCollum, 505 U.S. 42 (1992): preserve attention to actual governmental legal machinery in their jury-selection settings; neither makes billboard advertising an exclusively governmental function or supplies a general rule for all publicly benefited corporations.
- Jackson v. Metropolitan Edison Co., 419 U.S. 345 (1974), and Blum v. Yaretsky, 457 U.S. 991 (1982): distinguish attribution of a private entity's particular act from identifying an institution that is itself governmental under the combined rule.
- Hess v. Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp., decided November 14, 1994: keeps the distinct inquiry into an entity's state sovereign immunity separate; absence of immunity does not imply constitutional privacy.
- American National Red Cross v. S. G., 505 U.S. 247 (1992): preserves its express charter-jurisdiction rule, which supplies no constitutional actor classification.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Federal charter, subsidy, regulation, governmental property, public benefit, a single appointment, temporary investment, or merely the current ownership of shares is not independently sufficient under this rule. Permanent authority to appoint the governing majority, governmental objectives, and special governmental creation must be present together. The Court decides no universal agency status, sovereign immunity, Treasury obligation, federal tort-liability rule, or charter jurisdiction. The 1994 recodification from former 45 U.S.C. §541 to 49 U.S.C. §24301(a) does not itself adjudicate constitutional status. The statutory board includes the Secretary of Transportation; Amtrak's president; three Presidentially appointed, Senate-confirmed directors representing the statutory labor, state-executive, and business categories; two Presidential selections from commuter-authority nominees under the statute; and two preferred-shareholder selections. The governmental preferred shareholder supplies those last two selections on this record. Neither the entire board's consideration of this particular refusal nor a new direct federal order to reject it is found.

##### Governmental status requires a remand, not an immediate display order

**Controlling proposition:** Reversal of the Second Circuit's governmental-action ground restores the First Amendment claim for adjudication with Amtrak treated as governmental, but does not establish that this rejection was unconstitutional or that Lebron is entitled to mandatory display. The lower courts must address the unreviewed speech and forum questions, lawful relief, and the related disposition of the contract claim and TDI counterclaim under their own governing rules; the district display injunction is not automatically reinstated.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's remand portion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Eight Justices adopt the same threshold reversal and limited remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that governmental responsibility identifies the institution's constitutional duties without deciding whether the challenged restriction violates them. The Court of Appeals stopped at attribution and did not adjudicate the First Amendment merits. The district court's contrary decision and mandatory-display order therefore cannot be restored merely by correcting that threshold. The character of the advertising forum, the grounds and operation of the speech restriction, and the conditions for equitable relief remain separate questions. Young preserves the distinction between the existence of substantial federal jurisdiction and the legal defeat of a federal claim; restoration of the claim accordingly requires reconsideration of associated state-law dispositions without prescribing their answers. Tome illustrates only the limited principle that correcting one ground does not automatically establish ultimate relief; its hearsay and criminal-error rules do not govern this civil remand. No damages amount, contract entitlement, forum classification, or validity of a substitute advertising policy is determined here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Young v. Northern Illinois Conference of United Methodist Church, decided January 17, 1995: applies the distinction between statutory jurisdiction and legal defeat on the federal merits without extending its ministerial-selection defense.
- Tome v. United States, decided January 10, 1995: uses only its separation of threshold legal error from automatic ultimate relief by analogy; no evidentiary or criminal harmless-error standard is transferred.

**Remedial limits:** The Court awards no display, damages, final contractual relief, or adjudication of TDI's requested declaration permitting termination. The Second Circuit's dismissal directions must be reconsidered consistently with the restored federal claim and ordinary law governing the separate state-law issues. The lower courts may not treat this actor-status ruling as a merits judgment approving the advertisement or invalidating every political-advertising policy.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Yee and Bray preserve the distinction between a legal supporting argument, a separate claim, and the actual question presented; the Court exercises bounded discretion over this deliberately disavowed argument. San Francisco Arts & Athletics, Jackson, and Blum retain their private-actor scope. Edmonson and McCollum concern governmental jury machinery. Hess addresses immunity and Red Cross charter jurisdiction, neither of which settles constitutional actor status. Young and Tome support only the stated separation of thresholds, merits, and relief. No earlier holding is overruled.

Effective February 21, 1995, special federal creation for governmental objectives combined with permanent federal authority to appoint a governing majority establishes First Amendment governmental status despite a statutory nonagency label. That rule governs Amtrak on this record without requiring an external governmental command for the particular refusal. The decision also permits consideration of this included legal argument despite its earlier disavowal on the stated claim, question, and record conditions, without a universal rule requiring review of abandoned arguments. The advertising merits and final remedies remain unresolved.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice O'Connor would affirm. Lebron preserved his First Amendment claim, but deliberately excluded the theory that Amtrak itself was a governmental entity in his presentations below. The express governmental-actor question here permits consideration of that theory; it does not require the Court to disregard the litigant's earlier choice. Her objection is prudential. She does not claim that the Court lacks jurisdiction, that the entire constitutional claim was waived, or that entity status falls outside the question presented. Nor does she find an additional concession or actual reliance prejudice on a record that establishes neither.

She would decide the conduct-attribution theory on the private-entity premise on which it was litigated. Jackson and Blum require a supported governmental connection to a private actor's particular choice, while her Edmonson dissent distinguishes such choice from governmental assistance. The reported funding, property, stockholding, and board relationships do not establish an external governmental direction causing this refusal or an exclusively governmental advertising function. The political subject of the artwork does not itself supply the missing connection. On that limited path she would leave the appellate rejection of the federal claim and disposition of the unresolved state-law matters in place. She does not convert the failure of the federal theory into a universal jurisdictional dismissal rule.

O'Connor recognizes that the permanent public governance would support the Court's structural conclusion if that alternative were reached. That conditional agreement supplies no vote for a holding she declines to adjudicate and no judgment vote for reversal. Even an affirmative actor ruling, she explains, would not itself establish the right to the district court's mandatory-display injunction; forum, speech, and equitable questions would still require decision.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The mandate reverses the Second Circuit judgment and remands for proceedings consistent with Amtrak's governmental status for First Amendment purposes. The lower courts must decide the unresolved constitutional and remedial issues and reconsider the associated state-law dispositions under ordinary law. The Court does not reinstate the mandatory-display injunction, set a display deadline, award damages, determine a rent amount, or resolve either side's final contract rights. Review here ends; proceedings continue below.

### American Manufacturers Mutual Insurance Co. v. Sullivan, No. 97-2000

Merits decision, March 3, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 19, 1999; decided March 3, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, 139 F.3d 158. The questions concern attribution of private insurers' initial withholding pending utilization review and an asserted constitutional right to payment before the reasonable-and-necessary character of disputed treatment is established.

The action includes private carriers and distinct public parties. The proposed class was not certified. The additional notice and employee-statement procedures were excluded from petitioners' challenge; individual treatment, prior-order and public-party issues must not be conflated with the two questions presented.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Statutory permission does not itself make the private carrier's payment choice governmental

**Holding and operative rule.** These private carriers' independent decisions to dispute treatment and withhold payment pending utilization review are not state action merely because state law permits that course, requires compensation coverage and supplies review machinery. Attribution requires an actual governmental basis for responsibility for the challenged choice, such as compulsion, significant encouragement, joint participation or a function exclusively reserved to government; those predicates are not established for this initial withholding.

**Authority.** The seven-Justice coalition for Kennedy's Part I, identified above, controls this holding.

**Controlling explanation.** Blum and Rendell-Baker distinguish regulation and public support from responsibility for a particular private decision. Lugar requires both the relevant state-created framework and sufficient state involvement; invoking the first does not dispense with the second. Bureau screening for proper invocation and assignment of a reviewer do not substantively approve the carrier's medical objection or order its initial suspension. Later adjudication is a different act.

Edmonson involved private participation in constituting a governmental jury; workers' compensation insurers do not perform that exclusively governmental function. Lebron prevents government from escaping its own constitutional obligations through an institution it creates and permanently controls, but these carriers do not satisfy that institutional-status ground. Mandatory coverage and a statutory source for compensation do not establish the required connection to their particular withholding decision.

This conclusion does not immunize the legislature's own acts or every decision in the public review process. Denver Area requires attention to the actual challenged governmental act, and Tulsa likewise does not dispense with significant governmental involvement merely because a statutory procedure is not self-executing. Actual public obligors, official review decisions or a distinct proved attribution basis require their own analysis. The Court decides only the private-carrier theory supported here.

##### General eligibility does not establish an unconditional right to advance payment of every disputed service

**Holding and operative rule.** General eligibility for workers' compensation does not by itself establish an unconditional constitutional right to constant payment of every disputed medical service before its reasonable-and-necessary character is established. This rule neither extinguishes a protected claim to fair adjudication nor decides against a claimant an existing service-specific entitlement, operative prior award or legally sufficient individual expectation of continued payment merely because an insurer invokes review.

**Authority.** The eight-Justice coalition for Kennedy's Part II, identified above, controls this holding. Stevens supports the payment disposition on a different ground.

**Controlling explanation.** Pennsylvania's substantive promise concerns reasonable and necessary care. General program eligibility therefore does not make every contested invoice presently due without regard to that condition. The facial demand for constant advance payment cannot rest on that inference alone.

Logan protects a statutory claim from arbitrary destruction; it does not equate a right to fair determination with unconditional payment before the substantive condition is established. Loudermill likewise prevents government from defining substantive property away through procedure. The Court preserves that distinction rather than making an insurer's objection conclusive or requiring a new adjudication before every payment.

The allegations concerning earlier orders and ongoing treatment are not resolved by assuming the orders concern different services or that no worker has an existing entitlement. Individual reliance on prior payments also remains open. Nor does reimbursement and interest necessarily replace medical care lost during an interruption. Those limitations prevent the confined negative rule from becoming a universal no-property holding.

The categorical advance-payment/severance requirement is set aside only to this extent. The Court does not decide every procedure adequate under Mathews, impose new exhaustion or proof requirements, or adjudicate unchallenged notice/input. Distinct preserved public-party and individual claims return for ordinary resolution under their own predicates.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Blum v. Yaretsky, 457 U.S. 991; Rendell-Baker v. Kohn, 457 U.S. 830:** Applied to distinguish regulation or public support from governmental responsibility for the particular private act.
- **Lugar v. Edmondson Oil Co., 457 U.S. 922:** Applied; a state-created procedure and sufficient governmental participation remain separate requirements.
- **Edmonson v. Leesville Concrete Co., 500 U.S. 614:** Distinguished for actual participation in the governmental jury function; its attribution inquiry remains effective.
- **Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp.:** Preserved for governmental institutional creation and permanent control and for the proper scope of an included question; it supplies no finding of such carrier status here.
- **Denver Area Educational Telecommunications Consortium:** Preserved at its actual component holdings; it creates neither universal immunity for permissive legislation nor automatic attribution of every permitted private choice.
- **Tulsa Professional Collection Services v. Pope, 485 U.S. 478:** Preserved; a non-self-executing procedure does not eliminate the need to identify significant governmental involvement.
- **Logan v. Zimmerman Brush Co., 455 U.S. 422; Cleveland Board of Education v. Loudermill, 470 U.S. 532:** Preserved for protected claims and the distinction between substantive entitlement and procedure; neither establishes unconditional payment of every disputed bill.
- **Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319:** Its procedural inquiry is not replaced or applied to decide every preserved individual or public-party claim.

#### Law After Decision

Private initial withholding requires its own governmental attribution basis. General benefit eligibility alone supplies no unconditional advance-payment right. Actual governmental conduct, fair adjudication of protected claims, prior service entitlements and properly supported continuation interests remain distinct. The decision establishes no universal immunity or absence of property and designs no additional statewide procedure.

#### Separate Writings

**Ginsburg concurs in Part II and in the judgment.** Rejecting constant payment of every medical bill pending the reasonable-and-necessary determination is sufficient; she does not reach private attribution. Claims for medical benefits still require fair adjudication. She agrees with Stevens that the original absence of adequate notice and employee input was deficient and that the revised features answer that particular objection, without deciding every delay, lost-care or prior-award claim.

**Breyer, joined by Souter, concurs.** They join the private-attribution holding and the confined rejection of the facial payment demand. Prior payments may create a reasonable expectation of continuation in an individual case. The present ruling neither finds nor forecloses that property interest and does not assume that every interruption is brief or reparable with money. Their qualification is not an unconditional right to every disputed bill.

**Stevens concurs in part and dissents in part.** The substantive claim to reasonable and necessary care is property, and government must determine it fairly. The original procedure did not adequately warn of suspension or permit meaningful employee input. He would retain the notice and statement protections imposed below and reject additional categorical advance payment because withholding pending a fair determination is not inherently unfair. He declines the Court's attribution and entitlement rationales, does not declare the carriers governmental, and would leave the remaining actual claims for proper resolution. He dissents from the additional attribution adjudication while agreeing that further automatic payment requirements cannot stand under the revised procedure.
