### Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah, No. 91-948

Source: `terms/OT1992/output/OT_1992CHUNK8.md`.

| Opinion of the Court, Parts II-A-1 and II-A-3 | Kennedy | Joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Thomas (5 total). Objective nonneutrality in text, definitions, exemptions and operation. White and Souter do not join these portions. | Invalidate all four as presented |

| Concurrence in the judgment | Blackmun | Joined by O'Connor (2 total). Broader substantial-burden test; discriminatory laws necessarily fail its strict scrutiny. | Reverse and remand |
#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy’s Part II-A-2, joined by Stone-Zsela and Stevens, treats the sequence of enactment and contemporaneous official statements as corroboration of the objectively established discriminatory object under Arlington Heights and Feeney. Public hostility is not converted into a finding that every speaker’s intent belonged to the council. That discussion has three joins and supplies no controlling motive test. Scalia concurs in part and in the judgment. He regards neutrality and general applicability as substantially overlapping and rejects the attempt to identify a single subjective motive in a collective legislature, consistent with his Edwards dissent. A law objectively targeting religious practice fails even if its sponsors profess only humane concerns; he joins all of Kennedy’s opinion except II-A-2. Souter concurs in part and in the judgment, joining I, III and IV. He distinguishes the settled prohibition on persecution from Smith’s broader claim that genuinely neutral and general laws need no free-exercise justification. In his view Smith’s relation to Sherbert, Yoder and the unemployment cases, its briefing history and its textual foundation warrant reconsideration in a case that requires it. He neither overrules Smith here nor claims novelty alone permits abandoning precedent; the Casey approach to stare decisis remains a separate, contextual discipline. Blackmun, joined by O’Connor, concurs in the judgment. They would require compelling justification and the least restrictive available means for substantial burdens on religious exercise even from general laws. They regard discrimination of this kind as necessarily failing that standard, while preserving the seriousness of animal-welfare concerns and reserving whether a sincere general anti-cruelty law must provide an exemption. These additional approaches remain noncontrolling.

### South Dakota v. Bourland, No. 91-2051

Source: `terms/OT1992/output/OT_1992CHUNK9.md`.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing / author | Joined by | Scope | Judgment relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thomas | White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy | Opinion of the Court | Supports reversal |
| Blackmun | Souter | Dissent on treaty and inherent authority | Would affirm former-trust-land ruling |
| Stone-Zsela | No joiners | Separate treaty-ground dissent | Would affirm and preserve fee-land remand |
#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun, joined by Souter, dissents. Dion requires evidence that Congress considered the disputed treaty right and chose abrogation. Building a dam and assuring recreation can coexist with conservation regulation; loss of absolute exclusion does not prove loss of every incident of territorial sovereignty. Wheeler and Indian-law construction principles support retention absent congressional action or necessary incompatibility. The compensation settlement does not identify every surrendered governmental power. Superior federal project law remains controlling.

Stone-Zsela separately dissents on the treaty ground. He would sustain compatible hunting-and-fishing regulation on former trust land because public acquisition removes exclusive possession without clearly removing this distinct power. He does not require express words in every statute, treat retained personal privileges as a new delegation, or decree exclusive tribal control of access. He preserves the fee-land Montana inquiry and adjudication of concrete conflicts with federal access requirements.

Blackmun, joined by Souter, concurs in the remand. ### Landgraf v. USI Film Products, No. 92-757

Source: `terms/OT1993/output/OT_1993CHUNK4.md`.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Exact scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg in full; Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas only in the background and statutory-direction discussion | Affirm | The five full joiners adopt the general retroactive-effect framework and its damages/jury application. The three partial joiners agree that the effective-date text and separate exceptions do not supply the necessary retrospective direction; they do not join the general framework or its controlling explanation. |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Scalia | Kennedy, Thomas | Affirm | Identify the activity regulated and require a clear textual statement or unavoidable necessary implication for application to earlier activity. |
| Dissent | Blackmun | No other Justice | Would reverse for compensation and the consequent conditional jury election | Pending-case compensation under the enacted temporal provisions and, alternatively, remedial principles; punitive damages expressly reserved. |
#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia, joined by Justices Kennedy and Thomas, agrees with the statutory-direction discussion and the judgment but uses a different general method. A court should identify the activity the statute regulates and require a clear textual statement or unavoidable necessary implication before applying the new law to earlier activity. Section 102 regulates the discriminatory conduct by attaching compensation and punishment; the later date of judgment does not transform that liability into regulation of future judicial procedure. His Kaiser concurrence supplies this approach, while his Bowen concurrence distinguishes new consequences for past events from regulation of future activity affected by old facts. A testimony rule ordinarily regulates the taking of testimony, a jurisdictional rule the exercise of judicial power, and prospective relief future conduct. Those examples require identifying the relevant event, not assigning dispositive labels such as substantive, procedural or vested. He does not adopt an open-ended fairness exception or treat legislative history as a substitute for the necessary textual command. The conditional jury provision fails with the unavailable damages claim. The concurrence does not require overruling Bradley’s actual fee holding or deciding every fee statute’s relevant event.

Justice Blackmun would make compensatory damages available in this pending action and permit the consequent statutory jury election. He reads §402(a)’s immediate effectiveness together with the separate exceptions in §§109(c) and 402(b): Congress expressly confined the extraterritorial amendments and excluded the defined older disparate-impact cases, supporting application of compensation in other pending cases. Alternatively, Bradley’s current-law principle and his Kaiser dissent join support remedial compensation for conduct already prohibited. Bennett’s protection of substantive obligations is the strongest contrary precedent, but he distinguishes changing the prohibition from supplying relief for its violation. An employer has no vested entitlement to discriminate. He does not treat the legislative history as reliable evidence defeating his reading. Punitive damages present a harder question not squarely addressed here, on which he expresses no opinion. His proposed remand would determine the remaining compensation predicates and proper trial scope; it would not award damages, find malice, order every issue retried or erase every earlier finding.

### Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. Federal Election Commission, No. 95-489

Source: `terms/OT1995/output/OT_1995CHUNK10.md`.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Principal opinion, Part I | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer | Opinion of the Court on the limited independent-advertisement and statutory-predicate propositions | First Amendment protection of this genuinely independent advertisement; party status cannot itself supply the candidate-connection predicate for the alternative contribution theory |
| Principal opinion, Parts II–III | O'Connor | Souter, Breyer | Plurality explanation supporting vacatur and remand | Complete independent-expenditure framework and proposed separate consideration of the remaining constitutional counterclaim |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Kennedy | Scalia, Thomas | Joins Part I and the relief protecting this advertisement; seeks additional constitutional relief | Party-authored Senate-campaign expression remains protected despite cooperation with a candidate; distinct transfers and genuine donor conduits remain outside that proposed rule |
| Dissent | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Ginsburg | Would affirm enforcement, with penalty assessment below | Institution-specific contribution-channel, corruption and circumvention grounds; no equalization ground or finding of actual coordination |

No majority joins the complete narrower framework or the broader coordinated-expression rule. Part I controls through its six express joins. The Court does not derive an additional controlling rationale by combining the other writings.
#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor's Parts II–III, joined by Souter and Breyer.** These Justices apply the independent-expenditure framework while preserving a separate inquiry into actual candidate resources and preventive contribution controls. Buckley supplies the functional distinction; MCFL and NCPAC support protection of independent expression; Day preserves both collective association and realistic conduit regulation. They give legitimate predictions about corruption their due but reject replacing the advertisement's actual circumstances with a party label. They do not demand a proved bribe for every preventive rule.

They would address the remaining counterclaim separately because party-authored expression, direct payment of a candidate's bills and undifferentiated transfers may involve different constitutional and statutory questions. The relation between the party allowance and ordinary contribution limits, and any necessary severability issue, should receive proper treatment before a broader declaration. The counterclaim is not jurisdictionally barred, and the advertisement need not be retried merely to recreate a fact of coordination. Their proposed restraint is supported by these three Justices; it is not the full rationale of the six-Justice holding.

**Kennedy's concurrence, joined by Scalia and Thomas.** These Justices accept Part I's protection of the independent advertisement but find that relief insufficient. A party pursues its own political program through the candidates it supports. Its own campaign message does not become another person's speech merely because the party and candidate cooperate. Buckley's valid treatment of ordinary contributions therefore does not decide the distinct restriction on that expression. Kennedy's Austin position and Day's protection of political association support the distinction; Day's preserved contribution rule continues to govern genuine contribution channels.

The broad Senate-election counterclaim and allegation that the ceiling chilled additional spending warrant resolving that party-expression claim now, in their view. They would invalidate §441a(d)(3)'s ceiling on the Party's own Senate-campaign expression, including expression produced in cooperation with its candidate, and remand for residual statutory and remedial consequences. They do not invalidate every §441a(a)(2) recipient limit, decide presidential financing, immunize undifferentiated party transfers or candidate cash, or protect donors using earmarked or sham conduits. Thomas joins this party-specific ground within Buckley's framework; he proposes no general merger of contributions and expenditures.

**Stevens's dissent, joined by Stone-Zsela and Ginsburg.** These Justices accept that the advertisement was independently arranged. They nevertheless regard the official party's continuing selection, support and financing of its candidates as a distinctive contribution channel. Donors who may give more through party channels than directly to a candidate could evade lawful candidate limits if all party election spending were uncapped. The official nominating institution's relationship survives the fact that this particular advertisement preceded nomination. Congress may address that institutional risk without proving an actual bargain or consultation for each advertisement.

They would uphold this election-related, inflation-adjusted allowance because it leaves meaningful collective advocacy while constraining the contribution channel. Ordinary issue discussion outside the statute's relevant candidate-election coverage is not automatically included. Buckley supplies legitimate contribution and anti-circumvention interests, and Day preserves realistic conduit regulation while requiring fit: Day's poorly fitted $100 intermediary ceiling does not itself decide this different party allowance. They do not rely on equalizing influence, reducing campaign costs or disliking expensive speech, and they do not extend their conclusion to every PAC, individual, press organization or nonprofit. The dissent would affirm enforcement under §441a(d), leave the penalty amount for the lower court and reject the requested facial invalidation without adjudicating every hypothetical coordinated transaction or finding an independent §441a(a)(2) violation.

### Felker v. Turpin, No. 95-8836; associated application A-890

Every principal-opinion portion has its stated controlling coalition. No general freestanding-innocence burden or mandatory direct application of §§2244(b)(1)–(2) is adopted.

**Signed application explanations and express adoptions.** O'Connor's Part V supplies her signed reasons. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer expressly adopt it: actual examination is complete, and no substantial remaining claim or unfinished legally required process calls for temporary protection; Stevens and Souter retain the qualification stated in their concurrence. Kennedy expressly adopts it while preserving the possible remedy for established innocence and the conditional nature of required-process protection. Scalia expressly adopts it, with his additional concurrence-B position. Stone's signed adoption and narrower claim grounds are stated in his concurrence.
#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela's concurrence.** Stone joins the channel, limited temporal, statutory-reservation, constitutional, independent original-denial and stay grounds. He does not decide whether the reasonable-doubt charge was constitutional. Its trial basis was available earlier, and the claim does not identify the required newly established retroactive rule or newly discovered predicate warranting extraordinary successive relief. He treats the medical evidence seriously against the full record, including Whitaker's independent support for the earlier chronology, but finds no extraordinary showing for original relief. He keeps that conclusion distinct from the Court's additional trial-process and exact gateway holdings. His signed A-890 position is denial: execution is irreparable, but neither claim demonstrates a sufficiently substantial prospect of relief through the available proceedings. The circuit's authorization refusal alone is not his reason.

**Stevens's concurrence, joined by Souter.** They retain their Herrera position that probable actual innocence can support a constitutional claim and warrant relief in an appropriate case. Their Schlup and Lonchar positions require serious consideration of the complete probative evidence and protection of actually required process. Neither creates an automatic additional return whenever expert affidavits dispute chronology. The present affidavits are substantive and have been examined with the contrary medical and circumstantial grounds; this submission does not require exceptional original intervention. They join the Court's denial without adopting a categorical no-right rule, a uniform Herrera burden or a presumption that a Georgia remedy is adequate.

**Thomas's concurrence A, joined by Scalia and Kennedy.** They reach the same temporal result by identifying the activity regulated: a post-enactment authorization determination and the ensuing request for review. They do not join Part I-B's general sequential explanation. Their method does not decide the temporal operation of unrelated Title I provisions or require a ruling that §§2244(b)(1)–(2) bind every original application.

**Thomas's concurrence B, joined by Scalia.** They preserve their Herrera position that new evidence of innocence alone supplies no freestanding constitutional right to reopen the conviction, and retain their stricter gateway view. Those are additional positions, not the Court's rule. They independently accept the common assumed-claim, particular-submission and extraordinary-writ denial, so the judgment requires no adoption of their categorical theory.

**Thomas's Circuit Justice statement C.** Thomas presents A-890 and personally denies the requested stay. He adopts Parts III, IV and V: the particular trial grounds and original submission fail, retained jurisdiction creates no substantial relief ground, and no identified required proceeding remains unfinished. His concurrence-B constitutional view is additional; denial does not depend solely on it or on the circuit's ruling. This is his own signed explanation of the referred request, not an order continuing or dissolving any previous stay.

### Agostini v. Felton and Chancellor, Board of Education of the City of New York v. Felton, Nos. 96-552 and 96-553

Source: `terms/OT1996/output/OT_1996CHUNK11.md`.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–IV | Stone-Zsela | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas in full | Vacate both judgments and remand for tailored prospective relief | Part I: modification of Lemon; Part II: onsite secular-service rule and precise overruling; Part III: present legal change under Rule 60(b)(5); Part IV: tailored removal with retained safeguards and lawful obligations |
| Dissent, Part I | Souter | Stevens and Ginsburg | Would affirm both judgments | Affirmative substantive objection to this public instructional support; Breyer does not join |
| Same dissent, Part II | Souter | Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer | Would affirm both judgments | Retention of precedent and absence of sufficient intervening erosion or factual change |
| Dissent | Ginsburg | Stevens, Souter and Breyer | Would affirm both judgments on an independent procedural ground | Rule 60 review cannot supply this means of rehearing the original constitutional ruling |
#### Separate Writings

##### Justice Souter, dissenting

Stevens and Ginsburg join the dissent in full. Breyer joins Part II only.

In Part I, Souter, Stevens and Ginsburg would retain Aguilar's and Ball's applicable substantive rules. Public employees conducting education within a religious school's ordinary instructional setting supply governmental educational support, not independently attributable private speech. Public staffing, neutral eligibility and safeguards do not alone resolve the institutional subsidy and entanglement concerns. Rosenberger's private publication and the private-forum decisions therefore do not decide this case. They reject the Court's modification of Lemon because its treatment of purpose, effects and entanglement within attribution and function understates the constitutional significance of continuing public instructional support. Their objection concerns this governmental program rather than a claim that every private religious act must become government speech. These Justices do not find proved proselytizing and do not require it for their structural objection. Breyer does not join this affirmative direct-aid rationale.

In Part II, all four dissenters would adhere to the direct precedents. The safeguards and anticipated costs did not newly arise. Kiryas Joel preserved Aguilar and Ball, Zobrest supplied no establishment merits holding, and the bounded expression decisions did not erode the public-instruction rule sufficiently to justify its abandonment. They oppose adopting the general methodological change as a basis for abandoning these precedents in this case. They would affirm denial of relief and preserve the injunction.

##### Justice Ginsburg, dissenting; joined by Stevens, Souter and Breyer

The four dissenters would also affirm on an independent procedural ground. Browder limits an appeal from denial of Rule 60 relief; it does not reopen the original judgment for indefinite rehearing. The district court followed still-binding Aguilar, and Rufo requires a qualifying change rather than an appeal whose own desired outcome creates the prerequisite for reopening. This Court may examine the continued force of its precedents in a proper case, but that power does not establish this vehicle's propriety. Their position does not turn an adjudicated injunction into a negotiated consent promise, deny Rule 60 relief after an actual qualifying change, or treat costs alone as sufficient. The Court's adoption of an updated Lemon test does not, in their view, cure that procedural defect. This writing addresses the route to relief independently of the substantive merits disagreement.

### Glickman v. Wileman Brothers & Elliott, Inc., No. 95-1184

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reversal and remand | Entire opinion; integrated-program ground and express limits |
| Dissent | Souter | Scalia | Would affirm the First Amendment judgment | Entire dissent, Parts I and II |

| Separate dissent | Thomas | Scalia | Additional speech-protection ground for affirmance | Part I: compelled financing does not lose speech protection |

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Souter dissents, joined by Scalia throughout and by Thomas on the sufficient compulsory-subsidy ground. Paying for expression remains a First Amendment burden even if the message is commercial, not personally attributed and not ideological. Abood, Keller and Lehnert justify charges through a legally required representative function, not every useful public-relations campaign. The program's regulatory breadth and shared commercial benefit therefore do not adequately justify this separate forced expressive expenditure. The dissent would affirm the judgment against the advertising assessments while leaving independently valid nonexpressive regulation untouched. In Part II, Souter and Scalia additionally retain Central Hudson's inquiry into substantial interest, direct advancement and reasonable fit, with the burden on government and without a least-restrictive-means or mandatory-study requirement. That framework discussion supplies no separate factual holding about this program's efficacy, geographic coverage or credits.

Thomas separately dissents. In Part I, joined by Scalia, he emphasizes that compelled financing does not cease to implicate speech merely because it uses money or promotes commerce. In Part II, joined by no other Justice, he rejects reduced constitutional protection and Central Hudson's discounted balancing approach. His observation that failure under a lesser standard would also establish failure under his stronger rule does not adopt that lesser framework or add a factual application absent from the record. He would affirm on the shared compulsory-subsidy ground, without prohibiting every compulsory regulatory fee or adjudicating unchallenged parts of the orders.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part:** They join the bounded §242(g) construction but conclude that §440(a) withdraws present direct-review jurisdiction. Their conditional assessment that *Yang* would support the agency's actual reasons if jurisdiction existed is not an operative merits join.

Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, concurs in the jurisdiction holding and dissents from the eligibility disposition. The operative event for section 440(d) is the Attorney General's present decision whether to grant relief. #### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Kennedy, dissenting, joined by Justices Scalia and Thomas.**

The dissent accepts Russell's close-fit standard and the sufficiently important anticorruption interest, but would affirm invalidation of this presented statewide ceiling. The large-gift episodes support some prophylaxis without adequately justifying this amount. The severe statewide contraction in funding, contested primaries and candidates, together with campaign scale, requires a stronger operational answer than general findings of campaign capacity, indexing or examples of successful financing. The contributor-count statistic does not establish the amount of funding lost, and a ceiling's being higher than Arkansas's invalid limits does not establish its fit.

The dissent does not treat the comparative trends as conclusive proof of causation, demand an exact optimal amount or completed bribe, dismiss the corruption evidence, or adopt expenditure-level strict scrutiny for all direct contributions. Kennedy's Russell separate opinion, joined by Scalia and Thomas, preserves tailored conduit and earmark regulation and meaningful association while rejecting inadequately fitted burdens. That ground supports invalidating this limit on this record without overruling Buckley, ordering a replacement ceiling or recognizing unlimited candidate giving.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He joins the funded-function rule and the determination that actual public financing of religious instruction or demonstrated religious use violates it. He would affirm invalidation at that as-administered scope instead of vacating the declaration and remanding.
