### Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc., No. 90-1038

Merits decision, June 24, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 8, 1991; reargued January 13, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, [893 F.2d 541 (January 5, 1990)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/893/893.F2d.541.88-5784.88-5771.88-5770.88-5732.html), following its earlier 789 F.2d 181 preemption ruling. The question is to what extent the 1965 and successor cigarette Acts preempt the specified New Jersey damages claims. The estate seeks removal of the preemption barriers, not a finding of liability by this Court.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter participated at the October 8 argument; Thomas had not yet taken office. All nine participated at the January 13 reargument and June 24 decision. Thomas's participation rests on the reargument.

#### Judgment

The judgment is affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded. The claims and statutory periods have distinct votes and boundaries.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The 1965 Act does not displace these common-law damages claims

**Controlling proposition:** Section 5 of the 1965 Federal Cigarette Labeling and Advertising Act bars positive enactments requiring particular cautionary statements on cigarette packages or in advertising; it does not preempt the common-law damages claims presented here. The required federal package warning does not itself establish immunity from those claims.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion on the 1965 Act, joined by White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter, and by Stone-Zsela as to this statutory construction and its application: seven of nine. Scalia and Thomas oppose restoration of warning claims under this Act but agree that liability for voluntarily made warranty and fraudulent statements is not barred by its statement requirement.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress's original prohibition concerned required cautionary statements, not every damages obligation connected with cigarettes. Rice supplies the presumption that traditional state police authority survives unless Congress clearly displaces it. The repeated reference to statements in sections 4 and 5 links the prohibition to prescribed warnings. The regulatory setting likewise concerned competing legislative and administrative warnings. English requires attention to the particular federal operation, and Gade rejects automatic displacement from regulatory overlap alone. A federal warning and a state damages remedy therefore do not inherently conflict. The manufacturers answer that damages can influence conduct. Garmon establishes that possibility, but it does not decide what this narrower cigarette provision covers. Congress could distinguish a compulsory statement from compensation for a private wrong, and this text does so. The Court rejects the asserted uniformity defense to these claims. It decides neither design-defect liability nor the operation of later amendments, and adopts no universal immunity from independently established actual conflict.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 331 U.S. 218 — applied to retain traditional state remedies absent clear statutory displacement.
- English v. General Electric Co., 496 U.S. 72 — applied; additional tort consequences do not establish displacement without the necessary statutory or conflict basis.
- San Diego Building Trades Council v. Garmon, 359 U.S. 236 — its recognition that damages may regulate is preserved; the labor statute does not determine these cigarette clauses.
- Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Association (June 18, 1992) — applied only for its shared negative overlap rule; its noncontrolling broader theories are not adopted.

##### The successor clause reaches covered warning and warning-neutralization duties

**Controlling proposition:** For cigarettes whose packages comply with the federal labeling requirements, amended section 5(b) reaches common-law duties and preempts liability requiring additional or clearer smoking-health warnings in advertising or promotion, including a duty forbidding advertising because it neutralizes the mandated warning. Preemption turns on the duty necessary to liability, so a warning claim cannot avoid the clause merely by being pleaded as fraud.

**Authority:** Stevens's amended-Act analysis, joined by Stone-Zsela, White and O'Connor, and Scalia's separate express-preemption analysis, joined by Thomas, expressly support these duties' inclusion and displacement: six of nine at this operative scope. The shared statutory proposition controls directly; it does not depend on treating Scalia's broader preemption position as a join to Stevens's whole opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the amended words include duties enforced through damages. Garmon explains why a legal obligation may regulate conduct even when a court enforces it by compensation. Congress replaced the original reference to statements with requirements or prohibitions imposed under state law and extended the activity description to advertising or promotion. Those words cover the asserted obligation to add health warnings to promotional material. A prohibition on material because it neutralizes the required warning imposes the same kind of obligation from the other direction. Calling that claim intentional fraud does not change the showing on which liability depends. Morales confirms that an express clause must receive its enacted reach, but its airline language does not supply a cigarette-wide immunity. The Court decides only the covered duty and preserves the federal-label compliance condition. State-law liability, causation and damages remain for the lower courts, and genuinely independent duties are addressed separately.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Garmon, 359 U.S. 236 — applied to common-law duties as legal requirements; no labor-specific exclusivity rule is imported.
- Morales v. Trans World Airlines, Inc. (June 1, 1992) — distinguished by statutory language; its recognition of enforceable express boundaries remains consistent with this construction.

##### Voluntarily undertaken express warranties survive

**Controlling proposition:** Amended section 5(b) does not preempt an otherwise valid claim enforcing a manufacturer's voluntarily undertaken express warranty, because the substantive promise is supplied by the manufacturer rather than imposed by a state smoking-health command. The presence of the promise in an advertisement does not itself change that result.

**Authority:** Stevens's analysis, joined by Stone-Zsela, White and O'Connor, supplies the narrow controlling ground under Marks for this component. Blackmun, Kennedy and Souter also vote to restore the warranty claim on their broader ground that the amended Act reaches no common-law damages claims. The voluntary-warranty category is a true subset of that broader nonpreemption rule, with the same result whenever it applies; the two judgment-supporting groups total seven. The broader exemption is not adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that enforcing a promise does not prescribe its substantive content. The warranty claim requires proof that the manufacturer made an assurance that became binding under state law and then failed to honor it. Its terms measure the obligation. State law supplies enforceability, but that does not transform every freely assumed promise into a smoking-health requirement imposed by the State. Norfolk & Western explains that contracts depend on law for their force; its broader exemption from all other law does not erase the narrower words Congress chose here. An assurance printed in advertising is therefore treated like the same assurance made elsewhere. This conclusion neither finds a warranty nor supplies reliance, breach, causation or damages. Those questions remain open under New Jersey law and the existing appellate posture. Marks permits the claim-specific ground shared by the judgment's narrower and broader nonpreemption positions to control without converting the broader position into law.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Norfolk & Western Railway Co. v. American Train Dispatchers Association, 499 U.S. 117 — distinguished; an exemption from all other law does not define this prohibition of state-imposed substantive requirements.
- Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 — applied component by component to the narrow warranty ground; no broader common-law exemption follows.

##### Independently actionable nonpromotional conduct remains outside the clause

**Controlling proposition:** Amended section 5(b) does not preempt claims resting solely on testing, research or other conduct unrelated to advertising or promotion, or concealment claims resting on an independently actionable duty to disclose through nonpromotional channels. This limit does not preserve a disguised demand for additional advertising warnings or establish that any asserted state-law duty actually exists.

**Authority:** Stevens's analysis, joined by Stone-Zsela, White and O'Connor, supplies the narrow component ground under Marks, together with Blackmun, Kennedy and Souter's broader nonpreemption position: seven judgment supporters. The rule concerning claims independently outside the activity boundary is a logical subset of their broader common-law exemption. Scalia and Thomas disagree with restoring the presented consumer-warning theories merely by assigning communication to other channels, while reserving duties genuinely unrelated to advertising.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the clause's activity boundary must remain effective. The relevant inquiry is what the plaintiff must establish as the defendant's legal duty. A claim that rests solely on research or testing practices unrelated to advertising does not impose an advertising requirement. Likewise, an independently existing obligation to disclose facts through a nonpromotional channel does not become promotional because the facts concern smoking. English requires examination of the actual state duty rather than its general association with a regulated industry. Morales does not replace this statute's particular activity words with a boundless effects inquiry. The manufacturers' practical-compulsion objection remains relevant to identifying a disguised advertising duty, but it cannot erase every independent nonpromotional obligation. The Court finds no particular duty to report to an agency and no breach of one. The lower courts must determine the viable allegations and state-law elements, preserving the distinction between the duty asserted and advertising offered as evidence of another wrong.

**Precedent treatment:**

- English, 496 U.S. 72 — applied to examine the particular duty and its actual relationship to the federally regulated activity.
- Morales (June 1, 1992) — distinguished; its airline relation clause and specific fare effects do not abolish this statute's activity limitation.
- Marks, 430 U.S. 188 — applied to the surviving nonpromotional category only; no complete nonpreemption theory is extracted.

##### General intentional falsehood and surviving concealment conspiracies are not barred

**Controlling proposition:** Amended section 5(b) does not preempt liability for intentional material falsehood under the general duty not to deceive, even when the false statement appears in advertising, if liability does not depend on prescribing additional warning content or punishing warning neutralization. Conspiracy to misrepresent or conceal material smoking-health facts survives only to the extent its predicate deceptive duty survives; a conspiracy label creates no exemption of its own.

**Authority:** Stevens's claim-specific analysis, joined by Stone-Zsela, White and O'Connor, is the narrow controlling ground under Marks for these components, with Blackmun, Kennedy and Souter supporting the same restoration on the broader ground that no common-law damages claim is preempted. The protected falsehood and surviving-concealment categories are subsets of that broader rule, giving seven judgment supporters; Scalia and Thomas dissent on the presented amended-Act claims.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the subject of a falsehood does not invariably identify the legal basis of the duty it violates. A claim requiring proof of a knowingly false material representation enforces the general obligation not to deceive. It does not necessarily ask a jury to choose different warning content. The retained federal authority over deceptive advertising supports reading the statute as a warning boundary rather than permission to make false statements. Garmon establishes that damages can regulate, but it does not eliminate the statutory distinction between a smoking-health warning command and ordinary intentional deception. The contrary approach would convert the clause into protection for wrongdoing Congress did not identify. At the same time, relabeling the warning-neutralization theory as fraud cannot revive it. A conspiracy claim follows its predicate: surviving intentional deception or independently actionable concealment remains available, while a prohibited warning duty does not. The Court leaves every state-law element and the merits of the allegations to further proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Garmon, 359 U.S. 236 — its regulatory insight is preserved without making every damages claim a covered smoking-health requirement.
- Marks, 430 U.S. 188 — applied separately to these surviving claim categories; Blackmun's complete exclusion of common law remains noncontrolling.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No earlier Supreme Court decision is overruled. Marks operates only at the three specified surviving-claim components; the six-Justice express warning proposition controls without Marks.

Effective June 24, 1992, the original Act leaves these common-law damages claims available, while the amended Act bars covered promotional warning/neutralization duties and preserves the specified warranty, falsehood and independent nonpromotional categories. Each preserved category remains subject to state-law elements. The amended-Act holdings have the component authority stated above; no complete amended-Act opinion commands five formal joins. The claim-specific Marks rules do not make Blackmun's exemption of all common law controlling, and Scalia's wider displacement does not become law. Gade's negative overlap holding and Morales's distinct airline construction remain in force. No generic prohibition of independently established impossibility preemption, field immunity for cigarettes, constitutional advertising rule, design-defect ruling or construction of later amendments is adopted. Before this decision there was no cigarette-specific construction; the Acts' separate boundaries now govern the presented defense.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun, joined by Kennedy and Souter, reads both clauses as confined to positive regulatory commands and would preserve all the common-law claims. He objects to treating fraud and warnings at different levels of generality. His broader exemption supplies judgment support for the surviving claims but does not become the Court's general rule. Scalia, joined by Thomas on the express statutory analysis, treats warning requirements under the original Act and the presented health-related advertising duties under the amended Act as displaced; he rejects the warranty and general-falsehood distinctions and would include consumer-warning duties that practically compel advertising. They reserve duties genuinely unrelated to advertising. Thomas's partial join does not adopt Scalia's broader defense of implied field or obstacle preemption.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. On the ordinary mandate the Third Circuit must implement the partial affirmance and partial reversal and provide further proceedings applying the separate statutory periods and predicate-duty boundaries, together with preserved state-law trial issues. The Court awards no damages, reinstates no particular verdict, finds no fraud or causation and decides no design-defect claim. No retained Supreme Court proceeding, new filing, hearing date, mandate-issuance date or later outcome is established.

**End of entry: Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc., merits decision, June 24, 1992.**

### Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council, No. 91-453

Merits decision, June 29, 1992

Argued March 2, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of South Carolina, 304 S.C. 376, 404 S.E.2d 895 (1991), which reversed a trial award of $1,232,387.50. The question is whether a land-use restriction eliminating all economically beneficial use requires compensation despite its public-harm purpose, including the effect of a new permit avenue and background ownership limits. Lucas seeks reversal of the no-compensation judgment. All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas.

The Court reverses and remands, 6–3. Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support reversal. Blackmun and Stevens dissent; Souter would dismiss the writ as improvidently granted, declining to decide this case rather than affirming on the merits.

Scalia delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in its entirety by Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor and Thomas. Kennedy concurs in the judgment without joining the Court's categorical rationale. Blackmun and Stevens each dissent, and Souter files a statement favoring dismissal. Each separate writing has no additional joiners. The Court's express five-member opinion controls.

A later discretionary permit procedure does not bar review of compensation for an earlier categorical building prohibition where permission under that prohibition was unavailable and the state supreme court finally rejected the compensation claim on its merits. Any compensable taking must be remedied for its actual duration; the later procedure and unresolved factual issues remain relevant to future use, duration and valuation, and the permanent-value award is not automatically reinstated.

Scalia's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor and Thomas, controls with five votes. Kennedy separately concurs in the judgment and agrees that the earlier interval is reviewable, but does not join the Court's writing. The holding rests on the express five-Justice opinion without combining separate rationales.

The Court holds that a new opportunity to seek future permission cannot eliminate a completed deprivation from judicial review. Williamson County and MacDonald ordinarily require a final determination of permitted development so that a court knows the restriction it must assess. Here the Council stipulated that the original Act allowed no building permit, and the state supreme court rejected compensation on a categorical merits ground after the amendment existed. First English establishes that ending a restriction does not eliminate compensation for a period in which it effected a taking. The Council's request for further permitting therefore cannot dispose of the earlier interval. Yee preserves the difference between a distinct unpresented claim and an argument supporting the claim actually decided; the earlier deprivation belongs to the compensation dispute already presented. These conclusions do not establish that a taking occurred or fix its value. The Court leaves the new permit avenue, the extent and duration of actual loss, and the defenses the state supreme court did not decide for further proceedings.

Williamson County Regional Planning Commission v. Hamilton Bank, 473 U.S. 172 (1985), continues to supply ordinary finality requirements. The Court distinguishes an absolute prohibition for an already elapsed period.

MacDonald, Sommer & Frates v. Yolo County, 477 U.S. 340 (1986), continues to require a determination of permitted development. An unavailable original permit need not be sought to test the earlier prohibition.

First English Evangelical Lutheran Church v. County of Los Angeles, 482 U.S. 304 (1987), requires compensation for an actual temporary taking. The Court applies that rule without deciding whether this regulation effected a taking or setting the amount.

Yee v. City of Escondido, April 1, 1992, supplies the distinction between a presented claim and a distinct unpresented taking theory. Its application here creates no new physical-taking rule.


A land-use regulation that removes all economically beneficial use of the relevant property effects a taking requiring just compensation unless the prohibited use was already outside the owner's title under background state property or nuisance law. After the owner establishes complete deprivation, the State must identify and substantiate an applicable background limitation that would independently prohibit the claimed use in the property's circumstances; a new legislative harm declaration alone does not suffice, and ordinary adjudication supplies the proof requirements without a special heightened quantum.

Scalia's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor and Thomas, supplies five votes for the complete rule and its explanation. Kennedy supports reversal through an expectations-sensitive analysis, without joining this categorical formulation. The express five-Justice opinion supplies the controlling rule.

The Court holds that public authority to restrict land and responsibility to pay for its complete economic loss are separate questions. Pennsylvania Coal recognizes that otherwise valid regulation can impose a compensable taking. Agins supplies the complete-use formulation, which the Court now specifies as a categorical rule with an ownership limitation. Penn Central continues to govern ordinary partial burdens. A purpose-only exemption would let the State describe the same sacrifice as preventing harm or conferring a benefit and thereby determine its own compensation duty. Mugler and the older noxious-use cases do not establish that exemption for proved elimination of all beneficial land use. Roth and Scranton explain why the owner's entitlement remains bounded by existing law: prohibiting an entitlement never held takes nothing. The trial finding supplies the premise of this review, but does not settle the Council's preserved factual objections. South Carolina must identify genuine background principles and their application on remand. Changed knowledge may affect nuisance law's application; a new legislative label cannot replace it. The Court decides no parcel-definition rule or percentage shortcut.

Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon, 260 U.S. 393 (1922), recognizes that regulation can go too far despite valid public purposes. The Court applies that principle without attributing today's exact categorical rule to that decision.

Agins v. City of Tiburon, 447 U.S. 255 (1980), supplies the complete-economic-use formulation that the Court specifies and extends into today's rule and necessary exception. Its distinct legitimate-interest formulation is not adjudicated.

Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City, 438 U.S. 104 (1978), retains its contextual analysis for partial deprivation, including economic impact, expectations and the character of governmental action. No percentage automatically defeats or establishes compensation.

Mugler v. Kansas, 123 U.S. 623 (1887), Hadacheck v. Sebastian, 239 U.S. 394 (1915), Miller v. Schoene, 276 U.S. 272 (1928), and Goldblatt v. Hempstead, 369 U.S. 590 (1962), retain their actual outcomes and protection of lawful protective regulation. The Court limits a broad noxious-use reading that would make any legislative harm characterization conclusive despite complete beneficial-use deprivation.

Keystone Bituminous Coal Association v. DeBenedictis, 480 U.S. 470 (1987), concerned productive enterprise and partial interests. The Court distinguishes that setting without reversing its actual holding or adopting a new denominator for measuring loss.

Board of Regents v. Roth, 408 U.S. 564 (1972), supplies existing rules and understandings as the basis for identifying a protected property interest. Applying that principle here decides no procedural due-process claim.

Scranton v. Wheeler, 179 U.S. 141 (1900), supplies the principle that title is subject to antecedent limitations. A previously existing servitude is distinct from a newly imposed public burden.

Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp., 458 U.S. 419 (1982), and Kaiser Aetna v. United States, 444 U.S. 164 (1979), retain their protections against physical occupation and interference with exclusion. They illuminate the difference between existing limitations and a newly imposed servitude; this decision concerns regulatory loss.

Webb's Fabulous Pharmacies, Inc. v. Beckwith, 449 U.S. 155 (1980), supplies the prohibition on converting private property into public property by governmental assertion alone, which the Court applies here.


The Court assumes the trial court's total-loss finding to decide the presented legal issue; it makes no new appraisal and does not resolve the Council's preserved challenge to that finding. No unconditional entitlement to the trial award, final application of state nuisance law, right to anticipated profits, new parcel rule, or disposition of post-amendment permission is adopted. Legitimate environmental purposes remain sufficient to justify regulation; compensation is a distinct consequence. The Court overrules no earlier decision.

Effective June 29, 1992, proved complete deprivation of economically beneficial land use triggers the categorical compensation rule unless background state ownership law already forecloses the use. The State must identify and substantiate that exception through ordinary adjudication. The rule specifies Agins's complete-use formulation and limits broad purpose-only readings of the noxious-use cases without establishing a percentage rule, parcel definition or heightened proof requirement. Penn Central remains operative for partial burdens; First English governs compensation after an actual taking. Public regulatory authority and ordinary finality requirements outside this setting remain intact. No precedent is overruled.

Kennedy agrees that the earlier interval is reviewable and reversal appropriate, but would test complete-loss restrictions against objectively reasonable investment-backed expectations drawn from the whole legal tradition. Common-law nuisance alone is too narrow, particularly for vulnerable coastal land; actual ability and intent to develop and the duration of loss remain for remand. Blackmun disputes finality and the unsupported premise of complete economic extinction, and rejects the categorical rule in favor of the protective public purpose and contextual precedent. Stevens would withhold premature constitutional adjudication and, on the merits, sustain the general coastal measure under Penn Central, which supplies contextual review, and Keystone, which addressed productive enterprise and partial interests. He questions the zero-value boundary and a rigid nuisance exception. Souter would dismiss because the unreviewable and questionable total-loss premise prevents sound resolution of both the category and its exceptions. These separate positions do not control.

The case returns to the Supreme Court of South Carolina for proceedings consistent with the holdings. The no-compensation judgment cannot rest on public-purpose characterization alone. The state court must address background property and nuisance law and its remaining unaddressed defenses, including the extent and duration of economic loss. Compensation covers any actual taking period; neither permanent loss nor the trial award is automatically restored. The post-amendment permit procedure remains available, and its effect is reserved. The Court determines no final state-law liability, new appraisal, compensation amount or entitlement to a permit. Further proceedings occur on remand.

**End of entry: Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council, merits decision, June 29, 1992.**

### INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights, Inc., No. 90-1090

Judgment reversed and case remanded, December 16, 1991

*INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights, Inc.*, 502 U.S. 183 (1991), No. 90-1090. Argued November 13 and decided December 16, 1991, on certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 913 F.2d 1350. The question is whether §242(a) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) prohibits the general bond condition against unauthorized employment pending deportability proceedings.

The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit's judgment and remands the case, 9–0. All nine Justices—Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas—participated and support that disposition. Stevens delivered the opinion for a unanimous Court.

The Court holds that INA §242(a) permits a generally applicable release condition forbidding unauthorized employment when the regulation is construed not to reach authorized workers and retains individual status determinations and discretionary relief. That facial statutory ruling does not resolve constitutional objections or particular misapplications.

Stevens's unanimous opinion supplies this holding for all nine Justices.

Section 242(a) authorizes release on conditions prescribed by the Attorney General; it does not restrict every condition to securing physical appearance. The regulation's heading, exception, contemporaneous explanation, and operating instructions establish that its target is unauthorized employment. *Witkovich* requires delegated immigration authority to remain connected to the statutory scheme, and Congress's labor-market protections supply that connection. *American Hospital Association* permits general rules to structure delegated discretion so long as legally required individual determinations remain. Here officials must determine existing work authorization, address colorable citizenship or status claims, and consider the regulation's avenue for discretionary relief. The narrower construction therefore defeats the proposition that every application exceeds statutory authority. The Court does not approve interference with lawful employment, presume disputed deportability, decide the complaint's constitutional claims, or foreclose an as-applied challenge where the represented procedures are unavailable or misused.

The Court applies and preserves *Witkovich*, *Carlson*, *Seminole Rock*, and *American Hospital Association*. *Ardestani* remains confined to the Equal Access to Justice Act's (EAJA) §554 cross-reference. Effective December 16, §242(a) supports the narrowed bond rule against unauthorized work, with individual determinations of status and relief.

Enforcement is permitted only under that construction and the described individualized process. The facial statutory injunction is reversed; constitutional and as-applied claims—challenges to particular applications of the rule—remain open, and other preserved claims return to the lower courts.

**End of entry: INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights, Inc., Judgment reversed and case remanded, December 16, 1991.**

### Taylor v. Freeland & Kronz, No. 91-571

Merits decision, April 21, 1992

Argued March 2, 1992; decided April 21, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, [938 F.2d 420](https://static.case.law/f2d/938/html/0420-01.html), which reversed the district court's affirmance of a bankruptcy order requiring turnover of $23,483.75 plus interest. Davis claimed her discrimination-lawsuit proceeds exempt with unknown value. Taylor knew of the claim but sought neither a timely objection nor an extension. The suit settled for $110,000, after which Taylor sought turnover. The question is whether a trustee may contest a claimed exemption lacking a colorable statutory basis after the objection period expires. The Court reviews that deadline issue and excludes the new §105(a) question. The decision is reported at [503 U.S. 638](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep503/usrep503638/usrep503638.pdf).

All nine Justices participate. The Court affirms the Third Circuit, 8–1. Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support affirmance; Stevens dissents and would reverse and remand for the exemption inquiry. Thomas delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter, including its rule, application and express reservations. Stevens files a dissent, joined by no other Justice.

The Court holds that an exemption claim becomes effective when the time to object expires without an objection or a timely extension. Under 11 U.S.C. §522(l) and the applicable Bankruptcy Rule 4003(b), property claimed as exempt becomes exempt when no party in interest objects within 30 days after the creditors' meeting concludes or a relevant amendment to the exemption list is filed, whichever is later, and the court grants no further time within that period. The trustee cannot later contest the claimed exemption merely because it lacked a colorable statutory basis. This holding concerns the scheduled proceeds claimed here and does not decide a different claim's scope, an unscheduled asset, or an unpreserved fraud or §105(a) theory. Thomas's opinion controls because Stone, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter join its rule and explanation in full, forming an eight-Justice majority at this level of generality.

The Court holds that a trustee must use the objection procedure while it remains available. Section 522(b) identifies substantive exemptions, while §522(l) states the consequence of a claim left unchallenged. Rule 4003(b) supplies the time to object or obtain an extension, and Rule 9006(b)(3) confines enlargement to that rule's conditions. Ron Pair directs courts to enforce a coherent statutory scheme according to its terms. Taylor knew about the lawsuit and its possible substantial recovery. Uncertainty about its value permitted timely inquiry and an objection; it did not require waiting for collection. A colorability exception would reopen the very merits dispute that the objection period closes. The possibility of an undeserved exemption does not authorize that revision. Misconduct remedies remain available only under their own requirements, and the Court imposes none. The §105(a) argument was not presented below or in the petition and is not decided. Nor does this record require a decision about fraud, unscheduled property, or differently limited exemption language.

United States v. Germain, decided March 9, 1992, supplies a consistent rule that statutory text governs and provisions doing different work may coexist: substantive exemption eligibility and the procedure for contesting a claim perform different work here. ### Foucha v. Louisiana, No. 90-5844

Merits decision, May 18, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 4, 1991; decided May 18, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 to the Louisiana Supreme Court, 563 So.2d 1138 (June 14, 1990), which affirmed continued psychiatric confinement after an insanity acquittal. The question is whether the State may continue that confinement on dangerousness and the acquittee's failure to prove safety after disclaiming present mental illness.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the judgment sustaining Foucha's continued psychiatric confinement and remands for implementation.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Continued custody under Louisiana's challenged insanity-acquittee provision | Reversed, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | End confinement on that basis; remand for implementation. No indefinite continuation while seeking another rationale. Independently lawful custody requires its own authority and procedures. |

The equal-protection theory supplies no separate claim for additional relief and no majority holding. The Court does not find Foucha harmless or prescribe conditions for every possible alternative preventive statute.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | White | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter | Reversal and implementation remand | Parts I (record and posture), II-A (ended predicate), II-C (alternative dangerousness regime), IV (mandate); six Justices support each part |
| Opinion of the Court | White | Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter | Independent due-process ground for reversal | Part II-B (distinct psychiatric grounds and procedures); five Justices support this part |
| Plurality opinion | White | Blackmun, Stevens, Souter | Supports reversal; no controlling equal-protection holding | Part III (equal protection); four Justices support this part |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | O'Connor | No other Justice | Joins White's Parts I–II and IV and the judgment | Emphasizes reservation of narrower preventive schemes |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins White's Parts I, II-A, II-C and IV and the judgment | States the ground concerning the particular authority for custody and the immediate implementation limit |
| Dissent | Kennedy | No other Justice | Would affirm | Distinct authority arising from the acquittee's criminal adjudication |
| Dissent | Thomas | Scalia | Would affirm | Acquittee distinction and adequacy of review procedures |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The insanity judgment no longer authorizes this psychiatric confinement

**Controlling proposition:** When the State concedes that an insanity acquittee is no longer mentally ill, it may not continue his psychiatric confinement under the existing insanity-acquittee authority solely because he cannot prove that he will be harmless. The original acquittal supports neither a suspended criminal sentence nor indefinite psychiatric custody after its illness predicate disappears.

**Authority:** White's Part II-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this proposition and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Louisiana must identify a present lawful basis for the restraint it imposes. Jones v. United States permits initial commitment because an insanity verdict supports an inference of illness and dangerousness; its rejection of a hypothetical criminal-sentence ceiling rests on those continuing conditions. O'Connor v. Donaldson establishes that a permissible commitment cannot continue after its supporting basis disappears. Jackson v. Indiana requires the nature and duration of custody to remain reasonably related to its purpose. Louisiana no longer claims that Foucha is mentally ill. His antisocial personality and the doctor's unwillingness to guarantee safety do not restore that conceded absence. The State's substantial interest in protecting the public therefore cannot perpetuate this particular psychiatric commitment. The original criminal conduct does not make the acquittal a conviction authorizing punishment. The Court neither finds Foucha harmless nor sets the maximum duration of an otherwise valid commitment. Any separate lawful restraint must rest on its own established authority and procedures.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Jones v. United States, 463 U.S. 354 (1983): applied to the continuing illness-and-danger basis of insanity commitment; initial verdict-based commitment and rejection of an automatic hypothetical-sentence ceiling remain intact.
- O'Connor v. Donaldson, 422 U.S. 563 (1975): applied to the requirement that confinement end when its authorizing basis disappears; the Court does not equate a conditional-release recommendation with a finding of harmlessness.
- Jackson v. Indiana, 406 U.S. 715 (1972): applied to the required relationship between confinement's nature, duration and purpose.

##### 2. Psychiatric custody requires appropriate grounds and procedures

**Controlling proposition:** Once the insanity-acquittal basis for holding Foucha as a mental patient has ended, Louisiana cannot preserve that psychiatric custody without constitutionally adequate proceedings establishing the grounds required for it. If the State invokes ordinary civil commitment, it must establish current mental illness and dangerousness by clear and convincing evidence; additional procedure cannot supply an absent substantive ground.

**Authority:** White's Part II-B, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: five of nine participating Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground. Stone-Zsela does not join this section's separate Vitek-based procedural characterization.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a hearing about dangerousness alone cannot authorize confinement as a mental patient after the State disclaims mental illness. Vitek v. Jones recognizes that placement in a psychiatric institution imposes a distinct deprivation requiring appropriate justification and procedure, even for someone already lawfully confined. Jackson v. Indiana requires a new lawful basis and the protections belonging to it when the original commitment purpose no longer supports custody. Addington v. Texas supplies the clear-and-convincing burden for ordinary civil commitment. Louisiana instead requires Foucha to disprove danger, although its conceded facts cannot presently establish the illness required for that civil route. The State's reliance on a counseled release hearing does not answer this defect: fairness in deciding the wrong statutory condition does not establish the missing condition. Baxstrom v. Herold reinforces that an expired original basis does not remove the safeguards of the new proceeding. The Court does not prescribe a universal burden for every possible preventive regime or disturb the distinct rules governing initial insanity commitment.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Vitek v. Jones, 445 U.S. 480 (1980): applied to psychiatric institutionalization's distinct liberty deprivation and need for appropriate grounds and procedures.
- Jackson v. Indiana, 406 U.S. 715 (1972): applied to the need for an independently authorized commitment proceeding after the initial basis expires.
- Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S. 418 (1979): applied unchanged to the clear-and-convincing burden for ordinary civil commitment; that burden is not made universal for every preventive custody channel.
- Baxstrom v. Herold, 383 U.S. 107 (1966): relied on for the ordinary protections attending a new civil commitment after the prior detention basis ends; no separate majority equal-protection holding is created here.

##### 3. Public safety does not sustain this unbounded dangerousness regime

**Controlling proposition:** Louisiana's scheme permitting indefinite psychiatric confinement of a no-longer-mentally-ill acquittee because he has not proved nondangerousness violates due process. A legitimate preventive purpose and periodic review do not cure the absence of a carefully limited substantive authority; the Court leaves materially narrower preventive regimes undecided.

**Authority:** White's Part II-C, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this independently sufficient rejection of Louisiana's asserted alternative preventive justification. The holding adopts no new general scrutiny tier or universal dangerousness proof standard.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the exceptional power to prevent danger cannot be detached from the limits that justify the particular restraint. United States v. Salerno sustained a narrowly focused pretrial scheme with an identified threat, government proof, an adversarial hearing, review and strict temporal limits. Louisiana's rule instead permits confinement without an endpoint while requiring the acquittee to prove safety. The doctor's reluctance to certify Foucha as harmless is enough under that rule, although the State concedes that the psychiatric illness has ended. A fair procedure for predicting danger does not supply substantive authority for an otherwise unauthorized restraint. The original verdict supplies no punitive authority under Jones. If later conduct constitutes a crime, ordinary criminal proceedings remain available; the Court makes no new finding that a particular assault occurred or warrants conviction. A better hearing alone cannot cure this regime's substantive defect. The Court reserves narrowly drawn alternatives rather than converting today's holding into a universal prohibition of preventive detention.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739 (1987): distinguished because its confined pretrial detention authority and safeguards do not authorize Louisiana's indefinite post-acquittal psychiatric restraint.
- Jones v. United States, 463 U.S. 354 (1983): applied to the absence of authority to punish an insanity acquittee as a convicted offender.

#### Current-Law Effect and Limits

Effective May 18, 1992, Jones's distinct initial-commitment rule remains in force, but Louisiana cannot continue this psychiatric confinement after conceding the end of illness on the acquittee's failure to prove safety. Five Justices additionally require appropriate psychiatric grounds and procedures; ordinary civil commitment retains Addington's clear-and-convincing burden. Six reject the indefinite dangerousness alternative under this scheme. These are applied and clarified channel limits, not a new universal scrutiny tier or dangerousness standard. No majority adopts the plurality's equal-protection rule. Maximum duration of an otherwise valid commitment, materially narrower preventive regimes, and the scope of state insanity defenses remain undecided. No Marks inference is needed because each controlling due-process proposition has at least five direct joins.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

White, joined by Blackmun, Stevens and Souter, concludes in Part III that Louisiana also denies equal protection by withholding ordinary commitment safeguards from a sane acquittee while not permitting comparable indefinite dangerousness confinement for other persons whose original custody basis has ended. The four would require a particularly convincing justification for that disparity; this is a plurality position, not law.

O'Connor emphasizes that the holding concerns this indefinite psychiatric regime. She leaves open detention carefully limited in nature and duration to substantial safety concerns and questions extended confinement beyond what a conviction could have permitted, without deciding that question. No constitutional requirement that a State provide an insanity defense is adopted.

Stone-Zsela agrees that the psychiatric channel's ended predicate requires relief. He reserves independent equal protection, a new general scrutiny tier, universal proof rules and narrowly defined alternative regimes. Implementation cannot allow indefinite continuation while the State looks for a new justification; separate custody requires its own established authority and procedures.

Kennedy would affirm because the insanity adjudication follows proof of criminal conduct and belongs to a distinct legal channel. He views state definitions of criminal insanity and incapacitation as constitutionally relevant and rejects automatic transfer of ordinary civil-commitment rules to this setting.

Thomas, joined by Scalia, would affirm because acquittees differ from ordinary civil committees, the State may attach continued safety-based custody to that status, and periodic counseled review is adequate here. They reject an expansive new substantive right against this confinement and distinguish its lack of a fixed endpoint from a refusal ever to provide release review. Neither dissent controls.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The Louisiana courts must implement reversal by ending custody under the challenged insanity-acquittee basis. The mandate permits no indefinite continuation of that custody while the State searches for a substitute rationale. Any independently lawful custody requires its own established authority and procedures; the Court orders neither automatic release from a separate lawful restraint nor a new civil commitment. No future hearing date, factual diagnosis, new charge, compliance finding or retained Supreme Court proceeding is created.

**End of entry: Foucha v. Louisiana, merits decision, May 18, 1992.**

### Reno v. Flores, No. 91-905

Merits decision, March 23, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 13, 1992; decided March 23, 1993. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit's en banc judgment of August 9, 1991, 942 F.2d 1352, affirming the district court's May 25, 1988 relief against the juvenile-release regulation. #### Judgment

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Statutory authority for the juvenile release regulation

**Controlling proposition:** Section 1252(a)(1) permits the Attorney General to use the family-custodian and discretionary-release arrangements in 8 C.F.R. § 242.24 for temporary juvenile custody pending deportation proceedings; the regulation is not facially beyond that authority because it does not mandate release to every willing responsible adult. Custody remains subject to the statute's reasonable-dispatch limitation, its habeas remedy upon the requisite conclusive showing of unnecessary delay, the regulation's release avenues and the existing conditions agreement.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion for the Court, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six votes for this rule. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on a narrower statutory and facial-review ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress left room for reasonable arrangements governing detention and release while deportability is determined. Carlson v. Landon recognizes discretion within the immigration statute and requires a reasonable foundation for its exercise. The National Center for Immigrants' Rights decision permits general release conditions serving the immigration scheme, with individual safeguards, and does not confine every condition to physical attendance alone. Caring for a juvenile for whom an approved custodian is unavailable fits that statutory responsibility. The regulation permits parental designation and exceptional release, so it does not establish an absolute family-only barrier. The contrary reading would turn a discretionary custody statute into a command to entrust every otherwise releasable child to any willing adult. Neither the text nor the facial record requires that command. The Court does not decide the legality of particular prolonged custody, eliminate habeas review for unnecessary delay, or excuse breach of the conditions agreement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524 (1952): applies discretion bounded by the immigration statute; it does not authorize indefinite custody.
- National Center for Immigrants' Rights, December 16, 1991, decision: applies general immigration-release authority with preserved individual safeguards; does not enlarge that decision into universal detention authority.
- United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739 (1987): applies facial-review limits to the whole-regulation remedy.

##### 2. The facial substantive-due-process challenge fails

**Controlling proposition:** The Constitution does not give a juvenile without an available parent, guardian or close adult relative a fundamental right to placement with a willing unrelated adult instead of temporary, nonpunitive government care under this regulation and the binding conditions agreement. On this facial record, the family preferences and other release avenues reasonably serve care and attendance interests; individual unconstitutional confinement and enforcement of the agreement remain open.

**Authority:** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. O'Connor and Souter also explain the substantial liberty interest in avoiding institutional confinement in a separate concurrence; Stone-Zsela does not join this formulation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the particular placement entitlement asserted here is not a fundamental constitutional right. Schall v. Martin recognizes that juveniles are subject to protective custody and that their liberty interests operate within the need for responsible adult care. Parham v. J. R. likewise rejects an assumption that a minor can simply choose complete freedom from adult custody. The challenged rule selects custodians and provides alternatives; it does not prescribe punishment. The conditions agreement supplies the protective setting against which this facial case must be judged, without establishing that every facility in fact complies. Youngberg v. Romeo preserves constitutional limits on institutional treatment, and Foucha requires a lawful ground for continued confinement after an authorizing predicate ends. Neither establishes a general right to unrelated-adult placement during lawful temporary immigration custody. The Court rejects the claim that the regulation must fall in all applications. Particular unlawful restraints and violations of the agreement remain subject to appropriate proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Schall v. Martin, 467 U.S. 253 (1984): applies the distinction between protective juvenile custody and punishment.
- Parham v. J. R., 442 U.S. 584 (1979): applies the special circumstances of juvenile care; does not extinguish a child's liberty interest.
- Youngberg v. Romeo, 457 U.S. 307 (1982): preserves constitutional limits on institutional care.
- Foucha v. Louisiana, May 18, 1992, decision: distinguishes psychiatric confinement after its predicate ends; no universal custody test is inferred.

##### 3. Request-based review is not facially inadequate

**Controlling proposition:** The described examination, notice and opportunity to obtain immigration-judge review on request do not facially require replacement with an automatic hearing for every detained juvenile. That result depends on the available procedures and the absence of a record establishing general inability to invoke them; it does not approve ineffective notice, denial of requested review, improper delay or unlawful custody in a particular case.

**Authority:** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. Stone-Zsela agrees with setting aside the universal automatic-hearing remedy but reserves a distinct Mathews-based implementation inquiry.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the facial record does not justify an automatic immigration-judge hearing in every case. For a juvenile not seeking voluntary departure, the examination and custody decision occur within twenty-four hours; a different officer examines unless no qualified alternative is available. The notice form provides English and Spanish notice of review, and a waiver can be withdrawn by requesting a hearing. Immigration-judge review, followed by administrative and judicial review, supplies a means to contest custody. Fare v. Michael C. recognizes that juveniles can knowingly waive even criminal protections; age alone therefore does not prove universal inability to request this review. The Japanese Immigrant Case supplies the underlying entitlement to due process in deportation proceedings. A proposed individualized comparison with every willing adult restates the rejected placement entitlement. Respondents have not established universal ignorance of the review option or inevitable excessive delay. The Court does not decide a supported claim that notice, assistance or delay makes review ineffective for a child or class.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Fare v. Michael C., 442 U.S. 707 (1979): applies the recognition that juveniles can make a knowing waiver; no universal finding of comprehension follows.
- The Japanese Immigrant Case, 189 U.S. 86 (1903): applies aliens' entitlement to due process in deportation proceedings.

##### 4. The challenged classifications do not establish facial unequal treatment

**Controlling proposition:** The regulation's distinction between juveniles with available family custodians and those without them survives the facial equal-protection objection because family availability bears a rational relation to safe placement and attendance. The separate federal juvenile-delinquency release scheme does not require identical release rules for immigration custody, and this ruling does not resolve unequal administration in a particular case.

**Authority:** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on this component and adds no ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the classifications challenged here do not make the regulation facially unconstitutional. Family members and unrelated applicants for custody stand differently in relation to the child's continuing care, and the government may use that difference when arranging temporary custody. The regulation does not make family status an absolute bar to other placement: it provides parental designation and unusual-and-compelling-circumstances discretion. The objection therefore cannot proceed as though every child without an available relative must remain confined regardless of the regulation's alternatives. Schall v. Martin supplies the relevant recognition that protective juvenile arrangements may account for responsibility and care. The existence of different release provisions for federal juvenile-delinquency proceedings does not erase differences between that statutory setting and deportation proceedings. The Court does not hold that all children in the former system are citizens, that aliens lack equal-protection safeguards, or that discriminatory administration would be permissible. Its conclusion concerns the challenged classifications on this facial record.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Schall v. Martin, 467 U.S. 253 (1984): applies the legitimate relevance of protective care in juvenile custody; no general exemption from equal protection follows.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No precedent is overruled. National Center for Immigrants' Rights' individual-status and discretionary-relief safeguards remain controlling in their own channel. Foucha's psychiatric-custody holdings remain unchanged; the decision neither adopts a universal preventive-detention standard nor treats the conditions agreement as a finding of actual compliance.

Section 242.24 survives this facial attack. Subsection (b)(1) requires release in order of preference to a nondetained parent, legal guardian, or adult brother, sister, aunt, uncle or grandparent unless detention is required to secure timely appearance before the Service or immigration court, or ensure the juvenile's safety or that of others. If the only listed custodian is detained, (b)(2) provides discretionary case-by-case consideration of simultaneous release. Under (b)(3), a parent or guardian detained or outside the United States may designate another capable and willing person by sworn affidavit before an immigration or consular officer; that person must agree to care for the child and ensure presence at all future proceedings. Subsection (b)(4) permits other agreeing adult custodians only in unusual and compelling circumstances, at the district director's or chief patrol agent's discretion. These are distinct release avenues, not one undifferentiated exception.

Requested custody review and the enforceable 1987 conditions agreement remain. Section 1252(a)(1) makes the custody determination reviewable by habeas upon a conclusive showing that the Attorney General is not proceeding with the reasonable dispatch warranted by the particular facts and circumstances to determine deportability. No thirty-day legal cap is created. The twenty-four-hour examination and custody process described in the holding concerns a juvenile not seeking voluntary departure; the separate voluntary-departure safeguards are not adjudicated as universally sufficient here. No right to release to every willing unrelated adult and no automatic hearing for every detained juvenile is created. The Court does not decide particular excessive detention, defective notice, ineffective access to review or properly supported systemic implementation claims. Stone-Zsela's proposed means of remedying those defects, including necessary assistance or automatic review, remain noncontrolling.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O'Connor, joined by Souter, emphasizes that institutional custody seriously restrains a child's liberty even when no parent is available. She accepts the Court's judgment because the governing agreement calls for care comparable to licensed juvenile facilities, with education, recreation and reunification efforts, rather than punitive imprisonment. The concurrence does not approve arbitrary or punitive confinement and rests on the difference between lawful protective care and institutional punishment. Both Justices join the entire Court opinion.

Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on the limited facial showing. He recognizes freedom from institutional custody as a core liberty interest and reads § 1252(a)(1), Carlson and Chevron to permit temporary care directed to attendance and safe placement. Lawful initial custody does not answer a properly presented narrower challenge to a mandatory barrier causing unlawful continued detention. Administrative convenience cannot authorize unnecessary or indefinite custody. Under Mathews, age, language, assistance, notice and actual ability to seek review matter. A supported individual or systemic defect can require effective assistance, automatic review where necessary, or another remedy fitted to that defect. The record does not establish universal inability to request review, justify prescribing a hearing schedule, or require placement with every willing adult. He leaves the 1987 agreement intact.

Stevens, joined by Blackmun, would affirm. The dissent treats freedom from needless institutional custody as the right at stake and argues that a willing, responsible adult can protect care and attendance interests without detention. Carlson, Witkovich and National Center for Immigrants' Rights constrain immigration discretion to legitimate statutory ends; administrative convenience does not itself supply those ends. Schall's demonstrated preventive need does not validate a categorical refusal to consider suitable nonrelatives. The dissent also regards a child's inability to invoke review reliably as a reason for the neutral hearing the lower court required. Its statutory and constitutional objections do not command a majority.

### Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, Inc., No. 91-1200

Merits decision, March 24, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 9, 1992; decided March 24, 1993. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit's October 11, 1991 judgment, 946 F.2d 464, affirming declaratory and injunctive relief against the City's selective commercial-handbill newsrack prohibition. The questions concern the fit required for restrictions on commercial speech and the City's alternative defense that the prohibition is a content-neutral regulation of the time, place and manner of speech. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms, seven to two.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Commercial-speech prohibition and alternative time, place and manner defense | Affirmed, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | White, Thomas | Maintain declaratory and injunctive relief against the selective prohibition. |

No strict-scrutiny rule for all truthful commercial speech is adopted. The district court's rejection of a separate procedural-due-process claim was not cross-appealed and is not reopened.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Supports affirmance; supplies both controlling holdings | Throughout; seven Justices |
| Concurrence | Blackmun | No other Justice | Also joins the Court; would extend protection beyond its commercial-speech framework | Separate concurrence |
| Dissent | White | Thomas | Would sustain the City's incremental regulation | Dissent; two Justices |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The selected commercial category lacks the required fit

**Controlling proposition:** When truthful, nonmisleading commercial publications concerning lawful activity and newspapers cause the same asserted physical harms through their newsracks, the City must justify why its selected commercial category reasonably fits its safety and aesthetic interests. Cincinnati has not done so: removing respondents' sixty-two racks while retaining approximately 1,500–2,000 physically similar newspaper racks violates the First Amendment; a small reduction is not invalid merely for being small, and no least-restrictive-means or all-sources-at-once requirement follows.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter: seven, throughout.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the content distinction must do constitutional work connected to the City's asserted objectives. Central Hudson requires a substantial interest, direct advancement and an appropriately limited restriction of protected commercial speech. Board of Trustees v. Fox makes that fit reasonable and proportional, not necessarily perfect or the least restrictive available. Here safety and appearance are substantial concerns, but respondents' racks do not create different physical harms from the many newspaper racks the City leaves untouched. The lower value assigned to commercial speech does not explain the choice of this category to address those harms. Removing sixty-two racks produces some reduction; its size alone is not decisive. The defect is the unexplained selection among equivalent sources of the stated problem. Neutral size, location, number and maintenance rules illustrate alternatives relevant to fit without becoming mandatory alternatives. The Court does not abolish commercial-speech distinctions or adopt the view that all truthful commercial speech receives full strict scrutiny.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S. 557 (1980): applies the existing commercial-speech test to the accepted classification.
- Board of Trustees v. Fox, 492 U.S. 469 (1989): applies reasonable proportional fit and government burden; preserves rejection of least restrictive means.
- Metromedia, Inc. v. San Diego, 453 U.S. 490 (1981): distinguishes the billboard treatment; does not permit an unrelated content distinction without the required fit.

##### 2. The selective prohibition is not content neutral

**Controlling proposition:** A newsrack prohibition determined by whether its publication is a commercial handbill is content based when the asserted safety and aesthetic harms do not justify that distinction without reference to the publication's content. Cincinnati cannot sustain this selective ban as a content-neutral time, place and manner restriction merely because its objective is benign; otherwise lawful neutral regulation of newsracks remains available.

**Authority:** Stevens's Court opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter: seven. This rejects the City's independent time, place and manner defense.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the City cannot convert this selective prohibition into a neutral regulation by describing its objective as safety or appearance. Ward v. Rock Against Racism and Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence permit appropriate regulation of the time, place and manner of speech when it is justified without reference to content. The operative distinction here requires looking at the publication and excludes commercial handbills while leaving newspapers in the same physical setting. The City's interests explain concern about racks generally but do not explain that selection. Renton v. Playtime Theatres permits attention to distinct secondary effects; the record identifies no comparable difference between the physical effects of these categories of racks. Benign motive therefore does not establish content neutrality. The Court does not hold that every regulation touching commercial expression is invalid or that the City lacks authority over sidewalks. A properly drawn neutral regulation remains subject to the established standards and may address the demonstrated physical problems.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781 (1989): applies justification without reference to content; a benign objective alone does not validate the selective line.
- Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence, 468 U.S. 288 (1984): preserves valid content-neutral time, place and manner regulation.
- Renton v. Playtime Theatres, Inc., 475 U.S. 41 (1986): distinguishes different secondary effects; no such difference justifies this rack classification.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Central Hudson and Fox remain the governing commercial-speech framework; Metromedia is distinguished, not overruled. Ward, Clark and Renton retain their existing operation. The decision creates no general rule equating commercial and noncommercial speech. Casey's bounded medical-information holdings and the other speech decisions remain in their distinct settings.

The particular selective ban fails reasonable fit and cannot be upheld as content-neutral time, place and manner regulation. Incremental regulation remains possible, and alternatives matter without imposing least restrictive means. A city must explain a content distinction's relation to the harms it invokes; physical rack rules remain available if otherwise lawful. No claim about all newsrack restrictions, misleading advertising, unlawful transactions or every commercial classification is resolved.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun joins the Court but would afford truthful, noncoercive commercial speech about lawful activity the full protection ordinarily afforded speech. Virginia Pharmacy protects the public's interest in receiving useful commercial information; commercial motivation alone does not justify reduced protection. Deceptive or coercive practices, offers of unlawful transactions and appropriate time, place and manner regulation remain regulable. His broader rule is not needed to invalidate this selective rack prohibition and does not replace the Court's Central Hudson/Fox analysis.

White, joined by Thomas, would uphold the ordinance's application. The dissent sees each removed rack as a real reduction of obstruction and visual clutter and allows the City to proceed incrementally. Metromedia permits differing treatment of commercial advertising, and Fox rejects a demand that government use the least restrictive means. In the dissent's view, the accepted distinction between commercial publications and newspapers permits the City to preserve the latter while addressing a portion of the physical problem. The dissent warns that the Court's use of the small affected fraction and alternative regulations undervalues local discretion. The Court answers that neither small scale nor incremental action alone is the defect; the unexplained content selection is.

#### Judgment

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |

| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | White, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy | Supports affirmance; supplies all three holdings | Throughout; five Justices |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Excusable neglect can include carelessness

**Controlling proposition:** For a Chapter 11 proof-of-claim deadline subject to Bankruptcy Rule 9006(b)(1), neglect includes inadvertence, mistake or carelessness as well as circumstances beyond the actor's control, but relief after expiration requires a motion and excusability. Excusability depends on all relevant circumstances, including prejudice to the debtor, length of delay and its effect on proceedings, the reason for delay including reasonable control, and good faith; this authority does not enlarge deadlines governed by Rule 9006(b)(2)'s prohibitions or (b)(3)'s specific-rule limitations.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's opinion for the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy: five.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that neglect is not confined to events a diligent person could not control. The ordinary meaning includes a failure to act through carelessness, and the rule's separate word excusable determines which failures warrant relief. The bankruptcy rules distinguish the broad enlargement authority in subsection (b)(1) from express exceptions in subsections (b)(2) and (b)(3). Taylor v. Freeland & Kronz enforced one such specific limitation; it does not eliminate the discretion the different rule grants here. Excusability is an equitable judgment considering the circumstances of the omission, including its reason and consequences. The contrary construction would exclude inadvertence before considering whether an unusual setting makes it excusable. Yet the rule does not make every missed deadline forgivable. Ordinary ignorance of clear rules or inadvertence ordinarily will not suffice, and good faith or absence of prejudice alone is not a substitute for explaining the failure. No ruling is made for deadlines whose own express provisions foreclose this enlargement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Taylor v. Freeland & Kronz, April 21, 1992, decision: distinguishes the Rule 4003(b)/9006(b)(3) exemption-objection limit; preserves its strict operation.
- Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation, 497 U.S. 871 (1990): distinguishes the requirement of a supported post-deadline request; does not confine every qualifying neglect to uncontrollable events.

##### 2. Counsel's conduct is attributed to the creditor

**Controlling proposition:** A creditor seeking relief under Rule 9006(b)(1) is accountable for the acts and omissions of its freely selected counsel; the court must evaluate the neglect of both client and lawyer together. Reasonable client reliance on counsel does not create an exception from that attribution or independently require an extension.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Court opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy: five; the dissent does not dispute attorney attribution, but its disagreement about excusability does not create another rationale majority.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that retaining a lawyer does not transfer the consequences of litigation conduct away from the client. Link v. Wabash Railroad treats the attorney as the party's representative and holds the party accountable for the representative's omissions. United States v. Boyle likewise rejects reliance on an agent as an automatic excuse for missing a known filing duty. These principles apply when a bankruptcy creditor requests an extension after the claims deadline. The Court of Appeals therefore erred to the extent it treated the creditors' reliance on counsel's assurance as a reason to disregard counsel's neglect. That error does not require reversal of the result because excusability must still be decided under the rule's equitable standard with the lawyer's conduct included. The Court does not absolve experienced counsel of responsibility, guarantee relief to an innocent client, or decide a dispute between client and attorney. It evaluates this application on the full attributed conduct and the circumstances of this notice.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Link v. Wabash Railroad Co., 370 U.S. 626 (1962): applies attribution of freely selected counsel's conduct to the client.
- United States v. Boyle, 469 U.S. 241 (1985): applies the rejection of reliance on an agent as an automatic filing excuse.

##### 3. This record supports allowing the late proofs of claim

**Controlling proposition:** With counsel's conduct attributed to the creditors, the unusually placed and ambiguous bar-date notice, the twenty-day delay, and the undisturbed findings of no prejudice, no adverse administrative effect and good faith support excusing the missed Chapter 11 deadline here. The late claims may be filed; the decision does not establish their substantive validity or amount, excuse ordinary disregard of a clear notice, or give dispositive weight to counsel's office disruption.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Court opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy: five; the Sixth Circuit's judgment is affirmed on the corrected attribution analysis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the particular notice and the undisturbed equitable findings justify relief. The deadline appeared inconspicuously in a notice styled as a meeting-of-creditors notice, without the clarity of the usual separate bar-date notice. That unusual presentation matters even though the creditors received notice and counsel bore responsibility for reading it. The bankruptcy court found no prejudice, no adverse effect from the twenty-day delay, and good faith by both creditors and counsel. The Court gives little weight to counsel's withdrawal from his firm and disruption of his practice; Link still charges his negligence to the creditors. The contrary view gives controlling force to responsibility for the omission while excluding the unusual notice and other circumstances from a single equitable evaluation. The Court does not excuse every inattentive lawyer or require prejudice before a deadline can be enforced. It permits these proofs of claim under this record, leaving their merits and the ordinary administration of the estate for the bankruptcy proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Link v. Wabash Railroad Co., 370 U.S. 626 (1962): applies full attorney attribution while permitting the separate excusability inquiry authorized by this rule.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Taylor's express exemption-objection deadline remains unchanged; Rule 9006(b)(1) does not displace (b)(3). Link and Boyle remain controlling on party responsibility for an agent. Lujan's requirement that post-deadline relief actually satisfy the applicable neglect rule is preserved. The Court adopts the four identified considerations as part of an all-circumstances inquiry here, not as a preexisting Supreme Court test or an exhaustive checklist.

A Chapter 11 movant under Rule 9006(b)(1) may establish excusable neglect even when the omission involves carelessness, but must justify it on all relevant circumstances with counsel's conduct attributed. No factor alone guarantees relief. Rule 9006(b)(2) bars enlargement for the actions governed by Rules 1007(d), 1017(b)(3), 2003(a) and (d), 7052, 9023 and 9024; Rule 9006(b)(3) permits enlargement only to the extent and on the conditions stated in Rules 1006(b)(2), 1017(e), 3002(c), 4003(b), 4004(a), 4007(c), 8002 and 9033. Those express limits remain distinct from the present Chapter 11 authority; in particular Chapter 7 Rule 3002(c) and Taylor's Rule 4003(b) do not gain a general equitable exception. Claims allowed to be filed remain subject to ordinary merits adjudication.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Souter and Thomas, would reverse. The dissent reads excusable neglect as requiring a sufficiently excusable reason for the missed deadline before favorable surrounding equities can support relief. It does not deny that neglect can include a careless omission or that circumstances matter, but it rejects treating no prejudice and good faith as a substitute for a satisfactory explanation. Rule deadlines must remain enforceable even when a late filing would cause little inconvenience. Counsel's conduct binds these sophisticated creditors under Link; they received the notice, and their lawyer had the file. The bankruptcy court found negligence and a degree of indifference. The dissent also objects that unusual notice was not found to have caused counsel's failure, so the Court should not turn an appellate observation into a lower-court causal finding. If further findings were indispensable, remand rather than outright allowance would be the proper response. These objections do not prevail; the majority considers the actual notice and the undisturbed equitable findings with full attribution.

Rose involved a separable erroneous presumption and therefore does not establish harmlessness where the entire adjudicative foundation is missing. Teague nonretroactivity is not jurisdictional. The State's legitimate interest in finality does not turn a nonjurisdictional defense into an unavoidable threshold in this procedural posture.

The Court applies Collins v. Youngblood for the nonjurisdictional character of nonretroactivity and the resulting discretion. As to Teague v. Lane, its substantive nonretroactivity rule and contemporary exceptions are left unchanged; they are not applied to dispose of this case.

Considering the intentional-killing aggravator in the initial sentencing phase of this prosecution did not itself place Schiro in jeopardy a second time. Acquittal-like capital determinations remain protected, but the jury's advisory recommendation against death was not a final acquittal of a statutory aggravator or sentence under this scheme.

The holding rests on O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, seven Justices.

The Court distinguishes a second prosecution from the first determination of punishment. Stroud and DiFrancesco establish that the ordinary sentencing process is not another trial for the offense merely because it considers facts used to determine punishment. Bullington and Rumsey protect an acquittal-like determination that the prosecution has failed to establish entitlement to death; they do not make every step within an initial prosecution a separate jeopardy. Here the jury's sentencing recommendation was advisory. The judge was required to consider it but was not bound by it, and the recommendation disclosed no finding that the intentional-killing aggravator had failed. The Court does not infer such a finding from the recommendation alone. Dixon's retention of Grady continues to protect against a later prosecution where its conditions are met; that trigger is not supplied by this initial sentencing. The separate question whether the guilt verdict necessarily rejected intent remains subject to its own analysis.

The Court applies Stroud v. United States and United States v. DiFrancesco to distinguish initial sentencing from successive prosecution. The Court preserves Bullington v. Missouri and Arizona v. Rumsey for acquittal-like capital determinations; distinguished from this advisory recommendation and initial sentencing. As to United States v. Dixon and Grady v. Corbin, successive-prosecution protection remains in force; this case does not supply its later-prosecution predicate. As to Sochor v. Florida, its distinction between an unparticularized recommendation and an actual aggravator finding remains intact.

Assuming issue preclusion can operate between guilt and sentencing in one prosecution, the claimant must show from the pleadings, evidence, charge and other relevant record that the jury necessarily decided the disputed ultimate fact in his favor. Schiro did not make that showing: the Count II felony-murder conviction and blank alternative forms could rationally reflect selection of one murder verdict without rejecting knowing or intentional killing.

The holding rests on O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, seven Justices.

Ashe requires a practical examination of what the jury necessarily decided, rather than treating an unused form as a finding by itself. Green protects an implied acquittal when the verdict actually carries that significance; it does not dispense with examining this record. Count I charged knowing killing, whereas the sentencing aggravator required intentional killing during rape. The ten forms included one general not-guilty form and alternative murder dispositions. The court did not expressly demand a separate verdict on each count. Counsel described selection of a single verdict, while Instruction 8's mental-state language supplied another possible understanding of the charge without conclusively establishing how jurors used it. These features permit a rational explanation of the Count II verdict that does not reject intent. Schiro's competing interpretation therefore does not establish the necessary determination required by Ashe and Dowling. This conclusion makes no affirmative finding of intent, decides no independent jury-sentencing claim, and leaves interphase preclusion's general availability unresolved.

The Court applies Ashe v. Swenson to the necessary determination of an ultimate fact on the full record. The Court preserves the implied-acquittal protection of Green v. United States; its result does not control these alternative forms and instructions. The Court applies Dowling v. United States for the claimant's burden to identify an issue actually and necessarily determined; no new evidentiary rule is adopted. The limits in Griffin v. United States and Sochor v. Florida on inferring specific findings from general verdicts and recommendations remain distinct from actual acquittal protection.

No decision is overruled. Dixon retains Grady; Bullington and Rumsey continue to protect acquittal-like capital determinations, and Ashe and Green continue to protect facts necessarily resolved by an actual or implied acquittal.

Effective January 19, 1994, late Teague presentation may be declined in this posture; initial sentencing is not itself a later prosecution; and this record fails to establish necessary rejection of intent. The Court does not decide whether issue preclusion generally operates between guilt and penalty phases, whether the judge correctly found intent on the merits, an independent constitutional jury-factfinding claim, or other unpresented capital claims. No categorical rule that blank verdict forms never constitute acquittals is adopted.

Stevens dissents, joined by Blackmun. They would reverse the denial of habeas relief as to death while preserving the felony-murder conviction. They read the completed guilt verdict as rejecting the knowing-killing accusation and regard intentional killing as incompatible with that rejection. Green's protection of an implied acquittal and Ashe's practical inquiry require the sentencing judge to respect the jury's choice, particularly where mental culpability was contested. They reject treating the prosecutor's description of one verdict as a judicial instruction. His separate ground does not acquit Schiro of felony murder, announce a general prohibition on judicial capital sentencing, or make the advisory recommendation alone dispositive.

### Heintz v. Jenkins, No. 94-367

Merits decision, April 18, 1995

Argued February 21, 1995; decided April 18, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, reviewing its May 27, 1994 judgment, 25 F.3d 536. The question is whether the FDCPA applies to an attorney who regularly collects consumer debts through litigation.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The Court unanimously affirms the Seventh Circuit's reversal of the dismissal, allowing Jenkins's FDCPA complaint to proceed without a categorical attorney-litigation exemption. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer support affirmance, 9–0. Actual regularity, liability for the letter or charge, contractual authorization, and defenses remain for further proceedings under the statute's ordinary terms. The Court makes no merits determination on those matters and awards no damages or fees.

Justice Souter delivers the sole opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. All nine directly adopt the regular-attorney and litigation-coverage rule, together with the reservations concerning ordinary elements and defenses, stated below.

The FDCPA applies to an attorney who regularly collects or attempts to collect covered consumer debts owed or asserted to be owed another, including through litigation, subject to the statute's actual definition, exceptions, elements and defenses. There is no categorical attorney-litigation exemption; the decision does not cover lawyers merely because they practice law or establish that this settlement letter violated the Act.

The Court holds that collection litigation is a means of collecting a debt within the enacted definition. The statute covers regular direct or indirect collection for another, and Congress deleted the former attorney exemption in 1986. Gardner supports enforcing those retained conditions without restoring an exclusion Congress removed. The strongest contrary argument is that rules regulating collection threats and communications can interact awkwardly with ordinary judicial proceedings. That concern does not justify excluding every litigating attorney from the definition. Congress retained an activity-specific exclusion for serving or attempting to serve legal process in connection with judicial debt enforcement, not a general occupational exclusion. Questions about a particular threat, communication or defense must be decided under the provision actually presented. An unsuccessful collection action is not automatically unlawful, and coverage does not prove the alleged insurance demand false or unauthorized. The complaint therefore returns for the ordinary inquiries into regularity, contractual authorization, alleged misrepresentation and defenses, without a liability or damages finding.

Brown v. Gardner, December 12, 1994, supports respect for enacted conditions and rejection of an added category restriction; it creates no new FDCPA proof rule and resolves no conduct liability. The Court rejects Green v. Hocking, 9 F.3d 18, insofar as it adopts a categorical litigation exemption inconsistent with the enacted definition and the repeal of the attorney exemption. That lower-court approach supplies no continuing exemption.

A covered debt is an obligation or alleged obligation of a consumer to pay money arising from a transaction whose money, property, insurance or services are primarily for personal, family or household purposes, whether or not reduced to judgment. The retained §1692a(6)(D) exception concerns a person while serving or attempting to serve legal process on another in connection with judicial enforcement of a debt; it does not create a whole-attorney exemption. No factual statutory exception is established here.

Section 1692e prohibits false, deceptive or misleading representations in debt collection; §1692f(1) addresses collection of an amount not expressly authorized by the agreement creating the debt or permitted by law. The alleged $4,173 charge remains to be tested under those provisions. Section 1692e(5) concerns threatened action that cannot legally be taken or is not intended; failure to prevail alone does not necessarily satisfy it. No authoritative resolution of those conduct applications is made.

The communication and defense provisions invoked in the objection retain their exact conditions. Under §1692c(c), a consumer's written refusal to pay or written request to cease communication bars further communications except to advise that collection efforts are terminated, to notify that the collector or creditor may invoke specified remedies ordinarily invoked by that collector or creditor, or, where applicable, to notify that the collector or creditor intends to invoke a specified remedy. A mailed notice is complete on receipt. These are alternative permissions, not mandatory notices. Under §1692k(c), the collector must prove by a preponderance that a violation was unintentional and resulted from bona fide error despite procedures reasonably adapted to avoid it; no general immunity for legal error is decided. Under §1692k(e), good-faith conformity with an FTC advisory opinion protects an act or omission even if the opinion is later amended, rescinded or held invalid; staff commentary is not automatically such an opinion. Section 814(d) denies the FTC and the other agencies assigned enforcement responsibility under §814(b) authority to promulgate trade-regulation rules or other regulations concerning collection of debts by debt collectors as defined in the Act. No defense is established on these allegations, and no comprehensive litigation exception or fee entitlement is adopted.

Gardner supports applying the statutory conditions without a judicial category exemption. Green v. Hocking's contrary lower-court exemption is rejected. The decision settles regular-attorney/litigation coverage, not authoritative interpretations of every conduct-regulating provision or defense.

Effective April 18, 1995, an attorney's regular collection of covered consumer debts for another is not exempt because it occurs through litigation. Coverage remains distinct from the ordinary elements and defenses of a particular claim. Neither a failed lawsuit nor the presence of a settlement letter automatically creates liability, privilege, good faith, damages or fees. The actual statutory exceptions operate on their stated terms, and no comprehensive litigation exemption is created.

The [Seventh Circuit opinion, 25 F.3d 536](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/025/25.F3d.536.93-2861.html) supplies the allegations, lower exemption ruling and procedural disposition. The [original FDCPA, 91 Stat. 874–883](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-91/pdf/STATUTE-91-Pg874.pdf) supplies the relevant definition, conduct, communication and defense terms. The [1986 amendment, 100 Stat. 768](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-100/pdf/STATUTE-100-Pg768.pdf) deletes the attorney exemption. No later pleading amendment supplies a rule here.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The complaint returns for proceedings on actual regularity, contractual authorization, alleged misrepresentation or unauthorized amount, and any applicable defense. The $4,173 charge remains disputed. No liability, damages, attorney-fee, litigation-privilege or good-faith determination is made by this coverage decision.

### Rubin v. Coors Brewing Co., No. 93-1631

Merits decision, April 19, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 30, 1994; decided April 19, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, reviewing 2 F.3d 355 (August 23, 1993). The question is whether the federal ban on truthful numerical alcohol-content statements on malt-beverage labels satisfies the First Amendment. The separately upheld, unappealed advertising restriction is outside the judgment under review.

Section 205(e)(2) prohibits malt-beverage alcohol-content statements unless state law requires them. Its wine clause requires alcohol-content statements only above fourteen percent by volume; it does not prohibit lower-strength wine disclosure. Section 205(f)(2), addressing advertising, contains an alcohol-information exception for malt beverages and wines, without the labeling clause’s state-required-disclosure exception. A later interstate malt-beverage paragraph expressly addresses both labeling and advertising and conditions their application on similar state requirements for comparable intrastate transactions. The agency administers the label ban except where state law affirmatively requires disclosure, but administers the advertising ban where a corresponding state advertising prohibition exists. This is the operating construction, not a holding that the later paragraph mentions only advertising or a new statutory challenge to that construction.

Under the challenged §7.26 regime, state-required statements follow the manner specified by the State; absent such a specification, they use percentage by weight or volume rather than proof, maximums or minimums. Low or reduced alcohol requires less than 2.5 percent alcohol by volume. Non-alcoholic requires the directly conjoined, readily legible, contrasting-background statement that the product contains less than 0.5 percent alcohol by volume. Alcohol-free requires no alcohol. These distinct permissions do not provide all useful numerical comparison information. Sections 7.29(f) and (g), concerning strength descriptions and numerical or design suggestions of strength, are outside this adjudication. Separate outdoor-advertising and publisher/radio provisions are not conflated with the state-law clauses or independently adjudicated. The April 1993 interim suspension of regulatory enforcement complied with the injunction; it was not statutory repeal or a concession of invalidity.

The Government invokes strength competition, possible shifts in consumer preferences, and assistance to state alcohol policy. Coors contests the effectiveness and breadth of suppressing accurate percentages while other channels and beverages receive different treatment. No independently verified fifty-state tally or future strength war is found. The Court assumes the state-assistance rationale may be considered and rejects its unsupported justification on the merits; it does not decide forfeiture.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated.

#### Judgment

The Court unanimously affirms the judgment sustaining the injunction against the challenged prohibition of truthful numerical alcohol-content statements on malt-beverage labels. Eight Justices join the Court’s commercial-speech grounds; Justice Stevens concurs in the judgment.

The unappealed advertising judgment remains undisturbed. The Court orders no national label format, alcohol ceiling, tax, or replacement regulation and invalidates no separate state statute or descriptive-strength provision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The challenged scheme does not materially advance its asserted health interest

**Controlling proposition:** The Government may not suppress truthful, nonmisleading numerical alcohol-content statements on lawful malt-beverage labels unless the restriction directly and materially advances a substantial interest. Preventing strength competition and its health harms is substantial, but the challenged scheme fails that requirement on this record; predictive judgment is permissible, while abstract concern and unexplained inconsistencies do not establish the necessary connection.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight direct votes. Stevens agrees that the scheme fails in practice but supplies a distinct judgment ground rather than a framework join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this numerical-label ban has not been shown materially to reduce the strength competition invoked to defend it. Central Hudson requires a real connection between the restriction and the substantial interest; Edenfield requires justification for the actual restraint. The operating scheme permits alcohol information for other beverages, permits malt-beverage advertising where no corresponding state prohibition applies, and yields to state-required label disclosure. Those features leave comparable incentives operating while suppressing useful factual labels. The Court does not demand identical rules for all beverages, controlled studies in every case, or regulation of all sources at once. Edge permits a demonstrated incremental accommodation, while Discovery Network and Lanphere require the chosen distinction to address the asserted harm. The Government’s predictive argument and concern that existing preferences reflect the ban deserve consideration. They do not explain how this scheme materially works despite its inconsistencies. The holding concerns accurate, nonmisleading percentages, not deceptive claims or direct control of alcohol strength.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S. 557: applies its substantial-interest and direct-advancement requirements.
- Edenfield v. Fane, April 26, 1993: applies the requirement of responsive justification for the actual commercial-speech restriction.
- Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993, and Lanphere & Urbaniak v. Colorado, April 18, 1995: apply the requirement that the selected speech restriction address the demonstrated harm, without an all-sources-at-once rule.
- United States v. Edge Broadcasting Co., June 25, 1993: distinguishes its supported state-based scheme; its holding creates no categorical vice-speech exemption.

##### The ban independently lacks reasonable proportional fit

**Controlling proposition:** Independently of the failure of material advancement, the categorical suppression of accurate numerical beer-label information lacks a reasonable fit to the asserted strength-competition harm in light of its unexplained breadth and workable measures directed more closely at that harm. Reasonable fit requires proportionality, not the least restrictive imaginable means, and the Court prescribes no particular substitute regulation.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Court opinion and the same eight direct joiners adopt this independently sufficient ground. Stevens’s distinct concurrence is not counted as a Fox-framework vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the label prohibition is disproportionate to the harm offered to justify it. Fox requires a reasonable fit, not perfection or the least restrictive conceivable choice. Here direct regulation of alcohol strength, focused controls on promotion emphasizing high strength, and a restriction directed to the allegedly affected market segment provide concrete comparisons with a ban on every accurate percentage. The Government answers that national strength limits could displace state choices and that a malt-liquor limitation might miss competition elsewhere. Those objections do not explain suppressing useful comparison information across the challenged category, including information that may assist selection of weaker products. Discovery Network and Lanphere require an account of that actual burden; Ibanez likewise requires a justification responsive to the selected restriction. The existing record and litigated district-court ground permit this alternative holding although the court of appeals stopped at advancement. The Court orders no tax, strength ceiling, national label design or replacement program, and leaves properly justified deceptive-claim regulation available.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Board of Trustees v. Fox, 492 U.S. 469: applies reasonable proportional fit and preserves rejection of a least-restrictive-means requirement.
- Discovery Network and Lanphere: apply their distinct fit inquiry to the burden and supported alternatives here.
- Ibanez v. Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation, June 13, 1994: applies the need for justification responsive to the actual truthful-information burden.

##### The asserted need to assist state policy supplies no alternative justification on this record

**Controlling proposition:** Assuming the Government’s state-assistance rationale is properly considered, its unsubstantiated assertion that federal suppression of accurate label information is needed to facilitate state alcohol policy does not justify this restriction. Legitimate accommodation of differing state policies remains possible when its need and operation are supported; federalism and the Twenty-first Amendment do not alone replace First Amendment scrutiny.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Court opinion and the same eight Justices directly adopt this rejection of the asserted alternative justification. The Court decides no independent forfeiture issue.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government has not established the particular state-policy justification advanced for federal label suppression. Edge recognizes that an effective accommodation of differing state policies may satisfy commercial-speech review. It does not treat an invocation of state authority as proof of the need for a federal information ban. Here the Government has not demonstrated why States cannot operate their own alcohol policies without this suppression of truthful percentages. The statutory state-law provisions and the agency’s differing administration of labeling and advertising describe the scheme; they do not themselves prove its necessity or effect. The Court assumes the rationale may be considered and therefore need not decide Coors’s preservation objection. Nor does it deny the States’ authority to address alcohol harms or hold that federal assistance can never be legitimate. On this record the asserted assistance supplies neither a sufficient independent justification nor an answer to the demonstrated defects in advancement and proportional fit.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Edge Broadcasting: preserves the legitimacy of supported state-policy accommodation while distinguishing the missing demonstrated mechanism here.
- Central Hudson: requires the asserted justification to support the actual speech restriction rather than stand as an unexamined label.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Central Hudson and Fox remain the commercial-speech framework. Discovery Network, Edenfield, Ibanez and Lanphere supply the actual requirements of a harm-related classification, responsive evidence, and reasonable fit. Edge preserves supported incremental and state-based regulation at its controlling scope. No precedent is overruled; no categorical vice exception, strict-scrutiny rule for all commercial speech, or least-restrictive-means requirement is adopted.

The challenged truthful numerical malt-beverage label ban fails both material advancement and, independently, reasonable proportional fit. The unsupported state-assistance rationale does not cure either defect. The advertising judgment, distinct strength-description and design rules, state enactments and other applications of §205 remain outside the relief. Lawful direct regulation and properly justified measures against deception remain available without a judicially prescribed replacement program.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Stevens concurs in the judgment. Accurate, unadorned information about a lawful product cannot be suppressed here simply because consumers may use it to choose a stronger beverage. His Edge dissent identifies the objection to keeping consumers uninformed, while Discovery Network requires the selected speech category to relate to an actual harm. He agrees that this scheme fails to advance the stated objective, but does not suggest that greater success at withholding truthful knowledge would cure the underlying objection. His Lanphere joinder in a bounded Central Hudson application did not abandon that distinct ground. He does not deny the Government’s authority to regulate alcohol’s harms directly, forbid deceptive claims, or require properly justified useful disclosures. He declines to join the Court’s commercial-speech framework, its acceptance of the information-suppression interest as framed, or its Fox alternative holding. His truthful-information rationale is noncontrolling; the eight-Justice Court opinion controls without combining separate grounds.

The Government may pursue lawful, appropriately justified alternatives, but the Court commands no particular replacement. The unappealed advertising judgment and separate descriptive-strength provisions are undisturbed.

**Stevens, concurring, joined by Ginsburg and Breyer.** These Justices join the Court's construction, full Bramblett treatment, and count-specific remedy. They supply an additional case-specific explanation for correcting the precedent. The judicial-function decisions have become an entrenched legal development attempting to protect adversarial proceedings from Bramblett's broad language. Those lower decisions do not authorize vertical disobedience or themselves overrule this Court. Their sustained development nevertheless deserves respect in assessing the consequences of the original construction. Correcting the entity boundary preserves their essential protection while dispensing with a function line the enacted language does not contain.

The statute's definitions and actual context supply the first-instance construction, but disagreement with Bramblett is not the entire precedent inquiry. Hilton and Allied-Bruce show why statutory precedent has institutional force and why settled arrangements matter. Here the combination of the particular definitional defect, the entrenched limiting development, and the qualified reliance record supports correction. The parties agree that prosecution guidance favored other charges in the described court-statement setting, but differ about its scope. That evidence weakens an assertion of indispensable, uniform reliance on litigation prosecutions; it proves neither total nonenforcement nor Hubbard's personal reliance. Congress's silence is not ratification. Crandon supports attention to criminal statutory setting and fair warning, without supplying a rule that every criminal precedent may be discarded.

The ordinary knowledge, falsity, and disclosure-duty requirements protect lawful advocacy but do not remove every cost of threatened prosecution or disputed classification. These Justices recognize that concern without finding an empirically established chilling effect or a right to lie. **Scalia, concurring, joined by Kennedy.** These Justices also join the Court's construction, express overruling, and precise remedy. They reject treating the lower courts' limiting decisions as a competing body of authority entitled to displace this Court's statutory construction. Those cases instead demonstrate the serious litigation risks generated over time by the erroneous expansion and the absence of a rational repair grounded in the text. That combination supplies the reason beyond statutory error for overruling here. It would be incoherent to preserve Bramblett's legislative rationale by treating its judicial language as dispensable, because the same all-branch construction produced both.

Patterson requires a special justification; neither Kennedy's Hilton opinion nor his Allied-Bruce join imposes unconditional retention of an erroneous criminal construction. Scalia's Crandon concurrence, which Kennedy joined, takes cautious legal advice and inconsistent executive practice seriously but expressly distinguishes such practice from established judicial precedent. Concrete reliance on applying §1001 to adversarial court filings is slight, while perjury, contempt, bankruptcy fraud, obstruction, and other enacted offenses remain available on their own terms. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The withdrawn review route and retained original jurisdiction

**Controlling proposition:** Section 2244(b)(3)(E) bars ordinary certiorari review of a court of appeals' grant or denial of authorization to file a second or successive district-court habeas application; the present certiorari request must be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. The provision does not repeal the distinct original habeas authority retained by §2241, which permits independent consideration of this petition without affirming or reviewing the circuit's authorization judgment.

**Authority:** Part I-A, joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The review bar expressly addresses the circuit's authorization determination. Section 2241 separately names this Court and its Justices as habeas decisionmakers, and AEDPA leaves that grant intact. Section 2244(b)(3)(A)'s advance-authorization mechanism expressly precedes filing in a district court; it does not direct applicants to obtain circuit permission before filing an original petition here. The amended circuit-transfer rule likewise does not name this Court.

McCardle requires respect for withdrawal of a specified appellate route. Yerger preserves a separate habeas source against an unwarranted implied repeal, and Bollman explains its statutory source and constitutional appellate character. An application filed directly here is called original as a matter of procedure; it is not an expansion of Article III's enumerated original jurisdiction. Section 1651 authorizes writs in aid of jurisdiction independently possessed, not a new freestanding source. The Court does not decide every possible certified-question, interlocutory or other supervisory route not presented here.

##### Temporal operation of the channel rule

**Controlling proposition:** The authorization and review proceedings begun after AEDPA's April 24, 1996 enactment are governed by its specified authorization-review bar. That conclusion does not decide the temporal reach of every Title I provision or apply a new substantive restriction backward merely because a petition is pending.

**Authority:** All nine adopt this channel result. Part I-B's explanation is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; Thomas, Scalia and Kennedy concur through the distinct relevant-activity method.

**Controlling explanation:** Landgraf requires attention first to Congress's temporal direction and otherwise to whether applying the particular provision would attach new legal consequences to completed events. A procedural or jurisdictional label alone supplies no answer. This provision governs the new May authorization determination and the ensuing request for its review. Its application to that post-enactment proceeding does not depend on retroactively changing the lawfulness of trial conduct. The express pending-case direction for chapter 154 does not automatically govern every different amendment; Georgia's qualification under that chapter is not established or assumed here.

**Controlling proposition:** The district-court authorization mechanism does not govern filing this original application. Sections 2244(b)(1)–(2) inform the Court's consideration of repeated applications, but whether those substantive provisions bind this Court directly is reserved. Retained jurisdiction does not itself entitle an applicant to an original writ.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by all nine Justices.

**Operative distinctions:** For the second or successive §2254 applications described by the statute, paragraph (1) requires dismissal of a claim already presented in a prior application. Paragraph (2) requires dismissal of a new claim unless either it relies on a previously unavailable new constitutional rule made retroactive to collateral cases by the Supreme Court, or both of these conditions are met: the factual predicate could not previously have been discovered through due diligence; and the facts, if proved and viewed with the evidence as a whole, establish clearly and convincingly that, but for constitutional error, no reasonable factfinder would have found the applicant guilty of the underlying offense.

Paragraph (3) requires permission from the appropriate court of appeals before district-court filing; a three-judge panel decides whether there is a prima facie showing and must act within thirty days. Its grant or denial is not appealable and is not subject to a rehearing or certiorari petition. Under paragraph (4), authorization does not finally establish entitlement: the district court must dismiss an authorized claim unless the applicant establishes the statutory requirements.

**Controlling explanation:** McCleskey recognizes lawful restrictions on abusive successive requests; it does not collapse these distinct statutory conditions. A request that this Court announce a new rule is not reliance on an already announced, previously unavailable rule made retroactive by this Court. New medical disagreement, an assertion of innocence or satisfaction of some other gateway does not automatically establish paragraph (2)(B)'s cumulative diligence, constitutional-error and clear-and-convincing conditions. The present submission does not establish those conditions; the Court creates no new default, waiver or affirmative diligence-failure finding to reach that assessment.

Rule 20 separately requires the petition to explain why relief was not sought in district court, state how and where state remedies were exhausted or §2254(b) otherwise satisfied, demonstrate exceptional circumstances warranting this Court's discretionary exercise, and show that adequate relief cannot be obtained in another form or court. Section 2254(b)(1)'s alternatives are exhaustion of available state-court remedies, absence of available state corrective process, or circumstances rendering that process ineffective to protect the applicant's rights. Paragraph (2) separately permits, but does not require, merits denial despite nonexhaustion. Under paragraph (3), a State is not deemed to waive exhaustion or estopped from invoking it unless the State through counsel expressly waives it. No exhaustion, process-availability, effectiveness or waiver question is decided here. The denial below explains the direct request, but does not prove its substantive sufficiency. The Court denies this petition for want of the exceptional original showing; it does not find an otherwise disputed state remedy conclusively available or adequate. Section 2241 permits a lawful transfer, but none is ordered.

##### The constitutional objection in this case

**Controlling proposition:** The specified review restriction and consideration of the operative successive scheme do not suspend constitutionally sufficient habeas review or prescribe a forbidden judicial outcome as applied to these claims. The original petition receives independent examination and fails on an adequate traditional original-writ ground, without deciding that every future statutory exclusion or every nominal surviving remedy is constitutional.

**Authority:** Part II's nine-Justice coalition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Suspension Clause protects the writ's constitutional function; it does not exempt every repeated application from source-bound procedural and substantive regulation. The retained §2241 route is used here, not merely named as an inaccessible alternative. The Court independently considers the actual claims and finds no entitlement under the traditional extraordinary standard. Thus no otherwise sufficient claim is denied solely because all meaningful review has been eliminated.

Yerger supports preserving the separate route, while Klein's distinction between changing governing law and prescribing an outcome prevents treating every jurisdictional amendment as an unlawful command. The provisions applied here establish channels and successive-claim standards rather than direct the Court to falsify a fact or decide under an unchanged rule in a prescribed way. The Court does not approve elimination of every meaningful forum, resolve a case of established innocence excluded from all relief, or decide the constitutionality and temporal operation of §§2254(d)–(e), all of Title I, or every possible supervisory-review restriction.

##### The reasonable-doubt claim

**Controlling proposition:** The challenged voir-dire statements and complete final charge do not create a reasonable likelihood that the jury convicted without proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every essential element. The presented constitutional instruction ground therefore supplies no habeas relief.

**Authority:** Part III-A, joined by all eight Associate Justices. Stone concurs in denial on successive-application and extraordinary-writ grounds without deciding this constitutional question.

**Controlling explanation:** Victor and Sandoval require examination of the complete instruction, not automatic approval or invalidation of the words moral certainty. This charge kept the burden on the State from beginning to end, distinguished criminal proof from civil preponderance, and defined doubt through evidence, lack of evidence or conflicting evidence. It required acquittal when jurors remained wavering, unsettled and unsatisfied. When the proof was entirely circumstantial, it required exclusion of every other reasonable theory; it forbade conviction on suspicion, conjecture or possibility and again directed acquittal for reasonable doubt.

That connected explanation, together with the court's direction concerning counsel's statements, defeats the proposed understanding that a lesser moral impression could replace evidentiary proof. Correct boilerplate alone would not cure a contradictory charge; the instructions' actual operation is what matters here. Sullivan would require reversal without harmless-error reweighing if the proof standard were constitutionally vitiated, but that predicate is absent. The Court does not use the strength of the prosecution evidence to excuse a defective instruction or decide a new instruction claim, waiver or retroactivity issue.

##### The medical-testimony trial-process claim

**Controlling proposition:** On this record, admission of Tillman's testimony and the submitted forensic criticisms do not establish constitutionally fundamental unfairness or the requested constitutional rule that only a physician may provide such testimony. This is a determination of the presented trial-process claim, not a finding that each challenged medical conclusion was correct.

**Authority:** Part III-B, joined by all eight Associate Justices. Stone rests on the separate extraordinary-review ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Lisenba asks whether the evidentiary treatment denied fundamental fairness. Ake, Ford and Eddings do not establish the proposed automatic physician-only rule. Tillman's credentials were exposed through examination, contrary medical testimony was offered, Whitaker supplied independent qualified testimony, and jurors were instructed to determine expert weight and reject an opinion whose factual assumptions they did not find true.

Spitz and Arden do more than criticize credentials: they attack the forensic basis of the earlier-death estimate, observations, samples and other interpretations. The Court considers that substance. But expert disagreement and criticism of the work do not on this showing prove knowing use of false evidence, concealed qualifications, deprivation of a constitutionally required expert or another established trial-process violation. Neither an altered estimate nor the experts' asserted certainty proves perjury. The ruling neither certifies every forensic statement nor invents additional claims from supporting observations concerning injury or binding.

##### Innocence as a gateway

**Controlling proposition:** This submission does not establish Schlup's gateway: reliable new evidence, assessed with all probative evidence, making it more likely than not that no reasonable, properly instructed juror would convict. That conclusion does not adjudicate the separately pleaded constitutional innocence theory or make the gateway and AEDPA's successive conditions interchangeable.

**Authority:** Part IV-A, joined by all eight Associate Justices. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas accept insufficiency under the governing standard while retaining their stricter view; Stone does not join this additional application.

**Controlling explanation:** The experts' three-to-five-day estimate would support the alibi and is supported by detailed criticism of the forensic chronology. Their review included Whitaker's testimony; the Court does not disregard their opinions merely because they leave his professional qualifications unchallenged. Whitaker nevertheless independently favored death near disappearance while acknowledging the later interval as possible. The disappearance, abandoned car, call from Felker's home and unchanged clothing remain additional evidence bearing on chronology.

Considering those grounds together, the new opinions do not remove reasonable adverse assessment to the probability required by Schlup. The Court does not find the affidavits false or use other-offense evidence beyond its stated motive, intent and state-of-mind purpose. The legal conclusion is about this gateway showing. Even a successful gateway would permit examination of an underlying constitutional claim; it would not itself prove that claim or establish paragraph (2)(B)'s distinct requirements.

##### The separate constitutional innocence claim and original relief

**Controlling proposition:** Even assuming a possible constitutional claim based on actual innocence, this considered submission does not establish exceptional circumstances warranting the Court's original habeas intervention. Original relief is denied without establishing a universal freestanding-innocence burden, rejecting every such constitutional claim, or requiring an additional return merely because new expert opinions are offered.

**Authority:** Part IV-B, joined by all nine Justices; this is an independently sufficient original-writ ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Herrera resolved the insufficiency of its particular submission while leaving the Justices divided on a general innocence right and its threshold. It supplies no single general burden to import here. Rule 20 requires an exceptional original showing even when jurisdiction exists and a constitutional theory is assumed.

The later opinions and their detailed physiological criticism receive consideration alongside Whitaker's estimate, his acknowledgment of the later interval and the circumstantial chronology. The criticism raises a substantive medical dispute. It does not, on the submission as a whole, demonstrate the extraordinary basis for this Court to intervene directly. The Court does not substitute the mere existence of a conviction or the circuit's denial for that examination, declare the experts dishonest, or assume that an unexamined Georgia remedy adequately resolves the claim. No new general innocence standard, factual hearing, transfer or further return is necessary to decide this original request. This ground remains distinct from both the trial-process holdings and Schlup's procedural gateway.

##### Pending application A-890

**Controlling proposition:** Execution's irreversibility does not alone establish entitlement to a stay. Because the independently considered claims show no sufficiently substantial basis for relief through the available proceedings and no identified unfinished legally required process warrants protection, application A-890 is denied.

**Authority:** Part V, joined and expressly adopted as application reasons by all nine Justices, with the signed explanations and qualifications identified below.

**Controlling explanation:** Bowersox requires attention to actual substantial relief grounds in a successive-petition request; the existence of an application or retained jurisdiction is insufficient. Lonchar does not authorize a separate equitable dismissal sanction, but it preserves the distinction between lawful petition consideration and stay discretion. The identified required-process condition recognized by five Justices there supplies no further proceeding once lawful claim-specific consideration is complete here. McFarland's counsel/preparation protection creates no newly established preparation entitlement on this record.

The Court denies the pending request after that independent consideration. It does not rely solely on the circuit's refusal of authorization, infer an automatic late-filing penalty, or adjudicate the legality of an execution on any specified date. No existing Supreme Court stay is altered.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Ex parte McCardle:** Applied to the specified withdrawn appellate route; no conclusion follows that every separate habeas source is repealed.
- **Ex parte Yerger and Ex parte Bollman:** Applied to the retained statutory habeas channel and its constitutional appellate character. They do not make issuance of an original writ automatic.

### 44 Liquormart, Inc. v. Rhode Island, No. 94-1140

Merits decision, May 13, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 1, 1995, in October Term 1995. The Court reviews under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) the First Circuit's October 24, 1994 judgment, 39 F.3d 5, reversing a constitutional declaration and directing judgment for defendants. The earlier July opinion and judgment were withdrawn and vacated.

Rhode Island General Laws §3-8-7 prohibits manufacturers, wholesalers, out-of-state shippers and covered license holders from advertising prices of malt beverages, cordials, wine or distilled liquor offered for sale in Rhode Island. Its exception permits price signs or tags attached to or placed on merchandise for sale within licensed premises, under departmental rules. Regulation 32 prohibits alcoholic-price references on signs visible from outside Class A premises. The exception does not authorize unrestricted indoor or exterior-visible price advertising.

Section 3-8-8.1 separately prohibits Rhode Island-based newspapers, periodicals, broadcasters and advertising businesses from accepting, publishing or broadcasting in Rhode Island advertisements stating or referring to alcoholic-beverage prices. It excepts trade journals recognized and authorized by the administrator. Violations carry a $50 first-offense fine and fines of at most $100 for additional offenses; publication or broadcast may also be enjoined on a retail licensee's or association's complaint. Section 3-8-8 supplies general enforcement and license-suspension authority but is not independently invalidated. Nonprice promotion remains permitted. Section 3-1-5 declares temperance and reasonable control of alcohol traffic as purposes.

44 Liquormart's 1991 advertisement displayed liquor bottles, prices for nonalcoholic goods and prominent WOW bursts. It paid a $400 administrative fine without appealing that proceeding. Peoples, a Massachusetts retailer, could not place truthful advertisements of lawful Massachusetts liquor prices in Rhode Island media. Both plaintiffs have standing; Peoples' distinct First Amendment claim remains, while its Commerce Clause claim was abandoned on appeal.

After trial, the District Court declared the challenged provisions unconstitutional and found no significant effect of this price-advertising ban on Rhode Island consumption. It did not decide the effects of general alcohol-advertising bans. The First Circuit regarded the economic inference supporting the ban as constitutionally sufficient and invoked an added Twenty-first Amendment presumption. The parties' treatment of the proposed speech as lawful and presumably truthful does not establish the disputed consumption effect.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. This record does not establish material advancement of temperance by the price-information ban

**Controlling proposition:** Even under ordinary Central Hudson review and treating temperance as substantial, Rhode Island has not demonstrated that these truthful-price restrictions directly and materially reduce consumption. This conclusion addresses the actual record and restriction, not every possible effect of advertising, price or demand.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg: five. Stevens affirmatively adopts this conditional sufficient ground without accepting consumer ignorance as a legitimate objective or ordinary scrutiny as the complete account.

**Controlling explanation:** The First Amendment protects the lawful, nonmisleading information at issue. Central Hudson requires material advancement, not merely an important interest and a plausible causal possibility. Saffer's theory supplies a real argument: suppressing comparison raises search costs and price dispersion, higher average prices tend to discourage purchases, and opposing experts acknowledged price responsiveness and a theoretically reasonable prediction. Their concessions cannot be omitted.

They nevertheless do not establish the necessary effect of this restriction on this record. The District Court credited contrary testimony and found no significant consumption effect. Saffer's international research concerned general advertising bans, with acknowledged control problems, rather than this price-information rule. The record lacked estimates connecting the projected Rhode Island price change to consumption, and market-share or profit gains could occur without greater total consumption. Smart's acknowledgment of the mechanism accompanied his criticism of its missing data; Pittman's theoretical concession likewise did not erase his contrary conclusion.

The Court separately assesses constitutional sufficiency while respecting the evidentiary character of findings and concessions. It neither decides credibility by counting experts nor treats the First Circuit's economic inference as a new trial finding. Supported prediction remains permissible; no state-specific numerical study, experimental certainty or universal study design is required. Here the whole showing fails the material-advancement requirement. The plaintiffs' expected private profits and the difficulty of measuring advertising effects do not discharge the State's burden.

##### II. The complete price-information restraint independently lacks reasonable fit

**Controlling proposition:** Even assuming material advancement, suppressing the challenged category of truthful retail-price information is disproportionate to the asserted temperance objective on this record. Concrete direct price and tax alternatives expose the inadequately justified fit without becoming mandatory replacements or imposing a least-restrictive-means rule.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven. O'Connor and Breyer assume advancement without deciding it; Souter independently adopts both failures; Stevens's fit application is conditional and independently sufficient.

**Controlling explanation:** Fox requires a reasonable proportional relationship between the speech burden and the regulatory end. Rhode Island seeks to affect the amount purchased through the price consumers pay, yet withholds accurate comparison information continuously. Saffer's own acknowledgment that minimum prices or higher taxes could serve the consumption objective supplies a concrete comparison with measures addressing price directly.

The State need not select the least restrictive conceivable policy or exhaust every alternative. But it must justify this substantial informational burden in light of practical alternatives. Its interstate-shopping objection does not show why its own indirect price-raising mechanism avoids the same leakage or why the blanket speech burden is proportionate. Midcal and 324 Liquor identify defects in particular price arrangements, not the illegality of every state-imposed price measure. The Court neither designs a replacement nor adjudicates an antitrust dispute.

Permitting nonprice promotion and the specific merchandise and trade-journal exceptions does not by itself defeat or establish fit. Price information may have a distinct competitive effect, and incremental regulation is not automatically invalid. The decisive problem remains the unexplained breadth of suppressing accurate prices to reach an objective directly addressed by the identified controls. Calling the price category complete retains those exact exceptions; it does not describe a ban on all alcohol speech.

This ground suffices even taking the predicted consumption effect seriously. It creates no new heightened tier, universal alternatives checklist or all-sources-at-once requirement. Those who separately favor broader protection do not turn this conditional application into assent by the whole coalition to that broader theory.

##### III-A. The Twenty-first Amendment supplies no added presumption validating this restraint

**Controlling proposition:** Rhode Island's alcohol-control authority supplies no additional presumption relaxing First Amendment protection for these off-premises truthful-price prohibitions. Authority over alcohol sales does not itself establish the validity of suppressing accurate information about lawful sales.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part III-A, joined by all eight other participating Justices: nine. This common proposition does not attribute Central Hudson balancing to Thomas or either sufficient balancing ground to Scalia.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2 of the Twenty-first Amendment addresses transportation and importation for delivery or use contrary to state law. It creates no express commercial-speech exception. LaRue and Bellanca involved regulation of conduct on licensed premises; those holdings do not decide this restraint on accurate off-premises information. The State's power to regulate the underlying product cannot alone carry its distinct First Amendment burden. Nor are constitutional speech rights surrendered simply by accepting a liquor license. The challenged bans fail without an added alcohol-based presumption; the Justices supporting the broader conclusion in III-B state that separate ground expressly.

##### III-B. The Twenty-first Amendment does not qualify freedom of speech; LaRue's contrary rationale is disavowed

**Controlling proposition:** The Twenty-first Amendment does not qualify the First Amendment's protection of speech. LaRue's reliance on the Twenty-first Amendment to enhance deference is disavowed, while its holding remains supported by ordinary police powers over the conduct at issue.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part III-B, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg: six. Stone-Zsela, O'Connor and Breyer do not join this broader treatment.

**Controlling explanation:** Constitutional authority over the traffic in alcohol is not authority to diminish a separate constitutional freedom. Giving a liquor-related restriction an extra speech presumption would permit the subject regulated to displace the protection owed to the means of expression. LaRue's result does not require that step: its licensed-premises conduct regulation can rest on the State's police powers. Removing the Twenty-first Amendment rationale therefore neither reverses that conduct holding nor invalidates Bellanca. The rule concerns the interaction with freedom of speech, not the validity of every direct alcohol restriction.

##### V-B. The completed record supports an implementing remand preserving the constitutional declaration

**Controlling proposition:** Reversal of the operative First Circuit judgment requires implementation of the district court's constitutional declaration against the challenged price-information prohibitions. No new merits trial is required on any adopted sufficient ground. The Court does not issue an additional injunction directive.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Part V-B, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight. Stone-Zsela separately would direct prospective injunctive relief.

**Controlling explanation:** The case was tried and the challenged provisions declared unconstitutional. The appellate reversal rested on legal treatment that cannot sustain these restrictions under the grounds adopted here. An implementing remand preserves the declaration rather than reopening resolved record development. It does not convert the paid fine or unappealed administrative order into a refund dispute, reach the waived Commerce Clause claim or require a substitute alcohol-control program. Stone-Zsela agrees with reversal but regards an express prospective injunction as necessary relief; that additional direction does not command the remedial majority.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Central Hudson and Fox remain operative. The Court's advancement and fit grounds are independently supported at their exact levels, and no majority creates a higher tier or Thomas's categorical rule. Discovery Network, Edenfield, Ibanez, Lanphere and Rubin retain their restriction-specific burdens and limits. Florida Bar invalidates its finite waiting period on its record. Edge retains its narrower, record-specific holding sustaining the border-broadcast restriction, not an automatic vice or federalism exception.

LaRue's Twenty-first Amendment rationale is disavowed by six Justices, with its conduct holding and Bellanca preserved. The broader attack on Posadas's informational paternalism, greater-power syllogism and lawful-vice exception has four votes in IV-C; it is not a wholesale Court overruling. The controlling fit application denies automatic Posadas authorization here under subsequent developed scrutiny.

Queensgate's summary dismissal is a merits disposition. Mandel confines its force to the issues necessarily decided; it does not adopt every explanation below or require that comparable liquor-price restrictions invariably survive later developed advancement-and-fit doctrine. This plenary decision applies that doctrine to the established trial record without deciding every other application of Queensgate or relying on an unestablished factual distinction.

Bigelow reinforces protection of Peoples' lawful information in Stevens's analysis, but no separate interstate-information immunity is adopted. The waived Commerce Clause claim remains outside review.

The challenged restrictions in §§3-8-7, 3-8-8.1 and Regulation 32 cannot constitutionally suppress the truthful price information presented by either plaintiff. Five Justices find inadequate material advancement; seven independently find unreasonable fit. Six adopt the broader Twenty-first Amendment/LaRue treatment, while all nine reject an added presumption for this restraint. Each coalition supplies its own authority.

The decision leaves ordinary Central Hudson/Fox scrutiny in place. Noncontrolling special-care, heightened-review, broader Posadas and categorical anti-ignorance theories do not replace it. No mandatory numerical study, least-restrictive-means standard, universal simultaneous-regulation rule or independent out-of-state advertising immunity follows. False or misleading speech and unlawful promotions retain their separate governing law, and direct alcohol controls are not invalidated. The Court's implementing remand preserves the declaration; the separate requested injunction is not part of its mandate.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Kennedy's noncontrolling Parts IV-A–C.** In IV-A, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, he emphasizes the consumer's interest in accurate commercial information and the special care appropriate when the State suppresses the entire relevant category to influence lawful purchases. That view is distinct from a rule invalidating every such restriction categorically. In IV-B, joined by Stevens and Ginsburg, he would apply more rigorous review to complete truthful-speech suppression unrelated to protecting consumers from deception or comparable injury. It is not announced as universal strict scrutiny for commercial speech. These Justices also adopt the narrower independently sufficient applications in I and II; Stevens's informational objection is not reduced to insufficient evidence alone.

In IV-C, joined by Stevens, Thomas and Ginsburg, Kennedy rejects Posadas's highly deferential permission to choose informational suppression for paternalistic ends, the inference that power to prohibit a product necessarily includes power to suppress truthful promotion, and a freestanding lawful-vice exception. This does not repudiate every Posadas holding. Thomas's join in this treatment does not add a balancing vote, and Souter does not join it. None of these portions commands five votes.

**O'Connor, concurring in part and in the judgment, joined by Breyer.** They join II, III-A and V-A–B. Ordinary Central Hudson/Fox review suffices, with temperance substantial and advancement assumed. Economic prediction and expert concessions deserve genuine weight; they do not decide that the State necessarily fails the advancement prong. The permanent burden on all covered truthful price references nevertheless lacks proportional fit in light of concrete direct controls. Their prior support for supported prophylaxis and finite privacy restrictions does not validate this different burden. They require neither definitive empirical proof nor selection of the least restrictive policy. They distinguish LaRue's licensed-premises conduct and apply subsequent searching doctrine rather than join III-B or IV-C. No new tier, categorical anti-paternalism ground or interstate rule is necessary. ### Kansas v. Hendricks / Hendricks v. Kansas, Nos. 95-1649 and 95-9075

Consolidated plenary merits decision, June 23, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 10, 1996. On writs of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Kansas.

The Kansas Supreme Court's March 1, 1996 judgment, In re Hendricks, 259 Kan. 246, 912 P.2d 129, invalidated Hendricks's commitment under the 1994 Sexually Violent Predator Act because it considered the statutory mental-abnormality definition constitutionally insufficient. It did not decide the preserved Double Jeopardy or Ex Post Facto claims, presented through Hendricks's cross-petition. Kansas seeks reversal and restoration of the commitment; Hendricks seeks affirmance on due process or the alternative punishment grounds. The granted questions concern the Act's substantive commitment criteria and whether this confinement is punitive. Both dockets concern the same commitment and judgment.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4, in both consolidated dockets.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, who would reverse and reject both punishment-dependent challenges.

The invalidation of Hendricks's commitment is sustained because this application imposes punishment for conduct predating the Act. He may not remain confined under that application. The disposition is as applied: it does not facially invalidate every prospective application or decide whether another independently lawful civil-commitment route is available. No new factfinding remand or release date is prescribed. The due-process discussion is not a separate reversal component; Double Jeopardy is not reached.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Signed lead opinion, Parts II–III: opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | Affirms on the as-applied Ex Post Facto ground | Civil-intent and clearest-proof method, cumulative punitive application, retrospective punishment, remedy and limits |
| Same lead opinion, Part I: non-dispositive due-process discussion | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Does not independently support affirmance or a separate reversal | Bounded rejection of a categorical diagnostic-vocabulary objection; Ginsburg reserves the issue |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would reverse and reject both punishment-dependent challenges | Civil classification on the original record and resulting rejection of Ex Post Facto and Double Jeopardy; agrees with Part I of the lead opinion |

Parts II–III command five direct joins. Part I's due-process discussion is not a ground for the judgment or a separate judgment component. Ginsburg joins Parts II–III and reserves the due-process question.

#### Holdings

##### I. Civil intent remains rebuttable in this confinement scheme

**Holding and operative rule:** In assessing whether Kansas's civil commitment of Hendricks constitutes punishment for Ex Post Facto purposes, the Court first determines the Legislature's intended civil or criminal character; an intended civil scheme is treated as punishment only upon the clearest proof that its purpose or effect is punitive. The Act's civil designation is relevant but not conclusive, and neither confinement, a connection to crime, indeterminate duration, criminal-style procedural protection nor treatment difficulty alone decides that demanding inquiry.

**Authority:** Parts II–III of Breyer's opinion are the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg. Those five Justices adopt each controlling proposition below, its application, qualifications and remedy. Part I's separate due-process discussion is not a ground for this judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the asserted civil purpose must be tested against what this confinement does. United States v. Ursery supplies the civil-intent and clearest-proof inquiry for the forfeiture statutes it considered; United States v. Ward supplies the distinction between legislative intent and the demanding showing necessary to overcome it. The same distinction applies here, but Ursery's conclusion about forfeiture does not answer a question about personal confinement. Kennedy v. Mendoza-Martinez permits examination of punitive purpose and effect without making a single indicator conclusive.

Kansas expressed a purpose of control, care and treatment, assigned custody to its social-services agency, and conditioned confinement on a present qualifying condition and danger. These are substantial civil features. The statute also commands care, review and release procedures. Their existence matters; it does not establish that the initial confinement operated as promised. The contrary claim that a civil label resolves classification would prevent the very inquiry Ward and Ursery require. Conversely, a court may not replace clearest proof with a demand for successful cure or treat each deficiency as a criminal penalty. The Court assesses the supported original operation as a whole, without a new exhaustive checklist or a general rule that every preventive confinement is punishment.

**Precedent treatment:** Ursery's method is extended to this confinement question without importing its forfeiture result; Ward's demanding intent-and-effect inquiry is applied; Mendoza-Martinez's purpose-and-effect analysis remains nonexclusive and no single indicator becomes dispositive.

##### II. This retrospective application imposes punishment

**Holding and operative rule:** The cumulative circumstances of Hendricks's original commitment—additional confinement after his sentence, selection tied to earlier sexual offenses, prisonlike restraint, and initially inadequate treatment sustained on the prospect of a future specialized program despite acknowledged possible treatment—provide the clearest proof of punitive operation in this application. Because the Act imposes that additional punishment for conduct predating its enactment, its application to Hendricks violates the Ex Post Facto Clause, and the judgment invalidating this commitment is affirmed.

**Authority:** The five-Justice opinion of the Court identified above; this is the sufficient ground for the judgment in both consolidated dockets.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these circumstances must be considered together. At the October 1994 hearing, neither proposed specialist contract had been signed and the dedicated clinical program was not established. The trial court nevertheless left commitment in force on the possibility that the treatment program would become available. That is the initial treatment deficiency material here, rather than a finding that no clinician or treatment opportunity existed.

The contrary evidence is real: Kansas pursued procurement, had direct-care staff and hospital professionals available, anticipated individualized assessment, and had previously offered Hendricks a prison treatment program. His testimony included both a pay choice and an unexplained qualification concerning a parole-board member. These facts prevent an inference that all treatment was deliberately withheld. They do not remove the punitive significance of the additional offense-linked, prisonlike restraint and the prospective basis on which its treatment justification was sustained. The Court finds the demanding showing satisfied by their cumulative operation, not by a missing job title or an isolated contracting failure.

Kansas's independent interest in protecting the public and its statutory care and release obligations do not by themselves answer that showing. United States v. Sahhar permits custody beyond a hypothetical criminal maximum only on an independently lawful civil basis; it does not classify this commitment. No rejected meaningful alternative or sham release procedure is established or presumed. No reason for the failed bids, deliberate deferral of a particular effective therapy, complete absence of a clinical plan, or specified later treatment regimen is found. The additional punitive confinement follows a later enactment applied to earlier offenses. The Ex Post Facto Clause therefore forbids this application without requiring a separate decision about double jeopardy.

**Precedent treatment:** Sahhar's rejection of a hypothetical sentence ceiling is distinguished because its holding presupposes independently lawful civil custody; it does not answer the punitive-operation question here or supply Kansas with the federal statute's procedures.

##### III. Scope of the judgment and questions not decided

Hendricks may not remain confined under this application of the Act for conduct predating it. The Court affirms the invalidation of this commitment on the as-applied Ex Post Facto ground; it does not facially invalidate every prospective application, decide the validity of every later genuinely civil program, or decide whether another independently lawful civil-commitment route is available. No new release date, stay, hearing schedule, clinical protocol or alternative-custody authorization is prescribed.

The Double Jeopardy claim is not reached. Punitive classification does not by itself establish attachment of jeopardy or identity of offenses, and the Court does not decide those distinct elements. Part I's rejection of a categorical objection to the words mental abnormality is non-dispositive discussion, not an independently sufficient ground for affirmance or a holding that this commitment satisfies all due-process requirements. Ginsburg reserves that issue. Treatment-related due process, broader propensity-only definitions, a universal personality-disorder rule, a requirement of absolute inability to control conduct in every circumstance, and a new jury instruction are not decided. No Marks synthesis is needed for the five directly joined Ex Post Facto holdings.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Ursery; United States v. $405,089.23 in United States Currency, June 24, 1996:** Extends their rebuttable civil-intent and clearest-proof method from the specified forfeiture schemes to classification of this personal confinement for Ex Post Facto purposes. Their forfeiture result is not a classification of the Kansas commitment; their distinct Double Jeopardy limits remain.
- **United States v. Ward, 448 U.S. 242:** Applies the distinction between intended civil character and the demanding proof needed to establish punitive purpose or effect.
- **Kennedy v. Mendoza-Martinez, 372 U.S. 144:** Applies examination of punitive purpose and effect without making an individual indicator decisive or creating a mandatory exhaustive checklist.
- **United States v. Sahhar, December 16, 1996:** Distinguishes its rejection of a hypothetical criminal-sentence ceiling for independently lawful §4246 custody. That decision does not establish that this Kansas confinement is civil; its federal placement, proof and release provisions are not transplanted into Kansas law.

No precedent is overruled. The non-dispositive Part I discussion draws on Foucha v. Louisiana's requirement of current lawful grounds and its reservation of materially narrower preventive regimes, and on Sahhar's insistence on independent civil authority. It does not change those holdings, Addington's ordinary civil-commitment proof protection, or Heller v. Doe's rejection of a particular unequal proof arrangement. The Court does not adopt a new due-process rule through that discussion. California Department of Corrections v. Morales's meaningful-parole-opportunity holdings remain confined to their subject; they supply neither a general speculative-risk rule nor a substitute for proving punitive operation here. Lynce v. Mathis's exhaustion disposition supplies no credit-withdrawal merits rule. Blockburger and the retained Grady rule are not applied because the independent Double Jeopardy elements are not reached.

#### Law After Decision

Effective June 23, 1997, the civil-intent and clearest-proof inquiry governs the Ex Post Facto classification of this commitment. The supported cumulative original operation establishes punishment as applied to Hendricks, and its imposition for pre-enactment conduct is unconstitutional. No single feature is made automatically punitive, and no new burden shift, mandatory alternatives inquiry, treatment protocol or release procedure is created.

The decision does not invalidate all prospective applications of the Act or all later civil programs. It leaves the availability of another independently lawful commitment route undecided. The substantive-due-process discussion creates no additional controlling rule, and Double Jeopardy's separate elements remain unresolved. Current lawful civil grounds and effective safeguards retain their independent importance under Foucha and Sahhar.

#### Separate Writings

**Part I's non-dispositive due-process discussion.** Breyer, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, rejects a categorical constitutional objection resting on the statutory words mental abnormality. The discussion is confined to the serious recognized condition, evidence of impaired control and connection to dangerous conduct shown here. A past offense and a prediction alone do not supply that substantive limitation; neither a statutory verdict nor a high proof burden substitutes for it. Foucha concerns custody after its conceded illness predicate ends and leaves narrower preventive regimes open; Sahhar requires a new lawful civil basis rather than making every legislative diagnosis sufficient. This agreement does not dispense with the constitutional safeguards needed for a lawful civil commitment. The discussion does not decide treatment-related due process or every application of personality disorder. It supports no separate reversal of the Kansas judgment and is not the ground of the Court's affirmance. Ginsburg does not join it and reserves the question.

**O'Connor's dissent, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** The dissent agrees with Part I's bounded rejection of a diagnostic-vocabulary requirement but would reverse the Kansas judgment and reject the punishment-dependent challenges. Applying the demanding civil-intent and clearest-proof inquiry, it finds the supported original record insufficient to turn the commitment into punishment. The current condition and danger requirements, social-services custody, separate setting, care duty, procurement efforts, interim professional resources and actual review and release routes support an independent preventive purpose. The lack of a completed specialist program is serious, but does not establish punitive purpose or effect when the contrary original evidence is considered. Neither difficult treatment nor an indefinite duration alone defeats otherwise lawful civil custody.

The dissent does not find that treatment was adequate, that contracting failed for a benign reason, or that later treatment-hour representations cure the original deficiencies. It preserves proper challenges to unconstitutional conditions and requires genuine continuing predicates; a civil description would not protect a proved punitive operation. The dissent relies on the actual statutory sequence. Section 59-29a08 requires yearly examination, a report and judicial review. Probable cause that the condition has changed so the person is safe at large and will not commit sexual violence requires a further hearing; there the State must prove beyond reasonable doubt that the abnormality or disorder remains such that the person is unsafe at large and likely to engage in sexual violence if released. A full release trial does not occur automatically each year. Under section 59-29a10, the Secretary must authorize a petition upon determining that the condition has changed so the person is not likely to commit predatory sexual violence if released; a hearing and the State's reasonable-doubt burden follow. Section 59-29a11 permits petitions without permission, but a first or later unauthorized petition may be denied without hearing as frivolous. After an earlier unauthorized petition was found frivolous or insufficient condition change was found, a later petition must be denied unless its facts support sufficient change to warrant a hearing. These commands do not establish compliant administration.

On its civil classification, both Ex Post Facto and Double Jeopardy fail at their necessary punishment prerequisite. The dissent makes no unnecessary ruling on attachment, offense identity or every retrospective regulatory burden.

The Kansas Supreme Court's judgment stands at the scope necessary to invalidate Hendricks's commitment on the alternative as-applied Ex Post Facto ground. ### Kolster v. INS, First Circuit No. 96-1194

Merits decision, June 23, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the First Circuit's December 4, 1996 dismissal of a petition challenging a deportation order. Heard on the merits and decided June 23, 1997. The questions are whether AEDPA §440(a) governs the pending petition, whether withdrawal of ordinary appellate review is lawful, and what judicial consideration remains for substantial legal and constitutional objections. Kolster disputes the legal eligibility rule that prevented consideration of §212(c) relief; he does not merely seek a more favorable discretionary balance. The domicile merits are outside this review.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The direct petition ends. The Court does not decide the domicile calculation, grant a waiver or adjudicate, convert or transfer a collateral petition.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Souter | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports affirmance | Complete transition construction; eight direct joins |

Stone-Zsela agrees with Part I's disposition without joining its reasoning.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The saved proceeding remains subject to AEDPA's independent restriction

**Holding and operative rule:** Corrected IIRIRA §§306(c)(1) and 309(c)(1) preserve the former review scheme for this proceeding begun before April 1, 1997, subject to their particular exceptions; §242(g) reaches old proceedings but does not itself withdraw review of this legal objection to a completed adjudication. Section 306(c)(2) protects an actual judicial judgment or order entered under former INA §106, applying that section as amended by AEDPA §440, against an operation of §306(c)(1) that would invalidate it or require reconsideration; that protection neither supplies jurisdiction, reopens merits review nor bars ordinary appellate review of the judgment's correctness.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight direct votes. Stone-Zsela agrees with this transition disposition without joining its reasoning.

**Controlling explanation:** Yang supplies the April 1 commencement rule; Reyes-Hernandez, Hincapie-Nieto and Salazar-Haro distinguish the executive decisions or actions to commence proceedings, adjudicate cases and execute orders from review of a completed legal adjudication. Section 309(c)(2) permits election of new procedures only before the evidentiary hearing has commenced and with notice at least thirty days before it. Paragraph (3) permits termination and reinitiation only before a final administrative decision. No qualifying election is established. Paragraph (4) requires a final exclusion or deportation order entered more than thirty days after September 30, 1996; the January 30, 1996 order does not meet that condition. Public Law 104-302 made §306(c)(1)'s general operation subject to §309 while retaining §242(g)'s cross-temporal reach; it left §306(c)(2) unchanged. The First Circuit's December 4, 1996 dismissal applied former §106 as amended by §440(a), so paragraph (2) protects it against invalidation or required reconsideration attributable to paragraph (1), not against this ordinary correctness review. These savings do not erase AEDPA's separate restriction merely because the old procedural scheme otherwise survives.

##### II. Section 440(a) governs this ordinary petition

**Holding and operative rule:** AEDPA §440(a) withdraws ordinary appellate review of Kolster's final deportation order based on the admitted drug and aggravated-felony grounds, independently supported by the conviction documents, notwithstanding his February 28, 1996 petition. On the established facts, application of the channel restriction imposes no supported new legal disability on his plea, concession or another protected completed event; this conclusion establishes neither a universal actual-reliance requirement nor a rule that pleas or eligibility for discretionary relief can never support nonretroactivity.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support the dismissal on a different temporal ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Landgraf requires statutory temporal direction first and, absent sufficient direction, an inquiry into new consequences of completed acts. Rivers preserves that provision-specific method. Section 440(f)'s express timing clause concerns subsection (e), not a saving clause for subsection (a). Reyes-Hernandez protected an actual surrendered colorable defense; Kolster identifies none, and conviction documents independently supported deportability. Even under his preferred domicile calculation, his September 1992 plea preceded seven-year eligibility by three years. The Court does not infer his subjective motives from those facts. They establish no lost legal option of the kind asserted. Hincapie-Nieto and Salazar-Haro establish that filing and an expectation of review alone do not preserve this channel; their holdings do not make surrender of a defense the only possible protected event. Hughes Aircraft concerns creation of a previously unavailable private enforcement action, a new consequence not established here. A jurisdictional label alone supplies no answer, and §440(d)'s distinct substantive eligibility amendment remains unadjudicated.

##### III. Independent habeas jurisdiction survives for substantial legal and constitutional objections

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 440(a)'s replacement of a particular INA review channel does not clearly repeal the independent jurisdiction enacted in 28 U.S.C. §2241. When custody, proper respondent, forum, exhaustion and other applicable predicates are satisfied, that jurisdiction permits consideration of a substantial legal or constitutional claim that executive custody or deportation lacks lawful authority, including a claim that an unlawful eligibility rule prevented consideration of discretionary relief, and permits effective relief if the claim warrants it.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas do not join this construction.

**Controlling explanation:** Felker distinguishes repeal of a specified appellate mechanism from the separate statutory habeas authority that Congress leaves in place. McCardle and Yerger likewise require attention to the channel actually withdrawn; Heikkila distinguishes restricted ordinary immigration review from habeas. The replacement of former §106(a)(10) does not identify or clearly repeal §2241's separate grant. Unlike Hincapie-Nieto and Salazar-Haro, this case presents a developed legal objection: the agency refused to consider a waiver because of its construction of eligibility. Judicial reweighing of discretionary equities is different. A detention-only remedy required to assume the challenged legal rule valid would not answer this objection. Congress may channel presentation and impose valid procedural conditions, but the retained court must be able to determine the substantial authority claim actually presented and afford effective relief when warranted. This construction permits withdrawal of ordinary review without approving insulation of that claim from meaningful judicial consideration. The objection that no collateral petition is before the Court limits the disposition: the Court decides statutory capability, not whether Kolster satisfies its predicates or deserves relief. No freestanding jurisdiction arises from §1651, and no constitutional violation follows automatically from every statutory error. The Court reserves a demonstrated failure of this retained route and does not define every constitutional minimum or reopen factual and discretionary questions generally.

##### IV. The direct petition ends without a decision on eligibility

**Holding and operative rule:** The First Circuit's dismissal of the direct petition is affirmed. The Court neither decides the lawful-domicile calculation nor orders a waiver, agency reconsideration, conversion or transfer of the petition, collateral relief, a stay or execution of deportation.

**Authority:** Part IV of Souter's opinion is joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The absence of direct-review jurisdiction prevents adjudication of the eligibility merits through this petition. Recognition of a conditional collateral source does not establish custody, a proper respondent or forum, exhaustion, or an actual collateral filing. Hincapie-Nieto and Salazar-Haro preserve this channel-specific remedy. The sentencing judge's recommendation does not bind the immigration decisionmaker. Any properly brought collateral challenge must establish its own predicates before its legal claim and requested relief can be decided.

Section 440(a) withdraws this ordinary petition, while §2241 remains an independently enacted, conditional route for substantial legal and constitutional challenges to executive custody or deportation authority. The legal eligibility question may be considered through that route only if its actual jurisdictional and procedural predicates are met. #### Opinion Topology

Thomas delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Scalia filed a concurrence, joined by O'Connor and Thomas; all three also join the Court's opinion and judgment.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The final land-use decision does not depend on selling transferable rights

**Holding and operative rule:** When the responsible land-use agency has definitively prohibited development of the claimant's parcel and no further land-use procedure can change that decision, the final-decision requirement is satisfied. The owner need not first sell transferable development rights that cannot alter the parcel's permitted use; uncertainty about their value or ultimate constitutional significance does not make that completed regulatory determination provisional.

**Authority:** Thomas's Opinion of the Court, joined by all nine Justices directly. The Court adopts this common finality ground without an alternative inquiry treating the rights' saleability, award or value as potentially decisive to finality. No Marks synthesis is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** Williamson County Regional Planning Commission v. Hamilton Bank requires a final decision identifying how the regulation applies to the property. MacDonald, Sommer & Frates v. Yolo County likewise requires a definitive account of the development the agency will allow, rather than review of a hypothetical restriction. Here the permit denial and completed governing-board appeal settle that institutional question: Suitum may not develop this parcel. A sale of rights usable elsewhere cannot revise the answer. The Agency's objection that economic injury cannot be measured without more information concerns the later assessment of the restriction, not an unmade land-use decision. Lucas preserves ordinary finality while distinguishing reviewability from proof of complete deprivation and the ultimate compensation inquiry. The Court neither requires a market transaction as a condition of finality nor values the rights, fixes their role in taking liability or compensation, or rules that they are irrelevant to the merits. Those issues can be addressed under the applicable law below.

**Limits and remedy:** The Ninth Circuit's finality dismissal is vacated and the case remanded without the attempted-sale prerequisite. Any independently applicable state-compensation condition, the relevant parcel, the governing takings framework, liability, valuation, defenses and remedy remain open. The Court orders neither compensation nor a building permit and finds no complete economic deprivation.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Williamson County Regional Planning Commission v. Hamilton Bank: applies the final-decision inquiry to the permitted use of the regulated parcel; the independent state-compensation requirement is not decided.
- MacDonald, Sommer & Frates v. Yolo County: distinguishes remaining agency discretion over development from this completed prohibition; no futile market sale becomes a new land-use application.
- Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council: preserves regulatory finality and the distinction between reviewability, proven loss and compensation; its complete-deprivation rule is not applied as a merits finding here.
- Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City: leaves its contextual merits framework intact and the rights' ultimate role unresolved. The separate concurrence's proposed distinction of its transferable-rights discussion is not a holding of the Court.

#### Law After Decision

A definitive parcel-use prohibition satisfies regulatory finality even though the owner has not marketed rights permitting development elsewhere. The Court removes the attempted-sale barrier and leaves the substantive takings and compensation questions open. No precedent is overruled and no transfer-right valuation rule is adopted.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by O'Connor and Thomas, joins the Court's narrow finality opinion and concurs separately. For them, transferable rights allowing development of other land concern compensation, not whether the restriction of this parcel effects a taking. Williamson County and MacDonald ask the type and intensity of development permitted on the claimant's own property; an off-parcel right does not soften that prohibition. Lucas does not permit regulatory payment to make a taking disappear. The rights' value may mitigate an uncompensable regulatory loss or provide part or all of just compensation, so the concurrence does not condemn transferable-rights programs.

The three object that treating compensatory value as retained use could let government avoid full compensation by providing only enough substitute value to prevent a finding of total or severe loss. They distinguish Penn Central's transferable-rights discussion by the terminal owner's other nearby parcels, which could themselves benefit from transfer. If that distinction cannot sustain the passage, they would reject it rather than let third-party transferable compensation defeat taking liability. This proposed treatment is noncontrolling; the Court leaves Penn Central and the ultimate liability-versus-compensation question intact. Their complete join in the Court's narrow finality opinion does not abandon their position that the rights concern compensation rather than taking liability.

#### Source Notes

[Suitum, 520 U.S. 725](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/520bv.pdf#page=820) supplies the argument date and published account of the permit denial, governing-board appeal and lower-court posture. The administrative act is a final permit denial and appeal, not an independently established conventional variance proceeding. The decision uses the completed no-development determination without relying on additional lottery, transfer-sale or value concessions.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Ninth Circuit judgment is vacated. On remand the claim may not be dismissed for failure to obtain a final parcel-use decision merely because the transferable rights remain unsold. The lower courts retain the independent prerequisites, merits, valuation and remedy questions; no substantive result is dictated.

### Stewart v. Martinez-Villareal, No. 97-300

Decision, May 18, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 25, 1998; decided May 18, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 118 F.3d 628. The Court considers whether §2244(b)(3)(E) permits review of the circuit's determination that authorization was unnecessary and whether this renewed Ford incompetency-to-be-executed claim is second or successive.

Three earlier federal applications ended for nonexhaustion. The March 1993 application included the Ford claim, dismissed as premature at the State's request. The 1996 appellate mandate reversed relief on other grounds while preserving later litigation of that claim. After an execution warrant and state competency proceedings, the District Court dismissed the renewed motion for lack of jurisdiction. The Ninth Circuit held authorization unnecessary and returned the claim for district consideration.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

All nine agree that ordinary review of this threshold classification is available. The seven-Justice judgment permits first federal merits consideration of this particular renewed claim under otherwise applicable law. It grants no writ or stay and orders no new evidentiary hearing or execution.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer throughout; Scalia and Thomas in Part I | Affirms the district-return judgment | Part I: ordinary review channel; Part II: previously presented premature claim and resulting return for consideration |
| Dissent in part | Scalia | Thomas | Would reverse and require dismissal under §2244(b)(1) | Both join Part I but reject Part II's continuation classification |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The circuit's threshold classification is reviewable

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 2244(b)(3)(E) bars ordinary review of a circuit decision granting or denying authorization to file a second or successive application, but it does not bar review of this distinct determination that authorization is unnecessary. The Court may decide whether the circuit correctly placed this proceeding outside that mechanism; the circuit's label does not itself establish the answer.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion is adopted by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Felker gives effect to the statutory bar for an actual authorization decision. Triestman and Dorsainvil apply that restriction to authorization refusals. Here the circuit did not decide whether the applicant satisfied the statutory prima-facie permission criteria. It held those criteria inapplicable and transferred the proceeding back. Calderon likewise distinguishes an independently reviewable first-application action from the authorization process. Ordinary review therefore reaches this classification without opening review of a true authorization refusal or allowing a court to evade the statute by relabeling its action.

##### II. This previously presented premature claim may receive its first federal merits consideration

**Holding and operative rule:** A Ford claim actually presented in the initial merits application, dismissed as premature and expressly preserved for later consideration, is not a second or successive application when renewed after the impediment of prematurity has ended. This renewed claim may therefore return to the District Court without circuit authorization, subject to otherwise applicable habeas requirements.

**Authority:** Part II is adopted by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, including the author; these seven directly establish the rule and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The claim was before the federal court in March 1993, but could not then receive a decision on competency to be executed. The State requested its dismissal on that ground, and the later mandate preserved its return. The renewed filing seeks the federal merits consideration previously unavailable. It does not repeat a decision already made on that claim.

Second or successive is a procedural term, not simply a count of papers filed. The treatment of an application dismissed for nonexhaustion illustrates why an earlier nonmerits filing need not consume the available opportunity. Lonchar requires attention to the actual legal source and its dismissal predicates; it does not authorize a court to ignore an applicable statute. This conclusion construes the statute's class rather than creating an equitable exception to it. The final disposition of other claims does not turn this claim's prematurity dismissal into a merits adjudication. Calderon's rule for reopening completed merits therefore supplies no contrary answer.

The Court does not adopt the circuit's broader exemption for every competency-only application. A Ford claim first presented after an earlier federal application was rejected, other later-arising claims and every other type of nonmerits dismissal remain undecided. Ford identifies the constitutional claim; this judgment establishes neither incompetence nor any defect in Arizona's competency proceedings. Lindh and Carriger retain their provision-specific limits, and neither this classification nor first-review status guarantees a new live evidentiary hearing, a writ or a stay.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Felker v. Turpin: applied to actual authorization decisions; its review bar and distinct original-habeas route remain unchanged.
- Triestman v. United States and In re Dorsainvil: distinguished because they concerned actual authorization refusals; this case creates no exception permitting ordinary review of those refusals.
- Calderon v. Thompson: its action-specific channel distinction is applied; its completed-merits recall rule is distinguished from a claim dismissed only as premature.
- Lonchar v. Thomas: its requirement of legally supported dismissal predicates informs the classification; no equitable power to disregard enacted successive limits is recognized.
- Ford v. Wainwright: supplies the incompetency-to-be-executed claim without deciding competency or procedural sufficiency here.
- Lindh v. Murphy and Carriger v. Stewart: their provision-specific temporal treatment is preserved; they do not independently exempt all later filings from AEDPA.

#### Law After Decision

The particular previously presented and premature Ford claim remains available for first federal consideration when ripe. The statute continues to govern genuinely second or successive applications, and its ordinary-review prohibition continues to govern actual authorization decisions. No general competency-claim exemption, constitutional invalidation of AEDPA or substantive entitlement to habeas relief follows.

#### Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Thomas, dissenting in part.** They agree that the Court may review the circuit's threshold classification, but would reverse it. Section 2244(b)(1) addresses a claim presented in a second or successive application and requires dismissal when that claim appeared in the earlier application. Here the earlier merits application ended after other claims received merits adjudication; the Ford claim was dismissed, not retained in an ongoing proceeding. A wholly unexhausted application dismissed without prejudice presents a different situation.

For the dissenters, the court's reservation cannot create an exception that the enacted text does not supply, and the State's earlier prematurity request does not waive the statutory restriction. Ex parte Bollman's requirement of written legal authority reinforces their objection to restoring prior judicial practice after Congress changed the governing law. They would require dismissal of this repeated claim under §2244(b)(1), not an opportunity for discretionary authorization under an invented exception. They decide no competency question, order no execution and express no disposition of a genuinely separate lawful proceeding.

#### Source Notes

The procedural chronology and reported prior rulings appear in [523 U.S. 637–652](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-523/pdf/USREPORTS-523-637.pdf), 118 F.3d 628 and 80 F.3d 1301, 1309 n.1. [AEDPA §106](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-104publ132/html/PLAW-104publ132.htm) supplies the successive-application provisions. The state competency determination is a reported prior ruling, not a competency finding by this Court.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The circuit's return of the claim to the District Court stands. That court must consider the renewed Ford claim through otherwise applicable habeas procedures; the proper form of consideration and entitlement to relief remain for determination. No competency finding, new evidentiary-hearing requirement, stay, writ or execution direction is entered by this judgment.

**End of entry: Stewart v. Martinez-Villareal, decision, May 18, 1998.**

### Dodd v. Hood River County, No. 98-54; Ninth Circuit No. 97-35124

Merits decision, November 19, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 136 F.3d 1219, decided February 13, 1998, affirming summary judgment on the owners' federal takings claims.

The Dodds bought a 40-acre forest-use parcel in 1983 for a retirement home. Oregon proceedings determined that profitable timber use remained. The owners reserved their federal claim and obtained state judicial review. The Ninth Circuit applied issue preclusion narrowly to profitable use and separately rejected the remaining federal grounds; it did not adopt the District Court's equation of the entire state and federal takings inquiries. County authorization to build issued July 1, 1996, leaving the earlier period as the subject of the claimed deprivation.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela, who would preserve the narrow preclusion ruling but reserve and remand the federal issues outside the state determination.

All nine directly join the preclusive effect of the necessary, fairly litigated finding of profitable timber use. That agreement concerns a legal premise within this single judgment; it does not separately dispose of the federal cause or establish a precise residual valuation. Eight Justices join the independent federal grounds sustaining the judgment.

No new permit, compensation award or jury trial is ordered. The July 1996 permission limits duration; it does not itself erase an earlier taking.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Unanimous preclusion proposition; Stone opposes the final affirmance | Lawful Oregon effect, actual requirements and the limited profitable-use finding. |
| Opinion of the Court, Parts II–IV | Stevens | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Court, eight; complete affirmance | Independent federal merits, actual duration and resulting denial of further relief. |
| Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | Stone-Zsela | Unjoined | Joins Part I; would remand the remaining federal issues | Preclusion's limits and express reservation of federal questions outside the Oregon determination. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Reservation of the federal cause does not erase a necessary, fairly litigated state finding

**Operative rule:** Section 1738 gives the Oregon judicial determination its lawful state preclusive effect: reserving a federal takings cause does not exempt an identical factual issue actually and necessarily litigated with a full and fair opportunity by parties bound in proceedings Oregon recognizes. Those requirements establish preclusion of profitable timber use here, but not of the entire federal claim, every federal element, a precise residual valuation or the legal assessment of partial deprivation.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court's opinion has all nine Justices' direct support.

**Controlling explanation:** Baker requires lawful rendering-state effect without a general public-policy exception; Allen applies that principle to actually litigated issues. England preserves federal questions following federal abstention, not a unilateral right to contradict a necessary finding in separate state compensation litigation. Williamson County's then-governing compensation requirement does not create the claimed exemption, and the Dodds completed the state process. Richards requires lawful authority to bind and adequate opportunity; these owners were actual parties.

Each Oregon condition is satisfied at the proper level of specificity. The existence of profitable timber use is the same factual issue relevant to total federal deprivation. The competing appraisal and forester evidence were presented, and the use finding was necessary to the final state no-taking determination. Available evidentiary procedures, including a hearing available on request and witness examination, together with actual presentation and judicial review, supplied a full and fair opportunity. The Dodds themselves were parties. The LUBA adjudication and ensuing judicial review are proceedings Oregon recognizes for this purpose. The reported failure to request the available hearing does not eliminate the opportunity; Kremer distinguishes adequacy from use of every available procedure.

The federal cause remains distinct. The state finding establishes retained profitable use, not the federal significance of every restriction or a particular dollar value. The Court therefore rejects complete preclusion and separately addresses the federal grounds. No rule makes every administrative proceeding fair or every reserved federal issue precluded.

##### II. Profitable timber use defeats only the complete-deprivation category

**Operative rule:** The binding finding that this parcel retains profitable timber use defeats a Lucas claim that the restriction eliminated all economically beneficial use. It does not itself decide partial deprivation under Penn Central, and no background-property limitation need be found when the necessary complete loss has not been established.

**Authority:** Part II has the eight Associate Justices' direct support; Stone-Zsela does not join this federal merits determination.

**Controlling explanation:** Lucas supplies a categorical compensation rule after complete deprivation, subject to a substantiated antecedent property or nuisance limitation. Profitable timber use prevents the owners from establishing that categorical premise. The Court need not decide that Oregon's Goal 4 restriction already excluded the proposed dwelling from title or shift Lucas's background-law burden before total deprivation is shown. Prior enactment or a legislative description of harm would not alone satisfy that burden in a case that reached it. Del Monte Dunes preserves the separate partial-taking inquiry; the failure of the total-loss category does not end that inquiry here.

##### III. The independent contextual federal inquiry supports this summary judgment

**Operative rule:** A partial-taking claim requires consideration together of economic impact, reasonable investment-backed expectations and the character of governmental action; retained value, acquisition under an existing restriction or a legitimate regulatory objective alone is not dispositive. On this undisputed record, continued profitable timber use, the existing necessary-and-accessory dwelling condition, nonassuring County communications and the forest-management character of the restriction support rejection of the claim; the restriction also bears the required relationship to the specified forestry purposes under the received Agins inquiry, without a demanded conveyance triggering Dolan.

**Authority:** Part III has the eight Associate Justices' direct support, including its distinct contextual and purpose-relationship applications; Stone-Zsela does not join those merits grounds.

**Controlling explanation:** Penn Central requires the combined inquiry, not an equation between any remaining use and constitutional validity. The property continued to support profitable timber production. The claimed retirement dwelling was subject at acquisition to an existing statewide requirement that it be necessary and accessory to forest use. County communications did not promise the required permits. Those facts bear on the reasonableness of this particular expectation; they do not establish that later purchasers lose constitutional protection.

The regulation restricts forest use to serve actual forestry, fire-protection and chemical-application objectives. It neither occupies the parcel physically nor demands a property conveyance. Its actual relationship to those purposes satisfies the received Agins inquiry at the bounded scope preserved in Del Monte Dunes. Dolan's special nexus-and-proportionality burden for a demanded conveyance therefore does not govern.

The owners correctly distinguish their federal cause from the narrower Oregon finding and insist that retained use alone cannot answer their claim. The Court addresses the remaining legal restriction, actual communications, continuing use and governmental character independently. It neither fixes a percentage diminution nor calls an unknown loss trivial. The reported undisputed record supplies no material factual conflict requiring another trial under the governing standards. Del Monte Dunes protects a properly supported factual taking claim while retaining judicial sufficiency review; it does not guarantee a trial on a precluded fact or unsupported dispute. Personal residential plans receive ordinary property protection, and the result does not depend on labeling them noncommercial.

##### IV. Later permission limits the claimed duration without curing a prior taking

**Operative rule:** The July 1, 1996 building authorization ends treatment of this dispute as an ongoing prohibition but does not itself defeat compensation for an earlier proved taking. Because the independently considered total- and partial-taking grounds establish no antecedent violation here, the actual-period claim supplies no compensation or basis for new factfinding.

**Authority:** Part IV has the eight Associate Justices' direct support; Stone-Zsela would reserve and remand the federal issues rather than join this final resolution.

**Controlling explanation:** First English, Lucas and Del Monte Dunes preserve compensation for the attributable duration of an actual taking. Permission issued later does not make an earlier unconstitutional deprivation disappear. Equally, delay in permission does not establish the antecedent violation without the substantive inquiry. That inquiry has been resolved against the owners on the stated record. The Court therefore awards no compensation, orders no new permit and requires no additional factfinding. Criticism of how quickly the owners pursued development supplies no constitutional forfeiture rule.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Baker v. General Motors Corp. (January 13, 1998): applies its unanimous lawful-recognition component, not its distinct testimony-process application.
- Allen v. McCurry: applies ordinary issue preclusion to an actually litigated state determination; the entire federal cause is not equated with the state claim.
- Kremer v. Chemical Construction Corp.: preserves the distinction between an adequate available opportunity and failure to employ every procedure.
- Richards v. Jefferson County: preserves constitutional authority to bind and fair opportunity; these participating owners differ from absent litigants without a sufficient binding relationship.
- England v. Louisiana State Board of Medical Examiners: preserves federal questions after abstention without creating a unilateral exemption for necessary findings from separate state proceedings.
- Williamson County Regional Planning Commission v. Hamilton Bank: retains its then-effective compensation prerequisite at the boundaries preserved in Del Monte Dunes; no new ripeness rule is adopted.
- Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council: applies complete-deprivation requirements and preserves the conditional background-law burden and distinct partial-taking analysis.
- Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City: applies the combined economic-impact, expectations and governmental-character inquiry; no single-factor shortcut is adopted.
- Del Monte Dunes at Monterey, Ltd. v. City of Monterey (June 16, 1997): preserves the total/partial distinction, actual-period compensation, bounded purpose-relationship inquiry and ordinary judicial sufficiency review alongside its jury rule.

- First English Evangelical Lutheran Church v. County of Los Angeles: preserves compensation for a proved taking's actual duration; later permission is not a retrospective cure.

#### Law After Decision

A federal-claim reservation preserves questions outside the actual state adjudication but does not erase the ordinary preclusive effect of necessary, fairly litigated common facts. Here profitable timber use binds the owners and defeats complete deprivation. The distinct federal partial-taking and purpose-relationship inquiries are independently resolved on the actual record; continued use alone does not decide them. Later permitting limits duration without automatically defeating a temporary-taking claim. No compensation, universal notice-at-purchase bar or special disability for residential plans is created.

Stone-Zsela concurs in Part I and dissents from final resolution of the remaining federal issues. He agrees that §1738 governs the lawful effect of matters actually, necessarily and fairly decided in Oregon and that a federal reservation cannot erase the binding profitable-use finding. He also agrees that the whole federal cause and its unadjudicated elements are not precluded merely because state takings litigation occurred.

He would preserve that narrow preclusion and return the federal questions outside the state determination for adjudication. He does not join the Court's independent conclusions under Lucas, Penn Central, the purpose-relationship inquiry or the actual-period claim. His position neither finds a taking nor directs compensation, a permit or a jury trial, and it does not announce a separate substantive takings rule. The disagreement concerns final adjudication of those federal issues in this disposition after recognizing the limited state effect.

The complete [Ninth Circuit opinion, 136 F.3d 1219](https://openjurist.org/136/f3d/1219), including its preclusion and independent federal-merits discussions, supplies the state proceedings, available evidentiary procedures, actual County communications, remaining timber use and July 1, 1996 authorization. The Ninth Circuit judgment stands on the confined preclusion ruling and independently stated federal grounds. Supreme Court review is complete. No compensation, new permit, additional trial or further factfinding is ordered, and no further Supreme Court event is set.

### Reno v. Goncalves, No. 98-835

Merits decision, June 3, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 21, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the First Circuit, 144 F.3d 110, which reversed the dismissal of Goncalves's habeas petition and required administrative consideration of his request for discretionary relief. The questions concern whether section 2241 jurisdiction survives the 1996 immigration amendments, whether AEDPA section 440(d) governs a former section 212(c) application pending at enactment, and the interpretive weight due Matter of Soriano. Goncalves applied in September 1994, received an adverse discretionary decision in January 1995, and had a timely administrative appeal pending on April 24, 1996. The Board later applied section 440(d)'s eligibility bar. Following immigration custody, he filed this habeas petition in August 1997.

All nine Justices participated in argument and decision.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| District-court jurisdiction over the actual section 2241 petition | Sustained, unanimous | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | The statutory habeas channel remains available for this legal eligibility challenge |
| Eligibility disposition and administrative relief | First Circuit judgment affirmed, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Board consideration under the eligibility law applicable to the pending application when AEDPA took effect |

The Board shall consider Goncalves's former section 212(c) application under the eligibility law applicable to the pending application when AEDPA took effect and exercise the discretion that law supplies. The immigration judge's earlier discretionary denial does not replace the Board's decision on the timely appeal. No favorable exercise of discretion, waiver, release or termination of deportation proceedings is awarded; equal protection is not adjudicated.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; also Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas | Sustains actual section 2241 jurisdiction | Entire jurisdiction part; all nine |
| Opinion of the Court, Parts II–III | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Affirms the eligibility construction and administrative remand | Entire temporal, deference and remedy parts; six |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | — | Joins the Court's opinion and judgment | Additional pending-process and new-disability explanation |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Scalia | Kennedy and Thomas | Sustains jurisdiction but would reverse the eligibility relief | Joins Part I; rejects Parts II–III's temporal and resulting-remedy conclusions |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The actual statutory habeas channel survives

**Holding and operative rule:** AEDPA and IIRIRA did not clearly withdraw the independently enacted jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. section 2241 to decide Goncalves's substantial legal claim that the agency unlawfully denied eligibility for discretionary relief. That channel reaches substantial statutory and constitutional objections to lawful executive authority when its ordinary custody, respondent, forum, exhaustion and other predicates are met; it does not authorize judicial reweighing of discretionary equities or retrial of disputed deportability facts.

**Authority:** Part I of Stone-Zsela's opinion, joined by every other Justice, controls unanimously.

**Controlling explanation:** Kolster distinguishes withdrawal of ordinary INA review from repeal of the independently enacted writ and includes unlawful denial of legal eligibility within the writ's conditional scope. Here there is actual custody, an actual petition and a completed administrative legal determination; no separate jurisdictional defect is established. Felker, McCardle and Yerger require attention to the particular channel Congress withdrew. AEDPA section 401(e) deletes the specialized INA habeas provision; section 440(a) limits ordinary direct review. Neither clearly repeals section 2241. IIRIRA section 309(c)(4)(G) bars the covered transitional appeal but does not silently eliminate the independent writ. Reyes-Hernandez and Reno v. American-Arab Anti-Discrimination Committee confine section 242(g) to the named executive actions; this legal challenge to completed adjudication is not absorbed into that provision. The permanent section 242(b)(9) regime cannot be imported wholesale into these transitional proceedings. The Government's combined-channel argument therefore cannot do what the particular provisions do not establish. Jurisdiction rests on the surviving statute, not the seriousness of a claim alone. Reno v. American-Arab Anti-Discrimination Committee's separate section 1331 constitutional-selection route is neither needed nor enlarged. The Court does not decide the Suspension Clause's independent minimum or license review of discretionary merits.

##### II. Section 440(d) does not govern this pending application

**Holding and operative rule:** AEDPA section 440(d)'s new categorical eligibility restriction does not govern a former section 212(c) application filed before AEDPA took effect on April 24, 1996 and still awaiting final administrative action at enactment. Goncalves's pending administrative appeal must be considered under the former eligibility rule; the holding neither covers applications first filed after enactment nor determines the distinct temporal operation of IIRIRA's later repeal.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court; the six-Justice coalition joining Parts II–III, identified above, directly adopts this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** Landgraf and Rivers first require identification of Congress's temporal choice for the particular provision. Lindh permits ordinary statutory construction to establish that choice through express and omitted temporal directions. Sections 413(g) and 421(b) expressly reach pending applications, including applications for discretionary relief; section 440(d) does not. Section 440(f)'s separate instruction concerns subsection (e), not subsection (d). The enactment thus supplies more than silence considered alone. The objection that the Attorney General is deciding whether to grant present discretionary relief overlooks Congress's express treatment of pending discretionary requests elsewhere. Treating all eligibility restrictions as automatically applicable to such requests would erase that distinction. This amendment changes legal eligibility, not merely the tribunal or channel of review. Goncalves had invoked the discretionary process and pursued an appeal before enactment. The provision-specific construction preserves that pending application without requiring an invented plea bargain, subjective reliance or a vested right to a favorable waiver. Hincapie-Nieto, Salazar-Haro and Kolster resolved different review-channel restrictions; they do not settle section 440(d)'s substantive eligibility change. Reyes-Hernandez's completed surrender of a defense is not the exclusive possible temporal predicate, and no such surrender is found here. The Court adopts no rule that pending status alone preserves every former law.

##### III. Agency interpretation cannot override this statutory conclusion

**Holding and operative rule:** Matter of Soriano receives no controlling weight on section 440(d)'s temporal application because ordinary interpretation resolves that question against the agency's construction; its reasoning is not persuasive enough to overcome the enacted distinctions. The Attorney General likewise cannot conclusively determine the district court's section 2241 jurisdiction, and the Board must decide the pending request without applying section 440(d) as an eligibility bar.

**Authority:** Part III of the opinion of the Court; the same six-Justice coalition directly adopts this rule and its administrative remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** MCI enforces the limits of enacted authority; NationsBank and Aguirre-Aguirre allow agency interpretation within delegated authority and permissible statutory meaning. Those decisions do not empower an agency to override a temporal choice established through ordinary construction. Soriano gives insufficient effect to the express treatment of pending requests in neighboring provisions and would make silence in section 440(d) carry a consequence that the statutory structure does not support. The Court does not replace the existing deference framework or hold that every temporal question excludes agency expertise. Authorized judgments on genuinely unresolved statutory details, policy and technical administration retain their proper weight. The judgment removes a legal barrier to consideration; it does not decide how the Board must balance the lawful discretionary factors. Equal protection remains outside the grant.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Kolster v. INS:** Applied to an actual petition satisfying the previously reserved predicates; legal eligibility falls within its retained authority category, while discretionary reweighing remains excluded.
- **Felker v. Turpin:** Applied for channel-specific repeal analysis; withdrawal of one review mechanism does not itself repeal a separate jurisdictional grant.
- **Ex parte McCardle:** Its effect is confined to the appellate route Congress withdrew; it does not establish repeal of every independent habeas route.
- **Ex parte Yerger:** Supports preservation of an independently available writ despite withdrawal of another review channel.

- **Reno v. American-Arab Anti-Discrimination Committee:** Supplies the named-action/completed-adjudication distinction. Its separate constitutional section 1331 route is not extended.
- **Hincapie-Nieto v. INS:** Its ordinary-review restriction and rejection of pending status alone remain controlling; they do not decide section 440(d)'s different eligibility change.
- **Salazar-Haro v. INS:** Its review-channel rule remains intact and supplies no general immediate-effect rule for every substantive eligibility restriction.
- **Landgraf v. USI Film Products:** Supplies the sequence requiring identification of Congress's temporal choice; the Court applies ordinary statutory construction without adopting a universal pending-case exception.
- **Rivers v. Roadway Express, Inc.:** Preserves provision-specific temporal analysis and supplies no agency power to override Congress's choice.

#### Law After Decision

The surviving section 2241 route can decide this covered criminal alien's substantial legal eligibility challenge when its actual predicates are satisfied. Applications for former section 212(c) relief already pending on April 24, 1996 remain outside section 440(d)'s new eligibility bar. Eligibility permits the agency to exercise its lawful discretion; it does not predetermine the result. Ordinary-review restrictions, later-filed applications and IIRIRA's distinct repeal remain governed by their own provisions. No precedent is overruled and no general right to review discretionary immigration decisions is created.

#### Separate Writings

Stone-Zsela concurs and joins the Court's opinion. He also explains that Goncalves had invoked the former process, obtained an immigration-judge decision on discretionary merits and pursued an administrative appeal before enactment. Removing eligibility at that stage attaches a new legal disability to the pending proceeding, rather than merely changing its procedure. Under Landgraf and Rivers, that consequence reinforces the enacted temporal contrasts and the absence of a clear direction to impose this new categorical bar on already-filed applications. The argument preserves the opportunity for a lawful discretionary decision; it establishes no entitlement to a favorable waiver, covers no later-filed application and decides no distinct IIRIRA-repeal question.

Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, concurs in the jurisdiction holding and dissents from the eligibility disposition. The operative event for section 440(d) is the Attorney General's present decision whether to grant relief. Goncalves has received no waiver, and this record establishes no surrendered legal option requiring protection of a future discretionary grant. Sections 413 and 421 address different restrictions and do not, in the dissent's view, create a saving instruction for section 440(d). The dissent therefore regards Soriano's application to the unresolved agency decision as lawful, without treating the agency as authoritative on federal habeas jurisdiction or permitting deference contrary to enacted text. It would reverse the eligibility relief while retaining jurisdiction; it does not decide the ungranted equal-protection claim or declare all collateral grounds meritless.

The decision orders no new evidentiary hearing, automatic stay, release, waiver or execution of deportation and resolves no excluded equal-protection question.

### Greater New Orleans Broadcasting Association, Inc. v. United States, No. 98-387

Merits decision, June 14, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 27, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 149 F.3d 334 (1998). Louisiana broadcasters seek declaratory and injunctive relief against §1304 and its companion FCC restriction as applied to truthful advertisements for lawful private-casino gambling in Louisiana and Mississippi. The question is whether that application satisfies commercial-speech scrutiny in light of the federal scheme's actual exceptions and asserted interests.

Section 1304 prohibits federally licensed radio or television broadcasts of advertisements or information concerning a lottery, gift enterprise or similar scheme offering prizes dependent wholly or partly on chance, including prize lists, and prohibits an operator's knowing permission of such broadcasting. It authorizes a fine, up to one year's imprisonment, or both, with each broadcast day a separate offense. The materially relevant exceptions have distinct conditions:

- Section 1307(a)(1) permits information concerning a lottery conducted by a State under its law when the station is licensed in that State or another State conducting such a lottery.
- Section 1307(a)(2) covers a different scheme only if authorized or not prohibited where conducted and conducted by a nonprofit or governmental organization, or as clearly occasional commercial promotion ancillary to the organization's primary business. For this section, nonprofit status means an organization that would qualify as tax exempt under Internal Revenue Code §501.
- Section 2720 exempts tribal gaming conducted pursuant to and consistently with the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act's chapter requirements.
- Section 1307(b)'s distinct transport/mail exemptions concern state-conducted lottery material sent to in-state addresses or material intended for a lawful foreign lottery and sent to an addressee there; they do not independently exempt §1304 broadcasts. Its special definition of lottery concerns pooled ticket/chance proceeds distributed by chance and excludes sports bets; that definition applies to subsection (b), not automatically to the broadcasting prohibition.

These provisions do not allow every gambling advertisement other than private-casino advertising.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Government may not enforce §1304 against these truthful, nonmisleading advertisements for lawful private-casino gambling. Relief concerning the companion FCC restriction conforms to that same as-applied prohibition. Section 1304 and the rule are not facially invalidated; no damages or replacement gambling policy is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reversal and as-applied relief | The author and all seven joining Justices adopt both independent advancement and fit holdings. Stevens's application is conditional under existing scrutiny; Scalia retains broader doctrinal reservations while joining both applications. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Thomas | — | Same judgment and relief | Categorical objection to suppressing truthful lawful information to manipulate consumer choices; no join in either balancing holding |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Direct and material advancement

Section 1304 cannot be enforced against these Louisiana broadcasters' truthful, nonmisleading advertisements for lawful private-casino gambling because the federal scheme's actual exceptions and operation do not directly and materially advance its asserted gambling-harm reduction and state-policy accommodation interests. Potential reception in a State prohibiting private casinos does not itself make the advertised lawful transactions unlawful.

**Authority:** The Court's opinion controls through the eight-Justice coalition identified above. Stevens joins this application conditionally under the existing framework; Scalia joins the application while retaining broader doctrinal reservations. Thomas supports the judgment on a different ground and adds no balancing vote.

**Controlling explanation:** Central Hudson requires a substantial interest and direct material advancement; Edenfield requires real harm and material alleviation without invariably demanding studies. Even accepting the stated interests as substantial, the Government must explain how this restriction serves them in its actual setting. The conditioned state, tribal, nonprofit, governmental and occasional promotional exceptions allow materially comparable gambling promotion that undercuts the claimed demand-reduction mechanism. Different policies supporting those exceptions do not explain why suppression of these lawful private-casino advertisements materially serves that mechanism. Rubin requires attention to those contradictions rather than the prohibition viewed alone.

Edge upheld a licensing-State restriction that meaningfully reduced promotion and accommodated lottery and nonlottery policies on its record. This differently structured private-casino scheme does not establish that mechanism merely because some signals may cross a border. The Court does not require regulation of every source at once, regard leakage or any exception as automatically fatal, or demand numerical causal proof. It does not decide whether deficiencies in advertising/social-cost evidence independently fail the Government's evidentiary burden or find that advertising never increases demand. Stronger evidence of the assumed causal chain would not cure the structural defects identified here; this is not a remand to rescue this scheme with additional causal studies.

##### Reasonable fit

Independently, suppressing these truthful lawful-casino advertisements lacks a reasonable proportional fit with the asserted interests, given the scheme's extensive comparable permissions and available direct regulation of gambling. The Government may pursue genuine incremental policies, but the actual informational burden requires justification; the First Amendment does not require the least restrictive imaginable policy.

**Authority:** The same eight-Justice Court-opinion coalition directly joins this independently sufficient ground, with the same stated reservations.

**Controlling explanation:** Fox requires a reasonable fit, not the least restrictive means. Rubin and 44 Liquormart treat the burden on truthful information as an inquiry distinct from material advancement. Here the restriction broadly suppresses lawful gaming information while permitting the described comparable promotion. The ability to address gambling conduct directly bears on whether that informational burden is proportionate. Different interstate reach or administrative convenience does not itself explain the actual imbalance. The Court orders no tax, casino limit or replacement program and does not assume every alternative would achieve identical effects. It holds only that the restriction before it lacks justified proportionality on this record. Ordinary Central Hudson review remains governing; the decision creates no heightened tier, vice-speech immunity or general ban on incremental economic regulation. Relief is as applied to the advertisements and stations before the Court, not facial invalidation of §1304 or its implementing rule.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Central Hudson supplies ordinary lawful/nonmisleading commercial-speech review, substantial interest, material advancement and reasonable fit.
- Fox preserves the distinction between reasonable fit and least-restrictive means.
- Edenfield requires justification responsive to real harms and material alleviation without invariably requiring empirical studies.
- Ibanez tests the actual truthful-information restriction and its responsive justification.
- Rubin applies independent advancement and fit inquiries to a materially contradictory scheme and requires an actual state-accommodation mechanism.
- 44 Liquormart preserves those separate inquiries under ordinary scrutiny; its alcohol-specific rule does not create a gambling exception or a new tier.
- Edge retains its record-specific meaningful-reduction and state-accommodation holding; the different statutory permissions and mechanism here do not reproduce it.
- Posadas is not overruled through this as-applied decision, and no categorical constitutional rule against all vice regulation is adopted.

#### Law After Decision

The described lawful private-casino advertising is protected against this federal prohibition because each of two ordinary commercial-speech requirements fails independently. False or misleading advertisements, promotion of unlawful transactions, targeting minors and materially narrower evidence-supported restrictions remain outside the holding. Direct regulation of gambling survives. Other asserted applications require their own justification; neither §1304 nor the FCC restriction is erased in all settings.

#### Separate Writings

Thomas agrees with the judgment because government may not keep consumers ignorant of truthful information about lawful commercial activity in order to manipulate their choices. The casino advertisements present that concern directly; he does not require balancing the asserted interests or measuring precise market effects. His ground remains separate from the eight-Justice advancement and fit holdings and does not change the Court's governing scrutiny standard.

Statutory text: [18 U.S.C. §1304](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title18/html/USCODE-1994-title18-partI-chap61-sec1304.htm), [18 U.S.C. §1307](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title18/html/USCODE-1994-title18-partI-chap61-sec1307.htm), and [25 U.S.C. §2720](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title25/html/USCODE-1994-title25-chap29-sec2720.htm). [Official record recitations](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep527/usrep527173/usrep527173.pdf).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for declaratory and appropriate injunctive relief conforming to the established as-applied invalidity. The Government may not enforce the prohibition against the advertisements and stations before the Court. No new merits trial, causal-evidence reopening, damages award, gambling-policy replacement or relief for materially different advertising is ordered.

**End of entry: Greater New Orleans Broadcasting Association, Inc. v. United States, Merits decision, June 14, 1999.**

### Hartford Underwriters Insurance Co. v. Union Planters Bank, N.A., No. 99-409

Merits decision, May 30, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 20, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the en banc Eighth Circuit, 177 F.3d 719. The question is whether an administrative claimant other than the trustee may independently recover from secured property under 11 U.S.C. §506(c). Hartford sought $51,871.40 in unpaid workers' compensation premiums after Chapter 11 operations and conversion to Chapter 7; it was neither trustee nor debtor in possession.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinions

Thomas delivered the opinion of the Court. All eight other Justices joined the statutory claimant rule, its application and reservations, and the affirmance in full.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Section 506(c) gives the independent recovery power to the trustee

**Holding and operative rule:** Only the trustee, including a debtor in possession exercising the trustee's statutory powers, may invoke section 506(c) in its own right to recover reasonable, necessary costs of preserving or disposing of property securing an allowed secured claim, to the extent those costs benefit the secured claimholder. An unpaid administrative claimant's status and asserted benefit to the collateral do not create an independent section 506(c) action.

**Authority:** The unanimous Court opinion establishes the complete holding and bounded affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress named the trustee as the actor who may recover. Section 1107(a) expressly gives a debtor in possession the relevant trustee powers, showing how another actor receives that authority. Hartford holds neither status. The absence of the word only does not convert a power assigned to a designated estate representative into a right held by every claimant whose expenditure benefited collateral.

The contrary argument invokes historical surcharge practice, benefit to the lender and the risk that a trustee in an insolvent estate may lack an incentive to collect. Section 506(c) itself creates the collateral charge, so the issue is not whether all priority exceptions are forbidden. It is who may enforce this one independently. Sections 503 and 1109 do different work: estate-expense treatment and Chapter 11 participation do not give Hartford an independent collateral action in converted Chapter 7. Historical practice and policy cannot override that enacted allocation. No actual trustee refusal or misconduct is found.

Patterson and Taylor enforce coherent statutory text and procedure; Noland and CF&I preserve actual Code equity while rejecting replacement of enacted choices with general fairness. PBGC distinguishes underlying rights from allowance and distribution. Mertens similarly requires attention to the remedy Congress authorized; it does not decide this statute by analogy alone. Dewsnup's confined lien-meaning uncertainty supplies no reason to disregard the named claimant here.

The Court reserves authorized derivative pursuit, assignment, actual trustee recovery, its allocation or recovery before payment of the expense, debtor-in-possession action and genuinely separate remedies on their own predicates. Hartford's Rule 7071/Rule 71 financing-order theory was first raised en banc and rejected as an unpleaded matter outside that limited review; the reservation does not revive it. Ultimate collateral benefit and administrative allowance remain unadjudicated.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Patterson v. Shumate** and **Taylor v. Freeland & Kronz:** Applied for enforcing enacted bankruptcy text and distinct procedures; their actual exclusions, deadlines and reservations remain unchanged.
- **United States v. Noland** and **United States v. Reorganized CF&I Fabricators:** Applied to preserve authorized bankruptcy equity while rejecting a substitute allocation unsupported by the Code; no universal misconduct rule is added.
- **PBGC v. CF&I Fabricators:** Its distinction between underlying entitlement, allowance and distribution supports treating section 503 and section 506(c) as separate questions.
- **Mertens v. Hewitt Associates:** Applied for attention to the statutorily authorized remedy; its ERISA remedy holding does not itself answer the different bankruptcy text.
- **Dewsnup v. Timm:** Distinguished as a confined contextual construction of lien language; it creates no general authority to replace a named claimant with another.

#### Law After Decision

Section 506(c) confers no independent surcharge action on an administrative claimant merely because its expenses benefited collateral. The trustee's statutory office and the debtor in possession's express powers govern independent invocation. The decision creates no general ban on authorized derivative procedures, other actual remedies or bankruptcy equity exercised within its statutory source.

#### Source Notes

The [en banc Eighth Circuit opinion, 177 F.3d 719](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/177/177.F3d.719.97-3859.html), supplies the proceedings, amount sought, limited review and unpleaded financing-order theory. [The official reporter, 530 U.S. 1](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-530/pdf/USREPORTS-530-1.pdf), confirms the argument and decision dates. Sections 506(c) and 1107(a) supply the distinct recovery and debtor-in-possession authority. No finding of final collateral benefit or estate allowance is made.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The en banc Eighth Circuit's direction to vacate the surcharge and dismiss Hartford's independent section 506(c) request remains in force. The Court adjudicates neither a personal lender judgment, an estate administrative-claim entitlement, the expenditure's ultimate benefit nor any recovery amount. Separate estate claims and genuinely authorized alternative procedures remain subject to their own preservation and substantive requirements; this judgment orders none and revives no unpleaded claim.

**End of entry: Hartford Underwriters Insurance Co. v. Union Planters Bank, N.A., merits decision, May 30, 2000.**
