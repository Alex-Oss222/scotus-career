## Standards and Tests selection: Private enforcement of Title VI disparate-impact regulations

### Private enforcement of Title VI disparate-impact regulations

**Governing question:** Whether a Title VI effects regulation supplies a directly implied action or an individual right enforceable through §1983.

**Current rule:** Title VI does not imply a private action to enforce 34 C.F.R. §100.3(b)(2)’s additional disparate-impact prohibition merely because the regulation validly implements §602. Separately, §601’s intentional-discrimination protection and §602’s agency directions do not create the broader individual effects right asserted through §1983. A regulation can elaborate an existing statutory entitlement but cannot itself create the missing broader statutory right. Failure of the implied-action route does not itself decide §1983; the latter fails here at right creation, before any congressional-foreclosure inquiry.

**Authority by component:**

- No directly implied impact-only action: Powell v. Ridge — The regulation does not supply the directly implied action asserted in Count I, April 3, 2000.
- Absence of the broader individual statutory entitlement, distinct from foreclosure: Powell — Section 1983 does not create the missing individual effects right, April 3, 2000. Thomas’s opinion joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia and Kennedy controls both.

**Present operation effective:** Powell, April 3, 2000.

**Exceptions and limits:** Section 601’s established intentional-discrimination action, genuine regulatory elaboration of existing rights and authorized agency enforcement remain. The decision does not invalidate §100.3(b)(2), make every statistical disparity unlawful, or adopt a private exhaustion prerequisite from §602’s administrative procedures.

## Opening authority: Central Bank of Denver, N.A. v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A., 511 U.S. 164

Source: `state/holdings/business-and-commercial-law.md`.

#### Central Bank of Denver, N.A. v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A., 511 U.S. 164

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-854.

**Decided:** 1994-04-19.

##### The received private action includes culpable substantial assistance

**Holding:** The established private action under §10(b) and Rule 10b-5 permits recovery from a person who knowingly or with severe recklessness substantially assists an underlying securities fraud through affirmative conduct, subject to proof of that fraud, the defendant’s qualifying participation, legally sufficient reliance, causation and recoverable loss. This is accessory responsibility within the received investor action, not a finding that the assistant made a primary misrepresentation or a rule imposing liability through professional status or control alone.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt the existence of the confined action and its received-remedy rationale. No fractured-decision synthesis is necessary.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Superintendent of Insurance v. Bankers Life & Casualty Co., 404 U.S. 6 — applied as recognition of the received private action; it did not itself decide accessory liability.
- Musick, Peeler & Garrett v. Employers Insurance of Wausau, 508 U.S. 286 — applied for bounded development of the received action and distinguished as to its actual contribution holding; shared liability must still be established before allocation.
- Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc. v. Curran, 456 U.S. 353 — used by analogy for the significance of an established judicial remedy; its different statutory setting does not establish congressional enactment of this action.
- Santa Fe Industries, Inc. v. Green, 430 U.S. 462 — applied: the underlying wrong must involve the required manipulation or deception, not unfairness or fiduciary misconduct alone.
- Pinter v. Dahl, 486 U.S. 622 — distinguished: its construction of express seller liability under §12 does not settle this received §10(b) action; its own statutory holding remains intact.
- Ernst & Ernst v. Hochfelder, 425 U.S. 185 — its reservation of aiding and abetting is resolved here for the confined private affirmative-assistance action; Hochfelder itself did not decide that question.
- Herman & MacLean v. Huddleston, 459 U.S. 375 — its reservation of aiding and abetting is resolved here for the confined private affirmative-assistance action; Huddleston itself did not decide that question.

##### Knowledge or severe recklessness in affirmative substantial assistance

**Holding:** For affirmative substantial assistance, the defendant must know or severely recklessly disregard both the fraudulent character of the activity and the assisting role of its conduct; severe recklessness means an extreme departure from ordinary care presenting a specific danger of deception known to the defendant or so obvious that the defendant must have appreciated it. No independent disclosure duty is required for that affirmative participation, but negligence, valuation concerns alone, professional status, ordinary services without qualifying culpability and assistance, or mere silence without a relevant duty do not suffice.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg; all five adopt this confined mental-state and affirmative-assistance rule, not a universal recklessness rule for every securities claim.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Ernst & Ernst v. Hochfelder, 425 U.S. 185 — its exclusion of negligence remains controlling; its recklessness reservation is resolved only for this private affirmative-assistance setting.
- Santa Fe Industries, Inc. v. Green, 430 U.S. 462 — its deception-or-manipulation requirement prevents ordinary investment risk and professional error from becoming securities fraud.
- First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A. v. Pring, 969 F.2d 891 — its confined affirmative-assistance and extreme-recklessness analysis is adopted for the bank claim; inconsistent knowledge-only or categorical duty requirements for such affirmative assistance cannot govern after this decision. Its separate rulings concerning Pring are outside this review.

##### Triable issues require further proceedings, not a liability award

**Holding:** Viewed favorably to the purchasers at summary judgment, the appraisal warnings, internal concerns and agreement postponing an already demanded independent review until after the offering permit a genuine dispute over severe recklessness and substantial affirmative assistance; the Tenth Circuit’s reinstatement of the pleaded bank claim is affirmed and the case remanded. The purchasers must still prove the underlying violation, the bank’s qualifying assistance and culpability, legally sufficient reliance on the underlying fraud, causation and recoverable loss under the ordinary civil burden; neither the default nor the bonds’ face value establishes fraud damages.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt the record application, preserved proof requirements and limited affirmance and remand.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A. v. Pring, 969 F.2d 891 — affirmed insofar as it reverses summary judgment on the purchasers’ affirmative-assistance claim against Central Bank; triable issues are not findings of fraud or damages.
- Herman & MacLean v. Huddleston, 459 U.S. 375 — the ordinary preponderance standard is preserved; neither institutional status nor information held by the defendant shifts that burden.
- Basic Inc. v. Levinson, 485 U.S. 224 — its reliance requirement is preserved. Today’s decision establishes no presumption for these municipal bonds and, as part of the retained accessory action, requires reliance on the underlying fraud rather than on the assistant’s concealed participation.
- Musick, Peeler & Garrett v. Employers Insurance of Wausau, 508 U.S. 286 — its shared-liability prerequisite and contribution limits remain effective; this remand establishes no entitlement or allocation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide the full rules for duty-based omissions, duty-free inaction, agency attribution, a universal securities recklessness rule, other enforcement or statutory settings, or Stone's additional concurrence formulations. The §20(a) defense for a controller acting in good faith without directly or indirectly inducing the violation remains distinct. Reliance on the underlying fraud is required; investors need not personally rely on concealed assistance, and no reliance presumption is established for these bonds.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the Tenth Circuit insofar as it reinstated the pleaded bank claim and remand, 5–4. The bank's summary judgment cannot rest on the rejected categorical grounds or the disputed assistance and culpability record. No liability, damages, automatic indemnity, unpleaded primary claim or adjudication of another defendant's claim is supplied.

## Opening authority: Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, 503 U.S. 60 (1992)

Source: `state/holdings/civil-rights.md`.

#### Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, 503 U.S. 60 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-918.
**Decided:** February 26, 1992.

##### Whether compensatory damages are available in the established Title IX private action for intentional discrimination

**Holding:** When a cognizable federal statutory cause of action exists, courts may award appropriate relief absent clear congressional direction limiting it; recognizing that remedy does not create a new cause of action. In Title IX’s established private action, compensatory damages are available for proved intentional discrimination, subject to proof of recipient responsibility, causation and compensable loss.

**Proposition-level authority:** White’s opinion, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices control the remedial rule and application. Scalia and Thomas concur only in the judgment on a narrower statutory ground.

**Material application:** Franklin’s allegations concern completed sexual harassment and abuse while attending a federally funded school, with alleged official knowledge and inaction. After her departure, backpay and prospective student relief could not compensate the pleaded injury. Congress’s 1986 reference to legal and equitable remedies and subsequent preservation of private enforcement supply no direction barring compensation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Allegations are not findings. No new cause of action, final recipient-attribution rule, punitive remedy, or liability for unintended discriminatory effects is established; the source of Congress’s constitutional power need not be selected.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the categorical damages dismissal and remand for ordinary adjudication of liability, attribution, causation and compensable loss. No amount is awarded or prospective school-supervision order entered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Cannon v. University of Chicago*, 441 U.S. 677, supplies the established Title IX action and is not reopened. *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678, supplies appropriate relief absent congressional limitation. *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228, separates authority to sue from remedy. *J. I. Case Co. v. Borak*, 377 U.S. 426, opposes limiting relief merely because a statute does not enumerate remedies. *Guardians Association v. Civil Service Commission*, 463 U.S. 582, supplies intentional-discrimination remedial support without a single unified majority rationale. *Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Darrone*, 465 U.S. 624, supplies analogous monetary-relief support.

##### Whether Spending Clause notice categorically bars compensation for the intentional Title IX violation alleged

**Holding:** The Spending Clause notice principle does not categorically bar compensatory relief for an intentional violation of Title IX’s express prohibition of sex discrimination within its established private action. Ambiguous funding conditions, unintentional effects and the separate requirements for attributing misconduct to a recipient are not resolved.

**Proposition-level authority:** White’s opinion, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices reject the separate funding-notice ground for dismissal.

**Material application:** The enacted nondiscrimination condition and established private action defeat a categorical notice objection to compensation for the pleaded intentional discrimination. This conclusion does not establish when the school is responsible for the teacher’s conduct.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The notice objection does not sustain dismissal; compensatory relief may be sought on remand subject to proof of the violation and resulting loss.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman*, 451 U.S. 1, retains its fair-notice rule for funding conditions but does not bar relief for the intentional violation alleged. *Meritor Savings Bank, FSB v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57, informs the identification of sex-based harassment without importing a Title VII attribution formula. *Darrone*, 465 U.S. 624, defeats a categorical monetary-remedy objection based on spending legislation. *Cannon*, 441 U.S. 677, supplies the established enforcement premise.

## Opening authority: Blessing v. Freestone

Source: `state/holdings/civil-rights.md`.

#### Blessing v. Freestone

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-1441.
**Decided:** April 21, 1997.

##### What a claimant must identify before invoking §1983 for a statutory violation

**Holding:** The claimant must identify a particular provision intended to benefit the claimant, creating a binding obligation and a sufficiently definite individual entitlement for judicial enforcement. A beneficial statute, mandatory administrative duty, or useful remedy does not alone establish that right; qualitative terms are not universally unenforceable merely because they require judgment.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens), unanimous. Scalia and Kennedy's additional original-meaning reservation withholds no join and supplies no controlling limit.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Wright* and *Wilder* supply defined individual entitlements, not indivisible program promises. *Livadas* separates right, enforcement channel, foreclosure, defendant, and remedy. *Suter* retains provision-specific adjudication; *Pennhurst* requires clear notice without categorical state-plan immunity.

##### Whether Title IV-D creates an individual entitlement to substantial compliance or general administrative adequacy

**Holding:** Title IV-D's aggregate substantial-compliance measure creates no personal right to systemwide compliance. The same theory does not become an individual right when framed as sufficient staffing or satisfactory computer administration as such; genuinely distinct claims under particular provisions require separate assessment.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens), unanimous.

**Material application:** Section 654(3)'s organizational and staffing duties establish no beneficiary entitlement to a specified staff complement, caseload, or worker. Former and amended §654(16) and related §654a management requirements do not make the administrative system itself an individual entitlement. Aggregate compliance could coexist with a violation of an independently existing family-specific right.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Shortages, backlogs, and accounting defects may prove an established violation or inform fitted relief, but cannot create a right. No distinct standardized-computer, confidentiality, or individual transmission claim is established, and no particular clause of 45 C.F.R. §§303.20 or 307.10 is decided. Regulatory elaboration of an existing right is not categorically excluded.

##### Whether general federal administration forecloses all otherwise existing §1983 claims

**Holding:** An individual federal right is presumptively enforceable through §1983 unless Congress expressly excludes that route or provides a sufficiently comprehensive incompatible remedial scheme. Title IV-D audits, corrective plans, and funding sanctions alone do not establish chapterwide foreclosure; the actual provision and any specific incompatible remedy require separate analysis.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens), unanimous.

**Material application:** The federal oversight tools regulate funding and offer no general family-specific administrative forum. Their detail and significance do not alone show incompatibility with §1983.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Livadas*, *Wright*, and *Wilder* keep foreclosure separate from right creation. *Sea Clammers* and *Smith v. Robinson* retain their incompatible-remedy holdings, distinguished from this general oversight arrangement.

##### What effect state-plan placement has under §§1320a-2 and 1320a-10

**Holding:** A Social Security Act provision cannot be denied enforceability merely because it specifies a state plan or its contents. Sections 1320a-2 and 1320a-10 remove that categorical ground without creating an individual right or changing the preserved conclusion that §671(a)(15) is not privately enforceable.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens), unanimous.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The respective statutes apply to actions pending on October 20 and October 31, 1994 and those filed thereafter, including this action. Other established grounds governing private actions remain except the state-plan ground the legislation removes.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Suter*'s actual §671(a)(15) and (9) rights rulings and separate implied-action rulings remain, together with its preservation of the independent §671(a)(16) claim. The statutory reference imports no broader state-plan immunity.

##### Whether a general prayer for relief eliminates Madrid's particular distribution allegation

**Holding:** Madrid's allegation of nondistribution under former §657(b)(1) requires its own inquiry despite the broad programwide prayer. Each actual claim must be matched to its provision, conduct, remedy, governing version, and operative dates; an obsolete or not-yet-effective duty cannot silently justify an injunction.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens), unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The former distribution language is more specific than the aggregate benchmark, but the Court decides no individual right, eligibility, payment fact, defendant, or amount. It concerns the first $50 of periodic monthly support for a month received in that month, plus the first $50 for each prior month received then if the absent parent made that prior payment in the month due, without reducing assistance or affecting eligibility. It does not award a separate $50 for every installment regardless of timing. The rule applies to covered approved-plan collections for fiscal years after September 30, 1976, subject to the distinct foster-care rules in subsection (d).

**Material application:** The foster-care sequence first reimburses current maintenance; qualifying excess up to ordered support goes to the placement agency for the child's interests; the remainder reimburses unrepaid earlier foster-care or family-assistance payments with appropriate federal reimbursement; any balance goes to the agency for the child. The agency may reserve its payments for later needs or make them available to the person providing daily care. No applicable exception or payment fact is established for Madrid.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The remand must apply the following distinct statutory dates and qualifications, without inferring an Arizona implementation event or default:

- Pub. L. 104-193 §395(a) generally makes title III changes requiring state-law enactment or amendment under §666 or state-plan revision under §654 effective for periods beginning October 1, 1996; other provisions generally take effect on August 22, 1996. Specifically provided dates and the following separate grace rules remain controlling.
- Under §395(b), the applicable date is the later of the otherwise specified date or the effective date of implementing state legislation, but no later than the first day of the first calendar quarter after the close of the first regular legislative session beginning after August 22, 1996. Each year of a two-year session counts separately. Under §395(c), a State unable to comply without amending its constitution is protected from a noncompliance finding until the earlier of one year after the necessary amendment takes effect or five years after August 22, 1996. Neither rule establishes an Arizona event or compliance fact here.
- The former §654(24) operational-computer deadline was changed from October 1, 1995 to October 1, 1997 by the October 12, 1995 amendment; the old date establishes no present default. The 1996 text separately requires compliance by October 1, 1997 with requirements enacted on or before October 13, 1988, and by October 1, 2000 with requirements enacted on or before August 22, 1996. The latter deadline extends one day for every day the Secretary misses the final-regulations deadline of two years after August 22, 1996. Implementation deadlines remain distinct from enactment and general effective dates.
- Section 654a(g), added by §312(c), takes effect October 1, 1998. Section 312(d)(2) separately permits a State processing receipts through local courts on August 22, 1996 to continue that receipt processing through September 30, 1999, at its option, through the courts already processing those payments by enactment. That narrow exception concerns §654b(b)(1)'s unit receipt-handling requirement; it is not a wholesale extension of every computer-system obligation. No Arizona qualification for it is found, and these future duties are not April 1997 mandates merely because they appear in the compilation.

##### Whether relief may follow general administrative deficiencies without a proved individual violation

**Holding:** Relief requires an actual individual right, an available §1983 channel, a proper defendant, breach, causation, and a fitted lawful remedy. General administrative failure alone supports neither an undifferentiated compliance injunction nor a prescribed statewide staffing or computer redesign; rejection of the broad theory does not require dismissal of every concrete claim.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Stevens), unanimous.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Vacate and remand for provision-specific adjudication. No specified payment, damages award, structural injunction, or wholesale dismissal is ordered.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No directly implied Title IV-D action is decided. The Eleventh Amendment argument and proposed overruling of *Thiboutot*, neither raised below nor included in the questions granted, are not reached; defendant-specific issues remain subject to their own inquiry.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Maine v. Thiboutot* remains undisturbed. *Livadas*'s separate remedial inquiries prevent both an automatic structural decree and categorical rejection of particular claims.

## Earlier published decision: Powell v. Ridge

Source: `terms/OT1999/output/OT_1999CHUNK4.md`.

### Powell v. Ridge

Merits decision, April 3, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Certiorari granted December 13, 1999; submitted February 28, 2000. On review of the Third Circuit's judgment at 189 F.3d 387, reversing dismissal of two claims challenging alleged racial inequality in Pennsylvania's public-school funding. Count I seeks direct enforcement of 34 C.F.R. §100.3(b)(2); Count II invokes 42 U.S.C. §1983. Both seek declaratory and prospective injunctive relief, not damages.

The questions are whether Title VI's disparate-impact regulation is privately enforceable through either channel, and whether the regulatory theory permits relief without the intentional discrimination required by §601 itself. Parents and schoolchildren allege that districts with more nonwhite pupils receive less state funding at comparable poverty levels and that the resulting resource limits impair educational opportunities. These remain pleading-stage allegations, not findings of discrimination, causation or a funding entitlement.

All nine Justices participated at submission and decision.

#### Judgment

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting | Opposing | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Count I: directly implied action to enforce the disparate-impact regulation | Reverse, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reject the direct impact-only theory; remand for proceedings consistent with that holding. |
| Count II: §1983 enforcement of the asserted impact-only right | Reverse, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reject the asserted statutory effects right; preserve only genuinely remaining ordinary procedural possibilities. |

The complaint contains no separate statutory §601 count. A genuinely preserved intentional-discrimination theory or an ordinary amendment request retains its actual procedural footing; the Court creates no claim and guarantees no amendment. The judgment does not invalidate the regulation, restrict authorized agency enforcement or funding remedies, find nondiscrimination, or order a funding cutoff.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Thomas delivered the opinion of the Court, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy. All five join the distinct direct-action and section 1983 holdings, the remedy and every stated limit. Justice Stevens dissented, joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supporting both prospective enforcement channels and remand. Section 1983 is independently sufficient for the dissent; Stevens would also accept leaving the direct-action alternative unresolved if section 1983 were sustained, but does not join its rejection.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The regulation does not supply the directly implied action asserted in Count I

**Holding:** Title VI does not create a directly implied private action to enforce this §602 regulation's disparate-impact prohibition merely because the regulation validly implements the statutory program. Section 601's established private protection against intentional discrimination and §602's authority for agency implementation do not show congressional creation of the broader impact-only private remedy asserted here.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that regulatory authority and a private judicial remedy answer different questions. Section 601 protects persons against racial, color or national-origin discrimination in federally assisted programs; its established statutory action reaches intentional discrimination. Section 602 directs agencies to effectuate that protection through general rules consistent with the funding statute's objectives and supplies public compliance machinery. It contains no express private action for every condition those rules impose. Cannon's established beneficiary action does not automatically extend to an additional effects obligation. Guardians and Choate recognize the distinction between statutory intentional discrimination and regulatory effects, but their discussion does not decide the private remedy now asserted. The contrary reading draws force from the identified beneficiaries and express implementation authority. Those features support regulatory administration; they do not themselves demonstrate congressional creation of this broader private action. The Court neither invalidates §100.3(b)(2) nor holds that every implementing regulation lacks private significance. It resolves this claim's asserted remedy and preserves §601's established intentional-discrimination action.

##### Section 1983 does not create the missing individual effects right

**Holding:** Count II fails independently because §601's intentional-discrimination protection and §602's instructions to agencies do not confer the broader individual statutory right against disparate impact asserted here. A regulation may elaborate an existing statutory entitlement, but this regulation cannot by itself create the missing broader right for §1983 to enforce; rejection rests on right creation, not congressional foreclosure of an established right.

**Controlling explanation:** Blessing first asks for a particular provision intended to benefit the claimant, imposing a binding duty and defining a sufficiently definite individual entitlement. Only after that right exists does §1983's presumptive enforcement and congressional-foreclosure inquiry arise. Suter likewise keeps the right and directly implied action distinct, while the CAPTA decision shows that a binding agency obligation need not confer the personal entitlement asserted. Wright and Wilder permit enforceable statutory rights whose concrete content includes authorized regulatory elaboration; they do not allow a regulation to enlarge the substantive personal right beyond its statutory foundation. Here §601 identifies protected individuals but supplies an intentional-discrimination right; §602 addresses agency implementation rather than conferring the additional effects entitlement. Bruneau preserves §1983 enforcement of an identified Title IX right despite administrative funding controls and an implied action, but does not enlarge that right. The Court therefore does not treat failure of Count I as dispositive of Count II, rely on funding sanctions as comprehensive foreclosure, or categorically reject qualitative rights and regulatory elaboration. A useful enforcement vehicle cannot itself supply the missing substantive right.

##### Scope and unresolved matters

The children's alleged comparative treatment injury presents the controversy; the Court does not dismiss for lack of Article III standing. Prospective state officers are not categorically excluded as nonpersons, but the judgment establishes no defendant's liability or entitlement to relief. The municipal plaintiffs' separate standing is unnecessary to the decision.

Section 100.3(b)(2) concerns a covered recipient's criteria or methods of administering services, aid, benefits or facilities that cause the prohibited discrimination or defeat or substantially impair program objectives for protected individuals. It is not a prohibition of every statistical disparity. The Court decides no regulatory validity, recipient coverage, discriminatory purpose, causal funding effect, trial burden framework or statewide allocation requirement. Gebser and Davis govern attribution of intentional discrimination in their own settings and do not create this effects right. Properly presented intentional-discrimination claims remain subject to their own requirements.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Cannon v. University of Chicago preserves the established private-action setting for the statutory protection; it does not automatically supply a private action for the broader effects condition.
- Guardians Association v. Civil Service Commission and Alexander v. Choate preserve the statutory/regulatory distinction; their divided positions and remedial assumptions are not converted into an express majority holding on the private channels resolved here.
- Blessing v. Freestone supplies the particular-right inquiry and separate foreclosure analysis. Its allowance for qualitative entitlements and regulatory elaboration remains.
- Suter v. Artist M. preserves provision-specific rights and distinct direct-action analysis; it supplies no categorical immunity for funding statutes.
- Wright v. Roanoke and Wilder v. Virginia Hospital Association retain enforceable individual statutory entitlements and authorized elaboration, distinguished from creation of the additional effects right here.
- Doe v. District of Columbia supplies the distinction between a binding CAPTA agency command and the personal right asserted under §1983.
- Bruneau v. South Kortright Central School District preserves enforcement of an identified Title IX right without enlarging its substantive duty; administrative funding oversight alone is not automatic foreclosure.
- Gebser and Davis retain their intentional-discrimination attribution holdings; their recipient-responsibility rules do not decide this impact-only theory.
- NCAA v. Smith preserves the requirement of actual federal-assistance receipt separately from economic connection, without resolving coverage here.
- Will and Ex parte Young preserve the distinction between prospective relief against state officers and a categorical nonperson objection; they establish no merits liability.

#### Law After Decision

This Title VI impact regulation supplies neither the directly implied action nor the individual statutory right asserted through §1983. The two routes fail for separate reasons. Section 601 intentional-discrimination enforcement, genuine regulatory elaboration of existing rights, and authorized agency enforcement remain intact. Section 602's administrative conditions do not become private exhaustion requirements.

Agency termination or refusal of assistance remains subject to an express finding after a hearing opportunity, confined to the recipient and program or part found noncompliant; advice of noncompliance and inability to secure voluntary compliance; and a report to the appropriate congressional committees followed by thirty days before termination or refusal becomes effective. Other means authorized by law remain distinct. General rules remain subject to §602's statutory consistency and presidential-approval requirements. This judgment orders no agency enforcement action.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Stevens, dissenting and joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, would permit both prospective channels. Section 601 identifies the individual beneficiaries, §602 expressly authorizes implementation of their protection, and §100.3(b)(2) gives concrete content to nondiscriminatory administration of covered benefits. Cannon supports the established private-action setting; Guardians and Choate support the effects regulation's substantive account, without automatically deciding the remedy. The dissent reads that particular combination as authorized elaboration of individual protection, not a general rule that any valid agency regulation creates a private suit.

Section 1983 independently supplies an enacted enforcement vehicle once that right is identified under Blessing. Wright supports reading statutory protection with its authorized regulatory content. Section 602's general funding machinery offers no comparable personal remedy or demonstrated incompatibility, and Bruneau rejects automatic foreclosure from funding oversight or an existing implied action. Failure to imply a direct action would therefore not alone defeat §1983. The dissent rejects an additional §601-intent element for proof of this effects regulation, while retaining intent for the distinct statutory claim.

The dissent would affirm the reversal of dismissal and allow ordinary adjudication. It finds no discriminatory purpose, violation, funding amount, coverage or causation, and would require a covered recipient, prohibited administrative practice, causal injury and fitted relief. It orders no appropriation, equalization, statistical presumption, automatic discovery, damages or new statewide formula. Stevens regards §1983 as sufficient to leave the direct-action alternative unresolved if necessary, but does not accept rejection of that alternative.

#### Source Notes

[Powell v. Ridge, 189 F.3d 387](https://static.case.law/f3d/189/html/0387-01.html) supplies the complaint, requested prospective relief, two counts, lower judgment and the absence of a separate §601 count. [Title VI §§601–602](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1999-title42/pdf/USCODE-1999-title42-chap21-subchapV.pdf) and [34 C.F.R. §100.3 (1999)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-1999-title34-vol1/pdf/CFR-1999-title34-vol1-sec100-3.pdf) supply the governing instruments. The funding comparisons and educational harm are allegations accepted for the legal pleading question; no statistical model or actual discriminatory purpose is found.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Third Circuit judgment is reversed as to the impact-only theories in Counts I and II, and the case is remanded for a mandate consistent with those holdings. No separate §601 claim is treated as already pleaded. Any genuinely preserved intentional-discrimination theory or ordinary amendment issue keeps its actual procedural footing. No new claim, automatic amendment, liability finding, funding order or retained Supreme Court return condition is created.

## Earlier published decision: McClellan v. Cablevision

### McClellan v. Cablevision of Connecticut, Inc., Second Circuit No. 97-7156

Merits decision, January 7, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit, reviewing 149 F.3d 161 and its reversal of a pleading-stage dismissal. The question is whether section 531(e) authorizes the asserted implied private damages action after a cable operator's exclusion of public-access programming. The complaint also requested injunctions and asserted Connecticut law.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Reversed in part and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinion of the Court

Justice Souter delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The unanimous opinion covers the damages holding, explanation, reservations and confined remand.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The asserted federal damages action

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 531(e) of the Cable Act does not, by itself and in the statutory setting presented, create the asserted implied private damages action against Cablevision. A prohibition protecting public-access programming does not establish congressional intent to authorize that judicial damages remedy; independently authorized forms of relief must be identified and tested under their own sources.

**Authority.** Souter's unanimous Court opinion controls this rule, its damages-only review scope and the confined remand. All nine Justices join the full explanation.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the statutory command and the authority to recover damages present different questions. Cort v. Ash, Touche Ross and Transamerica require an inquiry into Congress's intent to authorize the particular action. Cannon permits contextual implication, so the concrete protection of programmers matters and an express remedy elsewhere is not automatically conclusive. But the differentiated provisions here and the limited judicial background do not support this new damages action. Section 532(d) expressly provides a particular leased-capacity action; section 531(c) addresses enforcement of franchise requirements. Neither is a general public-access damages authorization.

The strongest contrary argument combines the protected speakers, the direct editorial-control prohibition, the possible gap in franchise enforcement and Congress's later amendments. Those amendments followed only a few district-court recognitions, not the settled received-action background on which Curran and the existing securities action rest. The subsequent abandonment of one lower recognition is not attributed to Congress as foreknowledge. A gap in compensation does not itself supply the intended remedy, and the Court does not assume local enforcement is adequate or exclusive.

Suter preserves the separate action-creation inquiry. Franklin's appropriate-relief principle applies after a cognizable action exists; it cannot create its own antecedent. Central Bank and Musick, Peeler concern incidents of the received securities action and do not authorize importing it into the Cable Act. The question before the Court concerns damages. The Court therefore decides neither every prospective remedy nor the legality of Cablevision's actual exclusion.

**Application, remedy and limits.** The Second Circuit's judgment is reversed only insofar as it permits the asserted implied federal damages action, and the case is remanded. The complaint also requested injunctions and asserted Connecticut law. Any surviving relief requires an actual authorized source and the ordinary preservation, jurisdictional and remedial requirements; no equitable action is created by the request itself. Viewer-claim status remains unresolved, no unpleaded section 1983 claim is created, and no damages are awarded.

Section 531(e) remains subject to section 544(d). Its separate permission to refuse public-access programs or portions containing obscenity, indecency or nudity is permission, not an obligation, and does not automatically extend that public-access wording to every educational or governmental use. The constitutionality and application of that permission, channel forum status, operator state action and whether the actual blanket exclusion was lawful remain undecided.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Cort v. Ash; Touche Ross v. Redington; Transamerica Mortgage Advisors v. Lewis:** Applied to the intent necessary for this asserted action and remedy; no universal express-text prerequisite is created.
- **Cannon v. University of Chicago:** Its contextual inquiry is preserved; beneficiary language and contrast with express remedies are considered rather than made automatically decisive.
- **Merrill Lynch v. Curran:** Distinguished because the limited public-access decisions do not establish its settled judicial-remedy background.
- **Suter, March 25, 1992:** Applied to the independent question whether Congress authorized the asserted action, without deciding section 1983 here.
- **Franklin, February 26, 1992:** Its appropriate-relief rule remains operative within a cognizable action and does not supply the missing authority to recover damages here.
- **Central Bank, April 19, 1994, and Musick, Peeler, June 1, 1993:** Their received securities action remains intact; its development is distinguished from creation of this Cable Act damages action.
- **Denver Area, June 28, 1996:** Its limited PEG judgment and fractured rationales neither establish a universal public forum nor create a statutory damages action; the separate 1996 permission retains its own unresolved constitutional questions.

#### Law After Decision

The asserted implied section 531(e) damages route is unavailable. The substantive statutory restrictions remain, while any other relief depends on its actual independent authority. The decision neither validates the exclusion nor establishes a general immunity from suit, and it changes no received securities or other existing private action.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the lower courts. The reversal reaches only the asserted implied federal damages action, which cannot support relief. Any properly preserved remaining relief must satisfy its own authorization and ordinary procedural requirements. Supplemental jurisdiction and the reserved viewer questions remain for lawful consideration without a directed outcome. The Court makes no finding about the lawfulness of the exclusion or an affirmative alternative entitlement.
