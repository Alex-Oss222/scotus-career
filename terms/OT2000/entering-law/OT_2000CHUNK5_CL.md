## Standards and Tests selection: Tribal immunity from direct contract suits

### Tribal immunity from direct contract suits

**Governing question:** Whether commercial character or an off-reservation setting removes tribal sovereign immunity from a contract action.

**Current rule:** A federally recognized tribe retains sovereign immunity from a direct contract suit unless it clearly waives immunity or Congress unequivocally authorizes suit. The rule governs governmental and commercial activities, on and off reservation land.

**Authority by component:**

- Direct-contract immunity and waiver or congressional authorization: Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma v. Manufacturing Technologies, Inc., No. 96-1037. — controlling Holdings, 1998-05-26.

**Present operation effective:** 1998-05-26.

**Exceptions and limits:** Immunity from suit does not decide the underlying contractual obligation or whether substantive state regulation may apply. The rule does not immunize every separate entity or individual merely associated with a tribe.

**Holdings navigation:** Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma v. Manufacturing Technologies, Inc. — Direct tribal contract immunity outside reservation land.

## Standards and Tests selection: Independent determination and delegation of arbitrability

### Independent determination and delegation of arbitrability

**Governing question:** Who decides agreement to arbitrate arbitrability and how appellate courts review that determination.

**Current rule:** Courts independently decide agreement to arbitrate arbitrability unless ordinary applicable contract law establishes clear and unmistakable delegation. Appearing to object to a panel’s jurisdiction does not itself delegate that question, and a company’s submission does not establish an owner’s personal assent. Actual delegation brings the arbitrator’s arbitrability decision within limited award review. Appellate courts independently review district-court legal conclusions and review findings for clear error; arbitration adds no deference to the district court’s independent determination.

**Authority by component:**

- Antecedent assent and delegation: Antecedent assent and delegation, 1995-05-22. Breyer's Opinion of the Court, Part I; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer on the antecedent-assent rule and application; Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer alone on the additional conditional actual-delegation review rule (Stone-Zsela reserves that proposition) directly join this proposition.
- Ordinary appellate review: Ordinary appellate review, 1995-05-22. Breyer's Opinion of the Court, Part II; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly join this proposition.

**Present operation effective:** 1995-05-22; the component authorities identified above.

**Holdings navigation:** Antecedent assent and delegation; Ordinary appellate review.

#### First Options of Chicago, Inc. v. Kaplan

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-560.
**Decided:** May 22, 1995.

##### Antecedent assent and delegation

**Holding:** Courts independently determine agreement to arbitrate arbitrability unless ordinary applicable contract law establishes clear and unmistakable delegation. Presenting an objection while denying panel jurisdiction does not itself establish delegation; company submission does not itself establish an owner's personal assent. Where parties actually delegate arbitrability, limited award review governs that decision.

**Proposition-level authority:** Breyer's Opinion of the Court, Part I; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer on the antecedent-assent rule and application; Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer alone on the additional conditional actual-delegation review rule (Stone-Zsela reserves that proposition) directly join this proposition.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Broad clauses, incorporated rules and independent nonsignatory-binding theories are not resolved. The antecedent-assent rule has nine joins; the conditional limited-review rule for actual delegation has eight, excluding Stone-Zsela. No judgment-only or fractured-decision aggregation supplies either coalition.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- AT&T Technologies, Inc. v. Communications Workers, 475 U.S. 643: applies clear-and-unmistakable delegation.
- Volt Information Sciences, Inc. v. Board of Trustees, 489 U.S. 468: applies arbitration's contractual foundation.
- Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson, January 18, 1995: federal coverage supplies no missing assent.
- Mastrobuono v. Shearson Lehman Hutton, Inc., March 6, 1995: actual integrated contract terms preserve the antecedent-consent inquiry.

##### Ordinary appellate review

**Holding:** Appellate courts independently review district-court legal conclusions and review factual findings for clear error. Arbitration supplies no additional appellate deference to the district judge's independent arbitrability determination.

**Proposition-level authority:** Breyer's Opinion of the Court, Part II; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly join this proposition.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- AT&T Technologies, 475 U.S. 643: preserves decisionmaker/merits distinctions.
- Volt, 489 U.S. 468: actual agreement explains arbitral deference; it insulates no district-court legal conclusion.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the Third Circuit on arbitrability and appellate review. Vacatur of the award against the Kaplans personally remains effective; MKI's separate corporate submission remains distinct. No new FAA merits-vacatur ground or rehearing of agreed arbitration merits is authorized.

## Earlier published decision: Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma

Source: `terms/OT1997/output/OT_1997CHUNK9.md`.

### Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma v. Manufacturing Technologies, Inc., No. 96-1037

Plenary merits decision, May 26, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 12, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Oklahoma Court of Civil Appeals, First Division, after the Oklahoma Supreme Court declined review. The intermediate court affirmed a contract money judgment against the Tribe under an off-reservation commercial-activity exception to immunity. The question is whether federal tribal immunity permits this direct contract suit without a clear waiver or congressional authorization.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded with instructions to dismiss, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens, Thomas and Ginsburg.

The direct contract money claim against the Tribe must be dismissed. The three opposing Justices would remand under a limited off-territory commercial exception, with its factual predicate determined from the actual record; they do not unconditionally sustain a money judgment on an assumed location finding.

#### Opinion Topology

Breyer delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter. Stevens dissents, joined in full by Thomas and Ginsburg. All six members of the Court's opinion adopt the complete contract-immunity rule, its application, qualifications and dismissal mandate.

#### Holdings

**Holding and operative rule:** A federally recognized Indian tribe retains sovereign immunity from a direct suit on its contract, whether the activity is governmental or commercial and whether the contract concerns activity on or off reservation land, unless the tribe clearly waives immunity or Congress unequivocally authorizes the suit. No such waiver or authorization supports this action, so the direct contract claim against the Kiowa Tribe must be dismissed without deciding the debt's merits or resolving the disputed place of execution.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion for the six Justices identified in Opinion Topology controls the entire rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Tribal immunity and the substantive obligations a tribe may owe are different questions. Potawatomi preserves that distinction even when the underlying activity is commercial and the State has a valid substantive interest. Santa Clara Pueblo requires an unequivocal waiver; Puyallup distinguishes relief against the tribal sovereign from claims against members. Those cases do not already decide every off-reservation contract, but their federal immunity framework provides no adequate basis for the geographical and commercial exception adopted below.

The proposed exception has substantial practical appeal. Modern tribal commerce can affect nonmembers far from tribal land, and immunity can leave a creditor without the ordinary means of enforcing a debt. The doctrine's early foundations and present policy costs merit attention. But determining which commercial activities, locations, counterparties and reliance interests warrant an exception requires choices intertwined with Congress's treatment of tribal sovereign status. Congress can revise the rule and specify its boundaries. The Court declines to create this new exception through a direct contract action.

Mescalero and Chickasaw Nation address the substantive operation of state law; they do not establish that a court may enter a money judgment against an immune tribe. Here the note preserves sovereign rights, and neither signing it nor defaulting clearly consents to suit. Whether execution occurred in Carnegie or Oklahoma City therefore does not affect the immunity result. The decision does not extinguish contractual obligations, automatically protect individual agents, decide a tort claim, determine an asset's immunity from execution or establish that some adequate alternative remedy exists. A clear waiver or valid congressional authorization would present a different case.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Citizen Band Potawatomi Indian Tribe, 498 U.S. 505:** Applied for immunity distinct from substantive obligation and its rejection of a commercial-business limitation in the trust-land setting; the present holding extends the direct contract-immunity rule to the off-reservation commercial setting.
- **Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49:** Applied to the requirement of an unequivocal waiver and Congress's authority over the immunity rule.
- **Puyallup Tribe, Inc. v. Department of Game of Washington, 433 U.S. 165:** Applied to the distinction between the tribal sovereign and individual members; jurisdiction over others supplies no automatic authority to bind the Tribe.
- **Mescalero Apache Tribe v. Jones, 411 U.S. 145:** Distinguished as a rule concerning substantive state-law application outside reservation borders, not a direct contract-suit immunity exception.
- **Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Chickasaw Nation, June 14, 1995:** Its territorial and tax-incidence holdings remain intact and create no automatic contract remedy against the Tribe.
- **Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government, February 25, 1998:** Its separation of tribal recognition from Indian-country land status is preserved; loss of a territorial classification does not itself eliminate sovereign status or decide suit immunity.
- **Montana v. Crow Tribe of Indians, May 18, 1998:** Its requirement of an independently grounded restitutionary remedy is preserved; it establishes neither automatic tribal recovery nor an automatic private contract action.

#### Law After Decision

Direct contract immunity now expressly covers the off-reservation commercial transaction presented. It remains subject to clear tribal waiver and unequivocal congressional authorization. Substantive contract obligations and ordinary state-law applicability are separate from the power to adjudicate this direct money claim. Claims against nonimmune parties, collateral security and property enforcement depend on their own actual legal bases; the Court recognizes no particular such remedy here and decides no general tort-immunity question.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens, joined by Thomas and Ginsburg, would decline to extend tribal immunity to genuinely off-reservation commercial conduct unrelated to tribal land or sovereign functions. Potawatomi resolved a trust-land operation, and Puyallup protected the tribal defendant in its own setting; neither requires the broader extension. Mescalero's ordinary-state-law baseline informs, but does not itself decide, the distinct amenability question. The dissent regards the extension as new federal common-law displacement of state adjudicatory authority without an identified federal interest sufficient to justify it. Congress's power to legislate does not compel courts to enlarge the received doctrine meanwhile, and unusually broad immunity for outside commerce burdens parties dealing beyond tribal territory.

The dissent preserves established immunity within its received scope and infers no waiver from commerce or a promise to pay. It would remand for application of its limited rule to the actual transaction and valid existing findings. The note's Carnegie recital and the creditor's Oklahoma City account remain distinct; if existing findings settle the legally material location and commercial predicates, no new factfinding is needed. Only a genuinely unresolved material predicate would require further ordinary proceedings. The dissent does not convert the location dispute into a finding against the Tribe or direct an unconditional money judgment.

#### Source Notes

The reported transaction and procedural history appear in [the official United States Reports, 523 U.S. 751](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-523/pdf/USREPORTS-523-751.pdf). The note recites Carnegie, while the creditor asserts Oklahoma City execution, delivery and payment. Neither account becomes a new factual finding, and the controlling immunity rule makes that dispute immaterial to dismissal. No separate defendant, actual alternative forum or available secured remedy is established here.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The state courts must dismiss the presented direct contract action against the Tribe on immunity grounds. They need not resolve the competing execution-location accounts to implement that direction. Breach, damages, another defendant's liability, particular security or execution measures, and any actually authorized waiver-based or statutory action are not adjudicated by this mandate.
