### MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co., consolidated with United States et al. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co. et al., Nos. 93-356 and 93-521

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Chief Justice Stone-Zsela | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Affirm both consolidated dockets | Structural modification ground, statutory qualifications, deference analysis and remedy; all join fully. |
| Concurrence | Scalia | Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Affirm; all four also join the Court | Additional independently sufficient special-condition objection; four votes, noncontrolling. |
| Dissent | Stevens | Blackmun, Souter | Would reverse and remand | The modification power reasonably permits the rule; both asserted statutory barriers are rejected. |
#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia concurs, joined by Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg, while joining the Court's opinion in full. They would also hold that the general order independently fails §203(b)(2)'s special-circumstances-or-conditions requirement. Even assuming the modification power could reach the change, a substitute filing regime covering every long-distance carrier except AT&T and approximately forty percent of customers has the ordinary reach of sector regulation. Calling the category nondominant does not, in their view, establish the limiting condition for this order. They do not adopt a numerical cutoff or find that each nondominant carrier has market power; breadth is evidence of the order's regulatory character. They preserve general orders addressing genuine qualifying conditions and do not determine whether a smaller or differently conditioned waiver would qualify. The four Justices regard this statutory objection as sufficient by itself. It is not an alternative holding of the Court.

Justice Stevens dissents, joined by Blackmun and Souter. They would reverse the statutory-authority judgment and remand because the express power to modify any requirement, including by a general order for special conditions, reasonably accommodates the difference between dominant and competitive carriers. Nondominance can be special by character rather than by numerical rarity. The agency's actual competition rationale matters: mandatory advance public filing may burden competition without delivering the same protection against market power. The rule preserves voluntary filing, just-and-reasonable rates, nondiscrimination, complaint enforcement and the possibility of restoring filing obligations if conditions change. These features persuade the dissent that the Commission adjusted the statutory system rather than abandoned it. The 120-day ceiling limits an increased notice burden; it does not confine every relaxation to an insignificant detail. Boston & Maine supports contextual delegation, and the permissible constructions sustained in Health Care & Retirement and PUD No. 1 show why operative text and safeguards matter. Lechmere still requires respect for established statutory limits, but it does not resolve the scope of this express modification clause. The dissent rejects both the Court's modification ground and the concurrence's independent special-condition objection. Good cause, actual qualifying conditions and the agency's own reasons remain necessary; the dissent would not authorize elimination of §§201–202, sustain compulsory nonfiling, or adjudicate the separate complaint's monetary remedy.

### U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton / Bryant v. Hill, Nos. 93-1456, 93-1828

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing and scope | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Court opinion: both restrictions | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Affirm both dockets |
| Concurrence: both restrictions | Kennedy | No other Justice | Joins opinion and judgment |
| Dissent: both restrictions | Thomas | O'Connor, Scalia | Would reverse both |

Stevens writes for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Kennedy concurs and joins the Court opinion. Thomas dissents, joined by O'Connor and Scalia. Each writing addresses both House and Senate restrictions; Kennedy's concurrence adds no judgment vote.
#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy emphasizes that national representatives derive their constitutional place from the people within the federal structure; States' authority over their own officers does not extend to altering that national representative relationship. He joins the common Article I holding. Thomas, joined by O'Connor and Scalia, reads Powell as limiting Congress, not the people's retained electoral authority within each State. He considers this ordinary-ballot limitation permissible while write-in eligibility remains. The dissent must defend the substantive service criterion as an exercise of retained authority, not merely invoke a procedural label.

### Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia, No. 94-329

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship and scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Both program-specific holdings and remedy |
| Concurrence | O’Connor | Unjoined | Full bounded Court join; safeguards and compelled-fee challenge reserved |
| Concurrence | Thomas | Unjoined | Full Court join; separate civic-benefit/history explanation |
| Dissent | Souter | Stevens, Ginsburg, Breyer | Would affirm because of direct devotional-production support |
#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O’Connor concurs and joins the Court in full at its program-specific level. Organizational independence and disclaimers, approved vendor payments, a vigorous competing forum and the availability of a properly presented objection by students who oppose compelled fees provide safeguards; the last is a possible challenge, not an existing opt-out or refund award. Neutrality alone is insufficient. Thomas also concurs, joining the Court while explaining his own view that equal participation in broadly available civic benefits should not turn solely on a financial-versus-in-kind classification; that broader historical theory is not adopted by the Court. Souter, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer, dissents. Mandatory fees pay for devotional production, and routing money to the printer does not turn that support into mere facility access. Ball and Nyquist distinguish direct support of religious activity; the beneficiary’s own editorial choice is not an independent third party’s selection under Witters and Mueller. The dissent does not exclude all religious viewpoints or invalidate every neutral private religious benefit.

### Capitol Square Review and Advisory Board v. Pinette, No. 94-780

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing and part | Author and joins | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Part I: reviewability | Stone; Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer join | Opinion of the Court, 9 |
| Parts II–III: protected private access and rejection of Ohio’s categorical defense | Stone; O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer join | Opinion of the Court, 7 |
| Part IV: contextual attribution | Stone; O’Connor, Souter, Breyer join | Four-Justice reasoning; not an additional holding of the Court |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment: private equal access | Scalia; Kennedy and Thomas join | Three-Justice separate ground |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment: less restrictive attribution alternatives | Souter; O’Connor and Breyer join | Three-Justice separate ground |
| Dissent from the merits judgment | Stevens; no joins | Agrees with Part I only |
| Dissent from the merits judgment | Ginsburg; no joins | Agrees with Part I only |

The shared holding in Parts II–III controls at its stated level. Neither the four-Justice contextual explanation nor the three-Justice private-access explanation is declared controlling under Marks. One is not shown to be a logical subset of the other.
#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone’s Part IV, joined by O’Connor, Souter and Breyer.** Context determines whether private expression appears to be governmental religious speech. The forum’s actual history, genuinely private ownership and sponsorship, and effective means of explaining attribution defeat Ohio’s claim that this proposal must be excluded because of its location. An informed reasonable observer knows the setting’s general private uses without being presumed to know every permit rule or newspaper story. For the three joining Justices, adequate dissociation from the State remains material to their application. The generic, negotiable sign offer is part of the proposed display, not a finding that a completed sign worked or that the decree expressly required it. This reasoning does not approve public worship funding, government-sponsored displays, selective religious favoritism, a captive religious exercise, or a purported forum whose circumstances actually attribute the message to the State. It does not impose a constitutional label requirement on all religious speech.

**Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas.** Genuinely private religious speech in a publicly announced traditional or designated forum actually open to all on equal terms is not an establishment merely because an observer mistakes its message for the State’s. Public ownership and proximity alone do not convert ordinary permission into religious sponsorship. Widmar’s protection of private access and Lamb’s Chapel’s prohibition of religious viewpoint exclusion support the result; an endorsement-based veto does not supply their sufficient ground. An adequate disclaimer is not a prerequisite to their votes. Actual governmental selection, adoption, religious favoritism, unequal access or a sham forum would change the premises. Kennedy’s agreement does not make coercion the only possible establishment violation. Thomas does not add a factual holding that this particular application was exclusively political. Neutral identification measures remain possible, subject to their actual terms; no unproduced regulation is adjudicated.

**Souter, joined by O’Connor and Breyer.** Even if an unattended cross near the Statehouse can suggest governmental affiliation, complete exclusion is unnecessary where effective clarification can address the concern. A sufficiently clear and prominent disclaimer, or an appropriately identified private-display area, offers a less restrictive alternative. These are alternatives, not cumulative requirements. The contextual concern survives a formal forum label; an ineffective disclaimer or other indications of actual governmental adoption could change the result. The applicants offered clarification, but the sign was not yet written at the hearing, did not name the Klan, and its later effectiveness is not adjudicated. Souter reads the offered attribution measure as part of the proposal before the Court, without adding a condition absent from the decree or assuming Ohio agreed to final wording. This narrower ground rejects Ohio’s insistence that the location makes exclusion necessary regardless of clarification.

**Stevens, dissenting.** The unattended cross at the seat of government conveys governmental religious affiliation even though a private party owns it and other private expression is permitted. The objection concerns what the State’s setting communicates to ordinary viewers, including nonadherents; it does not depend on a finding that every religious speaker becomes the government. Lamb’s Chapel involved identifiable private use in a materially different setting, and Widmar does not automatically establish a right to erect every unattended structure. Identification does not cure the State’s apparent decision to place a potent religious symbol in this governmental setting. Stevens would reverse and remand consistently with that conclusion. His ground is broader than Ginsburg’s objection to the actual injunction’s terms.

**Ginsburg, dissenting.** An offer to use a disclaimer does not establish that the injunction secures an adequate one. The order required access without an express effective-attribution condition, and the record does not settle whether the sign later used adequately dissociated the unattended cross from Ohio. She therefore would reverse the unconditioned access judgment and remand. Her objection does not adopt Stevens’s view that no adequate sign can cure this setting. She reserves whether a materially different, properly conditioned order would satisfy the Establishment Clause; that reservation is not a promised vote to uphold such an order. No removal of an absent cross or categorical exclusion from future seasons follows from her position.

### Chabad-Lubavitch of Georgia v. Miller, No. 93-1047

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing and part | Author and joins | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Part I: reviewability and scope | Souter; Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer join | Court, 9 |
| Part II: protected private access; categorical defense; bounded judgment and mandate | Souter; Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer join | Court, 8 |
| Part III: contextual distinction between private expression and governmental religion | Souter; Stone, O’Connor, Ginsburg, Breyer join | Court, 5 at the stated general rule; no finding that an unspecified future sign is adequate |
| Part IV: tailoring and additional attribution inquiry in actual relief proceedings | Souter; O’Connor, Ginsburg, Breyer join | Four-Justice reasoning and proposed additional instruction, not a controlling mandate |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone; no joins | Explains contextual application and neutral-rule permission without joining Part IV’s additional requirement |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Scalia; Kennedy and Thomas join | Private equal-access ground, without a disclaimer prerequisite or endorsement inquiry |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Ginsburg; no joins | Explains distinction between this summary-judgment reversal and Pinette’s already entered injunction; joins Parts I–IV as specified |
| Dissent from the merits judgment | Stevens; no joins | Joins Part I only; would restore State summary judgment on Count II |

Direct joins support Parts II and III at their stated levels. No Marks aggregation converts the three-Justice private-access ground and four-Justice remedial inquiry into a different majority rule.
#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Souter’s Part IV, joined by O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer.** The State has not shown that complete exclusion is necessary when effective attribution alternatives remain available. A clear and prominent disclaimer or an adequately identified private-display area could address the concern; these are alternatives, not cumulative conditions. The district court did not separately determine why all such measures would fail. The four would require actual relief proceedings to determine and secure adequate dissociation from government before compelling this interior presentation. They do not find that the existing greeting performs that function, that the proposed disclaimer already exists, or that any unproduced wording will be effective. They also do not reopen the established forum classification, invent a new cause of action, or authorize perpetual access. Their insistence on an express additional inquiry receives four votes and is not a new condition in the Court’s mandate. The Court’s five-Justice contextual rule and other governing constitutional requirements remain applicable to actual future relief.

**Stone, concurring in part and in the judgment; no joins.** Viewpoint neutrality and actual attribution govern. Georgia opened the Rotunda to private unattended displays and may not preserve access for secular perspectives while excluding a comparable religious one. The menorah is privately owned and sponsored. The forum’s actual private uses, neutral admission and identification of the private speaker make this proposed access distinct from governmental worship or adoption of doctrine. Indoor location matters but is not conclusive. Identification or a suitable disclaimer can make the private sponsorship plain, without a ten-factor formula or a new general sign mandate. Stone joins the Court’s contextual rule and bounded judgment, but does not turn his permission for neutral identification measures into Part IV’s additional compulsory adequacy inquiry. The State must provide equal access under lawful comparable terms. It need not maintain the forum, grant preferential placement or duration, disregard physical or safety limits, or supply governmental assistance. He reserves a State-selected, designed, financed, controlled or adopted religious exhibition, a pretextually open forum, and obscured private sponsorship coupled with governmental adoption. The yellow greeting identifies Chabad; it is not described as an already adjudicated express governmental disclaimer.

**Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, concurring in part and in the judgment.** Genuine private expression in a designated forum actually open on equal terms does not become the State’s religion from indoor location or mistaken observer attribution alone. Pinette’s private-access concurrence supplies their sufficient ground, alongside Widmar and Lamb’s Chapel’s protection of comparable private religious speech. The Rotunda’s actual neutral admissions and comparable unattended exhibits distinguish it from an official religious presentation. A disclaimer is not a constitutional prerequisite to their votes. They join the bounded judgment but refuse Part III’s endorsement methodology and Part IV’s added inquiry before access. Ordinary proceedings may implement lawful access and neutral rules; they need not impose a fresh endorsement condition on unchanged genuinely private and equal-access predicates. State selection, privilege, adoption or a sham forum would be different. Kennedy does not make coercion the sole possible establishment violation, and Thomas does not recast the menorah as exclusively secular. No universal right to all government interiors or ruling on an unquoted identification rule follows.

**Ginsburg, concurring in part and in the judgment; no joins.** Pinette involved an already entered injunction commanding access without an express effective-attribution condition. An offer to negotiate a disclaimer did not secure one in that decree. The judgment here merely reverses summary judgment for Georgia. It does not itself command an unconditioned display, so affirming it leaves room to settle actual lawful relief. Georgia’s categorical exclusion cannot be sustained without showing why appropriate dissociation measures could not address the asserted affiliation. This distinction preserves, rather than abandons, her Pinette objection. Before an injunction compels the interior display, she would require enforceable adequate attribution on actual terms. Neither a holiday greeting naming Chabad nor a promise to negotiate proves that requirement met. She does not promise approval of any particular disclaimer and does not adopt Stevens’s conclusion that identification can never cure this presentation. Her decree-specific requirement is part of the four-Justice Part IV, while she joins the Court’s five-Justice contextual rule at its stated conditional level.

**Stevens, dissenting; no joins.** The prominent, isolated religious symbol in the Capitol’s central interior conveys governmental religious affiliation notwithstanding private title and ordinary admission of other expression. This presentation is more closely associated with the government’s own institutional setting than the outdoor forum in Pinette. He accepts Pinette’s limited holding but distinguishes this interior use, where the State also speaks in its own name. The greeting does not disclaim sponsorship, and he concludes that the offered identification alternative does not cure the particular governmental association. That is his contextual legal conclusion, not a finding that an installed sign was tested and failed. Widmar and Lamb’s Chapel protect private expression without requiring every unattended presentation at the seat of government. He would reverse and restore summary judgment for Georgia on Count II, without banning all religious expression, deciding Count I or ordering the Rotunda closed. A materially different presentation would require its own examination.

### Loving v. United States, No. 94-1966

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports affirmance | Entire confined statutory and constitutional rationale, including the four holdings below |
| Concurrence | Stevens | Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Joins the Court's opinion and judgment | Preserves the substantial open question of a capital service-connection requirement |
| Concurrence | Scalia | O'Connor | Joins the Court's opinion and judgment | Distinguishes assigned executive implementation from transfer of legislative power |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Thomas | No other Justice | Supports affirmance on his own ground | Military-specific basis; joins no part of the Court's rationale |

Kennedy's opinion and its seven joiners directly support every holding and explanation below. These are eight-Justice holdings; Thomas's separate ground is not counted toward them, and no combination of separate rationales is necessary.
#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Stevens, joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, joins the authority analysis while preserving the substantial question whether capital military prosecutions constitutionally require a connection to military service. Solorio's noncapital jurisdiction holding does not resolve that extension. The actual military connections suffice here: Fay was an active-duty soldier killed on Fort Hood, and Sharbino was a retired Army sergeant. The concurrence neither dismisses for want of jurisdiction nor adopts a new eligibility condition. Agreement with this disposition does not decide every military capital offense.

Justice Scalia, joined by Justice O'Connor, distinguishes implementation ancillary to the President's genuine executive and Commander-in-Chief functions from an impermissible transfer of legislative power. The extent of an assignment and absence of constraints can cross that boundary. Congress's actual offense, penalty and limiting provisions keep this task within execution of enacted military law. Scalia's distinction in Mistretta between ancillary rulemaking and lawmaking detached from an existing executive or judicial function explains his agreement. He rejects English Parliament–Crown arrangements as a basis for allocating powers under this Constitution; historical jurisdiction inquiries such as Solorio address a different question. That objection withholds no join from the Court's opinion, which contains no English-history ground, and supplies no independent Article II authority to disregard Congress.

Justice Thomas concurs in the judgment on the distinct constitutional responsibilities of Congress and the President concerning the armed forces. He assumes without deciding that additional aggravators are constitutionally necessary, and adopts neither the Court's use of nonmilitary delegation doctrine nor a general capital-delegation framework. If the added protection were unnecessary, that premise would remove the corresponding complaint; he does not hold the factors unnecessary. His military-specific deference operates within Congress's offense and punishment choices, consistently with the constitutional and statutory scrutiny required by Weiss and Ryder, whose requirements remain binding. He reserves any view of congressional authority to alter separation of powers outside this setting and rejects speculative English-history inferences. His separate sufficient ground supports the judgment alone.

### Agostini v. Felton and Chancellor, Board of Education of the City of New York v. Felton, Nos. 96-552 and 96-553

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–IV | Stone-Zsela | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas in full | Vacate both judgments and remand for tailored prospective relief | Part I: modification of Lemon; Part II: onsite secular-service rule and precise overruling; Part III: present legal change under Rule 60(b)(5); Part IV: tailored removal with retained safeguards and lawful obligations |
| Dissent, Part I | Souter | Stevens and Ginsburg | Would affirm both judgments | Affirmative substantive objection to this public instructional support; Breyer does not join |
| Same dissent, Part II | Souter | Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer | Would affirm both judgments | Retention of precedent and absence of sufficient intervening erosion or factual change |
| Dissent | Ginsburg | Stevens, Souter and Breyer | Would affirm both judgments on an independent procedural ground | Rule 60 review cannot supply this means of rehearing the original constitutional ruling |
#### Separate Writings

##### Justice Souter, dissenting

Stevens and Ginsburg join the dissent in full. Breyer joins Part II only.

In Part I, Souter, Stevens and Ginsburg would retain Aguilar's and Ball's applicable substantive rules. Public employees conducting education within a religious school's ordinary instructional setting supply governmental educational support, not independently attributable private speech. Public staffing, neutral eligibility and safeguards do not alone resolve the institutional subsidy and entanglement concerns. Rosenberger's private publication and the private-forum decisions therefore do not decide this case. They reject the Court's modification of Lemon because its treatment of purpose, effects and entanglement within attribution and function understates the constitutional significance of continuing public instructional support. Their objection concerns this governmental program rather than a claim that every private religious act must become government speech. These Justices do not find proved proselytizing and do not require it for their structural objection. Breyer does not join this affirmative direct-aid rationale.

In Part II, all four dissenters would adhere to the direct precedents. The safeguards and anticipated costs did not newly arise. Kiryas Joel preserved Aguilar and Ball, Zobrest supplied no establishment merits holding, and the bounded expression decisions did not erode the public-instruction rule sufficiently to justify its abandonment. They oppose adopting the general methodological change as a basis for abandoning these precedents in this case. They would affirm denial of relief and preserve the injunction.

##### Justice Ginsburg, dissenting; joined by Stevens, Souter and Breyer

The four dissenters would also affirm on an independent procedural ground. Browder limits an appeal from denial of Rule 60 relief; it does not reopen the original judgment for indefinite rehearing. The district court followed still-binding Aguilar, and Rufo requires a qualifying change rather than an appeal whose own desired outcome creates the prerequisite for reopening. This Court may examine the continued force of its precedents in a proper case, but that power does not establish this vehicle's propriety. Their position does not turn an adjudicated injunction into a negotiated consent promise, deny Rule 60 relief after an actual qualifying change, or treat costs alone as sufficient. The Court's adoption of an updated Lemon test does not, in their view, cure that procedural defect. This writing addresses the route to relief independently of the substantive merits disagreement.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, narrow facial-validity part | Breyer | Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg | First Amendment reversal | Meaningful, nonconclusive consideration in this selective arts program; five Justices |
| Same opinion, supplementary spending discussion | Breyer | Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy | Additional explanation for reversal | Program-specific spending priorities; four Justices only; Ginsburg does not join |
| Opinion of the Court, vagueness part | Breyer | Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Rejects distinct vagueness ground | Permissible imprecision in comparative artistic judgments; six Justices |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | Thomas | Supports reversal and rejects vagueness | Broader discretionary-subsidy rationale, without joining the full narrow explanations |
| Dissent, First Amendment part | Souter | Stone-Zsela | Would affirm facial relief | Mandatory viewpoint criterion in a program for independent private art |
| Same writing, vagueness statement | Souter | Author alone | Agrees with rejection of vagueness | Expressly joins the Court's narrow vagueness part; Stone-Zsela does not join this statement |

Souter does not join the lead's First Amendment or spending portions. Ginsburg joins both narrow controlling grounds but not the supplemental spending discussion. Stone-Zsela expresses no conclusion on vagueness.
#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Supplementary spending discussion by Breyer, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy.** These four Justices add that program-specific appropriations may define what government chooses to support without financing every activity. Rust and Maher illustrate distinctions between funding a defined program and prohibiting independent activity. The observation does not replace the narrower statutory construction or authorize every discriminatory arts award. Ginsburg withholds assent from this further discussion; it has no independent controlling force.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** Government may choose the art it subsidizes, including a preference for decency and respect, without thereby preventing a rejected artist from creating the same work independently. This broader account treats the statutory command as real and does not depend on reducing it to a harmless consideration. Rosenberger's broadly available printing benefit and Forbes's opened candidate opportunity differ from discretionary arts patronage. The concurrence likewise rejects importing speech-related vagueness doctrine from coercive regulation into ordinary grant selection as such. It supplies no approval of penalties on independently funded speech, disguised regulation, every possible unconstitutional condition or departures from the rules of a forum actually opened.

**Souter, joined by Stone-Zsela in the First Amendment part, dissenting.** Requiring officials to consider decency and respect for public beliefs directs attention to whether private art conforms to prevailing views. That criterion disfavors offensive, irreverent and challenging expression even if it is not dispositive in every case. Treating mandatory consideration as legally inconsequential would delete Congress's command, while panel diversity does not remove it. Rosenberger's protection of independent private viewpoints and Forbes's continuing viewpoint constraints cannot be evaded merely because artistic support is selective. The program supports private expression, not a governmental message. Genuine neutral judgments concerning merit, subject, audience, educational suitability, finances and administration remain available; invalidating the proviso would not require funding every applicant. These two Justices would retain facial First Amendment relief without deciding settled individual claims, unrelated remedial questions or public financing of religious exercise.

Souter alone adds that artistic comparative judgment necessarily permits imprecision and joins the Court's narrow rejection of facial vagueness. His objection is to the viewpoint criterion, not unavoidable uncertainty in assessing art. Stone-Zsela does not join this statement and leaves vagueness unresolved.

### FDA v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp., No. 98-1152

#### Opinion Topology

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela delivered the opinion of the Court, joined throughout by Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Those five Justices adopt both holdings and all their qualifications. Justice Stevens dissented, joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The dissent would reverse the categorical no-authority judgment and remand, distinguishing statutory coverage from lawful exercise without exempting the agency from otherwise applicable commands.
#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Stevens, dissenting, joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.** They would reverse the categorical authority judgment and remand. The structure-and-function definition reaches nicotine deliberately supplied for pharmacological effects, and the delivery-component definition can reach the apparatus used to supply it. Therapeutic advertising is not the only evidence of intended use. The earlier regulatory architecture at least permits that construction within the delegation recognized by Chevron, Sweet Home and Haggar.

They accept that restricted-device authority cannot erase misbranding, applicable approval or cessation duties. Their sufficient disagreement is that an obligation to remove a covered product, if established under the statute's own conditions, limits the FDA's permissible methods rather than proving that the product is outside the protective statute. They do not make section 360h(e)(1) elective merely because other remedies exist, and do not adopt a universal exception for withdrawal or substitute risks. The precise provision must permit consideration of those consequences.

The tobacco-specific statutes address their actual labeling, advertising, reporting and access subjects; they do not establish the categorical FDCA exclusion necessary to sustain the judgment. MCI's insistence on mandatory limits does not erase different, broad coverage language. Section 422 reserves the authority question and requires the earlier regime, supplying neither new power nor a new exemption. No new mission, classification shortcut or later review flexibility is used.

Their remand would address the actual combination-product method, particular restrictions and unresolved constitutional objections under ordinary procedures. It would not unconditionally reinstate the full rule, guarantee a lawful continuing tobacco market, order a ban or recall, or supply new agency findings. The statutory constraints remain enforceable at their own scope.

### Board of Regents of the University of Wisconsin System v. Southworth, No. 98-1189

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Ginsburg | Reversal of general compelled-subsidy liability; referendum-only remand | Entire opinion, including the educational private-expression framework, neutrality requirements, referendum treatment and all limits |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stevens | Souter, Breyer | Agrees with reversal and referendum remand | Actual program's sufficient justification; does not join the complete necessity framework |
#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Stevens, concurring in the judgment, joined by Justices Souter and Breyer.** They support reversal and referendum remand. The fee reaches an independent distribution program advancing no single ideology and supporting many competing speakers. That actual program, its educational purpose, the attenuated payer-message connection and stipulated neutral administration defeat the claimed ideological exemption. The recipients' political advocacy remains real; the conclusion does not depend on classifying the money as nonspeech, enrollment as waiver, taxation as immunity, or lack of attribution alone.

They would decide that the actual stipulated neutrality and program structure suffice, without declaring a newly necessary viewpoint-neutrality framework for every compulsory university-expression arrangement. They preserve Rosenberger's existing viewpoint-access rule and create no academic-autonomy immunity. They agree that the referendum falls outside the stipulation and requires further proceedings. The same unpresented religious-function and remedial limits apply. They do not join the Court's opinion; agreement with its judgment does not adopt its complete constitutional framework.
