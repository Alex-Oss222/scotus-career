#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in part and dissents in part. He joins the Court's explanation that the nationwide prospective ban requires justification for its actual operation and its rule against extending relief to unrepresented senior officials. The Government's anticorruption interests can sustain narrower restrictions, disclosure and conflict rules, but this record does not connect the broad burden on unrelated expression to actual or apparent misuse of office. His disagreement concerns the class remedy. He would keep the injunction for represented lower-level employees' compensated expression unrelated to their duties and would reserve connected payments, misuse of nonpublic information, genuine conflicts and materially different applications. He does not join the five-Justice refusal to narrow the payment scope of class relief. He joins the disposition retaining Crane's existing individual relief without adding a separate ground; that individual result does not extend his class rule to senior employees generally.

Justice O'Connor concurs in part and dissents in part. She finds the actual burden on unrelated expression unjustified under Pickering, but does not treat the existence of an advance workplace rule, by itself, as automatically increasing the constitutional standard for every employment policy. The breadth and operation of this restriction matter. Congress's preventive and administrative interests are legitimate; the record does not substantiate their application to the unrelated expression at issue. She would protect payments lacking the relevant employment connection while leaving connected applications for their own analysis. That narrower application remedy is distinct from rewriting the definition's series parenthesis as enacted general language. She joins the removal of nonparty senior relief and retains Crane as a named beneficiary at her narrower payment scope. Neither the Government's alternative request nor Crane's grade concedes the First Amendment merits of all his possible payments. She declines the broad class remedy and the broader prospective-rule rationale.

Justice Scalia dissents, joined by Justice Thomas. They would uphold the compensation restriction under the public-employment balance and vacate the constitutional injunction. They recognize that loss of compensation can discourage expression, but consider the retained ability to speak, expense treatment, charitable-payment route, Congress's predictive judgment and administrable preventive protection against corruption and its appearance sufficient. Their position does not treat public employment as surrender of every speech right. They reject demanding proof of completed corrupt transactions before Congress acts and regard the Court's treatment of the law's breadth as giving insufficient weight to the employer's preventive interests. Thomas's earlier interim opinion reserved the merits and is not a prior merits disposition. If invalidity were otherwise established, these Justices would prefer a narrower unrelated-expression remedy and would not remove Crane merely because he is outside the class; those conditional positions do not add votes to any injunction they oppose.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Ginsburg concurs while joining the Court’s opinion in full. She reserves whether an intelligent choice between bail and detention requires notice and comprehension of the consequences for sentence credit. That claim was not presented, so she decides no notice or due-process rule and supplies no new ground for statutory credit.

Stevens dissents without joins. He reads § 3585(b)’s term official detention in light of the restraint actually imposed, rather than treating related Bail Reform Act categories as an exclusive definition for all purposes. Crandon supports considering enacted words, context and purpose before any residual lenity. Court-ordered incarceration in an institution can be official even when a release caption is used, and Wilson allocates calculation without defining qualifying detention. The institutional-control theory does not authorize credit for ordinary curfews or every treatment placement. He would affirm the opportunity for lawful findings, not enter a credit award on allegations.

Stone-Zsela dissents without joins. He would hold that compulsory confinement in an institution under a court order can qualify when equivalent to incarceration, and that the conditional-release label alone cannot defeat the claim. The credit text does not say that only custody committed to the Attorney General counts; Wilson answers who initially computes credit, while Thomas Jefferson University concerns an agency’s own regulation and MCI confines administrative action to enacted authority. Those decisions do not make a program statement conclusive. Stone distinguishes official institutional incarceration from a free-floating hardship comparison. Ordinary home detention, curfew, work-release residence or voluntary rehabilitation is not categorically incarceration. Because the full conditions remain unproved, he would affirm a prompt bounded remand for actual compulsory conditions, the qualifying period and absence of duplicate credit, with the BOP’s computation conforming to § 3585(b) subject to lawful habeas review. He would not automatically award approximately 150 days, order release, revive equality, or replace the BOP as original calculator.


## Sheets v. Salt Lake County

Source: `terms/OT1995/output/OT_1995CHUNK6.md`

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Stevens delivers the Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. All seven directly adopt both controlling propositions, their explanations and the bounded affirmance. Justice Scalia dissents, joined throughout by Justice Thomas.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia, joined by Thomas, dissents. He accepts for purposes of review the supported intentional disclosure, causation and Gary's own intimate injury, but finds no established constitutional guarantee of the asserted kind. Whalen deliberately reserved unwarranted dissemination, and Nixon sustained protected governmental screening rather than recognizing this damages entitlement. Decisional-autonomy precedents do not themselves establish general control over information held by government. Paul's refusal to constitutionalize the arrest-record claim reinforces the need to identify the actual federal guarantee, without making every intimate disclosure identical to reputation harm.

The dissent therefore finds the majority's extension unsupported even though the conduct may violate a promise, state duties or ordinary privacy expectations. Scalia's Albright source objection and Thomas's distinct insistence on a demonstrated substantive guarantee support that conclusion; they do not establish a general rule that state tort remedies cure an otherwise recognized substantive violation. Hodge left this merits question open. The dissent does not infer prior publication from archive availability, deny the accepted intimate injury, make Kathy's authorship dispositive or require tangible economic loss.

The two would reverse the reviewed constitutional ruling, vacate the federal judgment dependent on this right and remand for disposition consistent with rejection of the §1983 theory. Consequential prevailing-party fee treatment would follow under governing law. They direct no new factual trial, state-tort adjudication, refund amount or independent fee rule, and make no new ruling on personal immunity or the reasonableness of the awards.


## INS v. Yueh-Shaio Yang

Source: `terms/OT1996/output/OT_1996CHUNK1.md`

#### Opinions and Joins

| Writing or position | Author or Justice | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Souter | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reaches the merits | Eight-Justice review rationale |
| Opinion of the Court, Parts II–III | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reversal and remand | Unanimous permissible-factor and policy rules |
| Concurrence in the threshold resolution | Stone-Zsela | — | Agrees that the merits may be reached | Does not join Part I; publishes no separate written opinion |

#### Holdings

##### I. Review under the corrected 1996 transition provisions

**Rule:** Review of this existing deportation proceeding remains available under former INA §106 and 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) on November 13, 1996. Corrected IIRIRA §306(c)(1) gives new §242(g) reach across past, pending and future proceedings when that provision commences; it does not advance commencement of the new jurisdictional rule from April 1, 1997, and the other review restrictions establish no present bar on this record.

**Authority:** Part I supplies the eight-Justice controlling rationale identified above. Stone-Zsela concurs only in the threshold resolution and does not join its reasons.

The Court reads the enacted timing instructions together. Section 309(a) sets April 1, 1997 as the general commencement date, expressly excepting §309 and §§303(b)(2), 306(c), 308(d)(2)(D) and 308(d)(5). Corrected §306(c)(1) applies the amendments as provided in §309, then separately extends §242(g) to old as well as new proceedings. The immediate-operation argument relies on those exceptions and the unrestricted reference to pending claims. But the exceptional clause specifies covered proceedings without supplying a replacement commencement date. Its present operation as an applicability instruction has independent work: after commencement, §242(g) reaches proceedings otherwise protected by grandfathering. This reading does not presume that jurisdiction can never change during litigation.

Corrected §309(c)(1), subject to its succeeding provisions, preserves the earlier proceedings and judicial review. No paragraph (2) or (3) agency election is established. Paragraph (4) requires a final exclusion or deportation order entered more than thirty days after September 30, 1996; it lists §§212(c), 212(h), 212(i), 244 and 245. The 1995 posture supplies no new triggering order, and that list omits former §241(a)(1)(H). Permanent §242(a)(2)(B)(i) instead lists §§212(h), 212(i), 240A, 240B and 245; clause (ii) separately addresses other statutorily discretionary authority, except §208(a) grants. Neither permanent clause governs this event.

Section 306(c)(2) protects existing §106 judgments and orders from invalidation or compelled reconsideration by paragraph (1); it does not independently establish appellate jurisdiction. Section 306(d)'s AEDPA-related criminal amendment supplies no bar without a covered conviction, which this record does not establish. The Court leaves open §242(g)'s scope after commencement, including its three subjects—commencing proceedings, adjudicating cases and executing removal orders—and its exception for review provided in §242. *Landgraf* and *Rivers* supply attention to Congress's actual temporal directions, not a new unanimous adoption of every earlier temporal rationale.

##### II. Eligibility does not exclude the continuing fraud pattern from discretion

**Rule:** Former INA §241(a)(1)(H), 8 U.S.C. §1251(a)(1)(H), permits the Attorney General to consider a continuing pattern of immigration fraud when choosing among eligible waiver applicants, even though particular acts are connected with entry. Eligibility does not erase that conduct or compel a waiver; the Court neither permits automatic denial to every eligible applicant because of the qualifying fraud nor decides whether the minimum qualifying fraud alone could sustain a particular individualized denial.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion controls unanimously.

The statute makes eligibility and favorable discretion distinct. It reaches covered entry fraud or misrepresentation, whether willful or innocent. An applicant must be the spouse, parent, son or daughter of a citizen or lawful permanent resident, have possessed an immigrant visa or equivalent document, and otherwise have been admissible at entry. The exceptions for labor-certification and immigrant-documentation grounds under §§1182(a)(5)(A) and (7)(A) apply only when those grounds directly resulted from the fraud. The separate exclusion for assistance in Nazi persecution or participation in genocide remains. An actually granted waiver also reaches entry-inadmissibility grounds directly resulting from the fraud. These provisions define eligibility and the consequence of relief; they do not remove conduct from the discretionary decision.

*Jay v. Boyd* supplies the distinction between statutory eligibility and discretionary selection. *INS v. Errico* construed a mandatory predecessor; its family-preservation concern does not convert the later discretionary provision into an entitlement. *National Center for Immigrants' Rights* supports discretion exercised within the statute while preserving required individual consideration.

The Board accepted eligibility and expressly disregarded the initial entry fraud. It considered the wife's false-identity scheme and sham marital arrangements, the later naturalization fraud, further marital arrangements and lack of candor, together with favorable residence, business, property, family and health considerations and Taiwan connections. Those are the Board's findings and assessments. The Ninth Circuit allowed dishonest testimony to count but wrongly excluded collaboration and naturalization conduct because of their connection with entry. The argument that waiver discretion must remain meaningful therefore does not justify its categorical rule. A continuing pattern distinguishes applicants without making every eligible applicant lose. The Court does not reweigh the balance.

##### III. Permissible factors remain subject to reasoned and consistent administration

**Rule:** Statutory permission to consider a factor does not excuse arbitrary decisionmaking, pretext, disregard of a binding rule, or an unexplained departure from a genuinely settled agency policy. Here the Board rationally distinguished continuing immigration fraud from the initial entry fraud it disregarded; the record supplies no policy reversal requiring a new administrative balance.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's opinion controls unanimously.

The Court separates statutory authority from the lawfulness of its exercise. *Smiley*, applying the administrative-change principles preserved from *Good Samaritan*, requires examination of the agency's actual reasons, prior guidance and legitimate reliance. A court cannot supply a litigation explanation for an unexplained change. An agency may revise a policy openly with adequate explanation, subject to binding law; the existence of discretion alone supplies no answer.

The Board retained its initial-fraud distinction and explained the significance of the continuing pattern. Its actual reasons defeat the claim that this decision irrationally abandoned that distinction. The Court neither finds a different binding policy nor commands a new explanation for an unproved reversal. The mandate removes the Ninth Circuit's compelled reweighing based on categorical exclusion. Any genuinely remaining preserved objection is for ordinary proceedings within the record and mandate; the Court directs neither a waiver nor its denial, reopening, a new hearing, or a fresh Board balance. This decision concerns the statutory immigration function presented, not classifications outside it.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia concurs, joined by Thomas. Both fully join the Court's assumed-right application but would also reject the proposed extension for lack of a demonstrated constitutional source. Whalen reserved the issue, and decisional-autonomy decisions do not establish general control over identified data held by public benefits administrators. Sheets now binds at its public-dissemination scope; extending it requires additional justification. They accept the intimacy and supported intentional conduct, and rely neither on absence of economic loss nor on state remedies as a cure for a federal violation.

Stevens dissents, joined by Souter and Ginsburg. Whalen distinguished rational safeguarded collection from unresolved abuses, and Sheets protects intimate entrustment and requires favorable treatment of supported trial evidence. They would extend that protection to deliberate identification or internal use of a serious medical condition without an adequate governmental connection. The jury-favorable evidence includes highlighted HIV/AIDS drugs, the question seeking HIV identities, the conceded lack of need for names, missing notice and safeguards, and Doe's limited confidential entrustment. A general cost-control interest cannot conclusively answer that distinct inquiry. Their rule requires adequate connection rather than indispensable necessity of every datum, and does not adopt Westinghouse's seven factors or turn emotional distress alone into a violation. They would vacate and remand for application of the bounded rule and consideration of properly remaining trial and damages questions. The record does not establish that every condition of the new rule was necessarily found, so they would not automatically restore $125,000.

Stone-Zsela dissents. Identified prescription records, particularly HIV information, fall within a limited protection that he would extend beyond Sheets's decided public-disclosure setting. Personally identifying access and internal use must be genuinely and proportionately connected to the actual public function, considering sensitivity, the need for identifiers instead of aggregate or coded information, recipients and existing safeguards. Legitimate utilization monitoring did not establish a right to continued identified review as a matter of law: the jury could find unnecessary names, deliberate research after sensitivity became apparent, and no safeguard against the excess. He would reverse the appellate Rule 50 direction, leave the District Court's denial of judgment as a matter of law in place, and remand unresolved preserved Rule 59 and damages matters. His test creates no automatic violation from lack of consent or universal least-intrusive-method duty. It preserves aggregate or coded audits, identified review tied to concrete fraud or eligibility concerns, legally required disclosure and confidential retention alone. He decides neither municipal liability, personal immunity, damages nor private-employer duties.


## Kansas v. Hendricks

Source: `terms/OT1996/output/OT_1996CHUNK11.md`

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Signed lead opinion, Parts II–III: opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | Affirms on the as-applied Ex Post Facto ground | Civil-intent and clearest-proof method, cumulative punitive application, retrospective punishment, remedy and limits |
| Same lead opinion, Part I: non-dispositive due-process discussion | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Does not independently support affirmance or a separate reversal | Bounded rejection of a categorical diagnostic-vocabulary objection; Ginsburg reserves the issue |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would reverse and reject both punishment-dependent challenges | Civil classification on the original record and resulting rejection of Ex Post Facto and Double Jeopardy; agrees with Part I of the lead opinion |

Parts II–III command five direct joins. Part I's due-process discussion is not a ground for the judgment or a separate judgment component. Ginsburg joins Parts II–III and reserves the due-process question.

#### Separate Writings

**Part I's non-dispositive due-process discussion.** Breyer, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, rejects a categorical constitutional objection resting on the statutory words mental abnormality. The discussion is confined to the serious recognized condition, evidence of impaired control and connection to dangerous conduct shown here. A past offense and a prediction alone do not supply that substantive limitation; neither a statutory verdict nor a high proof burden substitutes for it. Foucha concerns custody after its conceded illness predicate ends and leaves narrower preventive regimes open; Sahhar requires a new lawful civil basis rather than making every legislative diagnosis sufficient. This agreement does not dispense with the constitutional safeguards needed for a lawful civil commitment. The discussion does not decide treatment-related due process or every application of personality disorder. It supports no separate reversal of the Kansas judgment and is not the ground of the Court's affirmance. Ginsburg does not join it and reserves the question.

**O'Connor's dissent, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** The dissent agrees with Part I's bounded rejection of a diagnostic-vocabulary requirement but would reverse the Kansas judgment and reject the punishment-dependent challenges. Applying the demanding civil-intent and clearest-proof inquiry, it finds the supported original record insufficient to turn the commitment into punishment. The current condition and danger requirements, social-services custody, separate setting, care duty, procurement efforts, interim professional resources and actual review and release routes support an independent preventive purpose. The lack of a completed specialist program is serious, but does not establish punitive purpose or effect when the contrary original evidence is considered. Neither difficult treatment nor an indefinite duration alone defeats otherwise lawful civil custody.

The dissent does not find that treatment was adequate, that contracting failed for a benign reason, or that later treatment-hour representations cure the original deficiencies. It preserves proper challenges to unconstitutional conditions and requires genuine continuing predicates; a civil description would not protect a proved punitive operation. The dissent relies on the actual statutory sequence. Section 59-29a08 requires yearly examination, a report and judicial review. Probable cause that the condition has changed so the person is safe at large and will not commit sexual violence requires a further hearing; there the State must prove beyond reasonable doubt that the abnormality or disorder remains such that the person is unsafe at large and likely to engage in sexual violence if released. A full release trial does not occur automatically each year. Under section 59-29a10, the Secretary must authorize a petition upon determining that the condition has changed so the person is not likely to commit predatory sexual violence if released; a hearing and the State's reasonable-doubt burden follow. Section 59-29a11 permits petitions without permission, but a first or later unauthorized petition may be denied without hearing as frivolous. After an earlier unauthorized petition was found frivolous or insufficient condition change was found, a later petition must be denied unless its facts support sufficient change to warrant a hearing. These commands do not establish compliant administration.

On its civil classification, both Ex Post Facto and Double Jeopardy fail at their necessary punishment prerequisite. The dissent makes no unnecessary ruling on attachment, offense identity or every retrospective regulatory burden.


## Printz v. United States

Source: `terms/OT1996/output/OT_1996CHUNK13.md`

#### Opinion Topology

| Additional opinion concurring in Part I and dissenting from the judgment | Breyer | Author alone | Would affirm; also joins Stevens | Functional account of divided administration, supplementing the domestic constitutional ground |

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Souter, Part II of the opinion announcing the judgment.** His reason for remand is that appropriate executive assistance cannot, in his view, mean compulsory administrative support without an obligation to pay fair value. Federalist Nos. 27 and 44 describe an auxiliary executive role and the force of the constitutional oath; Nos. 36 and 45 reinforce the execution examples. They support properly directed assistance under an enacted command within the officer's branch function, not an automatic duty created by an oath alone. New York continues to protect legislative choice. Early administrative examples alone would not establish his conclusion.

The officers' assertions that their budgets make no provision for this work and that they face unauthorized-expenditure liability therefore require development and consideration. He regards that issue as a condition on sustaining the existing compulsory arrangement, not as a reason to invent a damages action. He finds no compensation amount, taking, liability, pleaded money cause or payment defendant established and orders no appropriation. The Court leaves the fiscal question open, permitting his stronger view to be considered without adopting it. No other Justice joins that affirmative fair-value rationale.

**O'Connor, concurring in the judgment and dissenting from Part I; Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas join the structural and conditional-judgment grounds.** New York's accountability principle cannot be avoided by addressing state executive officers instead of legislatures. The Brady inquiry and its implicit required acceptance of forms allocate federal implementation to unwilling state offices. Reasonable effort concerns the amount of compelled work, not the State's choice whether to undertake it. A valid commercial object and an assumed promise of reimbursement do not cure that structural defect. Garcia and Baker regulate state activity; Testa and FERC retain distinct adjudicative or cooperative settings; Voting Rights Coalition rests on constitutional election sources absent here.

They would reverse as to mandatory checking and implicit acceptance. They would not declare the destruction and requested-reasons clauses unconstitutional, adjudicate genuinely voluntary or separately triggered applications, or give officers a general license to retain or misuse information. General nonparticipation is not proof that a particular form or request never existed. They would reserve absent dealers' transmission and waiting questions, rather than declare the entire remainder severed and valid.

Part I nevertheless supplies the law governing these cases. Under that rule the actual fiscal objections remain unresolved, and leaving unconditional appellate approval intact would foreclose a real part of the controversy. They therefore accept this confined vacatur and remand while maintaining their preferred structural reversal. They join no compensation entitlement, fiscal cure, source-and-function rationale, state-law veto or search for hypothetical new claims. Their agreement with the mandate does not erase their substantive disagreement.

O'Connor adds, without the others' joins to this additional qualification, that voluntary participation and federally funded contractual cooperation remain available, and that purely ministerial reporting requirements are not resolved by the compelled investigation at issue. That reservation creates no exemption for every task described as ministerial.

**Stevens, concurring in Part I and dissenting from the judgment; Ginsburg and Breyer join.** They would affirm the confined interim duties and their conditional safeguards. Congress directly governs commercial handgun transfers, retains the substantive policy and coercive consequences, and requires temporary, resource-sensitive information assistance. The scheme requires no state legislation, assumption of private property and liability, independent enforcement regime, arrest or prosecution. New York's contrary setting remains binding; supremacy and oath obligations, Garcia, Baker and the cooperation recognized in FERC and Testa support appropriate assistance within an established federal power. Election duties retain their own sources.

The budget and state-law assertions deserve an answer, but do not establish a constitutional payment condition or justify returning this constitutional judgment. The duty's actual functions and the responsibilities left to the State supply their answer; they do not presume free implementation or nonexistent exposure. Reasonable effort can account for resources without becoming optional whenever a budget lacks a line item. Their affirmance decides no state-law liability, taking or every possible fiscal claim. It requires no severance and provides no general permission for permanent state conscription.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in Part I and dissenting from the judgment.** He would affirm both judgments and deny the requested injunction against the confined duties. Congress may require temporary, narrowly bounded assistance using lawfully available government records as an incident of otherwise valid private-transaction regulation, with federal substantive rules and federal responsibility for coercive enforcement. The mandatory eligibility assessment is real; calling supporting tasks ministerial does not eliminate it or its cost.

He reads reasonable effort in the whole interim scheme, its available-record tasks and the absence of a command to stop transfers or arrest purchasers. Including does not itself mean only. Ordinary checking and interpretation remain required; the construction adds no commerce element or administrative power and does not excuse all inquiry. He would authorize no compulsory independent field investigation, permanent state enforcement organization or transfer of general federal regulatory responsibility. The conditional explanations, destruction and information protections retain their separate statutory predicates; adverse dealer notification remains discretionary.

His position narrows his earlier categorical objection to compelled executive administration. The reason for that refinement is the difference between incidental records work and independent regulatory or coercive responsibility, not convenience or a claim that state work is costless. New York's compulsory-legislation prohibition, Lopez's source and no-rewriting limits, and the distinct election, state-court, preemption and voluntary-cooperation rules remain. His position creates no permanent duty, retroactive officer liability or reopening of a completed judgment. The asserted expenditures do not change his conclusion on these limited duties, and no severance or further fiscal proceeding is required. He leaves materially different commands and unpresented applications for another case.

**Breyer, additional opinion concurring in Part I and dissenting from the judgment.** Dividing execution between federal and state officials can preserve state autonomy and avoid expansion of a centralized federal bureaucracy. Federalism alone does not establish that separate federal implementation always protects liberty better. Comparative federal arrangements can illustrate that possibility, but are neither American constitutional authority nor proof that a foreign system can be transplanted here. His sufficient domestic grounds remain valid commerce power, necessary implementation, reasonable effort and the State's retained responsibilities. This supplements his full join in Stevens's opinion; it creates no general cost-benefit override, cheap-command exception or compensation cure.
