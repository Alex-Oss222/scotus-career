## Standards and Tests selection: Federal compensation taxation and occupational-license labels

### Federal compensation taxation and occupational-license labels

**Governing question:** Whether an occupational levy on federal services falls within 4 U.S.C. §111.

**Current rule:** An exaction operating as a tax on compensation for federal personal services falls within §111 despite an occupational-license label. Actual operation and the demand asserted control; a compensation measure alone does not validate a local permission requirement for exercise of federal office. Section 111 permits neither discrimination because of federal source nor genuine local control of federal functions.

**Authority by component:**

- Jefferson County v. Acker — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, “The levy operates as compensation taxation within §111”, 1999-06-21; Ginsburg Part III with Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas.

**Present operation effective:** Acker, June 21, 1999.

**Exceptions and limits:** The Article III Compensation Clause remains a separate question. The particular exemption comparison supplies no general approval of every occupational distinction or identical-treatment requirement across differently licensed occupations.

#### Jefferson County v. Acker

**Docket or dockets:** No. 98-10; October Term 1998.
**Decided:** 1999-06-21.

##### The levy operates as compensation taxation within §111

**Holding:** The levy asserted in these collection actions operates as a tax on compensation for federal personal services within 4 U.S.C. §111, notwithstanding its occupational-license label. Section 111's consent does not authorize discrimination because of the compensation's federal source or a local requirement of permission to exercise federal office.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's Part III is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; seven directly adopt this compensation-tax rule on the jurisdictional predicate stated above.

##### The presented source-discrimination defense fails

**Holding:** On this ordinance and record, taxing the respondent judges' compensation while exempting occupations subject to other specified state licensing charges does not discriminate because their compensation comes from a federal source. The intergovernmental-immunity judgment must be reversed, with remand consistent with that determination and the reservation of the separate Compensation Clause question.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, supplies seven direct votes for the source-discrimination determination and limited remand.

### Jefferson County v. Acker, No. 98-10

Merits decision, June 21, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 29, 1999; decided June 21, 1999. Certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit's en banc judgment, 137 F.3d 1314, sustaining the federal forum and holding the county occupational levy invalid as applied to the respondent federal judges. The questions concern federal-officer removal, the Tax Injunction Act, the operation of the levy under the federal salary-tax consent, and the renewed source-discrimination defense.

Jefferson County brought state-court collection actions against federal district judges William Acker and U. W. Clemon, who removed under 28 U.S.C. §1442(a)(3). Ordinance 1120, effective January 1, 1988, imposes one-half of one percent of compensation attributable to work within the County, with apportionment for work elsewhere. It includes federal, state and local officeholders, exempts persons paying specified state privilege, license or occupational taxes, and declares working without payment unlawful. No actual county restriction preventing a federal judge from sitting is established.

The district court rejected source discrimination and also accepted a distinct Compensation Clause ground. The earlier appellate account reports no challenge to the discrimination rejection; respondents renewed that defense here as an alternative ground for the same favorable judgment. The en banc merits disposition did not reach the Compensation Clause, which remains outside the questions decided here. The County first contests the federal forum and alternatively seeks rejection of the immunity judgment. The United States supports a different jurisdictional position: no Tax Injunction Act bar, but an insufficient official-act nexus for removal.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Federal-officer removal is proper for these collection actions

**Holding and operative rule:** A federal judge may remove a county collection action under the event-applicable §1442(a)(3) when the action has the required connection to official duties and the judge asserts a colorable federal defense. The work-linked levy and official-duty immunity defense establish those requirements here; the defense's eventual failure does not defeat removal.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Breyer, has six direct votes for this rule, explanation and scope.

**Controlling explanation:** Mesa requires a federal defense in addition to federal office, and Willingham preserves a federal forum for a colorable official-duty defense without requiring the officer first to prevail on it. The County's demand arises from the compensation attributable to these judges' performance of federal judicial duties, and the ordinance makes engaging in the work the asserted basis of liability. Their claim that this exaction impermissibly burdens those duties supplies the necessary connection and a nonfrivolous federal defense. The United States' contrary nexus argument correctly separates personal tax liability from an official act, but gives insufficient weight to the particular work-linked ordinance and defense presented here. Federal employment and a compensation measure alone would not authorize removal of every personal obligation. This holding neither dispenses with the office nexus nor imports a later expansion of the removal statute. Characterizing the defense as colorable decides the forum, not whether federal law ultimately exempts the judges from this levy.

##### The Tax Injunction Act does not bar the defensive adjudication

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1341 does not prevent a federal court with removal jurisdiction from adjudicating the federal defenses in a taxing authority's own collection suit. These collection actions seek payment, rather than anticipatory federal restraint of assessment, levy or collection by taxpayers.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's Part II has all nine Justices' joins, with Scalia, Souter and Thomas accepting the Court's removal ruling as the predicate for this independent question.

**Controlling explanation:** The Act limits federal injunctions, suspensions and restraints of state-tax administration; a tax collector's suit to obtain payment is a different proceeding. Removal changes its tribunal without transforming the defendants' defenses into an affirmative taxpayer action. Rejecting the County's asserted liability would resolve its own claim, not grant a separately requested anticipatory injunction. Farm Credit distinguished substantive tax immunity from access to a federal forum and involved private associations seeking affirmative injunctive and declaratory relief with an adequate state remedy conceded. Its entity-specific rule does not decide these removed judicial-officer collection actions. The Court therefore need not find a universal federal-officer exemption from §1341, treat the United States' amicus appearance as party joinder, or supply a new finding about the adequacy of Alabama remedies. Taxpayer-initiated federal restraint remains subject to its own statutory limits.

##### The levy operates as compensation taxation within §111

**Holding and operative rule:** The levy asserted in these collection actions operates as a tax on compensation for federal personal services within 4 U.S.C. §111, notwithstanding its occupational-license label. Section 111's consent does not authorize discrimination because of the compensation's federal source or a local requirement of permission to exercise federal office.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's Part III is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; seven directly adopt this compensation-tax rule on the jurisdictional predicate stated above.

**Controlling explanation:** Graves and §111 require attention to what the exaction does, rather than a constitutional exemption created by its state-law title. The County seeks a percentage of compensation attributable to services performed within its boundaries, with apportionment for work elsewhere. It has not established or exercised a local veto over the judges' performance of their duties. The unlawful-work language and exemptions for occupations paying other license charges are relevant to the ordinance's operation, but together do not convert the revenue demand presented here into an exercise of licensing control over federal adjudication. A compensation measure alone would not save every direct federal-function license; the actual demand and operation are decisive. Blaze preserves the distinction between legal incidence and indirect economic effect, while reserving direct federal burdens and actual instrumentalities; federal judges are not equated with independent contractors. The Court decides no hypothetical restriction preventing a judge from sitting and need not decide the County's alternative Buck Act theory. The separate Article III Compensation Clause question is not resolved by this intergovernmental-immunity holding.

##### The renewed discrimination defense may be considered on this record

**Holding and operative rule:** Respondents may defend the existing favorable judgment on the renewed, record-supported source-discrimination ground without seeking greater relief, and the Court elects to consider that alternative here. Its earlier rejection and reported intermediate nonpursuit do not establish an automatic cross-appeal defect, jurisdictional bar or intentional waiver.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's Part IV has the same seven Justices' direct joins for this confined decision to reach the defense. The permission to consider an alternative is distinct from an obligation to consider every omitted ground.

**Controlling explanation:** X-Citement Video applies American Railway Express's rule allowing a prevailing party to support the same judgment on a ground in the record, including one rejected below, without enlarging its rights or reducing its opponent's rights under that judgment. Here the district court adjudicated discrimination and respondents expressly renewed it in their merits brief. They seek the same exemption judgment, not additional relief. The intermediate nonpursuit remains relevant to review discretion, but the established tax and comparison record permits decision without a new factual theory or findings. Zafiro's prudential treatment of an individual petitioner's unpursued claim does not erase this favorable-judgment posture. AT&T declined to construct an unadvanced independent theory; this one is actually presented. Lebron preserves bounded discretion over included legal arguments, not a duty to revive every abandoned ground. Dandridge's remand option and West's distinct alternative-ground remand likewise create no mandatory postponement of this established and renewed legal defense. The Court chooses present consideration without finding an intentional waiver or assuming that the lower rejection establishes the defense's substantive failure.

##### The presented source-discrimination defense fails

**Holding and operative rule:** On this ordinance and record, taxing the respondent judges' compensation while exempting occupations subject to other specified state licensing charges does not discriminate because their compensation comes from a federal source. The intergovernmental-immunity judgment must be reversed, with remand consistent with that determination and the reservation of the separate Compensation Clause question.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, supplies seven direct votes for the source-discrimination determination and limited remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Davis forbids unfavorable treatment attributable to the federal source of compensation and requires attention to the relevant comparison. This levy includes federal, state and local officeholders; the record establishes that state judges pay it. The exemption for occupations paying other state or county licensing charges does not, on this record, distinguish state compensation from federal compensation. Federal judges' inability personally to fall within that alternative-license category does not by itself establish the forbidden source classification. The Court independently rejects the presented comparison rather than presuming the district court correct or treating all possible exemptions as necessarily valid. No unadmitted workforce percentages, individual licensing facts or withholding practices supply the conclusion. An exemption favoring comparable state officials, an actual federal-source distinction or a genuine local restraint of judicial performance would present a different question. The separate Compensation Clause ground was accepted below but not reached by the en banc court and is not decided here. Remand leaves its proper treatment below without reopening discrimination, quantifying a tax obligation or directing collection regardless of that remaining ground.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Scalia, joined by Souter and Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** The collection actions seek payment of the judges' personal tax obligations, and the sued-on nonpayment is not an official judicial act merely because the tax is measured by judicial pay. A colorable immunity defense supplies one removal requirement but cannot substitute for the statutory office nexus. They would set aside the federal adjudication and return the actions to state court, where the defenses could be heard. Their objection turns on the event-applicable removal text and the actual relief sought, not on denial that the tax defense is colorable. Once the Court establishes removal, however, they accept that resolution as the predicate for joining Parts II–IV: §1341 does not bar this defensive collector action, the levy operates as compensation taxation, the renewed discrimination defense fails, and the intergovernmental-immunity judgment should be reversed with the Compensation Clause reserved. Those conditional joins decide the independent questions without disguising their disagreement about the proper forum.

**Breyer, joined by O'Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They agree that removal is proper and §1341 poses no bar, but would affirm because the ordinance's cumulative operation imposes a direct charge on performing federal judicial functions. Liability is tied to engaging in work and entitlement to compensation; work without payment is declared unlawful; apportionment follows where the work is done; and occupations subject to other licensing charges are exempt. The argument does not rest on the license label alone or require proof that a county officer has already stopped a judge from sitting. Section 111 consents to compensation taxation, not to a local charge on the performance of federal functions merely measured by pay. The Buck Act addresses federal-area taxation and supplies no independent consent to that direct burden. Their reading of the operative ordinance, consistent with the direct-burden reservations in Farm Credit and Blaze, would sustain the existing immunity judgment. Because they find §111 consent inapplicable, they need not separately determine the source-discrimination defense or order an alternative Compensation Clause remand. They prescribe no universal checklist for every occupational levy.

### United States v. Cleveland Indians Baseball Co. — 2001-04-17

Authority: [controlling holdings](../records/United_States_v_Cleveland_Indians_Baseball_merits_2001-04-17.md#holdings); [precedent treatment](../records/United_States_v_Cleveland_Indians_Baseball_merits_2001-04-17.md#precedent-treatment).

**Common authority:** Justice Thomas's opinion is joined throughout by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Seven Justices directly adopt both holdings and the common regulatory explanation. Stone-Zsela and Scalia agree with the judgment and resulting payment-year applications on the distinct grounds described below; neither joins the Court's complete rationale.

#### 1. The established interpretation lawfully places these back wages in the payment-year tax account

**Holding and operative rule:** Treasury's fair and considered interpretation of the applicable rate and separate annual-base regulations requires these taxable salary awards to enter the employer's payment-year FICA and FUTA calculations. The interpretation fits the statutes and regulations and receives controlling weight under Auer; wage status alone does not settle timing, and the Court does not hold that the statutes unambiguously compel this treatment independently of the lawful administrative construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the actual, established interpretation governs this unresolved allocation question. The regulations distinguish remuneration paid or received in a calendar year from services performed in that year. The rate provisions select the payment-year percentage, while separate annual-base provisions identify the year's wage account. They do not expressly address backpay. Revenue Rulings 78-336 and 89-35 evidence a preexisting construction placing back wages in the payment year, rather than a position newly invented for this litigation.

Auer gives a responsible agency's fair and considered interpretation of its own regulation controlling weight unless plainly erroneous or inconsistent with that rule. Thomas Jefferson University requires the agency's actual ground, not a judicial substitute. The identified construction fits each payment and annual-base provision and remains within the statutes. Christensen prevents interpretation from amending clear regulatory text and distinguishes an advisory statutory view from an own-regulation construction; publication of a revenue ruling alone does not confer independent controlling statutory force.

Nierotko gives the Company's contrary argument real force. It classified backpay as wages and allocated it to earlier benefit periods despite paid-quarter language. But restoring a worker's service-based benefit account does not determine employer taxes under the later annual rate and base provisions. Common wage status does not require every statutory account to use identical timing. Payment-year taxation can disadvantage either the taxpayer or the Treasury. In Nierotko's benefit setting, inflexible payment-year attribution instead could only disadvantage the employee. That structural difference permits the differing allocation despite real tax anomalies; it does not eliminate them. The tax scheme permits the existing payment-based construction, while avoiding repeated reconstruction of prior payroll accounts supports its reasonableness. Administrative convenience neither creates authority nor overrides contrary text.

Gitlitz enforces an express statutory classification and sequence. It does not make this wage stipulation a concession about attribution or remove genuine interpretive room for a salary-damages substitute. The Company's preferred tax saving is not itself an enacted entitlement. The Court therefore sustains the actual regulatory construction without changing the governing deference rules or disturbing Nierotko's benefit holdings.

**Limits and matters not reached:** The applicable payment rule recognizes actual or constructive payment; no earlier constructive payment is established, and an earlier due date alone does not establish it. The separate nonqualified-deferred-compensation statutes use the later of performance of services and lapse of substantial forfeiture risk; no such plan is established here. The rule concerns these stipulated non-interest salary awards and does not decide the separate minor-cash deemed-payment category, other unpresented payment categories or individual benefit accounts. The independent binding force of revenue rulings as a general class is unnecessary and remains undecided.

#### 2. Rates and annual bases must each be applied under their 1994 provisions

**Holding and operative rule:** These 1994 payments use each tax's 1994 statutory rate and its separate 1994 annual wage-base rule; an unchanged percentage or ceiling does not permit combining the payment with another year's wage account. The operative component applications are:

| Employer-tax component | Governing 1994 treatment | Distinct consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Social Security rate, section 3111(a) | 6.2 percent | The 1986 and 1987 rate of 5.7 percent does not govern these payments. |
| Social Security annual base, section 3121(a)(1) | $60,600 | The earlier $42,000 and $43,800 bases do not carry forward with the salary losses. |
| Medicare rate, section 3111(b) | 1.45 percent | The same percentage in the earlier years does not resolve the base question. |
| Medicare wage base | No former wage ceiling | The separate Social Security ceiling does not limit this Medicare component. |
| FUTA rate, section 3301 | 6.2 percent | The earlier 6.0 percent rate does not govern. |
| FUTA annual base, section 3306(b)(1) | $7,000 per recipient in this employer's annual account | Its unchanged amount does not merge different years; the obsolete $3,000 printed regulatory figure cannot supersede the statute. |

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies the rate and base rules independently. Sections 31.3111-3 and 31.3111-2(c) address FICA attachment and rate, while section 31.3121(a)(1)-1(a)(2) supplies the separate annual-base framework. Sections 31.3301-2 and 31.3301-3(b), together with section 31.3306(b)(1)-1(a)(2), perform the corresponding FUTA work. Their established backpay interpretation places the payments in 1994 for each inquiry. The governing statute controls the current dollar ceiling when an older regulatory print states an obsolete amount.

The earlier wage maxima explain why the allocation matters for all but one recipient. They do not establish the exceptional recipient's final liability, convert Medicare's unchanged percentage into an unchanged tax, or fix a net tax from the displayed statutory percentages alone. The lower court must implement these legal rules on the settled payment record, rather than award a refund on the rejected earlier-year theory.

**Limits and matters not reached:** The judgment fixes the applicable allocation, rates and bases, not an unsupported final dollar computation. Employee credits, benefits classification, any employee election and unpresented interest payments are outside this employer refund action. No speculative factual hearing is required merely to repeat the agreed payment dates.

**Precedent treatment:**

- **Social Security Board v. Nierotko:** Preserved for wage classification and allocation to earlier benefit periods, including its consideration of paid-quarter language. Its benefit judgment does not compel the same allocation under the later employer-tax provisions, and its restriction of agencies to lawful statutory authority remains effective.
- **Auer v. Robbins:** Applied to the responsible agency's fair, considered and textually permissible construction of its own regulations; no new general deference rule is adopted.
- **Thomas Jefferson University v. Shalala:** Applied to the agency's actual established ground, without supplying a fictional interpretation or overlooking the statutory boundary.
- **Christensen v. Harris County:** Preserved for separate treatment of advisory statutory views and regulatory interpretation, and for the prohibition on altering clear regulatory text by interpretation. A revenue ruling's publication alone does not settle its independent force.
- **Gitlitz v. Commissioner:** Its express classification and sequencing commands remain effective. A stipulation of wage status does not resolve this distinct temporal-attribution question or authorize a policy override of clear tax law.

**Resulting law:** The existing lawful Treasury construction governs payment-year employer taxation of these back wages under the applicable separate rate and annual-base provisions. The controlling rationale depends on a fair, established regulatory interpretation within statutory bounds, and does not adopt independent statutory compulsion as its sufficient ground. Benefit attribution remains governed by its own law. The judgment preserves actual-or-constructive-payment qualifications and does not establish an earlier payment, deferred-compensation plan or employee election.

### United States v. Cleveland Indians Baseball Co. — 2001-04-17

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in the judgment; no other Justice joins.** He would derive the payment-year rule independently from the tax provisions' payment and annual-receipt structure. The regulations and settled administration confirm that reading; they do not supply the rule in place of enacted text. Nierotko's benefits account serves a different statutory function, so its treatment of similar words does not require identical tax attribution. Administrative consistency permits predictable payroll calculation but cannot cure contrary text. He preserves Auer and Thomas Jefferson University without relying on deference to decide an ambiguity in this case. He agrees that the employer refund based on earlier-year allocation fails and that any remaining work is computational implementation. His independent statutory rationale is not the Court's controlling explanation.

**Scalia, concurring in the judgment; no other Justice joins.** He views temporal allocation of this damages substitute as a statutory gap. Neither treating the payments as wages nor observing that money changed hands in 1994 makes ordinary wage-payment language itself decide that gap. Nierotko's corresponding paid-period language makes direct textual compulsion particularly doubtful. He rejects the Court's explanation that different tax and benefit inequities themselves justify different meanings, but finds payment-year taxation a reasonable administrative resolution through the actual regulations. He therefore agrees with the operative payment-year applications and reversal without joining the Court's complete explanation or Stone-Zsela's direct statutory construction. His approach preserves the agency's lawful bounds and leaves individual benefits outside this case.
