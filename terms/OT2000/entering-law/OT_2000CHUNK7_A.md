**Decided:** January 9, 1996.

##### Issues included in a permitted §1292(b) appeal

**Holding:** A permitted interlocutory appeal under §1292(b) reaches issues fairly included in the certified order, not only the particular question formulated for certification. The displacement question is included here because it underlies the appealed partial-summary-judgment order; jurisdiction reaches no unrelated order in the action.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Part I; all eight Associates join. Stone-Zsela agrees only in this component's judgment.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 1292(b) requires written district-court certification of a controlling legal question, substantial ground for difference of opinion, and material advancement of the litigation's termination; an application within ten days permits discretionary appellate acceptance. The application alone creates no stay. Those predicates were satisfied here and are not displaced.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Sustain review of the displacement issue within the certified order. The substantive death-remedy holding appears under Maritime Law, Yamaha, January 9, 1996, No. 94-1387.

#### Jefferson v. City of Tarrant

**Docket or dockets:** No. 96-957.
**Decided:** December 9, 1997.

##### Whether an interlocutory civil-rights remedy ruling satisfies §1257 finality

**Holding:** The interlocutory Alabama ruling limiting compensatory recovery does not satisfy 28 U.S.C. §1257(a), although it conclusively settles that issue under state law. Substantial liability, causation and other claim proceedings remain; the question need not survive every outcome, effective later correction remains available, and postponement does not establish destruction of the asserted federal interest.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Breyer), joined by every Justice except Stevens; eight Justices.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Cox permits immediate review of a finally determined federal issue when proceedings are practically over, the issue necessarily survives every further outcome, effective later review is unavailable whatever the outcome, or reversal would preclude the relevant litigation and delay seriously threaten federal policy. State certification, importance, or the opposing party's tardy jurisdictional objection supplies no independent route. Ritchie does not require absolute impossibility of later appeal or establish a confidentiality-only exception: its disclosure interest, possible prosecution outcomes, completed trial and appellate proceedings, and inadequate contempt alternative explain its practical result without becoming universal necessary factors. No freestanding exception arises whenever state law of the case settles an issue. Federal recovery, survival, municipal liability, discrimination and causation remain unadjudicated.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The writ is dismissed without affirming the Alabama ruling's federal merits, vacating it, awarding damages or directing a new trial. Later review remains subject to the judgment and jurisdictional requirements then applicable.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Cox's four practical-finality routes are preserved. Ritchie, including note 7, is distinguished because these remaining proceedings have substantive work and later review can effectively correct the restriction; merely identifying a possible later appeal would not suffice. Richards involved a conclusively terminated or preordained controversy, Sullivan an issue surviving further outcomes, Labron/Kilgore the State's lack of effective later review whichever side prevailed, and Casarotto threatened loss of the arbitration forum through continued litigation. None supplies the missing predicate here. Robertson's §1988 principles and Newport's municipal punitive-damages bar receive no new application.

#### Whren v. United States, 517 U.S. 806

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-5841.
**Decided:** June 10, 1996.

##### Investigative motive and customary enforcement in an ordinary traffic stop

**Holding:** An ordinary initial traffic stop supported by objective probable cause of a traffic violation does not become unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment because officers also investigate an unrelated offense. Validity does not additionally depend on whether reasonable officers ordinarily would enforce the violation absent that other aim. Independent constitutional limits on manner, scope, and duration remain; detention or search materially exceeding what the traffic violation reasonably justifies requires independent grounds.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Thomas), unanimous, including the application and qualifications.

**Material application:** The accepted observations of waiting unusually long at a stop sign, failing to signal, and unreasonable speed supply traffic probable cause. Petitioners' proposed objective “would-have” test receives the same rejection as a subjective-motive prerequisite: variable local priorities, uniforms, assignments, markings, and enforcement customs do not change constitutional authority for the known violation. A separate balance of ordinary traffic-safety gains against plainclothes-intervention anxiety supplies no additional requirement.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Inventory and administrative-inspection authority retain their genuine-purpose and standardized or other source-specific constraints; motive is not irrelevant to every constitutional question. General Order 303.1 compliance, its immediate-safety-threat exception for otherwise unauthorized plainclothes or unmarked enforcement, and its extreme-circumstances oral-warning exception are not adjudicated. Neither speed nor missing execution details establish an exception or constitutional execution finding. The later search is not independently presented.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Scott* applies objective assessment of justified action; *Robinson*, *Gustafson*, and *Villamonte-Marquez* retain their distinct lawful-arrest, search, and customs-inspection predicates without creating new power. *Prouse* distinguishes suspicionless discretionary stops; *Martinez-Fuerte* and *Brignoni-Ponce* do not require a further balance for this ordinary probable-cause stop. *Bertine* and *Wells* preserve inventory constraints and forbid investigatory ruses; *Burger* preserves purpose-limited administrative inspection. *Abel* and *Bannister*'s regular-procedure and no-pretext observations do not establish the proposed customary-enforcement condition. *Wilson* preserves reasonable-execution review with its supported danger, futility, escape, and evidence-destruction qualifications, leaving suspicion quantum and automatic suppression open. *Garner*, *Welsh*, and *Winston* retain their distinct deadly-force, home-entry, and bodily-intrusion limits.

##### Review of the accepted predicate and separation of discriminatory selection

**Holding:** The accepted traffic observations permit resolution under *Ornelas*: historical facts, credibility, and genuinely factual subsidiary findings or inferences receive clear-error review, with genuine due weight to grounded contextual inferences; the legal standard and ultimate constitutional sufficiency properly at issue receive independent judgment. No new factual inquiry is required to decide this legal-rule challenge.

**Proposition-level authority:** The same unanimous Court opinion.

**Material application:** Due weight means substantive consideration of supported trial and local-officer context, not a numerical weight, officer-credibility presumption, or intermediate standard insulating constitutional conclusions. Direct review imports no habeas presumption or clear-and-convincing burden and supplies no supposed antecedent drug probable cause.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Purposeful racial selection remains independently forbidden by equal protection; traffic probable cause does not establish nondiscrimination. No discrimination finding, police-stop discovery entitlement, immunity rule, suppression consequence, or damages remedy is decided, and assumed adequacy of another remedy is not a ground for judgment.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm rejection of suppression on the initial-stop ground, 9–0. The completed vacatur of lesser-included §841 convictions and §860 resentencing, including nonreimposition of Whren's fine, remain undisturbed. Later sentencing appeals are not resolved. No new trial, hearing, release, sentencing proceeding, or determination of every admissibility ground follows.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Ornelas*'s review allocation is applied unchanged. *Kimberlin* permits circumstantial proof when motive is an underlying element without adding one here. *Armstrong* remains confined to federal charging and discovery; its requirements and unresolved discrimination remedy do not become police-stop rules.

#### Ricci v. Village of Arlington Heights

**Docket or dockets:** No. 97-501.

**Decided:** 1998-05-04.

##### Probable cause does not automatically justify additional station-house custody

**Holding:** Probable cause and state or local authority to enforce a fine-only civil licensing ordinance do not, by themselves, establish that transporting an identified operator to a station and confining him is reasonable. The added restraint must reasonably serve the actual enforcement function; nonexclusive possible justifications include securing identification or appearance, ending an actual continuing violation, protecting persons or evidence, or necessary processing in the circumstances. The rule imposes neither least restrictive means nor a duty to issue a citation whenever one is conceivable.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg’s Part I is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor and Breyer. Those five, including the author, directly adopt this rule and the targeted reversal; Stone-Zsela does not join Part II’s affirmative reservation of actual reasonableness.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Fine-only status creates no categorical arrest immunity, and the listed justifications are possibilities rather than findings. The continuing operation, possible daily fines, processing papers, one-hour custody and recognizance release remain relevant supported circumstances. A municipal policy cannot supply its own constitutional justification; the separate warrant theory and settled or abandoned claims remain outside review.

##### No controlling conclusion resolves the actual custody’s reasonableness

**Authority of the judgment:** Five Justices support targeted reversal and remand, but only Stone-Zsela concludes the actual custody was unreasonable. Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer leave that application open; Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas find the supported short enforcement processing reasonable and would affirm.

Part I’s rule controls by direct majority agreement. Part II’s application reservation has four joins, Stone-Zsela’s contrary application has one, and the dissent’s favorable application has four. These positions cannot be combined into a majority holding that this custody was unlawful or lawful, and no Marks synthesis is adopted. The lower courts must proceed under the controlling rule without a Supreme Court finding of ultimate violation, liability or immunity.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The adverse summary judgment on the preserved custody-reasonableness claim is set aside by targeted reversal and remand, 5–4, with Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer supporting. The settled search claim and unappealed probable-cause dismissal remain undisturbed. Policy causation, individual responsibility, conduct-time immunity and relief retain their own predicates.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Whren v. United States:** Preserved for ordinary initial probable-cause stops and applied at its independent execution limit; it does not establish unlimited custody for every civil violation.
- **Ohio v. Robinette:** Its separate later-restraint inquiry informs the new application without a mandatory citation or warning script.
- **Ayeni v. Mottola:** Its purpose-bound execution principle informs the added-restraint inquiry; the home setting and separate immunity decision retain their limits.
- **Wilson v. Arkansas:** History remains relevant to reasonableness, and reversal for a deficient framework need not establish a completed violation. No state arrest rule is automatically constitutionalized.
- **Soldal v. Cook County:** Civil enforcement remains within Fourth Amendment coverage, while reasonableness and liability stay distinct; its summary-judgment remedy does not establish those predicates automatically.
- **United States v. Robinson; Gustafson v. Florida:** Their lawful-arrest premise is preserved; search consequences cannot establish antecedent arrest lawfulness by assumption.
- **County of Riverside v. McLaughlin:** Its timing rule for post-arrest probable-cause review does not itself authorize this custody.
- **United States v. Lanier:** Conduct-time legal clarity remains separate from a present constitutional ruling; no newly stated rule retroactively defeats personal immunity.

#### Knowles v. Iowa, 525 U.S. 113

##### A citation does not trigger the full custodial-arrest search exception

**Holding:** Issuing a traffic citation without a custodial arrest does not authorize the full search incident to arrest; unexercised authority to make an arrest cannot substitute for the custody that triggers that exception. A full search requires an independently applicable recognized basis, and the citation, general roadside risk and observed speeding supply none for this search; supported exit orders, protective measures, consent searches and searches based on independent probable cause retain their own conditions and scope.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part I of the Court's opinion has all nine Justices' direct support. Part II's ordinary-remedial-law return has the eight Associates' support; Stone-Zsela would require the specific suppression stated in his concurrence.

No alternative justification is established here, and the Court orders no acquittal, release, replacement sentence, civil relief or universal exclusion rule for traffic encounters.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II has the eight Associate Justices' direct support. Stone-Zsela joins reversal and remand but would add the express suppression direction stated in his separate opinion.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The constitutional rule is unanimous. The eight-Associate remedial return does not adopt Stone-Zsela's separate automatic suppression direction. No alternative search ground is established; independent protective measures, consent and probable-cause grounds retain their own predicates. No acquittal, release, replacement sentence or civil liability is ordered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- New York v. Belton: distinguishes the vehicle search incident to arrest from a full search after citation alone.
- Whren v. United States (June 10, 1996): preserves objective justification for the ordinary initial stop and independent limits on later search, scope and duration.
- Ohio v. Robinette (November 18, 1996): preserves the distinction between traffic business, subsequent restraint, voluntary consent and any prior illegality.
- Maryland v. Wilson (February 19, 1997): preserves the limited passenger-exit authority without creating automatic frisk or search power.

- Michigan v. Long: preserves a limited protective vehicle search on its distinct danger, weapons-access and scope predicates.

#### American Dredging Co. v. Miller, 510 U.S. 443

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-1950.

**Decided:** 1994-02-23.

##### State forum administration under the saving clause and Jones Act

**Holding:** A competent state court hearing this domestic Jones Act and accompanying in personam maritime action need not employ the federal doctrine permitting dismissal in favor of a more convenient forum; Louisiana may retain the action under Article 123(C). That choice changes neither federal maritime liability and remedies nor jurisdiction, and it decides no independent discrimination, Commerce Clause, or foreign-party question.

**Proposition-level authority:** The statutory/state-forum portion of Scalia's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, and Ginsburg; seven Justices adopt this sufficient ground for affirmance. Stevens joins this portion and the judgment, but not the separate Jensen analysis.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Missouri ex rel. Southern Railway Co. v. Mayfield, 340 U.S. 1: its state-forum choice for FELA actions carries through the Jones Act; its requirement of impartial local policy, without discrimination against federal claims or sister-state citizens, remains intact. A residence distinction that treats all nonresidents alike is not equated with citizenship discrimination.
- Bainbridge v. Merchants & Miners Transportation Co., 287 U.S. 278: applied to preserve state-court Jones Act venue rules; venue and discretionary dismissal remain distinct.

**Independent alternative holding:** Louisiana's refusal to dismiss this domestic in personam maritime action for convenience neither materially prejudices a characteristic feature of general maritime law nor interferes with its proper harmony and uniformity. Forum non conveniens governs forum administration rather than primary maritime obligations; a procedural label alone, however, does not protect a state rule that impairs a substantive maritime right or an integral burden or remedy.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** The Jensen portion of Scalia's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Souter, and Ginsburg; six Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground. Stevens does not join it. Souter also supplies a separate close-classification qualification without withdrawing this join.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205: applied and left in force; neither characteristic-feature protection nor necessary maritime uniformity requires the dismissal procedure here.
- Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert, 330 U.S. 501, and Piper Aircraft Co. v. Reyno, 454 U.S. 235: their federal convenience inquiry remains applicable in the courts governed by it, including the adequate-alternative-forum prerequisite, relevant private/public considerations, appropriate weight to forum choice, and abuse-of-discretion review; it is not imposed on this state court.
- Miles v. Apex Marine Corp., 498 U.S. 19: its statute-bounded uniformity of substantive maritime recovery is preserved, not converted into a uniform forum-management command.
- Sun Oil Co. v. Wortman, 486 U.S. 717: its context-sensitive substance/procedure distinction supports attention to legal function; it supplies no categorical maritime exemption for procedural labels.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The holding concerns a competent state court and domestic in personam claims. Maritime liability, remedies, integral burdens, independent discrimination and Commerce Clause objections, and foreign-party controversies are not resolved by treating forum administration as procedural. Jensen remains in force.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Louisiana Supreme Court is affirmed, preserving its reversal of the convenience dismissal and remand. The injury claims may proceed in state court. No interstate transfer, federal convenience reweighing or liability award is ordered; the unpursued personal-jurisdiction objection is not decided.

#### Yamaha Motor Corp., U.S.A. v. Calhoun, 516 U.S. 199

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-1387.
**Decided:** January 9, 1996.

##### Continued availability of state death and survival remedies

**Holding:** Moragne's federal maritime wrongful-death remedy does not itself displace otherwise available state wrongful-death and survival remedies for a nonseafarer killed in territorial waters where no applicable federal statute or controlling uniform maritime rule makes recovery exclusive. The rule covers persons outside Jones Act seaman and pertinent federal maritime-employment classes; it does not expand remedies in Congress's occupied seaman or high-seas fields.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's Part II and decree, unanimous.

**Material application:** The child killed in Puerto Rico's territorial waters was neither a seaman nor a covered longshore worker. General uniformity concerns do not extend a federal compensation regime beyond its coverage conditions.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court assumes rather than decides Moragne's extension to this nonseafarer and inclusion of survival. It selects neither Pennsylvania nor Puerto Rico law and determines no damages category, amount, liability standard, defect, negligence, causation or punitive liability. State labeling does not make substantive damages procedural or authorize alteration of characteristic maritime rights generally.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the Third Circuit's rejection of displacement, leaving its partial-summary-judgment dispositions and remand operative; no award is made. The §1292(b) scope holding is under Federal Courts, Yamaha, January 9, 1996, No. 94-1387.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Moragne adds a federal remedy without automatic exclusivity. Miles remains tied to Congress's seaman-remedy choices; Offshore Logistics protects DOHSA's exclusive high-seas field. American Dredging's state-forum practice holding is distinct and does not itself validate substantive state damages.

#### Dooley v. Korean Air Lines Co.

**Docket or dockets:** No. 97-704.

**Decided:** 1998-06-08.

##### No additional maritime survival recovery for this high-seas death

**Holding:** When the applicable Death on the High Seas Act governs a death, general maritime law does not supply the proposed parallel survival action for the decedent's predeath pain and suffering. The personal representative and beneficiaries retain recovery authorized by the governing statute and treaty; this ruling does not abolish maritime survival remedies outside the Act's field.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by all eight other Justices, supplies nine direct votes for the rule and explanation.

**Material application:** Section 765 addresses a personal-injury claimant who dies from the same wrong while an admiralty action is pending. It permits representative substitution and continuation for section 762 compensation; it does not compel conversion of every injury suit or preserve every personal loss. The distinct estate injury does not permit supplementation of Congress's selected high-seas compensation scheme.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No finding determines conscious suffering, its duration, liability or an amount. The Court decides no universal survival prohibition, every category of predeath economic loss, or an independent claim under section 764's foreign-law provision or section 767's state-remedy language. Section 767's geographic exclusions, including state territorial waters, the Great Lakes and the Panama Canal Zone, remain distinct. No later aviation amendment governs this case.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm rejection of the general maritime predeath pain-and-suffering claim. Liability and unrelated available recovery are undisturbed; no damages amount is ordered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Zicherman is applied for domestic-law damage categories and the high-seas pecuniary-loss boundary; its expressly reserved survival question is answered here, not treated as previously settled.
- Yamaha is distinguished by its nonseafarer territorial-water setting and absence of an applicable exclusive statutory regime. Its preservation of otherwise available state remedies remains intact.
- Mobil Oil Corp. v. Higginbotham supplies deference to Congress's actual compensation choices within DOHSA's field.
- Miles v. Apex Marine Corp. supplies the requirement that judicial maritime remedies respect applicable congressional remedial boundaries, without making every maritime setting exclusive.
- Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire preserves the Act's high-seas field and the distinction from territorial remedies.

#### Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, Inc., 504 U.S. 451 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-1029.
**Decided:** June 8, 1992.

##### Whether demand for separate parts and service and conditional parts sales create a triable tying claim

**Holding:** For a Sherman Act §1 tying claim, functionally related products are distinct when sufficient separate consumer demand makes separate provision efficient. Evidence that Kodak parts and service meet that test, and that Kodak conditions parts sales on buying its service or forgoing independent service, creates genuine disputes about distinct products and conditioning; respondents must still prove all remaining tying elements.

**Proposition-level authority:** Blackmun's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices.

**Material application:** Independent service providers, customer preference for their services and self-servicing customers permit a finding of efficient separate provision. Restrictions on parts sales and use of independent service permit a finding of conditioning; installation of parts during service does not conclusively merge the products. The tied-commerce volume is undisputed here.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No final tie, market power or liability finding is made. An independent original-equipment-manufacturer conspiracy claim is not adjudicated; the rule-of-reason theory unpreserved in opposition to summary judgment is outside review.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm reversal of summary judgment on the preserved §1 tying claim; respondents retain proof of its elements and the requirements for recovery.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde*, 466 U.S. 2, supplies separate demand, conditioning, tying power and tied commerce. *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, preserves the obligation to produce evidence on challenged elements at summary judgment.

##### Whether equipment-market competition categorically precludes aftermarket power

**Holding:** Competition in an equipment market does not as a matter of law preclude power in its parts or service markets. Rule 56 does not permit summary judgment solely through an economic prediction contradicted by evidence allowing a rational inference of power, including material information and switching costs and actual price or exclusion effects; unsupported speculation remains insufficient.

**Proposition-level authority:** Blackmun's opinion, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices reject the categorical defense.

**Current force:** Remains controlling as stated; equipment competition remains relevant evidence.

**Material application:** Costly equipment replacement and difficulty predicting lifetime service prices permit a finding that installed customers cannot readily escape adverse terms. Evidence of price and exclusion effects supports further proceedings. Kodak may establish that informed new customers and equipment competition adequately constrain its policies, but the conclusion is not inevitable as a matter of law.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court presumes neither market power from imperfect information nor monopoly status for every durable-product seller. Prices, effects and asserted policy changes remain disputed; no universal grandfathering, deception or retroactive-policy-change finding is made.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The preserved claims survive summary judgment, with actual markets, power and liability remaining to be proved.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 475 U.S. 574, preserves rational inference from evidence but is distinguished because immediate aftermarket gains differ from its prolonged loss-producing conspiracy. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, preserves the factfinder's role on genuine material disputes. *Jefferson Parish*, 466 U.S. 2, continues to require proof of actual tying power.

##### Whether one brand's parts or service can constitute a relevant market on this record

**Holding:** A relevant product market may consist of one brand's parts or service when actual commercial alternatives and competitive constraints support that definition; brand identity alone neither establishes nor defeats it. Kodak-specific compatibility, very high parts and service shares, and entry and switching barriers create a genuine dispute about monopoly power sufficient to resist summary judgment under §2.

**Proposition-level authority:** Blackmun's opinion, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices permit a market and power finding without conclusively establishing either.

**Material application:** Owners cannot necessarily substitute another brand's parts, and replacing the equipment may cost much more than the service at issue. Evidence supports nearly complete control of compatible parts and an eighty-to-ninety-five-percent service share, together with impediments to independent supply. Equipment competitors remain relevant to whether lifetime prices are constrained.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No universal single-brand-market rule or monopoly presumption follows from patents, proprietary design or numerical share apart from actual competitive constraints. Actual market definition and power remain unresolved.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm reversal of summary judgment on the preserved §2 claims, without a final market or power finding.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *United States v. Grinnell Corp.*, 384 U.S. 563, requires monopoly power in a relevant market, distinct from exclusion. *United States v. E. I. du Pont de Nemours & Co.*, 351 U.S. 377, supplies practical substitution and competitive-constraint analysis without a categorical bar to one-brand markets.

##### Whether evidence of exclusion and Kodak's business explanations conclusively resolve monopoly maintenance

**Holding:** Monopolization under §2 requires monopoly power plus willful acquisition or maintenance, distinguished from success through a superior product, business acumen or accident. Parts-access restrictions that may exclude service rivals create a genuine issue of unlawful maintenance, and Kodak's quality, inventory and free-riding explanations do not conclusively eliminate that issue here; no general duty to supply rivals or final pretext finding is imposed.

**Proposition-level authority:** Blackmun's opinion, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices.

**Current force:** Remains controlling as stated for evidentiary sufficiency and the distinct power and conduct requirements.

**Material application:** The quality explanation must be tested against self-service exceptions and independent-service evidence; the inventory explanation against evidence that parts needs do not depend on the service provider; and free-riding claims against the restrictions' operation and alternative-supply obstacles. Kodak may prove legitimate reasons and defeat liability.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Attempted monopolization remains distinct, with its own intent and dangerous-probability requirements. Ultimate markets, power, unlawful conduct, pretext, antitrust injury, causation, damages and remedial design remain unresolved. Injury to a competitor alone establishes neither antitrust injury nor damages entitlement.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm reversal of summary judgment on the preserved §2 monopolization and attempt claims. No damages, final injunction or compulsory parts-supply order issues; respondents must prove each claim and recovery requirement.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Grinnell*, 384 U.S. 563, distinguishes unlawful maintenance from lawful success. *Aspen Skiing Co. v. Aspen Highlands Skiing Corp.*, 472 U.S. 585, makes exclusion and business justification material without creating a universal supply duty. *Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc.*, 429 U.S. 477, retains the distinct antitrust-injury requirement. *Holmes v. Securities Investor Protection Corp.*, March 24, 1992, retains its RICO directness and SIPA holdings without deciding these Sherman Act elements or injury.

#### Spectrum Sports v. McQuillan, 506 U.S. 447 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-10.

**Decided:** January 25, 1993.

##### Conduct and intent cannot replace dangerous probability

**Holding:** Attempted monopolization under Sherman Act §2 requires anticompetitive or predatory conduct, specific intent to monopolize, and a dangerous probability of achieving monopoly power, evaluated through the relevant product and geographic market and the defendant’s economic power there. Conduct may support intent and other permissible evidentiary inferences, but neither conduct nor intent eliminates independent proof of dangerous probability; no categorical small-firm immunity or prohibition of circumstantial proof follows.

**Proposition-level authority:** White’s opinion of the Court for Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; nine Justices adopt the rule and its explanation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Swift & Co. v. United States, 196 U.S. 375 — applied: dangerous probability requires proximity and degree beyond intent alone.
- Copperweld Corp. v. Independence Tube Corp., 467 U.S. 752 — applied: unilateral conduct must actually monopolize or dangerously threaten monopolization for these §2 offenses.
- Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, June 8, 1992 — preserved at its stated scope: evidence may establish a single-brand market and power; no presumption replaces separate attempt elements.
- Lessig v. Tidewater Oil Co., 327 F.2d 459 — rejected insofar as its conduct-based inference dispenses with market and dangerous-probability proof.

##### The undifferentiated verdict requires further proceedings

**Holding:** The judgment against these petitioners cannot stand on a §2 verdict that may rest solely on an attempted-monopolization instruction dispensing with dangerous-probability proof, when the appellate affirmance rests on that legally erroneous route. Reverse and remand for application of the correct elements and consideration of any properly preserved independent grounds; this decision establishes neither defeat nor success on those grounds and orders no automatic new trial or judgment for petitioners.

**Proposition-level authority:** White’s unanimous opinion; all nine Justices adopt the remedial holding and explanation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Sunkist Growers, Inc. v. Winckler & Smith Citrus Products Co., 370 U.S. 19 — applied: the possibility of reliance on a legally erroneous submitted ground requires reversal here.
- Griffin v. United States, December 3, 1991 — distinguished at its stated scope: its factual-insufficiency rule does not validate an element-removing instruction or decide the unreviewed civil claims.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No market-share floor, small-firm immunity, general supply duty or automatic inference of market power from a proprietary product is adopted. Antitrust injury and other recovery elements remain necessary but are not separately decided. RICO, state claims and other parties’ judgments are not resolved.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse as to these petitioners and remand under the correct attempt elements and any preserved independent grounds. The undifferentiated verdict may rest on legal error; neither a universal new trial nor judgment for defendants is commanded.

#### United States v. Haggar Apparel Co.

**Docket or dockets:** No. 97-2044; judgment below, 127 F.3d 1460.
**Decided:** 1999-04-21.

##### Trade-court factfinding does not eliminate lawful delegated interpretation

**Holding:** A formally promulgated Customs regulation implementing Congress's actual delegation receives Chevron review in tariff litigation: courts first enforce unambiguous statutory requirements and the delegation's boundaries, then assess a reasonable agency choice within genuine ambiguity. The Court of International Trade's duty to decide on its own record does not repeal that legal framework; agency labels and notice and comment alone do not establish authority or validity.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy's opinion, Parts I–III, unanimous.

##### This regulation's statutory fit and actual application require the corrected inquiry

**Holding:** The judgment must be vacated because categorical nondeference left unresolved whether the regulation reasonably respects the tariff's incidental-assembly allowance and lawfully applies to this process. On remand the courts must enforce the enacted conditions, examine the regulation's actual introductory terms and examples, and resolve relevant application questions under the proper review standards; neither regulatory validity nor the claimed exemption is established in advance.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy's Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Breyer (seven Justices).

**Limits and questions not reached:** The tariff requires United States fabricated components exported ready for assembly without further fabrication, retention of physical identity and no foreign advancement or improvement except assembly and incidental operations including cleaning, lubricating and painting. The allowance reduces duties by qualifying component cost or value, with proportional treatment of specific/compound rates; it does not exempt the entire article. ### Gitlitz v. Commissioner — 2001-01-09

Authority: [controlling holdings](../records/Gitlitz_v_Commissioner_merits_2001-01-09.md#holdings); [precedent treatment](../records/Gitlitz_v_Commissioner_merits_2001-01-09.md#precedent-treatment); [operative procedure](../records/Gitlitz_v_Commissioner_merits_2001-01-09.md#procedure-after-action). The following holding and precedent blocks are copied from the Record; the Record controls.

#### I. Excluded discharge income passes through and increases basis

**Holding and operative rule:** Under the Code version governing the 1991 returns, an insolvent S corporation's debt-discharge amount excluded from gross income by section 108(a) remains an item of income under section 1366(a)(1)(A), passes through in each shareholder's pro rata share and increases stock basis under section 1367(a)(1)(A). Losses and deductions are then limited by section 1366(d)(1), using that adjusted stock basis and the shareholder's qualifying debt basis; exclusion remains capped by the corporation's insolvency immediately before discharge and supplies no free-standing deduction entitlement.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Those eight directly adopt both holdings and the statutory remand. Breyer dissents from this item's pass-through and resulting basis increase.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 61 identifies discharge of indebtedness as income. Section 108 excludes the qualifying amount from gross income without declaring that the item ceases to be income. Section 1366(a)(1)(A) expressly encompasses tax-exempt income, and section 1367(a)(1)(A) links the basis increase to that category. The Code therefore supplies both the income classification and the adjustment.

Section 108(d)(7)(A), as applicable in 1991, directs that subsections (a), (b) and (g) operate at the corporate level. That direction determines where exclusion and attribute reduction occur; it does not erase the income item or suspend the shareholder provisions. Section 1367(b)(1)'s return-inclusion restriction concerns amounts required to be included in gross income, and does not prohibit this increase for an excluded item that section 1366 recognizes. Bufferd preserves the different statutory functions of corporate and shareholder tax consequences. Hill likewise recognizes distinct statutory accounts, without a general prohibition against multiple tax benefits.

The Commissioner's objection that shareholders receive a benefit without fresh economic outlay cannot add a condition absent from these coordinated provisions. The result is confined to the presented statutory mechanism and version. It neither makes every excluded receipt a basis item nor exempts a claimed deduction from other applicable limits. Here the reported $2,021,296 discharge is less than the corporation's reported $2,181,748 insolvency; the holding determines the legal treatment rather than entering an unsupported final tax amount.

#### II. The discharge-year calculation precedes the prescribed attribute reduction

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 108(b)(4)(A) requires the corporate tax-attribute reductions described in section 108(b)(2) after determination of the tax for the discharge year. For these S corporation returns, that determination includes the pass-through income, basis increase and otherwise allowable shareholder losses; only losses still disallowed under section 1366(d)(1) are treated by section 108(d)(7)(B) as a corporate net operating loss for this attribute-reduction calculation.

**Authority:** The same eight-Justice Court opinion directly adopts the sequence. Breyer's conditional acceptance of the timing command for an item that properly enters shareholder tax is not a join to this actual item's pass-through, basis or sequence holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Determining discharge-year shareholder tax requires calculating basis and allowable losses. Section 1366(d)(1) expressly uses basis determined with the section 1367(a)(1) income adjustments. Only then can the court identify the losses still disallowed and apply section 108(d)(7)(B). That paragraph retains work when losses exceed properly adjusted basis; it need not suppress the income adjustment to operate.

The Tenth Circuit's reduction-first approach reverses the enacted sequence. Breyer's competing construction instead disputes whether the special corporate exclusion-and-offset mechanism permits this shareholder item at all. The Court rejects that antecedent restriction for the textual reasons in Part I. Once the item enters the calculation, the express after-tax command governs. A perceived double benefit cannot authorize a court to rearrange that command. Atlantic Mutual permits administrative construction within genuine interpretive room, but supplies no power to contradict an express statutory direction or a general revenue-protection override; no applicable authorized regulation identified here independently changes the sequence.

The judgment is reversed and remanded for recomputation of the deficiencies and permitted losses under these rules, followed by the applicable corporate attribute reduction. The reported half shares and claimed losses of $1,010,648 per shareholder are not automatic awards. Ordinary amount, basis and deduction limits remain for application. Later statutory changes, other entities and other tax years lie outside the question presented; no new evidentiary hearing or reopening of settled tax facts is directed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bufferd v. Commissioner: applies the distinction between S corporation and shareholder statutory tax functions; its return-and-limitations holding is not converted into an answer it had already given to debt discharge.
- United States v. Hill: applies its respect for distinct statutory tax accounts and retains its rejection of a general ban on multiple tax benefits; its mineral-basis rule is unchanged.
- Atlantic Mutual Insurance Co. v. Commissioner: preserves deference within actual statutory interpretive room while distinguishing a construction that would contradict the express sequence; no general revenue-protection exception is adopted.
- Commissioner v. Estate of Hubert: preserves the need to give operative statutory and regulatory qualifications independent work; it supplies no authority to replace this Code sequence with a categorical economic-outlay condition.

### Illinois v. McArthur — 2001-02-20

Authority: [controlling holdings](../records/Illinois_v_McArthur_merits_2001-02-20.md#holdings); [precedent treatment](../records/Illinois_v_McArthur_merits_2001-02-20.md#precedent-treatment); [operative procedure](../records/Illinois_v_McArthur_merits_2001-02-20.md#procedure-after-action). The following holding and precedent blocks are copied from the Record; the Record controls.

#### I. The particular preservation need and limited execution justify this restraint

**Holding and operative rule:** Officers may temporarily restrict unaccompanied entry into a home while obtaining a warrant when particularized probable cause, a reasonable actual risk of evidence destruction, a restraint reasonably confined to preservation and diligent pursuit of judicial authorization together justify that intrusion. The approximately two-hour restriction here satisfies those conditions in combination; probable cause, refusal of consent, awareness of an investigation, disposable drugs, jailability or duration alone creates no automatic exception or safe harbor.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion is joined in full by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those seven Justices adopt both holdings and the complete remand. Stone-Zsela dissents; Stevens would dismiss the writ as improvidently granted and, because the Court reaches the merits, would affirm suppression. Souter's additional concurrence does not limit his full join.

**Controlling explanation:** Payton preserves the home's special warrant protection. Whren's objective initial-stop rule does not authorize a greater residential intrusion, and Bond requires an independent basis for an added intrusion despite lawful presence. Edmond addresses suspicionless ordinary-crime checkpoints; its program-purpose threshold supplies no rule for this individualized preservation measure.

Probable cause was uncontested. McArthur's wife reported seeing him slide drugs under the couch; he knew officers were investigating and had ready access to the identified disposable evidence. These particular circumstances reasonably supported a destruction concern without requiring police to wait for an observed attempt. Refusal to consent supplies neither guilt nor an independent exigency. One officer and the wife pursued a warrant while another restrained unaccompanied reentry. McArthur made two or three entries for cigarettes and telephone use with observation just inside the doorway. The officers conducted no evidentiary search before the warrant. Their approximately two-hour effort was diligent and the actual restriction reasonably preserved the evidence pending a magistrate's decision.

Place gives duration and diligence independent importance; no fixed two-hour allowance follows. Welsh requires attention to offense gravity. These offenses were modest but jailable: up to thirty days for the marijuana count and one year for paraphernalia. That distinguishes Welsh's civil, nonjailable nighttime home-arrest setting without making jailability sufficient. The substantial home interest and modest offenses weigh against restraint, but the actual preservation need and limited warrant-directed execution make this measure reasonable.

Segura's independent-source result does not validate every earlier seizure, and its broader Part IV discussion had only two votes. The Court relies on the justification before restraint, not the later discoveries. Limited observational consent is not general search consent or abandonment of the home-access objection. No general power to occupy or search homes, police-created unlawful exigency, requirement to exhaust every imaginable alternative or universal warrant-exception checklist is decided.

#### II. The presented suppression ground cannot stand

**Holding and operative rule:** The Illinois appellate judgment is reversed insofar as suppression rests on this temporary restriction of residential access, and the case is remanded for ordinary proceedings. Any actually preserved independent warrant, admissibility or exclusionary-rule issue remains governed by its own law; none is found established or newly created here.

**Authority:** The same seven-Justice coalition directly joins this holding and mandate.

**Controlling explanation:** The lower court treated the temporary restraint as an unlawful seizure and suppressed the later warrant evidence on that ground. Because the actual restraint was justified, that ground fails. The Court does not give the later warrant retroactive force, independently resolve every feature of that warrant or determine guilt. Correction of this suppression ground requires further proceedings, not an automatic conviction, dismissal of charges, release, new trial or new evidentiary hearing.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Payton v. New York, 445 U.S. 573: preserves the home's warrant protection; no general residential-entry authority is created.
- Whren v. United States: preserves objective ordinary-enforcement analysis and independent limits on greater intrusion, scope and duration; traffic probable cause is not residential authority.
- Bond v. United States: applies the requirement of independent justification for an added intrusion; lawful presence and limited observation do not imply consent to search.
- City of Indianapolis v. Edmond: distinguishes suspicionless ordinary-crime checkpoints from this individualized preservation measure; its program-purpose holding remains intact.
- United States v. Place, 462 U.S. 696: applies attention to actual duration and diligence in temporary preservation; no fixed permissible interval is transferred from luggage to a home.
- Welsh v. Wisconsin, 466 U.S. 740: preserves the importance of offense gravity and home protection; distinguishes its civil, nonjailable nighttime arrest without making every jailable offense sufficient for restraint.
- Segura v. United States, 468 U.S. 796: preserves its independent-source holding while declining to treat it as approval of every initial seizure; the broader securing-premises discussion in Part IV remains a two-Justice position, not a majority holding.

### Lewis v. Lewis & Clark Marine, Inc. — 2001-02-21

Authority: [controlling holdings](../records/Lewis_v_Lewis_and_Clark_Marine_merits_2001-02-21.md#holdings); [precedent treatment](../records/Lewis_v_Lewis_and_Clark_Marine_merits_2001-02-21.md#precedent-treatment).

#### Adequately protected limitation permits the saved state remedy

**Holding and operative rule:** A district court may dissolve a Rule F injunction when the actual fund relationship, binding claimant stipulations and retained federal jurisdiction adequately protect the vessel owner's statutory limitation right. The saving-to-suitors clause does not require a state jury demand, and the Limitation Act supplies no separate exclusive federal exoneration right that compels restraint once limitation is protected.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's opinion is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt the rule, its application and reversal.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 1333(1) saves other remedies while granting federal admiralty jurisdiction. The Limitation Act protects the qualifying limit on liability, subject to its privity-or-knowledge condition. It does not convert that protection into a right to federal adjudication of every defense. Rule F permits the owner to request exoneration, but that permission cannot enlarge the substantive statute or erase saved remedies. A state action remains a remedy even when tried without a jury.

Langnes distinguishes continuing federal jurisdiction from a duty to adjudicate exclusively, and Lake Tankers permits state proceedings where the fund adequately protects limitation. Here the district court found the fund sufficient, Lewis limited his claim below it, relinquished limitation-related preclusion and preserved federal determination of limitation. Retaining the federal case protected the owner's ability to invoke that jurisdiction if an actual later development threatened the right. The dissolution therefore fell within the court's sound discretion.

The owner's concern for effective federal protection is legitimate but does not identify an actual deficiency in this arrangement. Lewis's stipulations cannot bind absent claimants without a legal basis; no identified additional claim defeats the district court's adequacy finding. If competing claims, an inadequate fund or ineffective stipulations leave limitation genuinely uncertain, federal adjudication of liability and limitation remains available. The holding establishes permission on adequate protection, not an automatic claimant entitlement from filing a document called a stipulation. American Dredging supports distinguishing forum administration from impairment of maritime rights; this order preserves the actual substantive safeguard.

**Limits and work remaining:** The adequate-fund ground disposes of this forum controversy without a universal single-claimant or multiple-claimant stipulation formula. Actual liability, damages, the owner's privity or knowledge and ultimate entitlement to limitation remain outside review. A materially changed claim or fund situation requires application of the protection rule to its actual facts.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Langnes v. Green: applies discretionary protection of saved state remedies while retaining federal jurisdiction over limitation.
- Lake Tankers Corp. v. Henn: applies the adequate-fund route; limitation jurisdiction does not invariably require exclusive federal liability adjudication.
- American Dredging: applies the distinction between state-forum administration and impairment of an actual maritime safeguard; no new general uniformity theory is necessary.
- Yamaha Motor Corp. v. Calhoun: preserves otherwise available state remedies within its nonseafarer field; it supplies no expansion of this seaman's substantive recovery.

### TrafFix Devices, Inc. v. Marketing Displays, Inc. — 2001-03-20

Authority: [controlling holdings](../records/TrafFix_Devices_v_Marketing_Displays_merits_2001-03-20.md#holdings); [precedent treatment](../records/TrafFix_Devices_v_Marketing_Displays_merits_2001-03-20.md#precedent-treatment).

#### Useful features remain available to competitors

**Holding and operative rule:** An expired utility patent is strong evidence that the feature it identifies as useful is functional. When the claimed feature is essential to the article's use or purpose or affects its cost or quality, source recognition and alternative workable designs do not make it protectable trade dress; a genuinely distinct ornamental, incidental or arbitrary appearance must be evaluated on its own rather than presumed functional merely because it appears in a patented product.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, directly controls the rule, application and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that trade-dress law does not continue exclusive control of useful engineering merely because buyers recognize its source. Inwood supplies the essential-use-or-purpose and cost-or-quality inquiry. Qualitex retains that inquiry while also addressing competitive disadvantage unrelated to reputation. Its competitive-necessity discussion does not require another inquiry after traditional utilitarian functionality is established. Wal-Mart separately requires source significance for product design; recognition cannot replace nonfunctionality.

The patents describe the dual springs' contribution to wind resistance and stability. That operation, supported by the record, establishes the functional character of the feature claimed here. MDI's argument concerns the overall stand, including legs, uprights and spring placement. Considering that combination does not reveal a distinct nonfunctional ornament in this record. Requiring a competitor to conceal, cover or rearrange the working springs would restrict use of the useful configuration itself. The availability of another workable arrangement therefore does not defeat the functionality ground.

The patent creates a substantial evidentiary obstacle, not a conclusive presumption covering every appearance detail. A claimant remains able to identify and support a genuinely separate nonfunctional feature. None has been established in the claim before the Court. The undisputed functional evidence is sufficient without deciding the temporal application of the 1999 burden amendment. Functionality disposes of this trade-dress theory, so secondary meaning supplies no further ground that must be reached.

**Limits and matters not reached:** The decision establishes no automatic constitutional bar to all trade dress associated with expired patents and announces no new aesthetic-functionality test. A distinct unasserted ornamental design lies outside this claim. Those questions are unnecessary or outside the granted scope, respectively; the functional dual-spring claim itself is resolved, not returned for another alternatives inquiry.

**Precedent treatment:**

- **Inwood Laboratories, Inc. v. Ives Laboratories, Inc.:** Applied for the traditional essential-use-or-purpose and cost-or-quality rule.
- **Qualitex Co. v. Jacobson Products Co.:** Applied and clarified: its competitive-disadvantage discussion complements, rather than displaces, the traditional utilitarian bar. No additional competitive-necessity showing is required once that bar is met.
- **Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Samara Brothers, Inc.:** Applied to keep source significance for product design separate from nonfunctionality; its actual distinctiveness rule remains unchanged.
- **Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc.:** Its protection of otherwise qualifying trade dress does not remove the independent functionality requirement; its existing scope remains intact.

**Resulting law:** A utility patent supplies strong, feature-specific evidence of functionality. Proven useful function independently defeats trade-dress protection despite source recognition or available alternative designs. The rule leaves protection of genuinely distinct nonfunctional appearance to its own requirements and leaves the 1999 amendment's temporal reach undecided.

### United States v. Cleveland Indians Baseball Co. — 2001-04-17

Authority: [controlling holdings](../records/United_States_v_Cleveland_Indians_Baseball_merits_2001-04-17.md#holdings); [precedent treatment](../records/United_States_v_Cleveland_Indians_Baseball_merits_2001-04-17.md#precedent-treatment).

**Common authority:** Justice Thomas's opinion is joined throughout by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Seven Justices directly adopt both holdings and the common regulatory explanation. Stone-Zsela and Scalia agree with the judgment and resulting payment-year applications on the distinct grounds described below; neither joins the Court's complete rationale.

#### 1. The established interpretation lawfully places these back wages in the payment-year tax account

**Holding and operative rule:** Treasury's fair and considered interpretation of the applicable rate and separate annual-base regulations requires these taxable salary awards to enter the employer's payment-year FICA and FUTA calculations. The interpretation fits the statutes and regulations and receives controlling weight under Auer; wage status alone does not settle timing, and the Court does not hold that the statutes unambiguously compel this treatment independently of the lawful administrative construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the actual, established interpretation governs this unresolved allocation question. The regulations distinguish remuneration paid or received in a calendar year from services performed in that year. The rate provisions select the payment-year percentage, while separate annual-base provisions identify the year's wage account. They do not expressly address backpay. Revenue Rulings 78-336 and 89-35 evidence a preexisting construction placing back wages in the payment year, rather than a position newly invented for this litigation.

Auer gives a responsible agency's fair and considered interpretation of its own regulation controlling weight unless plainly erroneous or inconsistent with that rule. Thomas Jefferson University requires the agency's actual ground, not a judicial substitute. The identified construction fits each payment and annual-base provision and remains within the statutes. Christensen prevents interpretation from amending clear regulatory text and distinguishes an advisory statutory view from an own-regulation construction; publication of a revenue ruling alone does not confer independent controlling statutory force.

Nierotko gives the Company's contrary argument real force. It classified backpay as wages and allocated it to earlier benefit periods despite paid-quarter language. But restoring a worker's service-based benefit account does not determine employer taxes under the later annual rate and base provisions. Common wage status does not require every statutory account to use identical timing. Payment-year taxation can disadvantage either the taxpayer or the Treasury. In Nierotko's benefit setting, inflexible payment-year attribution instead could only disadvantage the employee. That structural difference permits the differing allocation despite real tax anomalies; it does not eliminate them. The tax scheme permits the existing payment-based construction, while avoiding repeated reconstruction of prior payroll accounts supports its reasonableness. Administrative convenience neither creates authority nor overrides contrary text.

Gitlitz enforces an express statutory classification and sequence. It does not make this wage stipulation a concession about attribution or remove genuine interpretive room for a salary-damages substitute. The Company's preferred tax saving is not itself an enacted entitlement. The Court therefore sustains the actual regulatory construction without changing the governing deference rules or disturbing Nierotko's benefit holdings.

**Limits and matters not reached:** The applicable payment rule recognizes actual or constructive payment; no earlier constructive payment is established, and an earlier due date alone does not establish it. The separate nonqualified-deferred-compensation statutes use the later of performance of services and lapse of substantial forfeiture risk; no such plan is established here. The rule concerns these stipulated non-interest salary awards and does not decide the separate minor-cash deemed-payment category, other unpresented payment categories or individual benefit accounts. The independent binding force of revenue rulings as a general class is unnecessary and remains undecided.

#### 2. Rates and annual bases must each be applied under their 1994 provisions

**Holding and operative rule:** These 1994 payments use each tax's 1994 statutory rate and its separate 1994 annual wage-base rule; an unchanged percentage or ceiling does not permit combining the payment with another year's wage account. The operative component applications are:

|---|---|---|

| Social Security annual base, section 3121(a)(1) | $60,600 | The earlier $42,000 and $43,800 bases do not carry forward with the salary losses. |

Sections 31.3301-2 and 31.3301-3(b), together with section 31.3306(b)(1)-1(a)(2), perform the corresponding FUTA work. The governing statute controls the current dollar ceiling when an older regulatory print states an obsolete amount.

The earlier wage maxima explain why the allocation matters for all but one recipient. - **Auer v. Robbins:** Applied to the responsible agency's fair, considered and textually permissible construction of its own regulations; no new general deference rule is adopted.
- **Thomas Jefferson University v. Shalala:** Applied to the agency's actual established ground, without supplying a fictional interpretation or overlooking the statutory boundary.
- **Christensen v. Harris County:** Preserved for separate treatment of advisory statutory views and regulatory interpretation, and for the prohibition on altering clear regulatory text by interpretation. A revenue ruling's publication alone does not settle its independent force.

The controlling rationale depends on a fair, established regulatory interpretation within statutory bounds, and does not adopt independent statutory compulsion as its sufficient ground. Benefit attribution remains governed by its own law. ### Atwater v. City of Lago Vista — 2001-04-24

Authority: [controlling holdings](../records/Atwater_v_City_of_Lago_Vista_merits_2001-04-24.md#holdings).

#### Additional custody for a known fine-only criminal offense

**Holding and operative rule:** Probable cause for an observed fine-only criminal misdemeanor does not, by itself, justify additional station-house transport and confinement. That added custody must reasonably serve an actual enforcement function, considering supported identification, appearance, safety, continuing-violation, evidence-protection or necessary-processing needs; the examples are nonexclusive, are not presumed, and require neither the least intrusive imaginable alternative nor citation whenever conceivable.

**Authority:** Part I of Ginsburg's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor and Breyer, supplies five direct joins to this rule, the rejection of categorical summary judgment and the common mandate. The rule extends Ricci's civil-licensing holding to the fine-only criminal enforcement presented here.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that authority to stop a person and the reasonable extent of custody are different questions. Ricci requires an actual enforcement connection for added station custody after a fine-only civil violation. The criminal label does not, by itself, justify the same added restraint where imprisonment is unavailable as punishment. The State's penalty choice reinforces the need to assess the scale of the seizure, without granting immunity from arrest.

Whren preserves an ordinary initial stop on objective probable cause while retaining independent limits on greater restraint. Knowles keeps search consequences separate from the antecedent lawful-arrest question. Robinson and Gustafson presuppose a qualifying lawful arrest; their incident-search rules cannot supply the missing justification for disputed custody. Wilson v. Arkansas treats relevant history as part of reasonableness without constitutionalizing every state authorization. A categorical historical breach-of-peace rule is unnecessary to decide this actual-function question.

Clear administration is a legitimate interest. But optional booking paperwork cannot conclusively establish the need for the custody that creates it. The inquiry permits practical judgments without hindsight perfection or proof that every alternative would fail. The lower court's probable-cause rule omitted that inquiry.

The record must include both the missing physical documents and the supplied license number, address and known local residence. The earlier warning followed recognition that the child was belted; it does not establish another seatbelt offense. The friend took the children. These facts cannot be replaced by assumptions of failed verification, flight risk, continuing danger or compulsory station processing. Neither berating nor a routine booking preference alone supplies an enforcement need. The correct rule defeats defendants' categorical entitlement to judgment, while the Justices differ on the ultimate application and the remaining record determinations.

#### The common judgment does not establish a controlling application conclusion

**Judgment and operative rule:** The Fifth Circuit judgment sustaining the categorical custody summary judgment is vacated, and the custody claim returns for proceedings under the actual-enforcement rule. The return permits genuinely supported disputes and preserved defenses, requires no new evidentiary hearing, invents no enforcement justification and reopens no established fact by speculation; it does not invalidate the uncontested seatbelt convictions or award damages.

**Authority and application limits:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer expressly support this vacatur and remand. Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer also conclude that the supported plaintiff-favorable account does not establish a sufficient custody function and would reverse on that ground. Stone-Zsela does not join that application: he requires actual verification and custody-need determinations under the rule. Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas find the arrest reasonable on their stated grounds and would affirm. There is no majority holding that this custody was constitutional or unconstitutional; the four-Justice application and the distinct concurrence cannot be combined through Marks into one.

Personal qualified immunity remains a separate conduct-date inquiry using March 1997 law; Ricci, Knowles, McArthur and this decision supply no retrospective notice. Municipal liability separately requires an attributable policy or custom, applicable fault, causation and injury. No final finding resolves those predicates. Other punishment classes are outside the presented question. Ordinary application of the rule and liability work under it are distinct from the Court's lack of a controlling answer on this custody's ultimate reasonableness.

**Precedent treatment:**

- **Ricci v. Village of Arlington Heights, May 4, 1998:** Its actual-enforcement requirement is extended from fine-only civil licensing custody to the added custody for a known fine-only criminal offense. Its rejection of categorical arrest immunity, least restrictive means and a universal citation duty remains. Its record-specific application fracture supplies no automatic application answer here.
- **Whren v. United States, June 10, 1996:** Its objective initial-stop rule is preserved and its independent limits on greater restraint applied. Hostility does not replace the objective enforcement inquiry.
- **Knowles v. Iowa, December 8, 1998:** Its distinction between actual lawful custody and search consequences is applied; it supplies no authority to justify an arrest by its ensuing search or booking effects.
- **United States v. Robinson; Gustafson v. Florida:** Their incident-search rules remain conditioned on a qualifying lawful arrest. Neither decides the contested antecedent custody authority here.
- **Wilson v. Arkansas, May 22, 1995:** Relevant history remains part of constitutional reasonableness, without turning state arrest authorization into a complete federal justification.
- **Illinois v. McArthur, February 20, 2001:** Its particularized residential-preservation holding remains at that scope. Concrete need and fitted execution inform the comparison; its home setting and approximate duration create no traffic-booking permission or safe harbor.
- **Wilson v. Layne; Wallis v. Spencer:** Conduct-date immunity and independent municipal responsibility remain distinct from constitutional merits. Neither supplies a present finding of immunity, City policy, fault or causation here.

**Resulting law:** Ricci's actual-enforcement rule now governs added station transport and confinement for known fine-only criminal offenses as well as its prior civil setting. Probable cause and local arrest permission alone do not establish reasonableness; genuinely supported enforcement needs and the scale of restraint must be assessed. Neither a universal citation requirement nor categorical arrest immunity follows.

The Court supplies no controlling application conclusion about this custody, immunity or municipal liability. The four-Justice conclusion that the accepted account is constitutionally insufficient remains noncontrolling; the actual-function rule and bounded corrective mandate control further proceedings. Ordinary fact, defense and remedial work must respect the supported record rather than manufacture new justification.

### Arkansas v. Sullivan — 2001-05-29

Authority: [controlling holdings and authority limits](../records/Arkansas_v_Sullivan_summary-merits_2001-05-29.md#holdings).

#### 1. Federal motive rules do not dispense with separate authority for each intrusion

The Court holds that an ordinary traffic stop supported by objective probable cause is not invalid solely because the officer also investigates another offense. State courts must follow this Court's controlling federal constitutional interpretation; greater protection must rest on an actual independent state-law ground, not a different reading of the same federal rule.

**Authority for all three holdings:** The per curiam opinion is joined by all nine participating Justices.

Whren supplies the ordinary stop rule while preserving independent limits on detention and search. Oregon v. Hass distinguishes a state's own protections from its duty to follow controlling federal law. The lower court's motive-only federal rationale and asserted power to disregard that hierarchy cannot stand. The reported federal basis, rather than an invented independent state ground, supports review; Quarles supplies the analogous otherwise-evading-review route. Correcting that legal error does not establish the validity of every subsequent act.

#### 2. Added fine-only custody requires a supported enforcement function

The Court applies Atwater: added station transport and confinement for a known fine-only criminal offense must reasonably serve a supported actual enforcement function. Probable cause alone does not justify that added restraint; identification, appearance, safety, a continuing violation, evidence protection and necessary processing are nonexclusive possible functions, not presumed facts.

Atwater extends Ricci's actual-enforcement inquiry to this criminal setting. It requires neither the least restrictive means nor a citation whenever one is conceivable. Actual offense classifications and supported arrest grounds matter where relevant. This record does not conclusively establish that every asserted offense was fine-only, that the hatchet supplied a valid weapons predicate, that identification failed, or that processing required the restraint. Those matters remain for the proper court on the actual preserved record. The Court neither finds the custody unlawful nor treats investigative interest as an independent source of arrest authority.

#### 3. Impoundment and inventory require their own lawful grounds

The Court holds that impoundment and a claimed inventory must independently satisfy their lawful authority, caretaking purpose and standardized-practice requirements. Investigative interest does not invalidate every otherwise lawful act, but a search that is an investigatory ruse fails the inventory exception itself; a policy label does not establish genuine compliance.

Bertine and Wells supply the inventory conditions that Whren preserves. Knowles keeps a search's justification distinct from the antecedent encounter and requires actual lawful custody for its search-incident rationale. Drugs found later cannot supply antecedent probable cause. The reported pretext finding does not necessarily decide every inventory condition under the correct rule. Remand must address actual preserved custody, impoundment, inventory and independent state-law grounds. The Court orders no new hearing, speculative reopening of settled facts, admission or suppression on a newly assumed ground, conviction, acquittal, or unsupported search-incident result.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Whren v. United States: applied to reject a motive-only objection to the ordinary probable-cause stop while preserving independent detention and inventory limits.
- Ricci v. Village of Arlington Heights and Atwater v. City of Lago Vista: applied to require a supported enforcement function for added fine-only custody; neither becomes a universal citation duty or a final finding about Sullivan's custody.
- Colorado v. Bertine and Florida v. Wells: preserved as independent authority, caretaking and standardized-practice limits on inventories.
- Knowles v. Iowa: applied to keep search authority distinct and to reject automatic search consequences from a lawful stop.
- Oregon v. Hass: applied to the state court's federal-law duty while preserving genuine independent state protections.
- New York v. Quarles: supplies the analogous jurisdictional treatment of a finally settled federal issue otherwise likely to evade review.

**Resulting law:** The ordinary stop, added custody, impoundment and inventory remain distinct constitutional inquiries. A federal pretext theory cannot replace them, and probable cause for the stop cannot supply their missing predicates. The remand must apply the actual-function custody rule and genuine inventory requirements.

### Illinois v. McArthur — 2001-02-20

**Souter concurs and joins the Court opinion in full.** Exigency and permissible scope depend on the risk at the time of the proposed action. In his assessment, while McArthur was inside with immediate access to the hidden drugs, the actual destruction risk could have justified prompt entry and search. Stone-Zsela would affirm suppression for the search resulting from the unsupported restraint, subject to any independently preserved exclusionary-rule issue actually left unresolved. **Stevens dissents.** He first would dismiss the writ as improvidently granted because these slight possession offenses make an unsuitable occasion to define the limits of restraints on residential possession. Even accepting probable cause, diligent warrant pursuit, accompanied entries and the comparatively limited restraint, he concludes that the substantial interest in private use of the home outweighs this modest preservation interest. His position concerns these facts; it neither denies that the offenses permit imprisonment nor categorically prohibits every misdemeanor preservation measure. ### Jefferson v. City of Tarrant, No. 96-957

Jurisdictional dismissal, December 9, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 4, 1997. The Alabama Supreme Court resolved an interlocutory question concerning compensatory recovery for asserted federal civil-rights violations causing death and returned the action for further proceedings. The Court considers finality under 28 U.S.C. §1257(a).

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Dismissed, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Stevens.

The writ is dismissed for want of jurisdiction because the state-court judgment is not final for this review. The Alabama interlocutory ruling remains in its existing posture; its federal merits are not affirmed. No vacatur, new trial, merits remand, liability finding or damages award is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Breyer delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Justice Stevens dissents from jurisdictional dismissal and would exercise jurisdiction; he supplies no decision on the federal merits.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**This interlocutory ruling does not satisfy §1257's finality requirement.** Breyer's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, controls this jurisdictional application.

Section 1257 requires a final state-court judgment. Cox permits review of a finally resolved federal issue in bounded circumstances: proceedings are practically concluded; the issue necessarily survives every further outcome; effective later review is unavailable whatever the outcome; or reversal would preclude the relevant litigation and postponement seriously threaten federal policy. State certification and an issue's importance do not themselves establish finality.

None supports review here. Unlike Richards, the same petitioners' claims and disputed liability and causation remain for substantive adjudication. Unlike Sullivan's completed conviction issue, this damages question need not survive every lawful outcome. Unlike Labron/Kilgore, effective later correction is available following an appropriate adverse final judgment. Unlike Casarotto's threatened arbitration forum, reversal would permit damages litigation rather than protect against the relevant litigation itself.

Ritchie does not require absolute legal impossibility of a later appeal. Note 7 recognized a possible route but found renewed presentation served no useful finality purpose in its unusual setting. Disclosure injury, prosecution outcomes, completed trial and appellate review, and an inadequate contempt alternative informed that result; confidentiality alone was not dispositive. Those considerations create no universal checklist. Here later review can effectively correct the damages restriction, and resolving disputed facts and remaining claims serves a substantive purpose. Waiting entails no established comparable destruction of the federal interest. Ritchie supplies no freestanding exception for every issue settled under state law of the case.

The City's delay in objecting cannot enlarge statutory jurisdiction. The Court decides neither whether §§1983 and 1988 permit Alabama's restriction nor discriminatory conduct, causation, municipal liability, survivorship or damages. Dismissal does not approve the restriction or guarantee later jurisdiction.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Cox supplies four practical-finality routes with their actual predicates.
- Ritchie, including note 7, is preserved and distinguished on the consequences of waiting and the substantive purpose of remaining proceedings; a conceivable later appeal alone is not dispositive.
- Richards differs because the reviewed parties' controversy here is not conclusively terminated or preordained.
- Sullivan differs because this question need not survive every subsequent outcome.

### Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, Inc., No. 90-1029

Merits decision, June 8, 1992

Argued December 10, 1991; decided June 8, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1) to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit. In its May 1, 1990 decision, [903 F.2d 612](https://static.case.law/f2d/903/cases/0612-01.json), Wiggins, joined by Chambers, reversed summary judgment on the preserved Sherman Act §§ 1 and 2 claims; Wallace dissented. The Northern District of California had entered summary judgment for Kodak on April 18, 1988, after limited discovery, rejecting a tie and a duty to deal. The [official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep504/usrep504451/usrep504451.pdf) gives the argument and decision dates. All nine Justices participated at argument and decision; Thomas took his seat before argument.

Eighteen independent service organizations allege that restrictions on Kodak-compatible replacement parts exclude them from service. The questions concern whether parts and service are distinct products and whether their sales are conditioned on one another; whether competition in equipment categorically precludes power in its parts or service markets; and whether power, exclusion, and asserted business reasons present genuine factual disputes. Kodak seeks restoration of summary judgment. Respondents seek continued proceedings, not a present damages verdict. The Ninth Circuit did not reach a rule-of-reason theory that respondents had not raised in opposition to summary judgment. Review is limited to the preserved tying and § 2 claims; an independent claim of conspiracy with original equipment manufacturers is not adjudicated.

Respondents offer evidence of demand for independent service, exceptions allowing customers to service their own machines, restrictions on alternative parts supplies, information costs in predicting lifetime service expense, and substantial costs of replacing installed equipment. Their evidence supports estimates of nearly complete control of compatible parts and eighty to ninety-five percent of Kodak-equipment service. [Prices, effects, and the asserted policy changes remain disputed](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0064). These materials and reasonable inferences permit review of summary judgment; they do not establish actual markets, monopoly power, deception, or liability.

The Court affirms the Ninth Circuit's reversal of summary judgment, 6–3, on each of two components: the preserved § 1 tying claim, and the preserved § 2 monopolization and attempted-monopolization claims. Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter support affirmance on both. O'Connor, Scalia, and Thomas would reverse on both and restore summary judgment. Blackmun delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter. Scalia dissents, joined in full by O'Connor and Thomas. The six Justices adopt all four controlling holdings; there are no partial joins, judgment-only votes, or separate concurrences.

For a Sherman Act § 1 tying claim, functionally related products are distinct when sufficient separate consumer demand makes separate provision efficient. Evidence that Kodak parts and service meet that test, and that Kodak conditions parts sales on buying its service or forgoing independent service, creates genuine disputes about distinct products and conditioning; respondents may proceed but must prove all remaining tying elements. Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with six votes, including the author. The commerce volume is not disputed here; no liability judgment follows.

The Court holds that practical consumer demand, rather than a product’s functional relationship to another product, determines whether the two are distinct for tying purposes. Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde supplies that demand-based inquiry and the requirements of tying power and a substantial amount of tied commerce. The record shows that independent organizations have supplied service separately, that customers have preferred independent service, and that some customers service their own equipment. Those materials permit a finding that separate provision is efficient. Kodak’s restrictions on parts sales to independent organizations and on customers’ use of independent service also permit a finding of conditioning. The fact that parts are installed during service does not conclusively combine both into one product. Respondents still must establish the necessary market power and other elements; the Court holds only that the district court could not end this claim for want of separate products or an arguable tie.

Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde, 466 U.S. 2 (1984): applied for separate demand, conditioning, tying power, and tied commerce; functional integration alone does not merge products. Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317 (1986): applied to preserve respondents’ burden of producing evidence on the elements challenged at summary judgment.

Competition in an equipment market does not as a matter of law preclude market power in its parts or service markets. Under Rule 56, a party cannot obtain summary judgment solely through an economic prediction contradicted by evidence permitting a rational inference of power, including material information and switching costs and actual price or exclusion effects; unsupported speculation remains insufficient. Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with six votes. The holding rejects a conclusive defense, not the relevance of equipment competition.

The Court holds that Kodak’s proposed equipment-market constraint must be tested against the record. Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp. requires a plausible inference from evidence; it does not authorize a court to replace disputed facts with an economic model. Here the alleged conduct can produce immediate aftermarket gains, unlike the prolonged and implausible loss-producing conspiracy examined there. Evidence of costly equipment replacement and difficult lifetime service-price comparisons permits a finding that some installed customers cannot readily escape unfavorable terms. Evidence of price and exclusion effects supports testing that account at trial. Kodak may show that informed new purchasers and equipment rivalry adequately discipline its policies, but that conclusion is not inevitable as a matter of law. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc. preserves the factfinder’s role when a genuine dispute could affect the result. The Court neither presumes power from imperfect information nor treats every durable-product seller as a monopolist.

Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574 (1986): applied as a rational-inference requirement and distinguished on the alleged economic mechanism; it creates no categorical equipment-market defense. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242 (1986): applied to identify genuine material disputes; evidence and reasonable inferences receive the ordinary Rule 56 treatment. Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde, 466 U.S. 2 (1984): preserved; respondents must prove actual tying power despite rejection of a conclusive equipment-competition defense.

A relevant product market may consist of one brand’s parts or service when the actual commercial alternatives and competitive constraints support that definition; brand identity alone neither establishes nor defeats such a market. Evidence of Kodak-specific compatibility, a very high parts share, a high service share, and entry and switching barriers creates a genuine dispute about monopoly power sufficient to resist summary judgment on § 2. Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with six votes. The record permits a market and power finding; it does not conclusively establish either.

The Court holds that market boundaries turn on the alternatives buyers can actually use. United States v. Grinnell Corp. requires monopoly power in a relevant market, and United States v. E. I. du Pont de Nemours & Co. directs attention to substitution and competitive restraint. Owners needing compatible parts cannot necessarily substitute another brand’s parts, and changing equipment may cost substantially more than the aftermarket service at issue. Respondents also present evidence of Kodak’s dominant parts position, high service share, and obstacles to independent supply. Together these materials permit an inference of power to control prices or exclude competition. Kodak’s equipment competitors remain relevant because they may constrain lifetime prices; the Court does not exclude that evidence. But their existence does not settle the market question. The result establishes neither a single-brand rule for all manufacturers nor a monopoly presumption from a patent, a proprietary design, or numerical share considered apart from actual constraints.

United States v. Grinnell Corp., 384 U.S. 563 (1966): applied to require monopoly power in a relevant market, distinct from exclusionary conduct. United States v. E. I. du Pont de Nemours & Co., 351 U.S. 377 (1956): applied to practical substitution and competitive constraints; no categorical prohibition on a one-brand market follows.

Monopolization under § 2 requires monopoly power plus willful acquisition or maintenance of that power, distinguished from success through a superior product, business acumen, or accident. Evidence of parts-access restrictions that may exclude service rivals creates a genuine issue of unlawful maintenance, and Kodak’s asserted quality, inventory, and free-riding explanations do not conclusively eliminate it on this record; no general duty to supply rivals or final finding of pretext is imposed. Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter, controls with six votes. Attempted monopolization remains a distinct claim with its own intent and dangerous-probability requirements, not a consequence automatically established by this holding.

The Court holds that the reasons and competitive effect of Kodak’s restrictions cannot be settled on this summary-judgment record. Grinnell distinguishes unlawful maintenance of monopoly power from lawful commercial success. Aspen Skiing Co. v. Aspen Highlands Skiing Corp. makes the character of challenged exclusion and its business justification material without imposing a general obligation to help competitors. Respondents’ evidence permits a finding that restricting access to necessary parts protects Kodak’s service position. The asserted quality rationale must be considered with the continued self-service exception and evidence about independent service. Inventory explanations must be tested against evidence that equipment failures and parts needs do not change with the provider, while supplier restrictions may impede alternative sources. Calling independent service free riding because the providers did not enter the equipment and parts businesses does not conclusively justify exclusion. Kodak may prove legitimate reasons and defeat liability. The Court awards no damages, orders no compulsory supply, and leaves ultimate liability and injury for further proceedings.

United States v. Grinnell Corp., 384 U.S. 563 (1966): applied to distinguish willful monopoly maintenance from lawful success. Aspen Skiing Co. v. Aspen Highlands Skiing Corp., 472 U.S. 585 (1985): applied to the challenged conduct and offered business reasons; no universal obligation to deal is created. Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc., 429 U.S. 477 (1977): preserved; injury to a competitor alone does not establish antitrust injury or entitlement to damages.

Effective June 8, 1992, equipment competition remains relevant evidence but cannot categorically bar a sufficiently supported claim concerning the parts or service markets. The Court applies existing tying and monopolization law with ordinary Rule 56 burdens. It creates no automatic monopoly from a patent or brand, universal duty to deal, or present right to recovery. Power and exclusion remain separate elements, and success through superior products remains lawful. Monopolization and attempted monopolization remain distinct. No prior decision is overruled, and the dissent does not change current law. Holmes v. Securities Investor Protection Corp., decided March 24, 1992, retains its holdings on directness under the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act and participation under the Securities Investor Protection Act; those holdings neither decide these disputed Sherman Act elements nor establish injury here.

Scalia, joined by O'Connor and Thomas, would reverse and restore summary judgment on both preserved claims. They regard ordinary dependence on a brand's replacement parts after buying durable equipment as derivative power that equipment competition generally disciplines. In their view, the asserted information and switching costs do not warrant extending the category of tying treated as unlawful without a rule-of-reason inquiry, or treating every brand-specific parts or service market as an antitrust monopoly, absent the necessary primary-market power. They consider the Court's approach liable to burden ordinary integration, distribution choices, and proprietary component sales without the competitive power the Sherman Act requires. Their disagreement concerns the legal significance of the asserted dependence and the sufficiency of the evidence; they do not find respondents entitled to final relief. The separate rule-of-reason route was not preserved below. Their position does not displace the Court's inquiry into the evidence or create an immunity for manufacturers.

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The Ninth Circuit's reversal of summary judgment stands, and ordinary mandate implementation permits the properly preserved and supported claims to proceed below. Respondents must prove each claim's elements, including the distinct intent and dangerous-probability requirements of attempted monopolization and the requirements for any recovery. Ultimate markets, power, unlawful conduct, pretext, antitrust injury, causation, damages, and remedial design remain for further proceedings. The Court awards no damages, enters no final injunction, orders no compulsory parts supply, and makes no ultimate finding of liability. It retains no proceeding and imposes no nonroutine follow-on condition; later trial findings and relief require their own lawful proceedings.
