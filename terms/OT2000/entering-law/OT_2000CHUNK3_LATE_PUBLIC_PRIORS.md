# Published prior positions relevant to the four late matters

Verbatim topology and separate-writing excerpts from prior public renders. The opening Holdings slice supplies controlling law. These earlier positions are not votes on the pending matters.

| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Souter | No other Justice | Affirms all six judgments through a fuller institutional classification inquiry | Joins Part I; requires duties, jurisdiction, tenure and removal analysis in addition to a supervisory superior |

The statutory holding is unanimous. The combined-controls classification rationale has eight direct votes; Souter's distinct sufficient rationale is not an additional join in it.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Souter, concurring in part and in the judgment:** Souter joins the statutory authority holding and affirms every petitioner's judgment. He accepts a supervisory superior as necessary here but does not regard administration, removal and higher review alone as a sufficient classification inquiry. Under *Morrison*, the court must also examine the office's duties, jurisdiction, tenure and removal, with appropriate respect for the political branches' judgment in this close classification. The judges perform substantial, independent Article 66 review, but their jurisdiction and assignment-based tenure, the available removal and actual superior relationship, taken together, establish inferior status. His *Weiss* and *Ryder* positions preserve both constitutional appointment and meaningful adjudication; neither makes Article 67 a full factual rehearing or creates a blanket military exception. This fuller ground supports the same result without joining the Court's sufficient combined-controls rationale.

## Simmons v. South Carolina

Source: terms/OT1993/output/OT_1993CHUNK7.md

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Exact scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg | Reverse and remand | Seven adopt the narrow due-process rebuttal rule, its application and common reversal/remand. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Ginsburg | Require a new penalty proceeding if death is pursued | Five find the error not harmless on this record under Chapman and adopt the further-hearing direction. |
| Additional opinion, Part III | Souter | Stevens | Additional sufficient ground for reversal | Two adopt the Eighth Amendment right to a requested judicial explanation of a sentencing term reasonably likely to be misunderstood; not an opinion of the Court. |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins Parts I and II | Preserves the additional requirement of an authoritative answer to an express jury question concerning the actual legal alternative, within the future-danger setting addressed here. |
| Concurrence | Blackmun | No other Justice | Joins Parts I and II and accepts minimum case-specific relief | Independently objects to capital punishment as presently administered and would require lawful noncapital relief. |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment of reversal | O'Connor | Kennedy | Joins Part I; declines the additional direction in Part II | Narrow due-process ground; general reversal/remand without deciding the further harmlessness question. No join in Part III. |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would affirm | Finds no due-process violation and rejects the additional Eighth Amendment instruction ground; no conditional harmlessness vote. |

Parts I and II have separate direct majorities. Part III has two votes. No Marks synthesis supplies any holding or expands any join.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Part III of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Justice Stevens, supplies an independent Eighth Amendment ground. A capital defendant who requests an explanation of a legal term describing the sentencing alternatives is entitled to a judicial explanation when there is a reasonable likelihood a juror will misunderstand that term. They rely on Woodson's heightened reliability, Gregg's guided sentencing, Beck's concern with an intelligible capital choice and Boyde's reasonable-likelihood inquiry, together with Graham, Johnson v. Texas and Romano's distinct protections for an effective, responsible choice. The fixed legal meaning of life imprisonment and the jury's direct inquiry satisfy that rule here. This ground does not depend on the State's arguing future danger. Counsel's advocacy does not generally carry the authority of a judicial statement of sentencing law, so permission for defense argument alone does not satisfy their separate instruction principle. The trigger is a request plus reasonable likelihood of misunderstanding, not a duty to catalogue every possible release contingency at every capital trial. The ground has two votes and is not controlling.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the Court's due-process and remedy holdings and separately explains the consequence of an express jury question. In the future-danger setting presented here, the trial court should establish the actual state-law alternative and ensure an accurate explanation, either by an accepted defense presentation or an instruction. Once the jury directly asks about that legal alternative, it warrants an authoritative answer from the court. An ordinary-meaning direction is inadequate when ordinary understanding may include parole. No prescribed script or promise against commutation or future legal change follows. The State may still establish danger inside prison. This additional judicial-answer requirement is his own due-process formulation; he does not join Souter and Stevens's broader Eighth Amendment rule for cases without a State-raised future-danger issue. His join in the five-Justice remedy rests on this record's failure of constitutional harmlessness, not automatic reversal for every omission.

Justice Blackmun joins the case-specific holdings and accepts a new penalty hearing as minimum relief if death is pursued. He separately retains his objection that capital punishment as presently administered fails to reconcile consistent selection, individualized fairness and reliable punishment. Gregg and Jurek's guided-sentencing framework and Lockett and Eddings's individualized consideration seek those ends; his joins in Graham and Johnson v. Texas recognize enforceable mitigation safeguards, but he regards those safeguards as insufficient to resolve the broader selection problem. He would require lawful noncapital relief, without selecting a particular sentence or ordering release. He does not claim that removing this error resolves his independent objection or make a new finding about Simmons's innocence or discriminatory treatment. The broader position is his alone and does not displace the Court's limited rule or authorize noncapital relief in every case.

Justice O'Connor, joined by Justice Kennedy, joins Part I and the judgment reversing and remanding. Their due-process rule requires a State-raised future-danger issue and life without parole as the only noncapital alternative. Accurate defense explanation or adequate instruction can satisfy it; the Constitution does not require a particular natural-life formula denying the possibility of clemency. Outside that State-raised-danger setting, their analysis would leave the State the choice whether to inform the jury about parole, even when the alternative is life without parole. They do not join the independent Eighth Amendment instruction rule. They also decline Part II's conclusive harmlessness ruling and additional new-hearing command, leaving the ordinary constitutional remedial issue for appropriate further proceedings. They neither find this error harmless nor characterize it as structural, mandate automatic life imprisonment or prescribe a sequence for that further adjudication.

Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Thomas, would affirm. The prosecutor did not expressly assert that Simmons could be paroled, the defense presented prison-adaptation evidence, and the trial court directed jurors away from release speculation. Gardner's undisclosed adverse information and Skipper's excluded prison-conduct evidence therefore do not, in their view, establish fundamental unfairness here. Ramos supports substantial state authority over release-related information. They reject the proposed Eighth Amendment extension because legal parole information is distinct from the character, record and offense evidence protected by individualized sentencing. They distinguish Romano's responsibility holding concerning another jury's death judgment rather than treating it as a general evidentiary rule. They accept the actual statutory ineligibility; their disagreement concerns the federal disclosure duty. A materially misleading State assertion about release could present a different due-process issue. Their no-error conclusion supplies no alternative conclusion about harmlessness or the proper remedy if error were found.

## Ramdass v. Angelone

Source: terms/OT1999/output/OT_1999CHUNK8.md

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer throughout; O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas in Part I only | Five-Justice vacatur/remand; unanimous preservation of the factual-ground rejection | I: state-law definition, statutory applicability and cutoff, common statutory rules, and legal rather than factual classification; II: absence of entry is not conclusive; III: independent statutory and retroactivity inquiries; IV: bounded mandate and reserved effect inquiry |
| Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | Kennedy | Scalia and Thomas throughout; O'Connor in Part II only | Joins Court Part I; would affirm denial of the parole-information claim | I: no constitutional error at the jury stage; II: independently reasonable state adjudication under section 2254(d)(1) |
| Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | O'Connor | No other Justice | Joins Court Part I and Kennedy Part II; would affirm | Preserves the qualification for a legally foreordained ministerial entry and withholds the categorical no-error ground |

Part I of the Court's opinion has nine direct supporters. Parts II–IV have five direct supporters. No fractured-decision inference supplies any holding. The Court adopts neither four-Justice general allocation of the statutory clauses discussed in Williams v. Taylor (Terry Williams).

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Kennedy, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justices Scalia and Thomas throughout and by Justice O'Connor in Part II.** All four join the Court's Part I. Kennedy's Part I would also reject the constitutional claim: Simmons concerns the legal alternative presented when the jury chooses punishment, and the unentered verdict did not yet make ordinary parole unavailable under Virginia law. A later judgment differs from clemency or legislation changing an existing disability. The State could rely on the robbery conduct without representing that a third judgment existed. Entry before capital judicial sentencing does not retrospectively alter the jury-stage alternative; no distinct claim concerning the later judicial proceeding is decided.

Part II independently would deny relief under section 2254(d)(1). Simmons's actual-ineligibility premise, Gardner's adverse-information setting, Skipper's future-danger rebuttal and Ramos's state-law latitude permitted Virginia's distinction under the holdings available in November 1994. The remaining power to set aside the verdict differentiates this case from legally inevitable entry. This is an objective comparison, not a poll of judges or a requirement of identical facts. The dissent needs neither a Teague bar nor a causal-effect finding. It accepts Virginia's definition and rejects the District Court's factual characterization without finding an actual pending defect.

**Justice O'Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** She joins the Court's Part I and Kennedy's statutory Part II but not his categorical no-error ground. If state law had left only a legally foreordained ministerial entry, that formality could not defeat the protected function. High likelihood, eventual entry and absence of proof of a particular pending defect are not the legal inevitability that qualification requires. Rule 3A:15's surviving judicial authority supports the objective reasonableness of the state adjudication here. She would affirm on that independent statutory basis, without finding a violation, joining the remand or deciding every possible direct-review formulation.

## Department of Defense v. Federal Labor Relations Authority

Source: terms/OT1993/output/OT_1993CHUNK2.md

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Both reversals | Entire precedent-based statutory explanation and record-specific Exemption 6 balance |
| Concurrence | Ginsburg | No other Justice | Both reversals and full join in the Court's opinion | Preserved criticism of the original textual rationale and labor-policy consequences; adherence to statutory precedent |

Both holdings have nine supporters. No judgment-only join or Marks issue arises.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Ginsburg concurs and joins the Court's opinion in full. She accepts the statutory chain and application of retained Reporters Committee precedent but continues to doubt that its restricted public-interest measure was inevitable from FOIA's original words. Congress expressly valued collective bargaining and required representation of members and nonmembers; depriving federal unions of information ordinarily available to private-sector representatives creates a serious concern. Modest address privacy is not equivalent to the compiled criminal histories protected in Reporters Committee, whose result she does not question. Tax Analysts illustrates FOIA's general disclosure entitlement; a requester ordinarily need not first demonstrate a favored purpose. She regards recognition of a congressionally selected representational interest as distinguishable from choosing among requesters according to perceived worthiness. Nevertheless, statutory precedent controls the present inquiry, and her full join rests on the Court's confined explanation rather than abandonment of that criticism. Legislative correction remains possible. She resolves no routine-use issue and promises no alternative access; her additional explanation is noncontrolling.

## Mitchell v. United States

Source: terms/OT1998/output/OT_1998CHUNK6.md

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Exact scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Retained privilege and the limited effect of the plea and colloquy |
| Opinion of the Court, Parts II–III | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | No adverse inference concerning crime facts; prejudice and bounded sentencing relief |
| Concurrence in Part I and dissent from Parts II–III and the judgment | Scalia | O'Connor, Thomas | Would permit this inference and affirm without adopting waiver |
| Separate dissent | Thomas | No other Justice | Additional willingness to reconsider Griffin and Carter in an appropriate case; no present overruling |

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Scalia, joined by O'Connor and Thomas.** The three join Part I because the plea did not waive privilege on reserved quantity, but distinguish the right to refuse compelled testimony from an ordinary evidentiary inference drawn alongside credited evidence. They accept Griffin and Carter at their established guilt-phase scope and decline the sentencing extension. Baxter and Woodard illustrate the distinction between inference, compelled answers and an automatic penalty for invocation; sentencing's different evidentiary rules, recognized in McMillan, remain relevant. They dispute the majority's separation of offense circumstances from other sentencing information. Their position permits neither compulsory testimony, treating the plea as an admission of reserved quantity, an automatic privilege penalty nor proof by silence alone. Scalia retains his broader Jones penalty-range position without deciding a new quantity-classification claim here. They find no error and therefore do not reach harmlessness as a ground for affirmance. Conditional acceptance of lawful relief under the Court's contrary rule adds no vote for reversal.

**Thomas.** Thomas joins Scalia's opinion but separately questions the constitutional foundations of Griffin and Carter: an ordinary inference does not itself compel testimony, and a statutory no-comment policy does not necessarily establish a constitutional prohibition. He would consider reconsidering those decisions in an appropriate case. He casts no vote to overrule them here, and no other Justice joins this prospective recommendation. Scalia instead preserves their established operation.

## United States v. Hubbell

Source: terms/OT1999/output/OT_1999CHUNK7.md

#### Separate Writings

**Thomas, joined by Scalia, concurring.** They join Fisher's present act-of-production doctrine, the Court's application and dismissal consequence. They also reserve willingness in a proper future case to reconsider whether the original meaning of witness protected compelled furnishing of incriminating evidence beyond communicative acts. Founding-era usage, common-law document-production practice, the relationship to compulsory process, Boyd and Fisher inform that concern. They adopt no broader privilege today and do not equate limits on compelled production with limits on searches or seizures. The reservation is noncontrolling and supplies no independent basis for enlarging this judgment.
