## Event

Brewer v. West Irondequoit Central School District; review of 212 F.3d 738 (2d Cir. 2000); no separate Supreme Court docket supplied. Merits decision, January 9, 2001, on review of the Second Circuit's May 11, 2000 judgment vacating a mandatory preliminary-admission injunction. The question is whether equal protection permits a voluntary public-school transfer program to restrict eligibility by race to reduce racial isolation without a de jure segregation finding. No separate argument date is supplied. Haak's written acceptance for transfer from Rochester was withdrawn after officials verified that she was white.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

## Public Action

The Court affirms in part, vacates in part, and remands.

## Holdings

### I. The actual exclusion requires substantial justification

**Holding and operative rule:** A public-school program that changes a child's transfer opportunity because of race must establish an important lawful objective and a substantial, evidence-supported relationship between that objective and the classification's coverage, operation and actual individual burdens. It must give a justified response to concrete, practicable alternatives that would comparably advance the objective with materially less racial disadvantage; unsupported assertion, administrative convenience alone, demographic imbalance or a beneficial label does not discharge that burden.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice O'Connor's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, has seven direct joins. Part III has the same coalition. Scalia and Thomas accept the governing standard's binding force but reject this application and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Adarand supplies the common justification standard for federal, state and local racial classifications. Eisenberg applies it to school transfers and requires justification of the individual denial. Haak received written acceptance that was withdrawn after officials verified that she was white. Her lost opportunity is an actual racial burden, unlike Sinkfield's neighboring-district injury theory. The program must explain how its directional transfer restriction serves the educational conditions it identifies and why that child's exclusion has the required relationship to the objective. Brown's prohibition of state-imposed racial caste remains intact.

The inquiry does not demand a commissioned study, fixed ratio, statistical demonstration or proof that every conceivable substitute fails. Testimony, program records, historical experience, admissions, comparisons, observed operation and reasonable inferences may support the relationship. Concrete alternatives must receive the consideration the governing standard requires; an abstract preference for race-neutrality cannot replace that inquiry. Racial hierarchy, stereotypes, hostility, political favoritism and allocation for its own sake remain impermissible.

### II. Supported voluntary integration is a permissible educational objective

**Holding and operative rule:** Supported educational benefits from meaningful integration and avoidance of serious racial isolation in public elementary and secondary schools can constitute an important lawful objective without a prior finding of de jure segregation. That permission neither requires racial preferences nor validates a numerical target or the particular transfer restriction: the government must still prove its educational claim, substantial fit and justified individual burdens.

**Authority:** Part II of O'Connor's opinion has six direct joins: O'Connor, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Kennedy does not join this affirmative objective holding; his acceptance of Eisenberg's binding force is not an additional affirmative vote.

**Controlling explanation:** Eisenberg recognizes the educational objective and rejects a categorical decree prerequisite. Meaningful educational contact and access can matter independently of an existing judicial dismantlement duty. Absence of a decree establishes neither such a duty nor invalidity. The program's interdistrict design may operate differently from a restriction based solely on county percentages. Its longevity, limited scale and changing demographics are relevant evidence, but do not conclusively establish either constitutional adequacy or the absence of every educational benefit. A potentially lawful objective likewise cannot justify Haak's exclusion without evidence about the program's actual operation. The Constitution permits the supported objective within lawful governmental authority; it supplies no presumption validating this program.

### III. Correct the preliminary-relief inquiry without ordering placement

**Holding and operative rule:** The vacatur of the mandatory preliminary-admission injunction is affirmed, the appellate judgment is vacated insofar as its substantive directions employ the displaced constitutional standard, and the matter is remanded for prompt application of the common standard and ordinary equitable requirements. Existing supported findings and concessions retain their scope; further evidence or proceedings require an identified unresolved material issue, and any placement order requires current enrollment, continuing injury, educational-disruption and other material equitable facts.

**Authority:** Part III carries the seven-Justice coalition identified for Part I; Scalia and Thomas oppose this disposition because they would treat the preliminary merits prerequisite as already established against the exclusion.

**Controlling explanation:** Adarand and Eisenberg require correction of a displaced legal framework without automatically reopening proof or awarding individual relief. The preliminary record does not establish the program's complete justification, but neither does it conclusively disprove its claimed interdistrict educational benefits. State funding requires a qualifying program designed to reduce racial isolation and an annual demonstration. The appellate account of inevitable funding loss is not an express statutory prohibition on every outward transfer by a white city pupil. That distinction bears on the asserted necessity of the categorical eligibility line.

The mandatory-injunction merits requirement and other equitable prerequisites remain independent. The existing irreparable-harm determination does not alone establish entitlement to a mandatory seat order. The earlier conditional protection lasted only through 1999–2000 and only if Haak had begun attending; it creates no January 2001 placement right. The original mandatory injunction is not reinstated, and this Court orders neither admission nor removal. Final program findings and current interim relief are ordinary application and remedial work under the stated rules. Unappealed contract and estoppel grounds, and other claims outside the equal-protection question granted, are not decided.

## Precedent Treatment

- Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña: applies its common substantial-justification standard and bounded-remand rule unchanged; no displaced strict-scrutiny formulation is restored.
- Eisenberg v. Montgomery County Public Schools: applies its school-transfer standard, supported K–12 educational objective and independent equitable-remedy limits to this interdistrict program; its objective holding is reaffirmed by six Justices.
- Brown v. Board of Education: retains its prohibition of state-imposed racial caste; voluntary integration is not a license for racial hierarchy.
- Engineering Contractors Association of South Florida v. Metropolitan Dade County: applies the requirement of a justified response to concrete practicable alternatives without requiring rejection of every conceivable substitute.
- Sinkfield v. Kelley: distinguishes a claim lacking personal exposure from the withdrawal of this child's actual educational opportunity; the personal-injury requirement remains in force.

## Law After Decision

The common standard governs voluntary interdistrict transfer restrictions as it governs other governmental racial classifications. Supported integration may justify the educational objective without a decree, but neither the label nor numerical imbalance proves the restriction's fit. Lower courts must assess actual operation and individual burdens, preserve supported findings, and establish current equitable conditions before ordering placement. The program is neither finally sustained nor finally invalidated by this preliminary-relief decision.

His concurrence accepts Eisenberg as binding, including that absence of a decree cannot alone settle justification, but reserves his own affirmative adoption of the educational-benefit proposition. Thomas dissents, joined by Scalia. Both retain their stricter individual-equality objection to racial exclusion and their opposition to the affirmative K–12 justification. Independently, they conclude that the race-only revocation lacks demonstrated justification under binding Adarand. A possible integration benefit and an appellate characterization of funding consequences cannot prove the need for this child's categorical exclusion. Their Eisenberg position treats that present failure as sufficient to establish the preliminary merits prerequisite, rather than merely an occasion for renewed justification. They would remand for the remaining current equitable determinations. The expired conditional school-year protection supplies no present enrollment fact or automatic seat entitlement; they order no program-wide invalidation and decide no damages. Thomas's recognition of actual dismantlement duties when proved remains intact.

## Event

Fiore v. White, No. 98-942. Merits decision, January 9, 2001, on writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit, 149 F.3d 221. The Court retained jurisdiction when it certified the temporal state-law question on November 30, 1999. Pennsylvania's Supreme Court accepted it December 20, 1999, received submission March 14, 2000, and answered August 21, 2000. This return concerns whether due process permits the two no-permit convictions when the permit's absence was always an element and the Commonwealth concedes Fiore possessed the permit. The Third Circuit had reversed the district habeas grant on a new-state-law premise. No separate merits argument date is supplied.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Third Circuit and remands for proceedings consistent with the existing-element ruling and the stated habeas conditions.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Third Circuit must apply the existing-element constitutional ruling and resolve any properly preserved, undecided statutory prerequisite. If relief is available, both invalid convictions and their attributable sentence portions must be removed. Independent convictions and lawful custody remain; no unconditional aggregate release is ordered.

## Holdings

### I. The existing missing element defeats both challenged convictions

**Holding and operative rule:** A State may not sustain a conviction when its authoritative account of the law in force at the relevant time identifies a necessary element and the State concedes it produced no evidence of that element. Pennsylvania's clarification that actual absence of the required permit was always an element of the owner/operator offense therefore establishes a due-process violation in both of Fiore's challenged no-permit convictions; deviation from a permit's terms cannot substitute for its absence.

**Authority:** The per curiam opinion is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt both holdings and the bounded reversal and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** In re Winship requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every element. Jackson v. Virginia enforces that protection against a conviction unsupported on a necessary element. State law identifies the offense; the federal Constitution requires its elements to be proved. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court's August 21, 2000 answer establishes that Commonwealth v. Scarpone clarified the statute's existing meaning, including its meaning when Fiore's convictions became final. The Commonwealth does not dispute that Fiore possessed a permit. The asserted departure from its conditions thus cannot establish the different no-permit element.

The Third Circuit's premise that a new state rule was unavailable on collateral review cannot survive the authoritative answer. The Court decides no general obligation to apply genuinely new state constructions retroactively: there is no new construction here. Nor can the seriousness of other environmental misconduct supply the missing element. The distinct authorization, transportation-license and permit-compliance provisions do not become substitute offenses in this dispute. The ruling covers the no-permit conviction from each trial, not the other convictions among Fiore's sixty convictions.

### II. The constitutional ruling and the conditions for the writ remain distinct

**Holding and operative rule:** The Third Circuit's rejection of the constitutional claim is reversed and the case remanded to resolve any properly preserved, actually undecided statutory prerequisite to habeas relief under the applicable post-April 24, 1996 regime. If those prerequisites permit relief, the habeas order must remove both invalid no-permit convictions and end custody attributable to them, preserving independent convictions and lawful custody.

**Authority:** The same unanimous per curiam opinion directly adopts this remand and the following statutory conditions; the Court makes no finding that an unresolved gate is satisfied or violated.

**Controlling explanation:** Lindh v. Murphy distinguishes applications filed before enactment from later applications; Fiore's later filing is not exempt from the applicable amendments. Under section 2254(b), relief requires exhaustion, absence of an available state corrective process, or circumstances making that process ineffective to protect the applicant's rights. Merits denial despite nonexhaustion is permitted, not required; the State waives exhaustion only through counsel's express waiver. The appellate court expressly left exhaustion and state merits adjudication undecided.

If the relevant claim was actually adjudicated on its merits in state proceedings, section 2254(d) requires the statutory inquiry. Williams v. Taylor preserves the independent contrary-to and unreasonable-application clauses, the objective legal standard and the restriction to this Court's holdings at the actual state merits decision. Winship and Jackson are preexisting rules, not rules originating in today's decision. The separate statutory factual-review provisions retain their own triggers and force. A finding of constitutional error cannot silently resolve those questions; conversely, remand supplies no permission to reopen the conceded permit fact, the state court's authoritative answer or the decided constitutional issue. The court below must resolve a real preserved prerequisite and implement the resulting conviction-specific relief, rather than infer an unconditional all-count writ.

An authoritative clarification of an existing criminal element exposes a federal due-process violation when the State concededly proved no such element. This rule does not depend on retroactive application of genuinely new state law. In this case both owner/operator no-permit convictions are constitutionally invalid. The independent habeas prerequisites remain for actual application on remand; relief, if available under those prerequisites, reaches the two convictions and their attributable custody while leaving independent lawful custody intact.

## Event

Gitlitz v. Commissioner, No. 99-1295. Argued October 2, 2000; decided January 9, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, 182 F.3d 1143, which affirmed the Tax Court's deficiency treatment on a reduction-first theory. The questions are whether the insolvent S corporation's excluded 1991 debt-discharge income passes through and increases shareholder basis, and whether those adjustments precede corporate tax-attribute reduction. Gitlitz and Winn used their pro rata portions to increase basis and deduct losses, including suspended losses; the Commissioner denied that treatment.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Tenth Circuit and remands for application of the governing statutory sequence.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.
**Opposing:** Breyer.

The Tenth Circuit's judgment is reversed. The lawful deficiency and loss calculations must include the qualifying pass-through and basis adjustments before the prescribed corporate attribute reduction. No unsupported final tax amount or additional hearing is ordered.

## Holdings

### I. Excluded discharge income passes through and increases basis

**Holding and operative rule:** Under the Code version governing the 1991 returns, an insolvent S corporation's debt-discharge amount excluded from gross income by section 108(a) remains an item of income under section 1366(a)(1)(A), passes through in each shareholder's pro rata share and increases stock basis under section 1367(a)(1)(A). Losses and deductions are then limited by section 1366(d)(1), using that adjusted stock basis and the shareholder's qualifying debt basis; exclusion remains capped by the corporation's insolvency immediately before discharge and supplies no free-standing deduction entitlement.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Those eight directly adopt both holdings and the statutory remand. Breyer dissents from this item's pass-through and resulting basis increase.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 61 identifies discharge of indebtedness as income. Section 108 excludes the qualifying amount from gross income without declaring that the item ceases to be income. Section 1366(a)(1)(A) expressly encompasses tax-exempt income, and section 1367(a)(1)(A) links the basis increase to that category. The Code therefore supplies both the income classification and the adjustment.

Section 108(d)(7)(A), as applicable in 1991, directs that subsections (a), (b) and (g) operate at the corporate level. That direction determines where exclusion and attribute reduction occur; it does not erase the income item or suspend the shareholder provisions. Section 1367(b)(1)'s return-inclusion restriction concerns amounts required to be included in gross income, and does not prohibit this increase for an excluded item that section 1366 recognizes. Bufferd preserves the different statutory functions of corporate and shareholder tax consequences. Hill likewise recognizes distinct statutory accounts, without a general prohibition against multiple tax benefits.

The Commissioner's objection that shareholders receive a benefit without fresh economic outlay cannot add a condition absent from these coordinated provisions. The result is confined to the presented statutory mechanism and version. It neither makes every excluded receipt a basis item nor exempts a claimed deduction from other applicable limits. Here the reported $2,021,296 discharge is less than the corporation's reported $2,181,748 insolvency; the holding determines the legal treatment rather than entering an unsupported final tax amount.

### II. The discharge-year calculation precedes the prescribed attribute reduction

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 108(b)(4)(A) requires the corporate tax-attribute reductions described in section 108(b)(2) after determination of the tax for the discharge year. For these S corporation returns, that determination includes the pass-through income, basis increase and otherwise allowable shareholder losses; only losses still disallowed under section 1366(d)(1) are treated by section 108(d)(7)(B) as a corporate net operating loss for this attribute-reduction calculation.

**Authority:** The same eight-Justice Court opinion directly adopts the sequence. Breyer's conditional acceptance of the timing command for an item that properly enters shareholder tax is not a join to this actual item's pass-through, basis or sequence holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Determining discharge-year shareholder tax requires calculating basis and allowable losses. Section 1366(d)(1) expressly uses basis determined with the section 1367(a)(1) income adjustments. Only then can the court identify the losses still disallowed and apply section 108(d)(7)(B). That paragraph retains work when losses exceed properly adjusted basis; it need not suppress the income adjustment to operate.

The Tenth Circuit's reduction-first approach reverses the enacted sequence. Breyer's competing construction instead disputes whether the special corporate exclusion-and-offset mechanism permits this shareholder item at all. The Court rejects that antecedent restriction for the textual reasons in Part I. Once the item enters the calculation, the express after-tax command governs. A perceived double benefit cannot authorize a court to rearrange that command. Atlantic Mutual permits administrative construction within genuine interpretive room, but supplies no power to contradict an express statutory direction or a general revenue-protection override; no applicable authorized regulation identified here independently changes the sequence.

The judgment is reversed and remanded for recomputation of the deficiencies and permitted losses under these rules, followed by the applicable corporate attribute reduction. The reported half shares and claimed losses of $1,010,648 per shareholder are not automatic awards. Ordinary amount, basis and deduction limits remain for application. Later statutory changes, other entities and other tax years lie outside the question presented; no new evidentiary hearing or reopening of settled tax facts is directed.

## Precedent Treatment

- Bufferd v. Commissioner: applies the distinction between S corporation and shareholder statutory tax functions; its return-and-limitations holding is not converted into an answer it had already given to debt discharge.
- United States v. Hill: applies its respect for distinct statutory tax accounts and retains its rejection of a general ban on multiple tax benefits; its mineral-basis rule is unchanged.
- Atlantic Mutual Insurance Co. v. Commissioner: preserves deference within actual statutory interpretive room while distinguishing a construction that would contradict the express sequence; no general revenue-protection exception is adopted.
- Commissioner v. Estate of Hubert: preserves the need to give operative statutory and regulatory qualifications independent work; it supplies no authority to replace this Code sequence with a categorical economic-outlay condition.

## Law After Decision

For the S corporation mechanism and Code version governing these returns, excluded discharge income remains a pass-through income item that increases stock basis before the section 108(b)(2) attribute reduction. The current loss computation precedes identification of disallowed losses treated as a corporate net operating loss. Corporate-level exclusion, the insolvency ceiling, ordinary deduction and basis limits, and subsequent attribute reduction each retain their stated functions. The governing text controls despite the combined benefit; the ruling decides no later statutory version or final deficiency amount.

## Separate Writings

Breyer dissents. He reads section 108(d)(7)(A)'s corporate-level direction together with paragraph (B)'s capture of disallowed shareholder losses as a specific exclusion-and-offset mechanism. In his view, excluded corporate discharge should not first become shareholder income that releases the very losses designated for corporate reduction. His Hubert dissent emphasizes the coordinated scheme's economic operation rather than a bookkeeping label, and his O'Gilvie and Atlantic Mutual positions require attention to the receipt's actual statutory treatment.

He accepts that discharge excluded from gross income can remain income in a general sense. He also recognizes the after-tax timing command for an item that validly enters shareholder tax. The taxpayers' broad income language and sequencing argument therefore do not, in his view, answer the antecedent question whether this particular corporate exclusion permits the shareholder item. He would deny the basis increase and affirm on that statutory ground. The resulting benefit to a solvent shareholder reinforces his construction; it is not an independent policy veto, a universal reduction-first rule, an allegation of misconduct or a general prohibition of multiple tax benefits.

The case returns for lawful implementation of the pass-through, basis and loss calculation, followed by the corporate attribute reduction required by the applicable provisions. Ordinary statutory limits continue to govern. The Court directs no new factual hearing, supplies no final deficiency figure and authorizes no relief for other tax years.

## Event

Solid Waste Agency of Northern Cook County v. U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, No. 99-1178. Argued October 31, 2000; decided January 9, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, 191 F.3d 845, which sustained section 404 jurisdiction. A municipal landfill proposal would fill permanent and seasonal ponds in an abandoned sand and gravel pit. The Corps first disclaimed jurisdiction and later invoked part (b) of its 1986 bird-habitat explanation under 33 CFR section 328.3(a)(3). The questions are whether that statute authorizes jurisdiction over these isolated, nonnavigable intrastate ponds solely through migratory-bird habitat and, if so, whether the application is within Congress's commerce power. The separate permit-merits challenge was abandoned.

**Opposing:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

Set aside the challenged federal jurisdiction and permit-denial ground to the extent they depend exclusively on the asserted migratory-bird criterion. The judgment awards no permit and adjudicates neither another properly established statutory jurisdictional basis nor independent state or local requirements. The abandoned challenge to the separate merits of the permit denial is not revived.

## Holdings

### The asserted bird-habitat criterion does not supply section 404 authority

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 404 of the Clean Water Act does not authorize federal jurisdiction over the isolated, nonnavigable intrastate ponds presented solely because migratory birds use them. The Corps must identify authority within the enacted water program; an asserted interstate environmental benefit alone does not supply the delegated jurisdiction, and the challenged jurisdiction and permit-denial ground must be set aside to the extent they depend exclusively on that criterion.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas. Five Justices directly adopt this statutory ground, its limits and reversal with remand. The Court does not reach the contingent Commerce Clause question.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 404(a) authorizes permits for discharge of dredged or fill material into navigable waters. Section 502(7) defines those waters to include waters of the United States and the territorial seas. That expansion reaches beyond classical navigation without making the enacted water limitation meaningless. Riverside Bayview sustained adjacent wetlands because of their statutory and ecological relationship to covered waters and expressly left nonadjacent waters open. These isolated ponds are not wetlands under the Corps' vegetation definition and are not alleged to adjoin navigable waters. Bird use does not itself supply that missing statutory relationship.

Section 404(g)(1) allows state administration outside the waters reserved to federal administration, confirming coverage beyond traditional navigation without defining every remaining pond as covered. Section 101(a)'s biological-integrity and wildlife objectives operate consistently with the Act's provisions and alongside section 101(b)'s protection of state responsibilities. Neither purpose alone settles the disputed jurisdiction. MCI and FDA v. Brown & Williamson require authority in the connected statutory scheme before deference; Sweet Home construed a different enacted habitat-injury definition and supplies no substitute delegation here.

The Corps' change of position is not automatically disqualifying, and the preamble's form is not an independent reason to invalidate it. No universal rule governing Chevron, Skidmore or interpretation of an agency's own regulation is necessary: the asserted reach exceeds the statute on its terms. ## Holdings

### The Bureau may apply this reasoned firearm-conduct category

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 3621(e)(2)(B) permits the Bureau of Prisons to withhold a discretionary early-release reduction categorically from otherwise eligible prisoners whose current felony conduct involved carrying, possessing or using a firearm or other dangerous weapon, under the challenged 1997 regulation. A nonviolent conviction and successful completion of qualifying treatment are necessary conditions for a reduction, not an entitlement to individual selection or an award; any reduction remains limited to one year, and the category must remain within the statute and rationally applied to the actual qualifying conduct.

**Authority:** Justice Thomas's opinion of the Court is joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These six adopt the entire statutory ground, its public-safety application and affirmance. Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Kennedy dissent.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress distinguished treatment qualification, threshold eligibility for a reduction and the Bureau's discretionary choice whether to grant one. The permission to reduce custody does not require the Bureau to decide each recurring safety issue anew. Heckler v. Campbell permits valid rules within a statutory delegation; it supplies no authority to disregard that delegation. Here firearm involvement in felony conduct rationally bears on safe early release. Congress's nonviolent-conviction threshold does not direct the Bureau to disregard that conduct at the separate selection stage. The 1997 rule does not reclassify the conviction as violent.

Yang preserves statutory limits, reasoned administration and the distinction between eligibility and relief. Its immigration-waiver rule forbids using qualifying fraud automatically to defeat every eligible applicant; this subset does not extinguish the release incentive for all eligible prisoners. Koray likewise requires the statutory categories to do their work and supplies no independent deference shortcut. The Court's conclusion rests on this delegation and reasoned safety criterion, not administrative convenience alone.

The Bureau's separate duty to make appropriate treatment available to prisoners it determines have a treatable addiction or abuse condition remains. Residential treatment and aftercare are required for eligible willing prisoners with a substance-abuse problem, subject to available appropriations and release-date priority. After the Director finds successful residential treatment, the prisoner remains in custody under appropriate conditions; if those conditions differ because of completion, periodic testing is required and the altered conditions must end when abuse recurs. None of those commands makes early release mandatory.

Lopez's firearm enhancement presents the challenged category. The record establishes treatment admission eligibility, not successful completion or entitlement to a reduction. Ordinary objections to a misapplied category, disregard of binding rules or supported arbitrary administration remain distinct. The Court does not validate every possible exclusion or automatic denial to all eligible prisoners. The amici rulemaking-procedure objection was not raised or decided below or presented in the petition and is not reached; no constitutional liberty-interest question is decided.

## Precedent Treatment

- Heckler v. Campbell: applies the permission to resolve recurring issues by rules within the actual delegation; it does not make every categorical restriction lawful.
- INS v. Yueh-Shaio Yang: preserves eligibility/discretion separation and reasoned, consistent administration; distinguishes its prohibition of automatically defeating every eligible waiver application from this reasoned subset under a different statute.
- Reno v. Koray: preserves contextual statutory construction and its detention-credit holding; it supplies neither the present release rule nor an independently sufficient administrative-deference ground.

## Law After Decision

The challenged 1997 firearm-conduct category is a lawful exercise of selection discretion under section 3621(e)(2)(B). The nonviolent-conviction threshold, successful-treatment condition, one-year maximum and independent treatment obligations remain effective. The rule does not make the conviction violent, guarantee a reduction, authorize arbitrary administration or validate every other exclusion category.

## Separate Writings

Stone-Zsela dissents, joined by Stevens and Kennedy. Congress's nonviolent-conviction criterion and successful-treatment condition make a genuine opportunity for consideration part of the rehabilitative scheme. Permission to deny a reduction does not make that adjacent eligibility language idle. A firearm sentencing circumstance may strongly indicate danger, but it is not the conviction Congress specified and does not establish that treatment and individual circumstances can never matter.

The dissent distinguishes Campbell's rules resolving recurring subsidiary issues from a rule foreclosing the selection Congress made available. Yang likewise permits relevant discretionary judgment within the statute, rather than revision of statutory eligibility. The dissent accepts structured consideration, including strong or nearly decisive weight for preconviction firearm conduct and relevant postconviction circumstances. It rejects neither every category nor every consideration of offense conduct.

The dissent would set aside this categorical bar, require the Bureau to establish whether Lopez successfully completed the qualifying treatment, and, if so, consider a reduction through a reasoned decision addressing safety and rehabilitation. The record establishes treatment admission eligibility, not completion. The Bureau could still deny a reduction on lawful individual grounds. No particular reduction, immediate release, universal constitutional hearing right or liberty entitlement would follow.

Treatment obligations and ordinary review of a category's actual application remain subject to their own statutory conditions; the judgment directs no custody reduction or release.

## Holdings

### I. Civil designation does not foreclose proof of actual punishment

**Holding and operative rule:** A court first identifies whether the legislature intended a civil or criminal commitment scheme; civil intent may be overcome only by the clearest proof that the challenged commitment's purpose or effect is punitive. Actual design and operation must be assessed cumulatively, including contrary civil evidence: a civil label is not conclusive, but confinement, connection to prior crime, indeterminate duration, criminal-style safeguards, deficient treatment or an individual treatment failure alone establishes neither punishment nor a right to release.

**Authority:** Justice Breyer's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. All nine directly adopt both holdings, the specified limits and the scoped affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Hendricks permits proof of punitive operation in a nominally civil commitment and invalidated the demonstrated retrospective application there. Hudson preserves design-and-operation review and supplies no universal rule confining the inquiry to statutory wording. The Court must therefore distinguish an inadequately administered but genuinely civil program from custody that actually imposes punishment.

The superintendent's concern that day-to-day conditions should not transform a scheme's classification supports a demanding burden and careful remedial distinctions. It cannot make proved punitive operation legally irrelevant. Clinical testimony, treatment records and plans, staff and patient accounts, confinement practices, program design and other reliable direct or circumstantial evidence can bear on the cumulative question. No statistical study or successful cure is required, and officials cannot defeat strong combined proof merely by isolating every circumstance. Conversely, employee misconduct or poor care alone does not establish that the custody is punishment.

Young's allegations concerning security, confinement, abuse, staffing, therapy and practical release remain allegations. The separate Turay injunction and reported progress are evidence within their actual scope, not conclusive proof of punishment, compliance or cure of an earlier unlawful application. Statutory examination, review and release mechanisms are relevant civil evidence, but their existence does not prove their operation for Young. The Court neither finds punishment nor rewrites those mechanisms. The supported claim requires adjudication under the governing burden, rather than categorical foreclosure by the civil label.

### II. The existing punishment remand stands, with distinct constitutional and remedial predicates

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's reviewed punishment-remand portion is affirmed, preserving the opportunity to establish Young's supported claim under the former chapter 153 habeas framework applicable to his 1994 application. If the clearest proof establishes punishment and the challenged custody retroactively adds it for conduct predating enactment, custody under that unlawful application must end through appropriate habeas relief, while independent lawful custody is preserved; a conditions violation that leaves custody lawfully civil instead requires relief through its proper conditions channel.

**Authority:** The same unanimous Court opinion adopts the remand, temporal rule and remedy distinction. The separate due-process, equal-protection and commitment-procedure rulings Young did not seek to review remain undisturbed.

**Controlling explanation:** Lindh's application-date rule leaves this pre-April 24, 1996 application under former chapter 153 law; amended section 2254(d) does not govern its merits. Separately directed provisions and ordinary habeas requirements retain their operation. The Ninth Circuit already allowed an opportunity to prove punitive operation by the clearest proof. That disposition is proper under Hendricks and Hudson, so correcting the account of governing precedent requires instructions rather than vacatur of the opportunity itself. The remand creates no universal entitlement to an evidentiary hearing and no present writ or release order.

Punishment and retrospectivity require separate findings. The existence of a conditions remedy cannot defeat a proved unlawful-custody claim; conversely, conditions defects do not automatically require release. The court must consider the supported record and material genuine factual disputes under the former habeas rules. No replacement confinement authority is supplied.

Double Jeopardy remains distinct. A punitive character alone proves neither the relevant prior jeopardy nor that the challenged confinement is a second punishment for the same offense. Those matters require separate determination under the governing attachment and offense-identity law if the preserved claim remains necessary after the punishment and retrospective-custody findings. Hendricks did not decide them. The Court establishes no independent double-jeopardy entitlement here and need not add a second ground if Ex Post Facto relief fully resolves the custody claim. The remaining punishment, chronology, same-offense and custody determinations are adjudicative work under the stated rules, not permission to treat a civil label or shared past misconduct as conclusive.

## Holdings

### The pleaded funding arrangement preserves a genuine program choice

**Holding and operative rule:** The pleaded child-support and Temporary Assistance for Needy Families funding arrangement is not unconstitutional coercion merely because declining substantial related assistance is politically and financially difficult. Coercion remains possible when the structure and practical operation of threatened funding loss leave no genuine choice; clear notice, program relationship and the absence of an entitlement to unchanged grants are relevant but do not independently settle that question, and no fixed dollar, percentage or insolvency threshold governs.

**Authority:** Justice O'Connor's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt this actual-choice rule, its application to the pleaded arrangement and the limited affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Dole, New York's monetary-incentive holding and Steward Machine distinguish substantial inducement from compulsion. Child-support collection directly serves the funded family-assistance system and the resources available for supported families. The challenged arrangement does not impose administration duties after Kansas declines the supported program.

The Court accepts well-pleaded facts on review of dismissal, but the legal conclusion that refusal is impossible does not become an additional fact. The historical fiscal-year 1996 figures—about $29.3 million in child-support funds and $101.9 million in welfare funds, with federal support covering 66 percent of operations and 80 percent of computer expenditures—show a substantial incentive. They establish neither present forfeiture, a percentage of the State's total resources nor a concrete inability to exit. The alleged burdens on Kansas and beneficiaries are real considerations, but this arrangement does not plead practical conscription.

The inquiry considers the threatened funds, their connection to the conditions, notice and transition, and consequences of refusal where relevant, without a mandatory checklist. Competent qualitative evidence, program operation, administrative experience and feasible alternatives can establish compulsion; statistics are not the exclusive proof. A material allegation of actual compulsion could not be dismissed as irrelevant simply because participation is labeled voluntary. None is established beyond the dependence and difficulty alleged here, and discovery need not be ordered to search for an unpleaded theory.

Printz's actual source-and-function rules concern compelled executive work; they neither convert this funding choice into an unconditional command nor create a universal compensation right. Kansas cannot compel continuation of superseded grant terms, but that lack of entitlement alone would not defeat a genuine coercion claim. The statute's actual qualifications below prevent treating every implementation defect as automatic loss of both funding streams.

**Statutory limits of this application:** Section 602(a)(2) requires the chief executive's certification that the State will operate a child-support program under an approved part-D plan during the fiscal year. Complete refusal to operate that program differs from deficiencies in a continuing program.

For fiscal years beginning on or after October 1, 1997, section 609(a)(8) requires a finding of failure to achieve the paternity-establishment percentages or other performance measures, incomplete or unreliable data, or lack of substantial compliance with part D. Performance findings rest on section 654(15)(B) submitted data or a section 652(a)(4) review; data-reliability findings rest on section 652(a)(4)(C)(i) audits, and substantial-compliance findings on section 652(a)(4)(C) audits. The substantial-compliance branch excludes section 654(24), (27)(A) and (27)(B)(i). In the succeeding corrective fiscal year, the State must have failed to take sufficient corrective action to achieve required performance or compliance, or again submitted incomplete or unreliable data. The reduction is 1–2 percent, 2–3 percent for the second consecutive finding and 3–5 percent for the third or later consecutive finding. It reduces otherwise payable TANF amounts for quarters after that corrective year and before quarters following the first full quarter in which the required performance or substantial compliance is achieved. Technical noncompliance counts as substantial compliance only if the Secretary determines that it does not adversely affect program performance; technically incomplete or unreliable data count as adequate only on the Secretary's parallel determination that the defect does not adversely affect measurement of paternity establishment or other performance. No triggering finding or actual sanction is adjudicated here.

Under section 666(d), the Secretary may exempt a law or procedure required by or pursuant to that section if the State demonstrates, using caseload, processing-time, administrative-cost, average-collection and other requested data or estimates, that it will not improve effectiveness and efficiency. The exemption is discretionary, remains subject to continuing review and ends if circumstances change. It supplies neither a guaranteed exemption nor an exemption from every part-D obligation; the result does not depend on predicting an exemption for Kansas.

Section 654(26) continues to require safeguards against unauthorized use or disclosure; prohibits whereabouts disclosure to another party subject to a protective order and to a person where physical or emotional harm is reasonably feared; requires notice to the Secretary when those prohibitions apply and reasonable evidence of domestic violence or child abuse indicates harmful disclosure; and requires court screening before further disclosure of flagged information received from the Secretary. If the court finds further disclosure could harm the parent or child, the court and its agents must not disclose it.

**Scope and remedy:** The judgment dismissing the reviewed coercion challenge is affirmed. The granted question supplies no separate ruling on privacy, procedural due process, every collection or disclosure mechanism, or a particular sanctions application. The safeguards remain statutory commands, not findings validating every implementation. No funds, old-terms payment, penalty, severed replacement program or discovery order is awarded.

## Precedent Treatment

- South Dakota v. Dole: applies the distinction between inducement and coercion with general-welfare, notice, relatedness and independent constitutional limits preserved; its five-percent setting becomes no numerical safe harbor.
- New York v. United States: applies its monetary-incentive holding and distinguishes its unavoidable take-title command; no constitutional entitlement to retain grants on superseded terms follows.
- Steward Machine Co. v. Davis: applies its distinction between financial pressure and compulsion without treating every expensive choice as voluntary by definition.
- Printz v. United States: distinguishes its actual compulsory-assistance and fiscal-remand questions from conditional funded cooperation; its source-and-function limits and absence of a controlling compensation entitlement remain intact.
