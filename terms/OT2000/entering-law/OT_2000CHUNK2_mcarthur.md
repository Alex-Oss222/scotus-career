### Presented questions, subsidiary arguments and exceptional Supreme Court review

**Governing question:** When an issue outside the stated certiorari question may be considered.

**Current rule:** Rule 14.1(a) creates a strong prudential presumption against deciding questions neither stated in the petition nor fairly included, subject to exceptional circumstances. A distinct intervention denial is not fairly included merely because reversing it would permit reaching a settlement-vacatur question; discussion in the petition’s body and merits briefing does not alone suffice. A consistently asserted Teague defense may be subsidiary where it limits application of the very constitutional rule and habeas relief challenged by the petition. Geographic tailoring is fairly included in a challenge to exclusion from designated speech zones. An exceptional extension requires its own supported basis; adversarial briefing, preservation, logical priority or usefulness alone does not enlarge the questions. The duty to notice jurisdictional defects remains separate. A respondent may defend the existing favorable judgment on a record-supported alternative rejected below when doing so neither enlarges its own rights nor reduces the adversary’s rights under that judgment. The Court may also elect to consider a deliberately disavowed legal argument supporting the same preserved claim when it falls within the question presented and can be resolved on the established record without a new factual theory or additional findings. That discretion imposes no duty to consider abandoned grounds and does not erase limits on distinct claims, relief, factual stipulations or missing facts.

### Article III power before disputed statutory merits

**Governing question:** Whether a court may assume disputed constitutional jurisdiction to resolve contested federal statutory liability.

**Current rule:** A federal court may not assume disputed Article III power merely to decide a contested statutory merits question. Statutory enforcement-and-penalty jurisdictional wording does not automatically make every liability element jurisdictional; substantial federal claims remain distinct from success on their merits. A court may dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction before resolving subject-matter jurisdiction when personal jurisdiction is straightforward and presents no complex state-law question while subject-matter jurisdiction is difficult. Ordinarily an easily resolved subject-matter question should come first. The choice must respect statutory remand commands and review bars, comity and relevant forum interests, and may decide no contested merits; an established absence of subject-matter jurisdiction retains its statutory consequence.

**Authority by component:**

- Citizens for a Better Environment v. Steel Co. — 1. Constitutional power before this disputed statutory merits question, 1997-06-02; Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five, with O'Connor and Kennedy reserving other legitimate exceptional sequences

- Ruhrgas AG v. Marathon Oil Co. — Canonical Decision Record, Holdings, “A court may choose a straightforward nonmerits jurisdictional ground”, 1999-05-17; Scalia, all nine Justices.

**Present operation effective:** Ruhrgas, May 17, 1999, for bounded nonmerits sequencing in removed and originally filed cases; Steel Co.’s merits prohibition retains its June 2, 1997 date.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule prescribes no exhaustive hierarchy among thresholds and authorizes no assumed jurisdiction over disputed merits. Norton and Avrech retain their own controlled-merits and jurisdictional settings. A particular statutory priority, such as Price-Anderson coverage before tribal exhaustion, remains operative.

**Holdings navigation:** Citizens for a Better Environment v. Steel Co. (1997-06-02) — Constitutional jurisdiction before a disputed EPCRA merits question. [Ruhrgas AG v. Marathon Oil Co. — A court may choose a straightforward nonmerits jurisdictional ground](HOLDINGS.md#a-court-may-choose-a-straightforward-nonmerits-jurisdictional-ground-1).

### Exploratory tactile examination of closed personal luggage

**Governing question:** When touching luggage stored in a shared overhead area becomes a Fourth Amendment search.

**Current rule:** Ordinary shared storage exposes closed personal luggage to movement, incidental contact and associated compression, but not to an added physical examination revealing the shape and density of concealed contents. Objectively exploratory squeezing to acquire that information is a search even without opening the bag. The character of the examination, rather than undisclosed motive or touching alone, governs.

**Authority by component:**

- Protected contents and objectively exploratory contact: Bond v. United States — Exploratory touch can search closed luggage without opening it, April 17, 2000; Souter joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg.

**Present operation effective:** Bond, April 17, 2000.

**Exceptions and limits:** The decision prohibits neither ordinary incidental touching nor all compression and creates no new warrant exception or subjective-purpose test.

#### Whren v. United States, 517 U.S. 806

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-5841.
**Decided:** June 10, 1996.

##### Investigative motive and customary enforcement in an ordinary traffic stop

**Holding:** An ordinary initial traffic stop supported by objective probable cause of a traffic violation does not become unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment because officers also investigate an unrelated offense. Validity does not additionally depend on whether reasonable officers ordinarily would enforce the violation absent that other aim. Independent constitutional limits on manner, scope, and duration remain; detention or search materially exceeding what the traffic violation reasonably justifies requires independent grounds.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Thomas), unanimous, including the application and qualifications.

**Material application:** The accepted observations of waiting unusually long at a stop sign, failing to signal, and unreasonable speed supply traffic probable cause. Petitioners' proposed objective “would-have” test receives the same rejection as a subjective-motive prerequisite: variable local priorities, uniforms, assignments, markings, and enforcement customs do not change constitutional authority for the known violation. A separate balance of ordinary traffic-safety gains against plainclothes-intervention anxiety supplies no additional requirement.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Inventory and administrative-inspection authority retain their genuine-purpose and standardized or other source-specific constraints; motive is not irrelevant to every constitutional question. General Order 303.1 compliance, its immediate-safety-threat exception for otherwise unauthorized plainclothes or unmarked enforcement, and its extreme-circumstances oral-warning exception are not adjudicated. Neither speed nor missing execution details establish an exception or constitutional execution finding. The later search is not independently presented.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Scott* applies objective assessment of justified action; *Robinson*, *Gustafson*, and *Villamonte-Marquez* retain their distinct lawful-arrest, search, and customs-inspection predicates without creating new power. *Prouse* distinguishes suspicionless discretionary stops; *Martinez-Fuerte* and *Brignoni-Ponce* do not require a further balance for this ordinary probable-cause stop. *Bertine* and *Wells* preserve inventory constraints and forbid investigatory ruses; *Burger* preserves purpose-limited administrative inspection. *Abel* and *Bannister*'s regular-procedure and no-pretext observations do not establish the proposed customary-enforcement condition. *Wilson* preserves reasonable-execution review with its supported danger, futility, escape, and evidence-destruction qualifications, leaving suspicion quantum and automatic suppression open. *Garner*, *Welsh*, and *Winston* retain their distinct deadly-force, home-entry, and bodily-intrusion limits.

##### Review of the accepted predicate and separation of discriminatory selection

**Holding:** The accepted traffic observations permit resolution under *Ornelas*: historical facts, credibility, and genuinely factual subsidiary findings or inferences receive clear-error review, with genuine due weight to grounded contextual inferences; the legal standard and ultimate constitutional sufficiency properly at issue receive independent judgment. No new factual inquiry is required to decide this legal-rule challenge.

**Proposition-level authority:** The same unanimous Court opinion.

**Material application:** Due weight means substantive consideration of supported trial and local-officer context, not a numerical weight, officer-credibility presumption, or intermediate standard insulating constitutional conclusions. Direct review imports no habeas presumption or clear-and-convincing burden and supplies no supposed antecedent drug probable cause.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Purposeful racial selection remains independently forbidden by equal protection; traffic probable cause does not establish nondiscrimination. No discrimination finding, police-stop discovery entitlement, immunity rule, suppression consequence, or damages remedy is decided, and assumed adequacy of another remedy is not a ground for judgment.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm rejection of suppression on the initial-stop ground, 9–0. The completed vacatur of lesser-included §841 convictions and §860 resentencing, including nonreimposition of Whren's fine, remain undisturbed. Later sentencing appeals are not resolved. No new trial, hearing, release, sentencing proceeding, or determination of every admissibility ground follows.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Ornelas*'s review allocation is applied unchanged. *Kimberlin* permits circumstantial proof when motive is an underlying element without adding one here. *Armstrong* remains confined to federal charging and discovery; its requirements and unresolved discrimination remedy do not become police-stop rules.

#### Bond v. United States, 529 U.S. 334 (2000)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 98-9349.
**Decided:** April 17, 2000.

##### Exploratory touch can search closed luggage without opening it

**Holding:** Placing closed personal luggage in a shared overhead area exposes it to ordinary movement, incidental contact and associated compression, but does not expose concealed contents to an added physical examination of their shape and density. Exploratory squeezing that acquires that concealed information is a search; the inquiry concerns the objective character of the examination, not an officer's undisclosed motive or the mere fact of touching.

**Proposition-level authority:** Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg, supplies seven votes for the rule, its application and the bounded remedy.

**Material application:** The agent's squeezing disclosed the edges and density of concealed contents beyond ordinary overhead handling. Lawful immigration questioning provided no independent search authority and no individualized suspicion preceded the examination.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Ordinary repositioning and incidental contact remain outside this rule. No general canine-sniff or supported safety-search question, detention-duration claim or all statement-specific consequences are decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Fifth Circuit judgment is reversed for implementation of the actual drugs-and-confessions suppression motion. The drugs cannot rest on the rejected no-search theory, and the Government's disclaimed subsequent-consent ground cannot be reopened. No independent admission ground is found preserved; unreviewed causal and statement-specific issues remain. The mandate orders no automatic acquittal, release or new trial.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Katz and Place are applied to privacy retained in closed luggage despite travel in a public conveyance.
- The independent-luggage rule is applied: a bag's separately established privacy does not disappear with the surrounding vehicle's public accessibility.
- Dickerson is applied for the boundary between authorized contact and added exploration; its protective-frisk setting is not imported as search authority.
- Hicks is applied to additional physical acquisition of concealed information rather than mere observation.
- Ciraolo and Riley are distinguished because they concern information exposed to public observation; their objective approach remains intact.
- Flippo preserves the distinction between lawful presence, a visible exterior and concealed contents; no general presence-based exception is created.
- Wardlow preserves the sequence of objective justification and forbids reliance on later discoveries to justify the earlier intrusion.
- Robinette retains the separation between consent voluntariness and independence from prior illegality; no consent ground is adjudicated here after the Government's nonreliance.

### City of Indianapolis v. Edmond — 2000-11-28

Souter's seven-Justice opinion (Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer joining) holds that a checkpoint stop is a seizure before exterior canine examination. A program principally directed to gathering evidence of ordinary narcotics offenses requires an independently lawful justification for dispensing with individualized suspicion; brief systematic stops and ancillary license or sobriety checks do not supply it. The admitted primary objective resolves this program's purpose without a fact-finding remand. Program instructions, official statements and actual practices may establish purpose; no statistical study, universal efficacy threshold, catastrophe requirement or exhaustion of every less intrusive alternative is required.

Whren's probable-cause rule does not establish authority for stopping everyone without suspicion. Chandler requires an actual adequate need; Sitz's highway-safety and Martinez-Fuerte's border settings remain distinct. Prouse supplies no general authorization from systematic selection; Bond preserves the distinction between lawful presence and separate intrusion without adding a checkpoint-purpose test. Comparable ordinary crime-detection roadblocks fall within the rule, but genuinely justified programs and lawful action on evidence encountered during them retain their own requirements. The Court reserves expansion of a program principally justified by license or sobriety checking to narcotics detection. Urgent threats, emergency roadblocks, airport or government-building security, other border settings, individualized-suspicion searches and the particular canine examination remain outside the challenge. The antecedent seizure makes the dog-sniff classification unnecessary to decide. The 7-2 affirmance and remand require preliminary prospective restraint while litigation continues; no replacement program, suppression or damages follows.

### City of Indianapolis v. Edmond — 2000-11-28

**Thomas, dissenting, Part I, joined by Scalia.** The dissent would reverse because this program genuinely performs systematic license and sobriety checks that provide an independent roadway justification. Under the dissent's reading of Whren and the checkpoint precedents, the added narcotics objective and exterior dog use do not invalidate a stop whose actual operation stays within that legitimate scope. The dissent answers the concern about nominal checks by requiring real, independently supportable roadway functions and lawful execution; it does not approve a drug-only checkpoint merely because it efficiently detects crime. It would return the case for proceedings under that objective mixed-function rule rather than approve every execution detail.

**Thomas, dissenting, Part II, joined by no other Justice.** Thomas would apply the restrained scope and objective reasonableness analysis of the checkpoint cases to these brief systematic automobile seizures. He rejects a primary-purpose threshold transferred from suspicionless searches of bodies, homes, or businesses. He also questions whether indiscriminate stops accord with the Fourth Amendment's original understanding, but does not seek to overrule Sitz or Martinez-Fuerte here because that issue was not developed for decision. Scalia joins neither this additional critique nor that reservation.
