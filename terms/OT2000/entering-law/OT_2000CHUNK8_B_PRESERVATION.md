## Standards and Tests selection: Presented questions, subsidiary arguments and exceptional Supreme Court review

### Presented questions, subsidiary arguments and exceptional Supreme Court review

**Governing question:** When an issue outside the stated certiorari question may be considered.

**Current rule:** Rule 14.1(a) creates a strong prudential presumption against deciding questions neither stated in the petition nor fairly included, subject to exceptional circumstances. A distinct intervention denial is not fairly included merely because reversing it would permit reaching a settlement-vacatur question; discussion in the petition’s body and merits briefing does not alone suffice. A consistently asserted Teague defense may be subsidiary where it limits application of the very constitutional rule and habeas relief challenged by the petition. Geographic tailoring is fairly included in a challenge to exclusion from designated speech zones. An exceptional extension requires its own supported basis; adversarial briefing, preservation, logical priority or usefulness alone does not enlarge the questions. The duty to notice jurisdictional defects remains separate. A respondent may defend the existing favorable judgment on a record-supported alternative rejected below when doing so neither enlarges its own rights nor reduces the adversary’s rights under that judgment. The Court may also elect to consider a deliberately disavowed legal argument supporting the same preserved claim when it falls within the question presented and can be resolved on the established record without a new factual theory or additional findings. That discretion imposes no duty to consider abandoned grounds and does not erase limits on distinct claims, relief, factual stipulations or missing facts.

The reasoned dismissal establishes this question-presentation ruling, not the merits of the judgment below.
- Confined subsidiary Teague defense: Caspari v. Bohlen — The preserved nonretroactivity defense is fairly included here, 1994-02-23. It declined further independent conduct-language questions despite briefing. Those circumstances are not a universal mandatory checklist or permission to review every related clause.

#### Yee v. City of Escondido, 503 U.S. 519 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-1947.
**Decided:** April 1, 1992.

For this case’s other questions, see *Yee v. City of Escondido*, April 1, 1992, No. 90-1947: whether restrictions on voluntarily created pad tenancies compel permanent physical occupation (Property and Economic Rights — Physical takings and voluntary tenancies).

##### Whether the unpreserved substantive-due-process claim is reviewable

**Holding:** The Court declines a substantive due-process claim neither properly raised nor decided in the state courts; presentation only in a denied request for discretionary review does not supply the missing state-court decision. The Court need not decide here whether this review limitation is jurisdictional or prudential.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II-A of O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; six Justices adopt this procedural proposition. Stone does not join this rationale.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Due-process merits are not rejected. Whether the review limitation is jurisdictional or prudential is expressly undecided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the California judgment rejecting the physical-taking claim; award no compensation on that theory. Decline the due-process and regulatory-taking merits without deciding their validity; any properly available regulatory or as-applied claim requires an appropriate proceeding, not a new merits remand here.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 — applied: an unpresented state-court claim is not decided on review; the jurisdictional/prudential classification remains open.
- People v. Triggs, 8 Cal.3d 884 — applied only to the effect of denied California discretionary review, which expresses no merits determination.

##### Whether a new taking argument and this facial theory satisfy preservation and ripeness

**Holding:** A party that properly presents a federal taking claim below may offer a different supporting argument on review, subject to the separate limitation imposed by the question presented. Under the then-governing standards, this facial allegation that the ordinance never substantially advances a legitimate state interest is ripe without a rent-increase request because it does not depend on the particular property’s economic loss or compensation.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II-B–C of O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; six Justices adopt this procedural proposition. Stone does not join this rationale.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The holding uses the then-governing facial substantial-advancement theory and does not decide regulatory-taking merits or override the separate question-presented limit.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the California judgment rejecting the physical-taking claim; award no compensation on that theory. Decline the due-process and regulatory-taking merits without deciding their validity; any properly available regulatory or as-applied claim requires an appropriate proceeding, not a new merits remand here.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Bankers Life & Casualty Co. v. Crenshaw, 486 U.S. 71 — applied: new arguments may support a preserved federal claim.
- Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 — applied: argument and claim preservation are distinct.
- Williamson County Regional Planning Commission v. Hamilton Bank, 473 U.S. 172 — distinguished: the asserted facial theory does not require a property-specific rent determination.
- Agins v. Tiburon, 447 U.S. 255 — applied only to the then-current facial-ripeness proposition; no merits determination follows.
- Keystone Bituminous Coal Assn. v. DeBenedictis, 480 U.S. 470 — applied to distinguish facial allegations from property-specific application.

##### Whether the physical-taking question fairly includes regulatory-taking merits

**Holding:** Under Rule 14.1(a), a question asking whether the ordinance causes a physical taking does not fairly include the related but independent question whether it causes a regulatory taking. No exceptional urgency or economy justifies departing from that prudential limit here, so the regulatory merits remain undecided.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor, II-D, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas: seven votes; this section adopts no regulatory merits or ripeness conclusion.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Preserving a general taking claim does not enlarge the grant. Physical and regulatory taking questions are related but not subsidiary to one another, and no exceptional urgency or economy warrants enlargement here.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the California judgment rejecting the physical-taking claim; award no compensation on that theory. Decline the due-process and regulatory-taking merits without deciding their validity; any properly available regulatory or as-applied claim requires an appropriate proceeding, not a new merits remand here.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Stone v. Powell, 428 U.S. 465 — applied: departure from the question-presented rule remains exceptional.
- Lytle v. Household Manufacturing, Inc., 494 U.S. 545 — applied: developed arguments and lower-court consideration support disciplined review.
- American National Bank & Trust Co. v. Haroco, Inc., 473 U.S. 606 — applied by analogy: related questions are not necessarily fairly included within one another.

#### United States v. Williams, 504 U.S. 36 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-1972.
**Decided:** May 4, 1992.

For the other questions in *United States v. Williams*, May 4, 1992, No. 90-1972, see Federal Courts — Preservation and questions passed upon below (reviewability) and Criminal Procedure — Grand-jury presentation and supervisory dismissal (disclosure duty and dismissal authority).

##### Whether the Court may review a federal question passed upon below despite failure to renew an objection to circuit precedent

**Holding:** The Court may review an important federal question expressly decided by the court of appeals even though a party did not renew its challenge to binding circuit precedent in this case, where adherence to that precedent did not concede its correctness. This is a permissible exercise of review, not an obligation to decide every unpressed question or a rule excusing an affirmative waiver.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone’s reviewability opinion, joined by White, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter: five Justices adopt the rule and explanation. The four Justices who prefer dismissal of the writ do not join this rule.

**Material application:** The Tenth Circuit expressly applied Page. Adhering to binding precedent did not concede its correctness; the Government had opposed the rule when it was established.

**Limits and questions not reached:** This discretionary review does not displace trial-objection requirements, excuse affirmative waiver or decide review standards for state judgments.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Court reaches the question, 5–4: Stone, White, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor and Thomas prefer dismissal of the writ.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Stevens v. Department of Treasury, 500 U.S. 1 — applied: actual decision below can satisfy the pressed-or-passed-upon practice.
- United States v. Page, 808 F.2d 723 — its binding force within the Tenth Circuit explains the litigation posture; adherence does not establish agreement with its correctness.

#### Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp.

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-1525.
**Decided:** February 21, 1995.

For the other questions in this decision, see First Amendment — Government-created corporations (Special federal creation and permanent majority appointment make Amtrak governmental); Federal Courts — Relief after reversal of governmental-actor status (Governmental status requires a remand, not an immediate display order).

##### The Court may consider this included legal argument despite its earlier disavowal

**Holding:** The Court elects to consider Amtrak's governmental-entity status despite Lebron's earlier express disavowal of that argument because it supplies a legal basis for the same preserved First Amendment claim, falls within the governmental-actor question presented, and can be resolved on the established institutional record without a new factual theory or additional findings. This bounded exercise of review creates no obligation to consider every abandoned argument and does not dispense with limits on distinct claims, questions, requested relief, factual stipulations, or missing material facts.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's reachability portion, joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven Justices; Stone-Zsela does not join that rationale.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Yee v. City of Escondido, 503 U.S. 519 (1992): applies its distinction between a supporting argument and a new claim, together with its independent question-presented limit; the present decision separately addresses deliberate disavowal in this setting.
- Bray v. Alexandria Women's Health Clinic, 506 U.S. 263 (1993): distinguishes the unadjudicated separate hindrance theory, outside the granted questions and lacking a necessary finding; its scope limits remain operative.
- Bankers Life & Casualty Co. v. Crenshaw, 486 U.S. 71 (1988): applies, through Yee, the allowance of supporting arguments for a preserved federal claim, without erasing this Court's discretion over deliberately excluded grounds.
- Kamen v. Kemper Financial Services, Inc., 500 U.S. 90 (1991): preserves the distinction between legal arguments and the claim to which they relate; no unrelated claim is introduced.
- United States v. Mezzanatto, decided January 18, 1995: distinguishes the actual, limited evidentiary agreement there from this decision whether to consider an included argument; its waiver rule does not make every litigation choice an irrevocable surrender of an entire constitutional claim.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Second Circuit is reversed and remanded, 8–1, to adjudicate the unreviewed First Amendment and forum questions with Amtrak treated as governmental and reconsider associated state-law dispositions under their own rules. The display injunction is not automatically reinstated; no final display, damages, contract or TDI termination relief is awarded.

#### Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp.

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-1525.
**Decided:** February 21, 1995.

For the other questions in this decision, see Federal Courts — Preserved claims and disavowed legal arguments (The Court may consider this included legal argument despite its earlier disavowal); First Amendment — Government-created corporations (Special federal creation and permanent majority appointment make Amtrak governmental).

##### Governmental status requires a remand, not an immediate display order

**Holding:** Reversal of the Second Circuit's governmental-action ground restores the First Amendment claim for adjudication with Amtrak treated as governmental, but does not establish that this rejection was unconstitutional or that Lebron is entitled to mandatory display. The lower courts must address the unreviewed speech and forum questions, lawful relief, and the related disposition of the contract claim and TDI counterclaim under their own governing rules; the district display injunction is not automatically reinstated.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's remand portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight Justices.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Young v. Northern Illinois Conference of United Methodist Church, decided January 17, 1995: applies the distinction between statutory jurisdiction and legal defeat on the federal merits without extending its ministerial-selection defense.
- Tome v. United States, decided January 10, 1995: uses only its separation of threshold legal error from automatic ultimate relief by analogy; no evidentiary or criminal harmless-error standard is transferred.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court awards no display, damages, final contractual relief, or adjudication of TDI's requested declaration permitting termination. The Second Circuit's dismissal directions must be reconsidered consistently with the restored federal claim and ordinary law governing the separate state-law issues. The lower courts may not treat this actor-status ruling as a merits judgment approving the advertisement or invalidating every political-advertising policy.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Second Circuit is reversed and remanded, 8–1, to adjudicate the unreviewed First Amendment and forum questions with Amtrak treated as governmental and reconsider associated state-law dispositions under their own rules. The display injunction is not automatically reinstated; no final display, damages, contract or TDI termination relief is awarded.
