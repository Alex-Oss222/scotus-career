## Standards and Tests selection: Collateral attacks on ACCA predicates at federal sentencing

### Collateral attacks on ACCA predicates at federal sentencing

**Governing question:** Which attacks on prior state convictions may be heard in the federal ACCA sentencing proceeding.

**Current rule:** The ACCA sentencing proceeding does not supply a general collateral-review forum for attacks on prior state convictions based on ineffective representation or an involuntary counseled plea. A conviction obtained through an unconstitutional complete denial of counsel, without valid waiver, is the constitutional exception. Statutory objections to whether a conviction meets the enhancement’s predicate requirements remain distinct from such collateral attacks. A defendant may pursue authorized review in the proper forum and, after a predicate is invalidated there, apply for relief from the enhanced sentence; the decision promises neither reopening nor its remedy.

**Authority by component:**

- Forum limitation, complete-denial exception and distinct statutory objections: Custis v. United States — The ACCA supplies no general collateral-review procedure at sentencing; The constitutional counsel exception and the proper review channel, 1994-05-23. O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg; six direct joins to this statutory ground. The same six Justices join O'Connor's separate constitutional ground and its express remedial reservation; the three dissenters would allow the present attacks under their statutory construction.

**Present operation effective:** Custis v. United States, 1994-05-23.

**Open question:** The case does not resolve the position of a defendant who has no meaningful opportunity for authorized review because custody on the predicate has expired. Beecham’s separate conviction-selector rule is not supplied by this entry.

**Holdings navigation:** The ACCA supplies no general collateral-review procedure at sentencing; The constitutional counsel exception and the proper review channel.

## Standards and Tests selection: Habeas custody in a continuous consecutive sentence series

### Habeas custody in a continuous consecutive sentence series

**Governing question:** Custody under an earlier conviction after its first consecutive segment has been served.

**Current rule:** A prisoner serving a continuous, unexpired series of consecutive sentences remains in custody under an earlier conviction when invalidating it would advance release from the aggregate term, even though that first segment has been served. This establishes only the custody threshold; procedural gates and the conviction’s merits remain separate.

**Authority by component:**

- Custody under a continuous consecutive series: Custody under a continuous consecutive series, 1995-05-30. Ginsburg’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer; eight Justices directly support this rule.

**Present operation effective:** 1995-05-30; the component authorities identified above.

**Exceptions and limits:** Maleng’s rule for an independently expired sentence survives. Collateral consequences after all custody ends do not supply jurisdiction through this rule.

**Holdings navigation:** Custody under a continuous consecutive series.

## Standards and Tests selection: Collateral regularity of prior counseled pleas used for enhancement

### Collateral regularity of prior counseled pleas used for enhancement

**Governing question:** What instructions govern collateral regularity of prior counseled pleas used for enhancement?

**Current rule:** For collateral challenges to final counseled convictions used for sentence enhancement, due process permits Kentucky's procedure requiring the Commonwealth to prove the judgment and the defendant to produce evidence of infringement or procedural irregularity. After rebuttal, Kentucky requires the Commonwealth affirmatively to establish constitutional validity. Missing old transcripts alone, without alleged official misconduct, do not rebut regularity. Due process does not require clear-and-convincing extrarecord proof of plea validity in that setting. This is permission for that allocation, not a universal collateral proof quantum; direct-review waiver requirements and the exclusion of invalid uncounseled convictions survive.

**Authority by component:**

- Holding 1 — initial production burden.: Parke v. Raley — Holding 1 — initial production burden., 1992-12-01. O’Connor’s opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight votes at this level. Blackmun agrees with the result in a separate writing limited to sentencing.
- Holding 2 — measure of proof.: Parke v. Raley — Holding 2 — measure of proof., 1992-12-01. The same eight-Justice Court opinion; Blackmun independently agrees on his sentencing ground.

**Present operation effective:** 1992-12-01; Parke v. Raley, for the components identified above.

**Holdings navigation:** Parke v. Raley, 506 U.S. 20 (1992).

## Opening authority: Garlotte v. Fordice

#### Garlotte v. Fordice

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-6790.
**Decided:** May 30, 1995.

##### Custody under a continuous consecutive series

**Holding:** A prisoner serving a continuous unexpired series of consecutive sentences remains in custody under the earlier conviction when invalidating it would advance release from the aggregate term, although its first segment has been served. This establishes the custody threshold, leaving the conviction’s validity and remaining procedural and merits conditions for review.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer; eight Justices directly support this rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** An independently expired sentence's collateral consequences alone remain insufficient under Maleng. The rule concerns a continuous unexpired aggregate whose release date would advance; ordinary habeas procedural and merits requirements remain distinct.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Peyton v. Rowe, 391 U.S. 54: extends its aggregate treatment from a future segment to a completed first segment of a still-unexpired consecutive series when invalidation advances release.
- Maleng v. Cook, 490 U.S. 488: distinguishes a wholly expired independent sentence and preserves the rule that collateral consequences alone do not supply custody under that conviction.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the Fifth Circuit's jurisdictional dismissal and remand for remaining procedural and merits review, including review of the district court's merits denial. No conviction is invalidated, writ awarded or immediate release ordered.

## Opening authority: Parke v. Raley, 506 U.S. 20 (1992)

Source: `state/holdings/criminal-procedure.md`.

#### Parke v. Raley, 506 U.S. 20 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-719.

**Decided:** December 1, 1992.

##### Initial production burden

**Holding:** In Kentucky’s collateral challenge to a final counseled conviction used for sentence enhancement, due process permits the Commonwealth to prove the judgment and require the defendant to produce evidence of infringement or procedural irregularity. If the defendant refutes regularity, the Commonwealth must affirmatively establish constitutional validity under Kentucky’s procedure; a missing old transcript alone, without alleged official misconduct, does not establish invalidity.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight votes at this level. Blackmun agrees with the result in a separate writing limited to sentencing.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), distinguished as direct review without weakening its valid-waiver requirement. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 468–469 (1938), applied to collateral regularity. Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), preserved for constitutionally defective uncounseled convictions. Medina v. California (June 22, 1992), distinguished: its narrow competence-burden holding does not establish a controlling categorical ban on practical due-process analysis.

##### Measure of proof

**Holding:** Due process does not require the clear-and-convincing extrarecord proof demanded by the Sixth Circuit to establish validity of an old guilty plea in this collateral sentencing proceeding. Kentucky’s retention of the ultimate persuasion burden does not entail that heightened constitutional quantum; the fact of prior conviction and the validity inquiry remain distinct.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight votes at this level. Blackmun agrees with the result in a separate writing limited to sentencing.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Boykin is not extended into a clear-and-convincing collateral proof requirement. Zerbst supports the distinction between constitutional validity and a universally heightened burden. The Sixth Circuit’s Dunn v. Simmons, 877 F.2d 1275 (1989), cannot govern insofar as it demands that constitutional quantum and shifts the entire production burden solely because no transcript survives.

##### The 1981 plea

**Holding:** Under then-applicable 28 U.S.C. §2254(d), a competent state court’s factual determination, evidenced by written findings, an opinion or other reliable and adequate written indicia after a merits hearing involving the applicant and the State receives a presumption of correctness subject to the conditions and exceptions below; the ultimate federal question whether a plea was knowing and voluntary remains independently determined. On the established earlier warnings, contemporaneous 1981 charge form and jury warning, representation, education, unimpaired condition and testimony here, Raley’s 1981 plea was knowing and voluntary and the Sixth Circuit’s conditional new-hearing remedy is unwarranted. The presumption does not govern if the applicant establishes, it otherwise appears, or the State admits that (1) the state hearing did not resolve the merits of the factual dispute; (2) its factfinding procedure was inadequate to afford a full and fair hearing; (3) material facts were not adequately developed; (4) the state court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction or jurisdiction over the applicant; (5) an indigent applicant was denied appointed counsel in deprivation of his constitutional right; (6) the applicant did not receive a full, fair and adequate hearing; or (7) the applicant was otherwise denied due process in that proceeding. Under paragraph (8), the presumption also does not govern when the pertinent state record is produced and the federal court, considering that record as a whole, concludes that the factual determination lacks fair record support. At a federal evidentiary hearing, once the state factual determination is duly proved, and absent those circumstances, the applicant must establish its error by convincing evidence. That federal factual-review burden is distinct from Kentucky’s allocation and measure of proof of a prior plea’s constitutional validity. Inferences receive deference only when fairly supported by the facts; no presumption attaches to the ultimate federal legal conclusion.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight votes at this level. Blackmun agrees with the result in a separate writing limited to sentencing.

**Current-force modification:** The original statutory factual-presumption and independent-legal-review analysis remains controlling for proceedings governed by former §2254. AEDPA §104 replaces that scheme: new §2254(e)(1) presumes a state factual determination correct and places on the applicant the burden of rebuttal by clear and convincing evidence. New §2254(d) separately forbids granting relief on a claim adjudicated on the merits in state court unless the decision was contrary to or unreasonably applied clearly established federal law determined by this Court, or rested on an unreasonable factual determination in light of the state-court evidence. These new conditions are not the former eight exceptions; enactment supplies no judicial construction of their terms and does not reclassify the underlying constitutional question as a historical fact. The April 24 source admission does not decide this amendment's temporal application to a particular pending proceeding; the original adjudication is unchanged.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Marshall v. Lonberger, 459 U.S. 422, 431–438 (1983), applied to supported historical inferences and independent legal review. Boykin and North Carolina v. Alford, 400 U.S. 25, 31 (1970), applied to an informed voluntary choice. No later habeas deference standard enters.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The ultimate federal validity of the plea remains independently determined. No universal collateral proof quantum or categorical prohibition on collateral review is adopted. Kentucky's separate proof of the fact of prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt and prosecution burdens on the present offense remain. Earlier experience alone does not establish knowing waiver.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the Sixth Circuit conditional-writ judgment concerning the 1981 plea; the actual evidentiary showing warrants no new hearing. The unchallenged 1979 determination is undisturbed.

**Later-authority backlinks:** Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996, Pub. L. 104-132, Title I, enacted April 24, 1996: §104, replacement factual-presumption rules and the new state-merits relief conditions. This is a noncase-law change, not an overruling or new judicial holding.

## Opening authority: Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738

Source: `state/holdings/criminal-procedure.md`.

#### Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-8556.

**Decided:** 1994-06-06.

##### This Guidelines use of a valid fine-only misdemeanor is permissible

**Authority of the judgment:** The Sixth Circuit's sentence is affirmed, 5–4, by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. No complete controlling rationale exists under Marks. The express shared permission below does not make Souter a fifth vote for general overruling of Baldasar or for the plurality's warning rule.

**Independently majority-supported proposition:** An uncounseled misdemeanor conviction valid under Scott because no imprisonment was imposed may supply the otherwise authorized criminal-history point in the Guidelines setting presented here: the later prosecution is counseled and the sentencing framework provides a genuine opportunity to contest reliability, relevance and significant overrepresentation through its lawful procedures, including a permitted §4A1.3 departure. This limited permission neither makes a departure mandatory nor adopts a general rule for a scheme that conclusively requires enhanced incarceration without that opportunity.

**Proposition-level authority:** O'Connor's plurality, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, permits this application under a broader valid-conviction rule; Souter's concurrence in the judgment expressly permits this Guidelines application on the narrower reliability-and-opportunity ground. Five Justices support the stated permission at this level; no complete rationale has five joins, and Souter supplies no fifth vote to overrule Baldasar generally.

**Current force:** Controlling only at the precise scope of this express majority agreement; no complete rationale is added by combining the writings.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Under the applicable November 1990 Guidelines, §4A1.1(c) supplies one point per otherwise includable prior sentence outside (a) and (b), capped at four points; (a) gives three for imprisonment over one year and one month, and (b) two for imprisonment of at least sixty days not within (a). A prior sentence follows adjudication of guilt for conduct outside the instant offense. Section 4A1.2(e) counts over-one-year-and-one-month imprisonment imposed within fifteen years of offense commencement or producing incarceration during that period; other sentences count if imposed within ten years; older sentences outside those rules are excluded. Commencement includes relevant conduct; separate juvenile periods are not decided. Note 5 expressly counts DUI rather than minor traffic offenses; no other offense-classification or timing objection is presented. Note 6 excludes already reversed, vacated or constitutionally invalidated convictions without forbidding lawful consideration of underlying conduct. Section 4A1.3 permits departure on reliable information that the category significantly overrepresents seriousness or future-crime likelihood, using a lower category as reference, but not below Category I's lower limit on that ground. A prior arrest record itself cannot support the departure inquiry. Nichols made no such departure request. Point arithmetic is not optional, departure is not mandatory, and no independent proof of the DUI conduct or hypothetical departure is found.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Scott v. Illinois, 440 U.S. 367 (1979): preserves the actual-imprisonment boundary for the original appointed-counsel requirement; the fine-only conviction is treated as valid.
- Baldasar v. Illinois, 446 U.S. 222 (1980): its particular judgment and fractured reasoning remain; only four Justices vote for general overruling, and the Court permits the present Guidelines application without deciding a conclusive mandatory-enhancement scheme.
- Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 (1977): does not convert Blackmun's original-invalidity rationale in Baldasar into five votes for the valid-but-unavailable rationale, or make four present overruling votes five.
- Stinson v. United States, May 3, 1993: preserves commentary's authority subject to constitutional, statutory and Guideline limits; commentary does not itself answer the constitutional objection.
- Custis v. United States, May 23, 1994: distinguishes the ACCA collateral-attack channel from this challenge to a Scott-valid conviction's subsequent use; the counsel-deprivation exception is unchanged.
- Parke v. Raley, December 1, 1992, and Godinez v. Moran, June 24, 1993: preserve their distinct proof/regularity and actual-waiver rules; neither establishes waiver or nonindigency here.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No complete rationale commands five votes under Marks. Souter's express permission for the defined Guidelines application is within the plurality's broader permission, but the four do not adopt his conditions as universally necessary. Baldasar's judgment and fractured authority remain; four votes do not overrule it or establish the plurality's broad future-enhancement-warning rule. This is not Custis's challenge to constitutional validity, and no waiver or nonindigency finding is made.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Sixth Circuit is affirmed, 5–4. The 235-month sentence and eight-year supervised-release term remain undisturbed; no DUI-point deduction, vacation of the Georgia fine, new hearing, release or fixed 25-month reduction is ordered. The waiver and indigency alternatives are not decided.

## Opening authority: Almendarez-Torres v. United States

#### Almendarez-Torres v. United States

**Docket or dockets:** No. 96-6839.

**Decided:** 1998-03-24.

##### Section 1326(b)(2) requires an aggravated-offense allegation before its higher maximum may apply

**Holding:** The prior aggravated-felony conviction specified in §1326(b)(2) is an element of the aggravated unlawful-reentry offense. It must be alleged in the federal indictment and, unless validly admitted as an element of the charged offense, proved to the jury beyond a reasonable doubt; an admission to the fact of a prior conviction does not cure its omission from this indictment, so the existing conviction permits no sentence above §1326(a)'s two-year maximum.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part I of Scalia's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg. These five expressly adopt the statutory rule, its ordinary element consequences and the remedy. Four explain the construction through constitutional avoidance; Stone-Zsela gives a definitive constitutional ground separately. No majority adopts a general constitutional rule governing all facts that increase a statutory maximum.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The rule is statutory. No majority decides a general constitutional rule for maximum-increasing facts or a constitutional prior-conviction exception. The admitted convictions remain valid; neither subsidiary proof questions nor facts that leave the maximum unchanged are decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The enhanced sentence is vacated and the judgment reversed and remanded for sentencing within the two-year maximum on this indictment and plea. The plea is not withdrawn and no acquittal follows. The availability of a properly charged further proceeding is reserved.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Hamling v. United States:** Applied to require an indictment alleging each element of the aggravated offense.
- **United States v. Gaudin:** Applied after statutory classification; its jury-and-proof rule for actual elements remains controlling without deciding all sentencing classifications.
- **McMillan v. Pennsylvania:** Distinguished because its mandatory minimum left the statutory maximum unchanged; its limited holding remains intact.
- **United States v. Watts / United States v. Putra:** Preserved for ordinary sentencing within punishment authorized by the conviction; it does not resolve this higher-maximum classification.
- **Custis v. United States:** Distinguished as a collateral-review decision; the existing limits on attacking prior convictions remain intact.
- **Old Chief v. United States:** Applied to the distinction between element status and the form of adequate prior-conviction proof; no broader compulsory-stipulation rule is created.

## Earlier published decision: Custis v. United States

Source: `terms/OT1993/output/OT_1993CHUNK5.md`.

### Custis v. United States, No. 93-5209

Merits decision, 1994-05-23

The Court affirms the Fourth Circuit, 6–3, rejecting the present constitutional attacks on state convictions at sentencing under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA). Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg support affirmance; Justices Blackmun, Stevens and Souter dissent. The 235-month sentence remains intact against this objection, while available review of the original convictions and a possible later application through an authorized procedure remain.

Reported at 511 U.S. 485. Argued February 28, 1994; decided 1994-05-23. On certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, 988 F.2d 1355, which affirmed the enhanced sentence. The question is whether these constitutional attacks on prior state convictions may be heard at ACCA sentencing.

Justice O'Connor delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg on both the statutory and constitutional grounds and the limited reservation of later relief. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela also files an unjoined concurrence explaining the review channels and their conditions. Justice Souter dissents, joined by Justices Blackmun and Stevens, and would remand for adjudication of properly raised constitutional attacks.

Under 18 U.S.C. §924(e), a defendant may not use the federal sentencing proceeding to litigate these constitutional attacks on operative state convictions for ineffective assistance and involuntary counseled pleas. The statute does not supply a general collateral-review procedure; the distinct exception for an unconstitutional complete denial of appointed counsel remains, as do objections to whether the enhancement itself satisfies its statutory requirements.

The six Justices joining O'Connor's Opinion of the Court adopt this statutory ground.

The Court holds that an attack on a predicate conviction's constitutional validity must be distinguished from an objection to the federal enhancement's own requirements. Section 924(e) identifies previous convictions, while §921(a)(20) recognizes expungement, setting aside, pardon and civil-rights restoration under its express conditions. Congress expressly supplied a procedure for challenging convictions under the different enhancement scheme in 21 U.S.C. §851(c); it supplied no comparable general procedure here. Lewis v. United States distinguishes an operative conviction from an unresolved collateral objection, although its firearms-disability setting does not decide every sentencing question. Beecham v. United States identifies the jurisdiction whose law governs the removal of a conviction's effect; it does not create a collateral-review forum. Custis's Maryland predicates remained operative. The importance of ineffective-assistance and involuntary-plea claims does not itself create authority to decide them at this sentencing. This conclusion does not find those convictions constitutionally sound, import §851's procedure into the ACCA, or prevent challenges to identification, counting, statutory exclusion, or classification of a predicate.

Lewis v. United States, 445 U.S. 55 (1980), supplies the distinction between existing conviction status and an unresolved collateral challenge; its civil-disability rationale is not extended to every sentencing defect.

Beecham v. United States, May 16, 1994, retains its rule selecting the convicting jurisdiction for §921(a)(20), without adding an ACCA collateral-review procedure.

The Constitution does not require the ACCA sentencing court to hear Custis's ineffective-assistance and involuntary-counseled-plea attacks, but a conviction obtained through complete denial of appointed counsel in violation of the constitutional entitlement, without valid waiver, may be challenged there. Custis may pursue available review of the state convictions; successful invalidation permits an application concerning the enhancement through an authorized procedure, without a promised reopening or particular result.

The same six Justices join O'Connor's separate constitutional ground and its express remedial reservation; the three dissenters would allow the present attacks under their statutory construction.

The Court preserves the special treatment of an unconstitutional complete deprivation of counsel. Gideon v. Wainwright establishes the appointment right, and Burgett v. Texas and United States v. Tucker prohibit the pertinent subsequent reliance on convictions obtained in violation of that right. Custis alleges deficient representation and defects in counseled pleas, rather than that distinct deprivation. Extending sentencing review to those claims would require collateral inquiries into older proceedings without a statutory procedure authorizing them. Parke v. Raley recognizes the regularity of final judgments while permitting appropriate collateral challenges; it does not establish either universal validity or an additional forum. Custis remained in state custody, so review of the convictions through state procedures and authorized federal habeas remained pertinent. If he obtains invalidation, he may apply for relief concerning the enhanced sentence. The Court decides neither the disposition of that application nor a materially different case in which no meaningful lawful review opportunity existed. It adjudicates the channel, not the merits of the Maryland claims.

Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), retains the constitutional entitlement to appointed counsel and the boundary for valid waiver.

Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), and United States v. Tucker, 404 U.S. 443 (1972), preserve the exception for unconstitutional complete deprivation of counsel; they do not require the broader sentencing forum sought here.

Parke v. Raley, December 1, 1992, retains the rebuttable presumption of regularity and lawful collateral proof rules without deciding whether this forum is available.

No precedent is overruled.

Section 921(a)(20) excludes the identified antitrust, unfair-trade, restraint-of-trade and similar business-regulation offenses, and state misdemeanors punishable by two years or less. Conviction status is determined under the law of the jurisdiction in which the proceedings occurred. Its removal clause provides: “Any conviction which has been expunged, or set aside or for which a person has been pardoned or has had civil rights restored shall not be considered a conviction for purposes of this chapter, unless such pardon, expungement, or restoration of civil rights expressly provides that the person may not ship, transport, possess, or receive firearms.” No such removal event is established for these predicates. These conditions concern which convictions count; they do not themselves create a general sentencing forum for constitutional invalidation.

The channel limitation leaves the underlying claims undecided. It does not bar objections to identity, qualifying predicates, exclusions already effective under §921(a)(20), counting or classification. It preserves the qualifying complete-denial-of-counsel exception and does not decide expired-custody or no-meaningful-review cases.

Stone-Zsela concurs, joining the Court's judgment and both controlling grounds. He emphasizes that a meaningful opportunity to challenge a conviction carries the obligation to use the procedures the law makes available in their proper sequence. Rose v. Lundy requires ordinary state exhaustion before authorized federal habeas review, while the governing exhaustion law preserves exceptions when state corrective procedures are absent or ineffective. An adverse prediction is not itself unavailability. Coleman v. Thompson separately governs procedural default, including applicable cause-and-prejudice and miscarriage-of-justice exceptions; exhaustion cannot erase a default or establish entitlement to relief. Maleng v. Cook requires actual custody jurisdiction, rather than assuming that continuing consequences always suffice. None of these distinctions makes this sentencing an additional appeal after unsuccessful collateral litigation. Custis's continuing custody matters, but the Court has made no finding that he failed every required step. A later application following invalidation must identify jurisdiction, the authorized procedural vehicle, the effect on the remaining predicates, and the available remedy. The present sentence is not automatically stayed. Stone-Zsela reserves the case of a diligent defendant who never had a meaningful lawful opportunity to present the constitutional claim. Those elaborations do not impose new exhaustion findings or decide future applications.

Souter dissents, joined by Blackmun and Stevens. Congress legislated against Burgett and Tucker's concern with reliable sentencing predicates. They read the ACCA to permit properly raised constitutional attacks, subject to lawful collateral proof rules, rather than drawing a categorical forum exclusion from silence or §851's more detailed mechanism. Lewis addressed a disability attached to conviction status and need not control additional imprisonment. Constitutional avoidance supports that statutory reading without deciding every asserted constitutional entitlement. They would remand for adjudication of the attacks, not declare either conviction invalid, impose Boykin's original-review burden on collateral proceedings, or order an automatic sentence reduction.

**Source notes:** The complete official [United States Reports, 511 U.S. 485](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511485/usrep511485.pdf) supplies the record, statutory comparison and procedural posture. The account makes no merits finding on either Maryland conviction.

The sentence remains operative. Custis may pursue legally available review of the state convictions and, following successful invalidation, seek relief through an authorized federal procedure. No stay, automatic reopening, fixed reduced sentence or right to repeated merits review is ordered.

## Current-term public authority: Fiore v. White — 2001-01-09

### Fiore v. White — 2001-01-09

Authority: [controlling holdings](../records/Fiore_v_White_merits_2001-01-09.md#holdings); [precedent treatment](../records/Fiore_v_White_merits_2001-01-09.md#precedent-treatment); [operative procedure](../records/Fiore_v_White_merits_2001-01-09.md#procedure-after-action). The following holding and precedent blocks are copied from the Record; the Record controls.

#### I. The existing missing element defeats both challenged convictions

**Holding and operative rule:** A State may not sustain a conviction when its authoritative account of the law in force at the relevant time identifies a necessary element and the State concedes it produced no evidence of that element. Pennsylvania's clarification that actual absence of the required permit was always an element of the owner/operator offense therefore establishes a due-process violation in both of Fiore's challenged no-permit convictions; deviation from a permit's terms cannot substitute for its absence.

**Authority:** The per curiam opinion is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt both holdings and the bounded reversal and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** In re Winship requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every element. Jackson v. Virginia enforces that protection against a conviction unsupported on a necessary element. State law identifies the offense; the federal Constitution requires its elements to be proved. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court's August 21, 2000 answer establishes that Commonwealth v. Scarpone clarified the statute's existing meaning, including its meaning when Fiore's convictions became final. The Commonwealth does not dispute that Fiore possessed a permit. The asserted departure from its conditions thus cannot establish the different no-permit element.

The Third Circuit's premise that a new state rule was unavailable on collateral review cannot survive the authoritative answer. The Court decides no general obligation to apply genuinely new state constructions retroactively: there is no new construction here. Nor can the seriousness of other environmental misconduct supply the missing element. The distinct authorization, transportation-license and permit-compliance provisions do not become substitute offenses in this dispute. The ruling covers the no-permit conviction from each trial, not the other convictions among Fiore's sixty convictions.

#### II. The constitutional ruling and the conditions for the writ remain distinct

**Holding and operative rule:** The Third Circuit's rejection of the constitutional claim is reversed and the case remanded to resolve any properly preserved, actually undecided statutory prerequisite to habeas relief under the applicable post-April 24, 1996 regime. If those prerequisites permit relief, the habeas order must remove both invalid no-permit convictions and end custody attributable to them, preserving independent convictions and lawful custody.

**Authority:** The same unanimous per curiam opinion directly adopts this remand and the following statutory conditions; the Court makes no finding that an unresolved gate is satisfied or violated.

**Controlling explanation:** Lindh v. Murphy distinguishes applications filed before enactment from later applications; Fiore's later filing is not exempt from the applicable amendments. Under section 2254(b), relief requires exhaustion, absence of an available state corrective process, or circumstances making that process ineffective to protect the applicant's rights. Merits denial despite nonexhaustion is permitted, not required; the State waives exhaustion only through counsel's express waiver. The appellate court expressly left exhaustion and state merits adjudication undecided.

If the relevant claim was actually adjudicated on its merits in state proceedings, section 2254(d) requires the statutory inquiry. Williams v. Taylor preserves the independent contrary-to and unreasonable-application clauses, the objective legal standard and the restriction to this Court's holdings at the actual state merits decision. Winship and Jackson are preexisting rules, not rules originating in today's decision. The separate statutory factual-review provisions retain their own triggers and force. A finding of constitutional error cannot silently resolve those questions; conversely, remand supplies no permission to reopen the conceded permit fact, the state court's authoritative answer or the decided constitutional issue. The court below must resolve a real preserved prerequisite and implement the resulting conviction-specific relief, rather than infer an unconditional all-count writ.

**Precedent treatment:**

- In re Winship: applies its proof-beyond-a-reasonable-doubt requirement to an existing state-law element; the seriousness of other conduct cannot supply the omitted proof.
- Jackson v. Virginia: applies its protection against conviction without evidence sufficient to establish each necessary element.
- Fiore v. White, November 30, 1999: completes the reserved federal inquiry after the authoritative state answer; the earlier certification itself supplied neither state-law meaning nor a federal merits judgment.
- Lindh v. Murphy: preserves its filing-date distinction; this post-enactment application falls outside its pre-enactment exemption.

### Glover v. United States — 2001-01-09

Authority: [controlling law](../records/Glover_v_United_States_merits_2001-01-09.md#holdings); [precedent treatment](../records/Glover_v_United_States_merits_2001-01-09.md#precedent-treatment); [operative procedure](../records/Glover_v_United_States_merits_2001-01-09.md#procedure-after-action). The following public holding and precedent blocks are copied from the Record; the Record controls.

#### No minimum duration or offense-level threshold for sentencing prejudice

**Holding and operative rule:** When deficient representation allegedly causes a longer prison sentence, Strickland prejudice requires a reasonable probability of a shorter lawful sentence, sufficient to undermine confidence in the result; it contains no additional minimum measured in months of confinement or in offense levels. An alleged six-to-twenty-one-month increase caused by a two-level sentencing error therefore cannot be rejected as constitutionally insignificant, but deficient performance, the underlying error and the required causal probability must still be established.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt the rule answering both presented questions and the bounded remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Sixth Amendment protects a defendant against imprisonment attributable to constitutionally deficient representation. Strickland v. Washington supplies the reasonable-probability inquiry. It asks whether there is a reasonable probability of a different lawful result with competent assistance, sufficient to undermine confidence; it does not license a court to disregard an otherwise established loss because that loss is measured in fewer months or fewer sentencing levels.

Lockhart v. Fretwell excludes a benefit the substantive law does not recognize. It preserves ordinary prejudice analysis for a lawful sentencing benefit and adds no independent unfairness requirement. Williams v. Taylor confirms that distinction. Here the asserted benefit is application of the lawful sentencing calculation. Whether that calculation actually favors Glover must be decided, but its potentially modest effect is not a separate reason to reject the claim. An offense-level threshold makes the same error as a duration threshold: it substitutes the size of the alleged mistake for the probability that a lawful shorter sentence was lost.

The United States offers adequate performance, correct grouping and possible other sentencing consequences as grounds for affirmance. Those contentions do not sustain the categorical rule on which the Court of Appeals relied. Both questions assume deficient performance and calculation error; neither presents the whole assistance claim. The lower courts must determine the unresolved elements and the availability and merits of any properly preserved independent defense. Agreement that the added threshold is wrong establishes neither actual ineffective assistance nor entitlement to a particular sentence. The judgment is reversed and the case remanded, without an immediate sentence reduction or release.

#### Precedent treatment

- Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984): applies its separate performance and reasonable-probability requirements to an alleged increase in imprisonment; neither requirement acquires a minimum-duration or minimum-offense-level condition.
- Lockhart v. Fretwell, 506 U.S. 364 (1993): applies its distinction between an unavailable substantive benefit and a lawful sentencing protection; its limited unavailable-benefit rule remains intact and does not support a significance threshold.
- Williams v. Taylor (Terry Williams), 529 U.S. 362 (2000): applies its ordinary lawful-result prejudice rule. Its state-prisoner statutory review and actual capital-sentencing findings do not decide this federal prisoner's unresolved performance and calculation issues.

#### Law after decision

In a noncapital sentencing-assistance claim alleging additional confinement, ordinary Strickland prejudice governs without an extra minimum measured in time or offense levels. The decision expressly rejects both numerical barriers while preserving the requirement of deficient performance and a reasonable probability of a shorter lawful sentence. A changed range or an attorney mistake alone establishes neither requirement.

Correct grouping, counsel's performance, the full lawful sentencing calculation and the availability and merits of preserved independent defenses remain for lower-court determination because they lie outside the two presented questions and were not resolved by the judgment under review. These are applications and remaining claim determinations under the governing rule, not an unresolved legal standard for how much additional imprisonment counts. No separate legal question within the two prejudice questions remains undecided.
