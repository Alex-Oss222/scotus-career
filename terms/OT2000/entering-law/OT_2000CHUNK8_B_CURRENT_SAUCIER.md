### Saucier v. Katz — 2001-06-18

Authority: [binding mandate and law](../records/Saucier_v_Katz_merits_2001-06-18.md#holdings); [precedent treatment](../records/Saucier_v_Katz_merits_2001-06-18.md#precedent-treatment).

#### The binding remand instruction and its limited authority

**Operative rule:** The Court of Appeals must reconsider Saucier's claimed personal immunity under the existing objective qualified-immunity inquiry, separately from the Fourth Amendment force merits and on the facts properly assumed on interlocutory review. The judgment leaves both a grant and a denial of immunity available under that inquiry; it decides neither outcome.

**Authority:** Souter announces the judgment. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer join that exact mandate with him, supplying five votes. They do not join his complete rationale. This is a binding instruction in this case, not adoption by those five Justices of a general second reasonableness screen or a mandatory merits-first sequence.

**Controlling explanation and application:** The Court returns the claim for the specified legal inquiry without resolving the disputed historical account. Hunter v. Bryant supplies judicial immunity determination on supported summary-judgment assumptions. Johnson v. Jones and Behrens v. Pelletier exclude appellate reassessment of factual sufficiency; Sterling v. Borough of Minersville applies that boundary specifically to disputed personal participation. Those existing constraints prevent treating the petition's Parker-only account as an appellate finding. The lower courts may decide a separable legal question on the proper assumptions and leave genuinely material factual disputes to the proper forum. The competing demand for immediate immunity does not carry the judgment, and the mandate does not establish the contrary immunity result. No common further justification for the five votes is attributed to the Court.

#### Existing law, separate rationales and questions not decided

Harlow v. Fitzgerald and Anderson v. Creighton remain in force at their existing scope, as carried through Hunter, Elder v. Holloway, Wilson v. Layne and Denius v. Dunlap. Graham v. Connor continues to govern force merits. The Court adopts neither Stone-Zsela's replacement defense nor the three-Justice concurrence's force-specific objection as a new rule of law. Souter's ordinary merits-first proposal likewise lacks a controlling judgment-majority rationale here.

No controlling rationale can be derived under Marks v. United States: retaining a distinct objective defense, replacing it with actual authoritative reliance, and rejecting an overlapping force-specific reasonableness screen are not nested rules yielding the same results. Agreement on this remand does not make one a logical subset of the others. The judgment governs the parties; no broader unified rule is inferred from the separate writings or from agreement between Souter and the dissenters.

The Court makes no ultimate finding of constitutional violation, reasonable force, participation, immunity, causation or compensable loss. The First Amendment, false-arrest/probable-cause, supervisory and prospective-relief branches are outside the granted inquiry and are not reopened. The existing federal-officer damages channel is assumed for this review; no new cause of action, institutional liability or compensation scheme is established. These limits leave factual and remedial work under existing law, not an entitlement to either party's preferred result.

**Precedent treatment:**

- **Graham v. Connor:** Its complete, objective, on-scene force inquiry remains the merits rule, including the circumstances and reasonable factual perceptions confronting the officer. No physical-injury prerequisite, least-dangerous-tactic command or categorical security exception is added.
- **Harlow v. Fitzgerald:** Its existing objective personal-immunity defense remains in force; a separate proposal to replace it is not the Court's law.
- **Anderson v. Creighton:** Its particularized legal-notice inquiry remains available under the remand; neither an identical-case requirement nor proof of actual authoritative reliance replaces it.
- **Hunter v. Bryant:** Its judicial legal inquiry under proper summary-judgment assumptions governs the return; the court may not choose a more favorable competing factual account for the officer.
- **Elder v. Holloway:** Its consideration of all relevant preconduct law and ordinary preservation limits remain; later decisions cannot supply September 24, 1994 notice.
- **Johnson v. Jones:** Its exclusion of evidentiary-sufficiency disputes from immediate immunity review remains applicable to disputed participation.
- **Behrens v. Pelletier:** Its distinction between a separable assumed-fact legal defense and a factual-sufficiency appeal remains; the present judgment creates no additional appellate jurisdiction.
- **Sterling v. Borough of Minersville:** Its unanimous participation-review rule applies to the supported two-officer account. Its different confidentiality and April 1997 notice holdings do not answer this force claim or supply earlier notice.
- **Plakas v. Drinski:** Its contextual force analysis remains; its supported immediate armed attack and absence of a contrary material account do not establish the facts here. Its later decision supplies no September 1994 notice.
- **Wilson v. Layne:** Its distinction between constitutional violation and conduct-date personal immunity remains, without requiring a factual twin; its 1999 decision supplies no 1994 notice.
- **Denius v. Dunlap:** Its retention of the existing objective defense remains; its distinct disclosure claims do not settle this force application, and its 2001 decision supplies no 1994 notice.
- **Conn v. Gabbert:** Its reservation of mandatory immunity sequencing is not displaced by a controlling sequence holding here.
- **Marks v. United States:** No common narrower rationale is identified among the materially different judgment-supporting writings; it supplies no basis to combine them into a new rule.

**Resulting law:** Existing objective qualified immunity and Graham's force standard remain operative. This case must return for the distinct inquiry specified in the mandate, with the legal-notice inquiry fixed to September 24, 1994 and the proper interlocutory factual assumptions preserved. No general mandatory merits-first rule or authoritative-reliance replacement is adopted. No precedent is overruled. The absence of a common new immunity rationale does not unsettle the existing factual-review limits or the binding remand instruction. Personal immunity remains distinct from constitutional legality, actual participation, institutional responsibility and remedy.


### Saucier v. Katz — 2001-06-18

Published positions: [writings and scope](../records/Saucier_v_Katz_merits_2001-06-18.md#separate-writings). Each position below is noncontrolling; the exact five-Justice mandate governs the return.

**Souter, announcing the judgment.** Souter considers the merits and immunity inquiries legally distinct: an officer may perceive the facts correctly yet reasonably misunderstand the particular constitutional limit. Harlow and Anderson preserve that legal-error protection; Graham's allowance for reasonable factual perceptions does not itself decide it. General prohibition of excessive force is insufficient by itself, but a materially identical precedent is not indispensable. Hunter and Elder require the proper adverse factual assumptions and the full relevant conduct-date law. Souter would have ordinary lower-court immunity adjudication first determine whether those assumed facts establish a constitutional violation and, if so, whether the particular right was sufficiently established, so that the constitutional rule can develop. He recognizes the limits of the granted review here and assumes a possible violation; he does not require decision of a constitutional issue outside review or an advisory ruling. That sequencing view is his own, not a rule adopted by the judgment majority. Unlike the dissenters, he leaves the first distinct application to the lower court and directs neither immunity nor its denial. Unlike the other concurring Justices, he retains the distinct legal-error rationale in this force setting.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in the judgment.** The Chief Justice accepts the exact existing-law remand while proposing a different personal defense. He would displace Harlow and Anderson only insofar as they protect an official merely because the violated right was insufficiently clearly established, preserving their compatible particularized analysis and lawful summary-judgment requirements. First, the complete constitutional rule would account for urgency, reasonably perceived danger, reasonable factual mistakes and operational discretion. If a violation were established, the official would bear the burden of identifying binding precedent or enacted authority directly authorizing the conduct, genuine reliance on it and a reasonable basis for treating it as valid and applicable. An instruction accurately conveying that authority could implement reliance. Legal silence, lack of a materially identical case, department custom, an unsupported supervisor's assurance and general job authority would not suffice.

In his view, legitimate protection against unexpected legal change concerns affirmative authoritative permission. The existing defense's predictability and litigation-control benefits do not justify withholding an otherwise available remedy from the first claimant establishing a particular application of settled protection. Graham already accounts for the officer's practical circumstances; those same factual allowances should not excuse wrongful force again. On Katz's supported account, an unnecessary shove of an already controlled, unresisting demonstrator violates the force standard without requiring physical injury, while necessary proportionate security removal remains lawful. Actual necessity, resistance, perceptions and participation remain disputed. The record identifies no binding precedent or enactment directly authorizing the alleged unnecessary shove; general security authority is insufficient.

His proposed defense would govern this pending case, with a fair opportunity for bounded submissions on genuinely new reliance elements. An expectation of immunity from unclear law would not preserve the displaced defense; final judgments and ordinary preservation and preclusion rules would remain. The proposal concerns this personal legal-uncertainty defense and materially equivalent claims, not function-specific absolute immunity, sovereign immunity, proper defendants, causation, injury or the availability of a cause of action. Failure of the proposed defense would establish neither a constitutional violation, damages, institutional liability nor a new cause of action; each independent requirement remains. These are separate views: the Court's mandate imposes no reliance burden, transition rule or additional submissions requirement.

**Ginsburg, joined by Stevens and Breyer, concurring in the judgment.** These Justices join the exact vacatur and open application remand while maintaining that Graham already tests the practical choices and reasonable mistakes available to an officer using force. They reject a second overlapping reasonableness shelter for force found objectively unreasonable under that complete standard. That objection is confined to this excessive-force setting; they retain ordinary Harlow protection elsewhere and do not endorse the Chief Justice's general actual-reliance replacement.

The supported unnecessary shared shove of a controlled, unresisting person cannot be dismissed by treating every degree of force as justified by the initial need for security removal. Johnson and Sterling prevent reappraisal of the participation evidence on this appeal. Plakas permits early merits judgment on a properly supported no-violation account, but its immediate armed attack without a contrary material account is absent here. The continuous movement into the van is relevant context, not a factual answer to the alleged unnecessary force. Their separate analysis does not alter their vote for the mandate: neither immunity nor denial, automatic trial, liability or damages is directed.

**Kennedy, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, dissenting.** These Justices would reverse and direct personal damages immunity while assuming a possible constitutional violation. Harlow and Anderson protect reasonable legal error independently of Graham's merits inquiry. Accepting the plaintiff-favorable two-officer account, they regard the carrying and van entry as a continuous urgent removal from a protected event; the particular boundary of this brief nondeadly force was not sufficiently apparent in September 1994. They recognize the strongest contrary point: a controlled, unresisting person's forceful van entry may lack additional demonstrated need. They nevertheless treat this as uncertainty over the degree of force during removal, not a legally unmistakable separate assault after the seizure ended.

Their position does not depend on deciding that Parker acted alone, finding resistance, declaring the force lawful or making injury a prerequisite. Hunter and Sterling preserve the adverse assumed facts; their disagreement concerns the legal conclusion drawn from them. They support an ordinary merits-first approach for the reasons of legal development Souter describes, subject to the actual scope of review. Their agreement with him on that position is not a join in the judgment-announcing opinion and supplies no controlling sequence rule. They reject an open application remand because they would decide the legal defense now.
