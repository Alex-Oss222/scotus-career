### United States v. Alaska, No. 84, Original

Original-jurisdiction decision, June 19, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

United States v. Alaska, No. 84, Original. Argued February 24, 1997; decided June 19, 1997. Original action between the United States and Alaska on exceptions to the Special Master's report, not review of a lower-court judgment. The United States filed in 1979 over Beaufort Sea mineral leasing; Alaska counterclaimed to quiet title within the Petroleum Reserve and Arctic Wildlife Range. Hearings and briefing before the Master followed from 1980 through 1986.

The exceptions concern the coastline used for Alaska's Submerged Lands Act grant, Alaska's claimed ten-mile fringing-island rule in Stefansson Sound, Dinkum Sands' island status, and federal retention of submerged lands within the National Petroleum Reserve and original Arctic Wildlife Range. The Master favored the federal position on the first three issues but Alaska on the Range. The 1957 Range application, the pending-application regulation, statehood and the 1960 final reservation are distinct legal events. No final geographic decree is before the Court for entry today.

All nine Justices participated.

Scalia and Thomas accept that procedural mechanism subject to their different title outcomes. No final metes-and-bounds decree, new survey, oil-accounting award or damages determination is entered.

The Master's recommendations are adopted only insofar as consistent with these rulings.

#### Holdings

##### I. The normal baseline governs this coast

**Holding and operative rule:** Alaska's statutory three-mile submerged-land grant is measured from the Convention's normal low-water baseline, including applicable river and bay closing rules and separate belts of qualifying islands. The evidence establishes no firm and continuing general federal rule converting all waters behind fringing islands with entrances under ten miles into inland waters; the earlier Mississippi Sound judgment does not preclude that conclusion.

**Authority:** Parts I–II of O'Connor's opinion, joined by all nine Justices. This coalition also directly adopts the Dinkum holding.

**Controlling explanation:** United States v. California supplies the Convention framework. Article 4 permits straight baselines in an appropriate island-fringe setting, but does not require their selection; the United States made no such choice here. The international twelve-mile sea does not enlarge the statutory three-mile State grant.

Alaska claims a general federal practice, not specific historic title to Stefansson Sound, so its claim must be tested on that basis. The 1930 assimilation proposal used four-mile closures, its high-seas straits proposal used six-mile entrances, and its inland-sea proposal used ten-mile bay principles. The Chapman Line, Webb letter, Gulf submissions, 1949 materials and 1961 letter do not establish the asserted uniform ten-mile rule. Leasing convenience and avoidance of federal enclaves cannot supply the missing practice.

The 1985 Alabama/Mississippi decision established Mississippi Sound's particular historic status through specific assertions and foreign acquiescence, not the universal formulation now asserted. Preclusion therefore fails even assuming it is available; Mendoza's general rule against nonmutual estoppel against the United States requires no expansion or new original-action exception. The later Alabama/Mississippi and Massachusetts decrees retain their geographic force. The Nome permit decision addresses an artificial extension's added entitlement, not these natural features. No general authority to contract existing state territory or foreclose a different proved inland-water claim is decided.

##### II. Dinkum Sands does not satisfy the island definition

**Holding and operative rule:** A naturally formed feature must ordinarily remain above the agreed mean-high-water datum to qualify as an island under Convention Article 10(1). Dinkum Sands' frequent seasonal submergence below that datum defeats island status and a separate three-mile grant; isolated emergence does not create alternating island entitlement, and abnormal temporary inundation of an otherwise enduring island remains distinct.

**Authority:** Parts I–II, with all nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Article 10's high-tide requirement describes an ordinary characteristic of an island. Its drafting history distinguishes exceptional inundation from an ordinarily shifting gravel-and-ice formation. Borax and the California decree support the mean-high-water datum, averaged over approximately nineteen years, rather than an absolute permanence condition.

The joint monitoring study and subsequent measurements support the Master's finding that Dinkum Sands frequently falls below that datum. The survey found no reliably above-datum elevation; limited readings within the error band and later observed emergence do not establish an ordinarily above-water feature. The 1971 chart and 1979 map relied on an earlier survey, and visual appearance does not displace calibrated elevations. Alaska's argument that Article 10 contains no permanence adjective therefore does not warrant a legal coastline appearing and disappearing with ordinary seasonal changes in this formation. Natural accretion or erosion of an established island presents a different issue. No percentage-of-days test is adopted. Article 11's low-tide-elevation rule supplies no separate entitlement at the reported distances exceeding three miles from the mainland and nearby islands.

##### III. The Petroleum Reserve instruments clearly retain the disputed submerged estate

**Holding and operative rule:** Federal retention requires clear inclusion of the submerged lands and clear intent to defeat future state title for an appropriate public purpose; geography or general purpose alone does not suffice. The 1923 Petroleum Reserve order's boundary and petroleum function, together with Congress's specific Statehood Act treatment of that reserve, satisfy both requirements and retain the disputed submerged estate, under the same demanding inquiry for equal-footing and statutory territorial-sea lands.

**Authority:** Parts III–IV of O'Connor's opinion, directly adopted by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These seven also directly adopt the Range holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Shively recognizes Congress's territorial power for appropriate public purposes. Utah Division and Montana require separate clear inclusion and intent to defeat later state title. The order's ocean-side barrier boundary, closures across small lagoons and express three-mile qualification identify the submerged estate. Excluding submerged portions of the same petroleum deposits would defeat the reserve's national-defense purpose. Unlike Montana's unneeded riverbed or Utah Division's reservoir-control rationale, ownership of these deposits performs the reservation's central work.

Section 11(b) preserves exclusive federal legislative authority over federally owned military lands at admission and specifically names the naval petroleum reserve. Read with its defined estate and petroleum purpose, Congress confirmed continued ownership and ratified submerged inclusion. Legislative jurisdiction alone would not establish title, and §11(b) is not rewritten as a generic submerged-title clause. The specific recognition and operative estate answer the express-retention objection. Section 1313(a)'s requirement is not diluted by a presumption favoring federal ownership merely because the Act is a federal grant. Congress's confirmation makes it unnecessary to resolve the disputed independent reach of the Pickett Act; no broad Executive power to defeat future state title is announced.

##### IV. The prestatehood Range segregation comes within the wildlife exclusion

**Holding and operative rule:** The 1957 application and legally operative temporary-segregation regulation set the specifically described submerged lands apart for the proposed wildlife refuge before statehood. Statehood Act §6(e)'s exclusion of lands withdrawn or otherwise set apart as wildlife refuges therefore retained those lands, although the application was not a completed final reservation; lands outside its boundaries or lacking comparable legal effect are not decided.

**Authority:** Parts III–IV, with the same seven direct joins. Scalia and Thomas neither join the timing/legal-effect rule nor assent to the separate geographic determination.

**Controlling explanation:** Utah Division and Montana require clear inclusion separately from title retention. The changed extreme-low-water boundary, coastal submerged features and stated habitat needs establish inclusion. The application sought protection for polar bears, foxes, seals and whales, including the identified aquatic habitat.

The regulation immediately segregated the land from settlement, location, sale, selection, entry, lease and other public-land disposals, including mining and mineral leasing, only to the extent the proposed reservation, if effected, would prevent them. It left administrative jurisdiction unchanged. Those limits do not remove the presently effective, purpose-specific segregation while the Secretary considers the application. Section 6(e)'s broader alternative reaches land legally set apart in that manner; requiring a completed reservation underreads that alternative. The later choice of all or part of the area does not erase the current segregation of the described land.

Congress's wildlife exclusion supplies clear title-defeating intent; neither general federal ownership nor wildlife purpose alone suffices. Section 6(m)'s incorporation of the Submerged Lands Act performs different work. The 1960 order is not given retroactive effect, and the 1980 enlargement does not fix the original statehood boundary. Alaska's defensive inclusion argument overlaps the application's legal effect and is addressed despite its lack of a separate exception to the Master's geographic discussion. The resulting decree must implement these identified classifications, not assumed new boundaries.

#### Precedent Treatment

- United States v. California (1965): applied for Convention-based baseline definitions and preserved with its artificial-extension qualification; no universal domestic/international boundary identity is adopted.
- United States v. Louisiana (Alabama and Mississippi Boundary Case) (1985): distinguished as a specific historic-title determination, not a necessarily decided general ten-mile island-fringe rule.
- United States v. Mendoza: left within its existing nonmutual-estoppel scope; preclusion fails here without deciding a new original-jurisdiction exception.
- Borax Consolidated v. Los Angeles and the California decree: applied to the mean-high-water datum, not an absolute physical-permanence requirement.
- Shively v. Bowlby: applied to Congress's power to retain territorial submerged lands for appropriate public purposes.
- Utah Division of State Lands v. United States: applied to separate clear inclusion and title-defeating intent; its insufficient reservoir-control showing is distinguished from these instruments.
- Montana v. United States: applied to the strong state-title presumption; its riverbed unnecessary to the reservation's purpose differs from the identified petroleum estate and aquatic habitat here.
- United States v. Alaska, No. 118, Original (1992): preserved for boundary protection attributable to the Nome artificial extension; it does not decide natural-feature or prestatehood-title questions.
- The Alabama/Mississippi supplemental decree (1993) and Massachusetts supplemental decree (1996): preserved at their actual geographic scope; neither supplies an Arctic boundary or a general title test.

#### Law After Decision

The Arctic three-mile grant uses the normal baseline on this record, and Dinkum Sands supplies no island entitlement. Federal title remains in the identified Petroleum Reserve and original Arctic Range submerged estates on their distinct instruments and statutory bases. Clear inclusion and title retention remain separate demanding requirements; no general federal-reservation presumption or broad independent Executive retention power is created. The parties must implement these legal classifications through a proposed decree rather than treating the opinion as a complete geographic schedule.

#### Separate Writings

**Justice Thomas, joined by Justice Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They join the normal-baseline and Dinkum rulings. For the Petroleum Reserve, however, the Submerged Lands Act is the operative grant to this post-1953 State, and its exception requires express retention in terms embracing land under water. An inferred inclusion from exterior boundaries and petroleum purpose cannot replace that requirement. Section 11(b)'s legislative-jurisdiction and existing-ownership language does not expressly retain the submerged estate; congressional ratification cannot cure that defect. They do not deny Congress's constitutional power to retain adequately described lands or extend this statutory analysis to States admitted before the Act.

For the Range, a pending application protects a prospective decision from inconsistent disposal but does not already establish a refuge. The application leaves the eventual acreage for substantive determination, the segregation is limited to effects the proposed reservation would have, and administrative jurisdiction remains unchanged. The phrase otherwise set apart can encompass effective refuge creation by other means without including every pending application. They would overrule the federal exception on that timing and legal-effect ground and decline the unexcepted geographic determination; they make no contrary geographic finding. Their acceptance of an implementing decree is subject to these state-title positions.

The parties must submit a proposed decree conforming to the normal-baseline, Dinkum and reservation-title rulings. The Court retains jurisdiction to effectuate and supplement the determination and related rights. Each party bears its own costs. The case remains in the original-jurisdiction decree process; there is no appellate remand or final metes-and-bounds judgment today.

### Agostini v. Felton and Chancellor, Board of Education of the City of New York v. Felton, Nos. 96-552 and 96-553

Merits decision, June 23, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Agostini v. Felton, No. 96-552, consolidated with Chancellor, Board of Education of the City of New York v. Felton, No. 96-553. Decided June 23, 1997. Argued April 15, 1997, on writs of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit.

The Board and a new group of parents filed motions for Rule 60 relief in October and December 1995. On August 7, 1996, the Second Circuit affirmed denial of relief from the September 26, 1985 permanent injunction implementing Aguilar. The question under Rule 60(b)(5) is whether that continuing onsite prohibition should remain in light of the governing Establishment Clause law and the preserved request to reconsider Aguilar. The two dockets concern the same prospective injunction and share one decision. Public teachers provide supplementary secular Title I instruction to independently eligible children under public control; the injunction requires offsite delivery.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded in both consolidated dockets, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.
**Opposing:** Justices Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Second Circuit judgments affirming denial of relief are vacated. Proceedings return for a district-court order removing the injunction's onsite prohibition to the extent it rests on the categorical ground rejected today. Any genuinely independent basis must be identified and decided under the surviving law; absent such a basis the prohibition must be dissolved. The City must obey the operative decree until judicial modification. Existing lawful services may continue during prompt implementation, and children currently receiving them should continue to receive them with existing statutory duties preserved. No new service entitlement, fixed timetable, damages or repayment is imposed.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–IV | Stone-Zsela | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas in full | Vacate both judgments and remand for tailored prospective relief | Part I: modification of Lemon; Part II: onsite secular-service rule and precise overruling; Part III: present legal change under Rule 60(b)(5); Part IV: tailored removal with retained safeguards and lawful obligations |
| Dissent, Part I | Souter | Stevens and Ginsburg | Would affirm both judgments | Affirmative substantive objection to this public instructional support; Breyer does not join |
| Same dissent, Part II | Souter | Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer | Would affirm both judgments | Retention of precedent and absence of sufficient intervening erosion or factual change |
| Dissent | Ginsburg | Stevens, Souter and Breyer | Would affirm both judgments on an independent procedural ground | Rule 60 review cannot supply this means of rehearing the original constitutional ruling |

#### Holdings

All four holdings are delivered by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela in the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Each has five direct joins at its stated level of generality, including the modification of Lemon in Holding I. No aggregation of separate opinions supplies controlling authority.

##### I. Lemon governs through one inquiry into governmental religious authority and function

**Holding and operative rule:** Lemon remains the governing Establishment Clause test, modified so that purpose, effects and entanglement are evidence within one inquiry: a specific prohibition or distinct doctrine controls when it resolves the conduct; an established permissible relationship defeats the claim on that ground; otherwise a legally sufficient unresolved allegation is decided through attribution and function under ordinary procedure. These are three routes within the updated Lemon test, not freestanding prongs, exemptions from review or an alternative test for uncertain cases.

**Authority:** Part I of the Opinion of the Court; Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas join this modification in full, supplying five direct joins at this level of generality.

**Operative qualifications:** The court identifies the governmental act—an official message, organized event, exercise of supervisory power or expenditure—and the function it performs. For financing, the question is what government purchases or performs; all religious conduct of the recipient need not become government speech. Private-access authorization, religious identity and physical location are relevant in context but are not individually decisive.

Government may not organize worship as its own activity, prescribe religious profession or observance, adopt doctrine as governmental truth, solicit religious adherence through public authority, govern theology or confer civil authority on a religious basis. Denominational classifications retain their distinct strict-scrutiny rule. Independently identifiable secular work in a religious setting may be purchased with enforceable controls against devotional use and diversion. Restoration of private exercise restricted by custody or comparable governmental control remains subject to the applicable accommodation inquiry, not an automatic financing prohibition. Those distinct doctrines retain their own sources and burdens.

**Controlling explanation:** Lemon addresses governmental sponsorship of religion, financing of religious functions and the improper union of civil and religious authority. Its method must identify those acts rather than condemn an independently secular service because officials supervise it to remain secular. Aguilar exposes that defect: location and repeated contact become substitutes for examining what the public program actually does. The Court therefore modifies Lemon's operation while retaining its constitutional concerns.

Purpose and effects help identify the governmental function; institutional relationships show who directs it. Once prohibited conduct is established, actual conversion, religious success or a completed penalty is unnecessary. A secular objective cannot justify prohibited religious means. Conversely, a provider's religious motivation does not make its secular work devotional. Ordinary monitoring of secular performance is not itself establishment, even when repeated; civil control of doctrine and religious control of public authority remain forbidden.

This method requires real operational separation and reliable accountability for funded work, but not invariably separate institutional governance or wholly distinct personnel. Shared structures cannot excuse devotional financing, and they do not automatically defeat a genuinely severable public service. Here public selection, staffing, materials and supervision identify the remedial service to be examined. The school's entire religious mission is not the governmental act.

The dissent's institutional-support objection warrants scrutiny of the service, not a presumption that its secular and religious functions are inseparable. Existing specific holdings continue to govern their established relationships: Kiryas Joel restricts denominational allocation of civil power; Rosenberger permits its controlled publication benefit; Pinette and Chabad distinguish private expression and governmental attribution. The Court does not decide absent voucher, monument, legislative-prayer or accommodation programs, or declare every future application compatible with every preserved precedent.

**Precedent treatment:** Lemon v. Kurtzman remains governing, with its independent purpose, primary-effect and excessive-entanglement prongs modified into the three routes of this single inquiry. The actual holdings of Kiryas Joel, Rosenberger, Pinette, Chabad, Witters and Mueller remain in force; Zobrest supplies no Establishment Clause merits holding.

##### II. Separately administered secular instruction is not forbidden because of its religious-school location

**Holding and operative rule:** The Establishment Clause permits the described supplementary Title I service for children independently selected under neutral public eligibility criteria, delivered by public employees under public supervision with public secular materials, without religious instruction, team teaching or other cooperative instructional activity with religious-school teachers, or participation in worship, and with effective controls against diversion. Religious-school location, presumed inculcation arising from that location, incidental benefit to the school, and ordinary monitoring of that separately administered public service do not by themselves establish unconstitutional sponsorship, devotional financing or institutional control; Aguilar's categorical prohibition and Ball's corresponding Shared Time rationale are displaced at that scope.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court's opinion; the five-Justice coalition stated above directly joins the rule, its secular-control qualifications and its precise treatment of precedent.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the constitutional inquiry must distinguish the public remedial service from the religious school's own instruction. Lemon, as updated in Part I, identifies the governmental act and its religious or secular function, using purpose, effects and institutional relationships as evidence within that inquiry. Here the remedial purpose, independent selection of eligible children, public staffing and supervision, secular materials and restrictions on religious participation identify a supplementary public service. Those safeguards establish this program's severable secular operation; operational separation and reliable accountability do not invariably require wholly distinct personnel or separate institutional governance. Ordinary consultation about a child's secular educational needs is distinct from joint teaching. Neutral eligibility or public employment alone would not excuse devotional instruction or diversion.

Aguilar treated the supervision needed to preserve secular instruction as a reason to prohibit the instruction on religious premises. That categorical inference is unsound: supervising public teachers' secular work is not, without more, governing the school's religious affairs. The absence of a finding of indoctrination does not prove every possible defect absent, but location cannot substitute for the connection between the challenged public service and an actual constitutional objection. Nor does an incidental institutional benefit alone turn this separately controlled service into religious teaching.

These safeguards existed when Aguilar was decided. The legal defect is the categorical inference that confuses secular supervision with religious sponsorship and control, not a newly discovered factual transformation. Continuing expense and impaired access make the consequence substantial; they are not the constitutional rule. Property, contractual, completed-transaction and educational-service interests receive their actual weight. No identified concrete property or completed-transaction entitlement requires retaining the categorical ban; established obligations and service continuity remain protected in implementation. Ball's Shared Time program implicated the corresponding inference about supplementary public teaching. Its distinct Community Education program predominantly used religious-school teachers and principals, and today's holding neither approves that arrangement nor reopens its judgment. The onsite rule applies Lemon as modified in Part I; the Court does not approve every program considered in Ball or reopen its completed judgment.

**Precedent treatment:** Aguilar v. Felton is overruled as authority for categorically excluding this safeguarded public Title I service from religious-school premises. School District of Grand Rapids v. Ball loses its contrary Shared Time rationale at the same scope; its Community Education judgment and unaddressed grounds remain undisturbed. Lemon v. Kurtzman governs through the updated inquiry stated in Holding I.

##### III. The Court's present change supplies the legal change required for prospective relief

**Holding and operative rule:** When this Court expressly removes the constitutional premise supporting a continuing adjudicated injunction, that present change is a significant change in law under Rule 60(b)(5), and continued categorical enforcement on the displaced premise is inequitable. The Court may apply its ruling in the pending review of denied prospective relief without requiring a second motion merely to invoke the rule it has just announced; lower courts remain bound by directly controlling precedent until this Court changes it.

**Authority:** Part III of the Court's opinion; the same five Justices directly join the present-change rule and its distinction between this continuing decree and a completed judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Denial of Rule 60(b)(5) relief is reviewed for abuse of discretion, with its governing legal premises reviewed as questions of law. Rufo requires a significant change making continued enforcement inequitable and a modification fitted to that change. The legal change here is the Court's ruling today. It is not the expectation that the Court might reconsider Aguilar, an increase in the expense of compliance, or a supposed earlier overruling. Kiryas Joel expressly preserved Aguilar and Ball. Zobrest decided antecedent statutory and regulatory questions without deciding establishment. Rosenberger's actual printing benefit and Pinette and Chabad's private-expression holdings did not authorize this public teaching program. Rodriguez de Quijas required the lower courts to apply the directly governing rule.

The objection that Rule 60 review cannot become an untimely rehearing of the original judgment does not bar this relief. The motion addresses the decree's continuing prospective operation, and the preserved question presents the constitutional rule supporting that operation. The Court changes that rule expressly; it does not hold that the district court should have anticipated the change. Requiring a new motion solely to invoke today's decision would preserve a now-rejected prohibition without a remaining legal reason.

Rufo's limits remain. Dissatisfaction, a cheaper lawful alternative and anticipated doctrinal change are insufficient. Actual anticipation and acceptance of a risk can impose a heavy burden, but mere foreseeability is not an absolute bar and an unforeseeable catastrophe is unnecessary. A clarification of constitutional permission does not automatically erase a voluntarily accepted stricter consent obligation. This injunction enforces an adjudicated constitutional prohibition; the record establishes no negotiated promise independently requiring that prohibition. The Court neither reopens completed claims nor finds an independently sufficient factual change in costs.

**Precedent treatment:** Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail is applied to the present legal change and retained in its significant-change, accepted-risk and tailored-relief requirements. Rodriguez de Quijas v. Shearson/American Express, Inc. is applied to preserve lower-court obedience to direct precedent. Browder v. Director, Department of Corrections does not foreclose adjustment of continuing relief on the basis of the legal change actually adopted here; its limit on using a Rule 60 appeal to reopen the underlying completed judgment remains.

##### IV. Relief must remove the rejected prohibition while preserving independently lawful obligations

**Holding and operative rule:** On remand the district court must remove the injunction's prohibition of the described secular Title I service insofar as that prohibition rests on religious location, presumed inculcation from location or ordinary supervision of public personnel; if no independently supported legal basis sustains the onsite prohibition, it must be dissolved. Any remaining obligation must have an identified lawful basis, and any further proceedings must concern a specified material issue under the surviving law rather than require proof that future misconduct is impossible.

**Authority:** Part IV of the Court's opinion; the same five Justices directly join this tailored relief and its public-control limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Rufo connects modification to the proven change while preserving the lawful purposes of continuing relief. The change today eliminates the categorical site-and-monitoring ground. The district court therefore cannot simply reinstate that same ground, presume that public service and religious instruction are inseparable, or treat ordinary secular supervision as itself forbidden. It must remove the prohibition to that extent. Supported questions about actual religious instruction, diversion, religious control of public employees or another independent legal prohibition remain subject to ordinary procedure and the governing law; hypothetical misconduct does not justify preserving Aguilar's rejected inference.

No additional negotiated obligation is presumed. If an independent statutory, contractual or decree obligation is established, constitutional permission does not by itself extinguish it. Existing lawful services may continue while the mandate is implemented promptly. Children currently receiving those services should continue to receive them during implementation, with existing statutory duties preserved and no new service entitlement created. Any implementation period must address identified tasks and circumstances; the Court sets no number of days, and administrative delay cannot indefinitely preserve the rejected categorical prohibition. The district court modifies the decree under the mandate. Until it acts, the City must obey the operative injunction.

The holding permits the described program; it does not command onsite delivery, create an unconditional funding entitlement, approve a replacement program, award damages or repayment, cancel contracts, or excuse past disobedience. It makes no finding that every present implementation satisfies the required secular controls.

**Precedent treatment:** Rufo's suitable-tailoring requirement controls implementation and preserves any independently lawful obligation. Its consent-decree rule is not converted into a presumption that this adjudicated injunction contains a voluntary stricter undertaking.

#### Law After Decision

Effective June 23, 1997, Lemon remains the governing Establishment Clause test as modified in Holding I. Purpose, effects and entanglement inform a single inquiry with three routes: controlling specific prohibition or distinct doctrine, established permissible relationship, and residual attribution and function. Clear cases apply that test; its former independent prongs do not remain a separate or fallback method.

Aguilar no longer supplies a categorical constitutional bar to the separately administered, safeguarded public Title I service described in Holding II, and Ball's corresponding Shared Time rationale has no contrary force at that scope. Ball's completed judgment, Community Education and unaddressed holdings remain undisturbed. Independent prohibitions on governmental worship, devotional financing and improper institutional control remain; public employment and neutral eligibility do not immunize supported devotional conduct.

For this continuing injunction, the Court's substantive change satisfies Rule 60(b)(5)'s legal-change requirement. Removal of the now-unsupported onsite prohibition must follow through a tailored district-court order. Rufo's other requirements and Rodriguez de Quijas's rule for lower courts remain. Permission to provide the service is distinct from a statutory duty or funding entitlement. Absence of reported misconduct is not proof that every possible defect has been disproved.

The Court decides no tuition-voucher or provider-redemption scheme, monument, legislative-prayer program, general accommodation rule or all forms of direct aid. Parental selection does not itself answer what a particular expenditure purchases; no conclusion about an absent program displaces Witters or Mueller. Rosenberger remains a permissible financing arrangement on its actual controlled vendor-funded publication terms, not merely an access decision, and religious editorial viewpoint there is not automatically purchased devotional instruction. Kiryas Joel, Pinette and Chabad retain their actual holdings. Private speech, free exercise, institutional religious autonomy, denominational scrutiny and accommodation retain their distinct sources and burdens. ##### Justice Souter, dissenting

Stevens and Ginsburg join the dissent in full. Breyer joins Part II only.

In Part I, Souter, Stevens and Ginsburg would retain Aguilar's and Ball's applicable substantive rules. Public staffing, neutral eligibility and safeguards do not alone resolve the institutional subsidy and entanglement concerns. Rosenberger's private publication and the private-forum decisions therefore do not decide this case. They reject the Court's modification of Lemon because its treatment of purpose, effects and entanglement within attribution and function understates the constitutional significance of continuing public instructional support. These Justices do not find proved proselytizing and do not require it for their structural objection. Breyer does not join this affirmative direct-aid rationale.

In Part II, all four dissenters would adhere to the direct precedents. The safeguards and anticipated costs did not newly arise. ##### Justice Ginsburg, dissenting; joined by Stevens, Souter and Breyer

The four dissenters would also affirm on an independent procedural ground. Browder limits an appeal from denial of Rule 60 relief; it does not reopen the original judgment for indefinite rehearing. The district court followed still-binding Aguilar, and Rufo requires a qualifying change rather than an appeal whose own desired outcome creates the prerequisite for reopening. The Court's adoption of an updated Lemon test does not, in their view, cure that procedural defect. This writing addresses the route to relief independently of the substantive merits disagreement.

#### Chronology and Posture

#### Holdings

##### An ongoing federal property-right claim may proceed against responsible officers within real remedial limits

**Holding and operative rule:** The Eleventh Amendment does not bar a claim for prospective declaratory or injunctive relief against officials actually connected to an alleged ongoing violation of federal tribal property rights merely because adjudicating that violation requires resolving a disputed federal title question or affects important sovereign interests. The decree must address proved unlawful future official conduct and may not bind the immune State's title, award damages or retrospective compensation, execute against state property or require the State itself to convey title.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by the other four members of the five-Justice Court coalition identified in Opinion Topology. The rule has five direct joins and requires no fractured-opinion synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** Ex parte Young permits federal courts to restrain officers' continuing violation of federal law. Tindal and the officer-property reasoning of Treasure Salvors distinguish adjudication affecting an officer's lawful conduct from a judgment binding the sovereign's own title. Oneida supplies the federal character of the pleaded tribal property rights. The complaint identifies land-regulating officials, including Land Board officials and the water-resources director, whose challenged authority allegedly causes the interference; a general duty to obey law would not suffice.

The State's strongest objection concerns the actual breadth of the prayer: exclusive use and exclusion of state regulation could transfer practical control of the waters. Calling relief prospective does not answer that objection. But the Ninth Circuit expressly withheld a decree binding Idaho's title, and the case remains at the pleading stage. Whether a federal right exists and what lawful officer remedy follows must be adjudicated, not assumed. Every Young injunction constrains asserted state authority; the importance of that authority cannot itself decide the federal claim or create a property-specific exemption from review.

Edelman and Green preserve the limits against retrospective relief; Pennhurst bars federal commands founded on state law, not these pleaded federal rights. Seminole's actual officer holding is consistent with bounded enforcement but does not decide every statute or the separate State-amenability question. No IGRA procedure is transferred to this property dispute. The separate United States case covers only part of the area and supplies neither complete substitute relief nor waiver. Affirmance permits ordinary adjudication and tailored relief against connected officers; it does not approve every requested remedy or determine title.

**Precedent treatment:** Young is applied without a sovereign-lands exception or generalized state-interest balance. Tindal and Treasure Salvors supply the officer/sovereign property distinction; Oneida supplies federal-question character. Edelman, Green and Pennhurst retain their respective limits. Seminole remains at its actual officer-route scope and is not recast as a universal immunity or abrogation ruling.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Ex parte Young:** Applied to alleged ongoing federal violations by responsible officers; no categorical sovereign-lands exception or general state-interest balancing rule is added.
- **Tindal v. Wesley:** Applied to distinguish relief involving officers' possession from a judgment binding an absent sovereign's title.
- **Florida Department of State v. Treasure Salvors:** Its officer-property reasoning supports that distinction; the decision does not authorize a decree binding Idaho itself.
- **Oneida Indian Nation:** Applied for the federal-law character of the asserted tribal property rights, without deciding those rights' merits.
- **Edelman v. Jordan:** Preserves the bar on retrospective monetary recovery against the State.
- **Green v. Mansour:** Preserves limits on retrospective declaratory relief; a continuing violation must support the prospective officer action.
- **Pennhurst:** Preserves the distinct state-law restriction; the pleaded claims here arise under federal law.
- **Seminole Tribe:** Its actual prospective IGRA officer holding remains valid within that statute's sequence. Its separate State-amenability disposition supplies no controlling general rationale; Union Gas is not overruled by this decision.

#### Law After Decision

Prospective federal enforcement against responsible officers remains available when a disputed federal tribal title question must be decided, subject to genuine limits protecting the immune State and excluding retrospective or State-directed relief. The Court rejects a sovereign-lands exception to that route. The Tribe must still establish its federal rights, an ongoing violation and an appropriate remedy. The judgment supplies no title result or general Article I abrogation rule.

#### Separate Writings

**O’Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** They would reverse the officer portion because this actual demand seeks comprehensive practical ownership and exclusion of Idaho's sovereign regulation over navigable waters. In their view that combination is the functional equivalent of the barred sovereign-title decree, despite nominally prospective language and the limitation that Idaho is not formally bound. Tindal and Treasure Salvors do not, for them, permit this complete displacement of sovereign control. Their proposed exception turns on the unusual scope and operation of the requested relief, not every suit involving land, costly compliance or an important state interest. They preserve ordinary Young actions and reject free-ranging balancing, state-forum exhaustion and the assumption that the narrower United States case provides complete relief. They would dismiss the present comprehensive officer demand without determining title or foreclosing a genuinely different prospective claim.

Idaho, its agencies and the direct quiet-title claims remain dismissed; no automatic injunction or property transfer results. The separate United States action continues in its own posture.

### Lindh v. Murphy, No. 96-6298

Merits decision, June 23, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 14, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, which applied amended chapter 153 to Lindh's pending habeas application and denied relief. The question is whether amended §2254(d) and the related chapter 153 provisions govern a noncapital application filed before AEDPA's April 24, 1996 enactment. Lindh filed July 9, 1992; his appeal was pending when the Act became law. The underlying Confrontation Clause claim is not decided.

All nine Justices participated in the merits hearing and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The Seventh Circuit must adjudicate Lindh's appeal under the habeas law governing the application before AEDPA. No writ or other merits relief is awarded.

#### Opinions

Ginsburg delivered the opinion of the Court. Scalia filed a dissent, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, that would affirm the temporal determination without independently adjudicating the Confrontation Clause claim.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The chapter 153 amendments at issue do not govern this pre-enactment noncapital application

**Holding and operative rule:** AEDPA's chapter 153 amendments, including amended §2254(d), do not govern Lindh's noncapital federal habeas application filed before April 24, 1996. The application-date construction preserves separately directed provisions, chapter 154's express pending-case rule and the successive-petition provisions governed by Felker; it does not exempt every pending proceeding from every AEDPA provision.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Breyer. Six Justices directly adopt the statutory rule, its limits and reversal with remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Landgraf makes Congress's temporal choice the first inquiry. Ordinary statutory construction may establish that choice without a prescribed formula. Congress enacted the neighboring ordinary-habeas and capital-habeas chapters together and expressly directed chapter 154 to pending cases in §107(c), while omitting that direction for chapter 153. That deliberate contrast supplies the application-date inference here. The contrary reading stresses the capital chapter's distinct State-qualification system. Those differences do not deprive Congress's adjacent and express treatment of pending applications of significance. Applying the same pending-case reach to chapter 153 would erase that contrast. The changed standards governing an already filed request for relief reinforce the need to honor the enacted distinction; the Court need not classify every amendment as substantive or procedural to decide the case. Felker applied a particular authorization-review restriction to proceedings begun after enactment and expressly reserved other Title I temporal questions. It neither fixes chapter 153's present reach nor displaces the structural inquiry. Lindh filed on July 9, 1992, almost four years before enactment. His appeal must therefore be considered under the habeas law governing that application before AEDPA. The Court expresses no view on the psychiatrist cross-examination claim, bias, harmlessness, exhaustion, chapter 154 State qualification or ultimate entitlement to a writ. No constitutional right to permanent retention of every filing-date judicial rule is announced.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Landgraf supplies the prior inquiry into statutory temporal direction; ordinary construction, including the enacted chapter contrast, resolves that inquiry here. Its default completed-event framework remains intact.
- Rivers preserves the provision-specific method; the decision establishes no timing rule for every AEDPA amendment.
- Felker is distinguished because it applied its own authorization-review provisions to proceedings begun after enactment and reserved other Title I questions. Its successive-petition and retained-original-authority holdings remain in force.
- Hughes Aircraft preserves examination of the actual statutory change and does not replace the structural inquiry or the distinct temporal methods previously expressed.

#### Law After Decision

Pre-enactment noncapital applications such as Lindh's remain governed by pre-AEDPA chapter 153 law. Applications filed after enactment, chapter 154 cases within its express clause, successive-petition provisions governed by Felker and provisions carrying their own temporal commands retain their distinct treatment. The Court overrules no precedent and decides no underlying habeas merits.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, dissents. Chapter 154 addresses a distinct capital scheme dependent on State qualification, so its express pending-case clause does not reliably prescribe the contrary operation for chapter 153. Amended §2254(d) regulates the court's present authority to grant relief; the dissent would apply it to this still-pending adjudication. Hughes Aircraft's expansion of private enforcement exposure differs from this restriction on current judicial relief. The dissent would affirm at the temporal scope reviewed, without deciding the confrontation claim anew or making every AEDPA provision immediately applicable.

The application date and procedural history are stated in Lindh v. Murphy, 96 F.3d 856. AEDPA §§101–107, particularly §107(c), supply the enacted temporal comparison. This decision does not require findings about State qualification for chapter 154 or the merits of the cross-examination claim.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Seventh Circuit for adjudication under pre-AEDPA standards. The Court expresses no view on the Confrontation Clause claim or ultimate entitlement to habeas relief.

### Slack v. McDaniel, No. 98-6322

Plenary decision, April 26, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 4, 1999; reargued March 29, 2000. On certiorari to the Ninth Circuit's July 7, 1998 order denying appellate certification. The questions concern review of that denial, the applicable certificate regime, certification after a procedural dismissal and whether an application renewed after a without-prejudice exhaustion dismissal is second or successive, including its additional grounds.

The first federal petition was filed November 27, 1991 and dismissed February 19, 1992 without prejudice to renewal after state exhaustion, before any federal merits adjudication. The renewed proceeding commenced March 27, 1995 according to the lower account; the reproduced petition was filed May 30, 1995. Both dates precede AEDPA. Counsel was appointed February 13, 1997, and the third amended petition was filed December 24, 1997. The notice of appeal was filed April 29, 1998. The District Court's certificate denial was dated May 11 and filed May 12; the Ninth Circuit then denied certification. None has issued.

The March 31, 1998 district order dismissed grounds 2, 4(c), 5, 6 and 7 with prejudice as abusive: charging notice, the malice instruction, trial assistance, appellate assistance and cumulative error. Separately, ground 3's unexhausted federal due-process objection to sexual-misconduct evidence caused dismissal of the action without prejudice. Grounds 1, 4(a) and 4(b)—sufficiency, reasonable doubt, and premeditation/deliberation—were not among the five abuse dismissals. The State's separate exhaustion objections to the five grounds were not decided. Slack is serving consecutive life terms with possible parole; this is not a capital case.

All nine Justices participated at argument, reargument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Ninth Circuit's certificate denial is reversed and the case remanded for the proper certificate determination, with the second-or-successive classification rejected. No certificate or writ issues from this judgment.

#### Holdings and Controlling Reasons

##### I. The judicial certificate denial is reviewable

**Holding and operative rule:** A court of appeals' judicial denial of appellate certification is a case reviewable by certiorari under section 1254(1), whether the certificate was correctly or incorrectly required. That review does not itself issue a certificate or authorize the underlying merits appeal.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Ginsburg's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer, controls unanimously.

**Controlling explanation:** Hohn establishes the reviewability of the actual judicial certificate determination while preserving the separate conditions for certification and collateral relief. The Ninth Circuit decided that threshold judicial request; the absence of authorization for the merits appeal does not eliminate the case before it.

Felker bars ordinary review of an actual circuit decision granting or denying authorization for a second or successive district application. This proceeding reviews a certificate denial, not that different statutory determination. Stewart likewise requires attention to the actual judicial action rather than treating every habeas threshold as authorization review. The Court therefore may decide the certificate and classification issues without exercising an unrecognized review exception or awarding the underlying writ.

##### II. The post-enactment appeal requires the amended certificate

**Holding and operative rule:** Amended section 2253(c) governs an applicant's appeal initiated after April 24, 1996, even when the identified pre-AEDPA law governs district adjudication of the earlier habeas application. Slack's April 29, 1998 notice therefore invokes the certificate-of-appealability requirement; the prior CPC label and the State's earlier contrary legal position do not dispense with that required authorization.

**Authority:** Part II is joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas with its author, Ginsburg: five direct rationale votes. Stone-Zsela agrees with applying that regime but joins no Part II rationale. Stevens, Souter and Breyer would retain former CPC treatment.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2253 regulates taking an appeal. The district proceeding and the later effort to enter appellate review are distinct events for the provision governing each. Slack began the renewed district proceeding in 1995 under either supported date but sought appellate review in 1998, after the amendment's enactment.

Lindh's chapter comparison preserves the specified pre-enactment district applications and does not decide every later appellate provision. Carriger's capital extension concerns its identified district-law provisions and does not settle this noncapital certificate question. Felker applies a review restriction to the post-enactment proceeding that restriction regulates; it does not transfer every new merits limitation backward. Hohn expressly reserved certificate transition, so its review holding supplies no already settled answer here.

The contrary whole-pending-case construction gives one commencement date to all later steps. The text's focus on appellate entry warrants the separate treatment adopted here. Applying the new certificate requirement does not apply amended section 2254(d) to Slack's pre-enactment application or adopt a universal claim-by-claim temporal rule for amendments. The State's earlier legal concession cannot authorize an appeal without the certificate the governing statute requires. That conclusion neither makes every subsection jurisdictional nor excuses every possible forfeiture.

The request may be considered under its proper legal character despite the CPC label. Rule 22 permits district or circuit certification and treats a notice without an express certificate request as a request to the circuit judges. A request is not an issued certificate.

##### III. A procedural-dismissal certificate requires both constitutional and procedural showings

**Holding and operative rule:** When a district court dismisses on procedure without reaching the constitutional claim, a certificate of appealability requires reasonable debate both about whether the petition states a valid claim of denial of a constitutional right and about whether the procedural ruling was correct. Both are threshold showings; the court may address the more apparent component first, and any certificate must identify the qualifying issue or issues.

**Authority:** Part III has nine direct joins: Ginsburg, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. Stevens, Souter and Breyer adopt this interpretation conditionally while maintaining that former CPC governs this appeal; their Part III agreement does not enlarge the Part II coalition.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2253(c)(2) demands a substantial showing of the denial of a constitutional right. Barefoot supplies the reasonable-debate threshold, which requires more than a nonfrivolous assertion but not ultimate success. A procedural barrier does not eliminate the underlying constitutional claim from that inquiry. The State's categorical exclusion of procedural-dismissal appeals would insulate an erroneous barrier even when it blocks a substantial constitutional claim.

The opposite procedure-only approach also fails. A debatable procedural question or bare constitutional label alone does not make the required constitutional showing. The two inquiries must each be satisfied without converting certification into final adjudication of the constitutional claim. A plain and correctly applied procedural bar can defeat certification; a court need not decide the constitutional issue unnecessarily when the procedural answer is clear. It may instead begin with the constitutional component when that answer is more apparent. No universal sequence is prescribed.

Where a district court actually rejects the constitutional merits, the corresponding substantial showing asks whether reasonable jurists could debate that assessment or find it wrong. Slack's procedural dismissals are not silently reclassified as merits adjudications. Section 2253(c)(3) separately requires issue identification. Peguero addresses the effect of an actually issued certificate; none exists here. Neither this Court's review nor an unaccompanied notice supplies one. Rule 22's State-appeal exception remains distinct and is not implicated by this applicant's appeal.

##### IV. This exhaustion return remains a first application, including its additional grounds

**Holding and operative rule:** When an initial federal application is dismissed without prejudice solely to permit completion of state exhaustion before any federal merits adjudication, the renewed application is not second or successive merely because it follows that filing or adds grounds. Rule 9(b)'s new-ground abuse branch does not supply the missing second-or-successive predicate in this exhaustion-return setting; the contrary classification here is rejected, while actual independent default, exhaustion, timing and lawful case-management requirements remain.

**Authority:** Part IV of Ginsburg's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer, making seven direct votes for this rule, its application and reversal with remand. Scalia and Thomas dissent from this classification and disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** Rose's complete-exhaustion procedure contemplates return for a first federal merits opportunity. The February 1992 dismissal did not adjudicate Slack's claims and expressly permitted renewal after exhaustion. Rule 9(b) begins with the second-or-successive category. Its repeated-ground branch requires a prior merits determination; its new-ground branch addresses omission constituting abuse within that category. The latter does not erase the opening classification in this setting. Former section 2244(b)'s prior-merits setting reinforces that reading.

The State emphasizes the new-ground branch's separate wording, Sanders's treatment of a nonmerits denial and McCleskey's pleading and excuse framework. Those authorities preserve structured abuse inquiries, but a deficient pleading or completed merits application is not this without-prejudice exhaustion return. The State's adequate abuse pleading and Slack's response cannot supply a statutory or Rule category that does not apply. The additional claims therefore do not alone convert this application into a successive one.

Stewart supplies a confined analogy: its Ford claim had actually been presented, dismissed as premature and preserved. It did not already decide omitted claims or every nonmerits dismissal. Today's holding supplies the exhaustion-return rule. Lonchar requires governing dismissal predicates rather than general disapproval of repeated litigation. Legitimate concerns about serial filings may support lawful pleading rules, exhaustion requirements or properly communicated return conditions on their actual predicates; they do not establish intentional disobedience here.

The holding creates no immunity for actual deliberate withholding, a applicable abuse rule, state default, prejudicial delay or lawful anti-duplication measures. It confers no unlimited right to amend. The classification error settles the procedural objection under review; the Ninth Circuit must still determine the substantial constitutional showing before issuing an issue-specific certificate. Reversal of its denial does not decide the unreviewed exhaustion grounds, the constitutional claims or entitlement to collateral relief.

**Operative procedural qualifications:** Rule 9(a)'s distinct delay provision permits dismissal where filing delay prejudiced the State's ability to respond, unless reasonable diligence could not have disclosed the grounds before that prejudice arose. No such prejudice or diligence finding is made here. Rule 9(b)'s dismissal permission is not an automatic command, and former section 2244(b)'s exception to its prior-merits permission requires both an unadjudicated ground and absence of deliberate withholding or other abuse; that prior-merits predicate is absent here. No free-floating equitable sanction replaces an applicable source's conditions.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No universal rule covers every nonmerits dismissal or every later claim. Amended section 2244's restrictions retain their own applicability and classification predicates; no authorization determination or exception is decided here. The State's exhaustion alternatives and any actual independent, adequate and properly applied state default remain separate. No present claim is found substantial, exhausted, excused or meritorious, and the lower court's former reopening period is not renewed.

### Santa Fe Independent School District v. Doe, No. 99-62

Plenary merits decision, June 19, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 29, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, reviewing 168 F.3d 806 (1999). The granted question concerns the football policy. The District Court rejected its unrestricted form but permitted the nonsectarian/nonproselytizing backup; the Fifth Circuit rejected both football versions. The graduation disposition and affirmed damages denial are not reopened.

The October 1995 policy permits a brief invocation and/or message during home-varsity-football pregame ceremonies to solemnize the event, promote sportsmanship and student safety, and establish an appropriate competitive environment. Each spring the student council, upon the principal's advice and direction, conducts a secret-ballot election of the high-school student body on whether to include the statement or invocation. If the students vote to include it, they elect one volunteer, who may select the message or invocation consistently with the policy's goals. The backup repeats the policy with a nonsectarian and nonproselytizing condition if the unrestricted version is enjoined. It neither requires the selected student to pray nor opens the event to every student speaker.

The prior chaplain practice and August elections precede the challenged October policy. No delivered October prayer or official review of a particular message is found. The interim restrictions on official substantive input and textual preapproval remain distinct from the disputed question of their continuing effect; the Court's ground assumes no textual preapproval.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unrestricted October policy | Affirmed, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Scalia and Thomas | Official representative invocation mechanism restrained; dissent would reverse and remand for bounded adjudication of supported actual official adoption or unconstitutional pressure |
| Conditional nonsectarian/nonproselytizing backup | Affirmed, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Scalia and Thomas | Same official mechanism restrained; dissent would vacate and remand for lawful scope and severability of any needed relief, without restoring compulsory religious-content screening |

The judgment restrains the challenged official representative invocation mechanism. It preserves genuinely voluntary private prayer, independent student expression, lawful secular messages and genuine neutral access, and requires neither a new forum nor censorship of otherwise lawful private religious viewpoints. It does not decide graduation, damages or later incidents outside this record.

#### Holdings

##### The school may not delegate its official religious choice to a student majority

**Holding and operative rule:** A public secondary school may not organize an invocation as a representative component of its own football pregame ceremony and delegate to a student-majority election whether that official component will include religious exercise. The challenged board-created election mechanism violates the Establishment Clause under both its unrestricted and nonsectarian/nonproselytizing versions; relief reaches that mechanism while preserving genuinely voluntary private prayer, independent student expression, lawful secular messages and genuine neutral access.

**Authority:** Justice Breyer's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, supplying seven direct votes for the rule, explanation and limited affirmance. The particular government-created representative religious choice is essential to all seven joins; the Court adopts no independent religious-pressure holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Agostini requires identification of the governmental act and its function, using purpose, effect and institutional relationships as evidence within the modified Lemon inquiry. Here the board creates an invocation or message slot within its own ceremony; the student council conducts a majority election under the principal's advice and direction; the electorate selects a single volunteer; and the speaker must serve the board's prescribed purposes. This is an official representative component, not generally available access for independently chosen private speakers. Lee forbids official school organization of worship, and Ingebretsen explains why student initiation or delivery cannot alone make that exercise private. A majority vote cannot privatize the board's delegated religious choice.

The District stresses secular purposes, student authorship, no required prayer, optional attendance and the possibility of silence or a secular message. Those features do not remove the existing governmental mechanism. The Court assumes no official textual preapproval and no delivered October prayer; it does not rest on a finding that every secular purpose is a sham. The ability to decline use of the delegated religious authority does not transform it into neutral access. Pinette and Bronx Household protect actually private expression, where government has not adopted the devotion as its own event component. Tanford likewise distinguishes official organization from private composition, without making its adult setting identical to this school policy.

The backup's religious-content limits retain the same official mechanism and do not cure it. The Court does not direct officials to edit private religion into acceptable language. Nor does it prohibit every election, invited student speaker or religious message. The existing mechanism suffices to affirm; Lee and Ingebretsen's separate protection against substantial practical religious pressure remains intact without requiring legal compulsion or compulsory attendance. No additional pressure finding, universal facial-challenge rule, new forum entitlement, graduation ruling or damages award is necessary.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Agostini v. Felton:** Applied as the governing governmental-act/function inquiry; purpose, effects and entanglement remain evidence within that inquiry, not independent surviving prongs.
- **Lemon v. Kurtzman:** Continues in the form Agostini adopted; the Court revives neither an independent purpose test nor an independent endorsement test.
- **Lee v. Weisman:** Its official-organization rule applies to this school-created invocation mechanism. Its independently sufficient substantial-pressure rule remains, without a compulsory-attendance prerequisite; no new pressure holding is made here.
- **Ingebretsen v. Jackson Public School District:** Its rejection of automatic insulation by student delivery and its protection of lawful private expression apply. Its provision-sensitive interim ruling is not recast as a universal rule invalidating every future religious message.
- **Tanford v. Brand:** Preserved for its established official devotional program and its distinction between independent wording and private access; the Court does not transplant every feature of university commencement to football.
- **Capitol Square Review and Advisory Board v. Pinette:** Its actual generally available private forum is distinguished from this selective representative event component; religious content and government location remain insufficient by themselves to establish attribution.
- **Bronx Household of Faith v. Community School District No. 10:** Its independently organized private use remains protected; the school's own representative ceremony presents a different governmental function.
- **Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District:** Its protection of religious viewpoints within genuine comparable private access remains intact; no schoolwide private forum is inferred here.
- **Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia:** Its independently directed private-expression relationship remains intact; it does not authorize government-organized worship.
- **Chabad of Southern Ohio v. City of Cincinnati:** Its contextual attribution inquiry remains; neither a forum label nor location alone supplies the answer.
- **Strout v. Albanese:** Its distinction between genuine private expression and governmental performance or purchase of a religious function is preserved; no football-specific rule is imported from its tuition setting.

#### Law After Decision

The official character of the challenged representative religious-choice mechanism supplies the constitutional defect even without a delivered October prayer or official editing of its words. Government cannot make its own religious choice private by delegating it to a student majority. Agostini's unified governmental-act/function inquiry governs; no independent purpose or endorsement test returns. Genuinely private religious expression retains its protection. The independent substantial-pressure rules of Lee and Ingebretsen continue without requiring legally compulsory attendance; this decision need not decide that additional ground.

#### Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, dissenting, joined by Justice Thomas.** They would reverse the facial invalidation of the unrestricted October policy and remand for bounded adjudication of any actual official-adoption or unconstitutional-pressure application. Agostini requires examination of what government actually does, and they accept its prohibition on government-organized worship. But the present wording permits a secular message, a religious message or no message and leaves the selected volunteer the content choice. In their view, the election and prior prayer history do not sufficiently establish that every implementation of this policy is official devotion. Their Ingebretsen objection distinguishes anticipated official participation from supported implementation; Pinette and Bronx Household preserve genuine private access. They do not require a government-written script before attribution is possible, treat student authorship as conclusive, or deny Tanford's established official-attribution holding. Tanford's adult ceremonial dispute supplies no general permission for official secondary-school worship.

For the conditional backup, they would vacate and remand its football disposition with the unrestricted-policy proceedings so that the lower courts can determine the lawful reach and severability of any needed relief. They would not restore mandatory screening of private speech for nonsectarian and nonproselytizing content, and they do not hold the backup independently valid. It becomes operative only if the unrestricted version is enjoined; no independent continuing application is presumed. Their component remedies preserve genuine voluntary expression while leaving supported actual official worship and substantial practical pressure open to adjudication. They would neither reopen graduation or damages questions nor authorize suppression of private religious viewpoints.

The separate graduation and damages dispositions remain outside this review.
