## Allied-Bruce / terms\OT1994\output\OT_1994CHUNK2.md

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice O'Connor joins the Court's opinion and concurs separately. She continues to believe that Southland originally misconstrued the Act's application in state courts, for the reasons set out in her Southland and Perry dissents. Statutory stare decisis nevertheless weighs strongly against reopening an interpretation that has governed for a decade without a demonstrated inability to administer it. Once that interpretation is retained, a broad and uniform actual-commerce rule is appropriate; narrowing commerce coverage is not a substitute for overruling Southland. Her original-construction reservation does not subtract from her join in the Court's present rule or mandate.

Justice Thomas dissents, joined by Justice Scalia. He reads the Act's structure and federal-court judicial directions as failing to create the substantive command to state courts that Southland announced. He would overrule Southland to that extent and affirm the denial of arbitration on the ground that the Act does not govern this state proceeding. He does not decide the alternative scope of commerce coverage or the factual success of a general contract defense. The dissent presents no additional independently sufficient specific-performance holding under §§3–4.

Justice Scalia also dissents separately. His earlier acceptance of Southland when its overruling was not requested does not resolve the express request presented here. In his view, reliance on a statutory interpretation cannot by itself preserve a state-court command the statute does not establish. He joins Thomas's state-court ground and would affirm without deciding the Court's commerce construction. These proposed changes to Southland do not control; the Court retains that precedent.

## Vernonia / terms\OT1994\output\OT_1994CHUNK8.md

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Ginsburg concurs. She joins the Court's opinion because the actual competitive athletic setting, safety findings and bounded safeguards justify this program. She emphasizes that the decision does not approve compulsory testing of every student or every extracurricular participant. Athlete influence on classmates cannot, without more, extend the rationale to a population with materially different privacy expectations and activity risks. Her caution is a limit on the proposition adopted, not judgment-only agreement or a refusal to join the Court's five-Justice program rule. The prescription holding remains facial and confined; actual intrusive disclosure demands and materially different protocols receive no approval.

Justice O'Connor dissents, joined by Stevens and Souter. They would affirm the federal invalidation while recognizing that children remain subject to legitimate school supervision and that the District faced a genuine drug and safety problem. T.L.O. relaxed warrant and probable-cause demands but did not approve suspicionless testing. Skinner and Von Raab establish exceptions, not an absolute rule requiring suspicion in every conceivable school setting; the dissent disputes whether this record establishes enough need to dispense with workable individualized suspicion. The District's observations identified suspected users and misconduct. Neutral drawing reduces arbitrary selection, but does not itself justify obtaining bodily samples from an innocent child. Collection remains an intrusion even with limited observation, drug-specific analysis and restricted use. Athlete participation is no constitutional waiver. Stevens's concern that school constitutional safeguards teach civic values reinforces, rather than replaces, the required legal justification. The dissent does not demand a universally catastrophic risk, deny every protective school interest, or prohibit reasonable-suspicion testing. It joins the limited facial prescription-verification holding because the policy permits a confidential laboratory method; it does not base invalidation on a demand that prescriptions necessarily be delivered to school personnel. Different evidence and a narrower program remain open.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela dissents. He would affirm the Ninth Circuit's invalidation because the District has not established that individualized reasonable-suspicion testing or materially less intrusive measures are impracticable. Urine collection and chemical analysis are searches. A genuine non-law-enforcement safety need permits adjustment of warrant and probable-cause defaults, but recurring bodily and medical searches of a broad class require a proportionate justification for dispensing with individualized cause. Coaching, fitness examinations, communal changing and athletic rules do not waive excretory or medical privacy. Random selection, some disclosure limits and noncriminal uses reduce burdens without answering whether suspicionless testing is justified. The findings concerning athletes leading drug groups establish serious concern, not that athletes as a whole caused the problem or that observation and suspicion-based testing cannot address sport-specific risks. He would permit ordinary disciplinary rules, individualized reasonable-suspicion testing, testing after a specific safety incident, fitness-directed examinations and materially narrower safety programs supported by evidence that individualized methods cannot address the risk. He does not authorize law-enforcement use or unrelated medical testing. His medical-information concern does not establish or adjudicate a separate surviving disclosure claim. His proposed necessity rule is noncontrolling; no other Justice joins this writing.

## Boggs v. Boggs / terms\OT1996\output\OT_1996CHUNK9.md

#### Separate Writings

**Justice Breyer, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justices O'Connor and Ginsburg.** They join the Court's federal-route, survivor-annuity and ancillary-fee portions. Section 1055 forecloses the actual demand for Sandra's annuity share even when sought by accounting or as its lump-sum equivalent. They would nevertheless affirm rejection of preemption for the lifetime pension receipts, rollover IRA and delivered ESOP shares insofar as the accounting runs only against recipients or Isaac's succession, leaves future plan payments and duties unchanged, and excludes Sandra's protected annuity.

In their view, Louisiana's ownership rules and its rules allowing lifetime use and enjoyment can impose that recipient obligation without creating a new plan beneficiary. Guidry forbids a constructive trust on a pension but does not resolve every duty to account after distribution. Mackey requires attention to the statutory categories. Travelers and Dillingham require interference with a protected operation, while Board of Trade's mandated covered-plan benchmark differs from an ordinary succession accounting. Their answer to the Court's antecedent-title argument is that recognition of a recipient's obligation after lawful payment is distinct from enforcing an assignment against the plan. They claim neither a general domestic-relations exemption nor immunity merely because the plan is not a defendant. Their proposed limitation is a legal condition on permissible relief, not a finding that the sons abandoned the annuity claim. They would remand within those limits without fixing an amount.

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He joins the survivor-annuity holding: the sons may not recover Sandra's protected annuity share directly, through an accounting or through an equivalent lump-sum award. He would reverse only that component, affirm the remainder and remand for an accounting confined to Dorothy's community one-half interest in pension installments paid to Isaac, the IRA and distributed ESOP property. Isaac's own half and the remaining community assets after satisfaction of the legatees' claim would remain in his succession for disposition under Louisiana law.

He reasons that family, property and probate law retain their ordinary force unless Congress clearly displaces their particular operation. Louisiana's general ownership rule neither singles out plans nor changes their fiduciary duties. In his view Dorothy's interest arose by operation of community-property law, not assignment, and the pension anti-alienation provision does not bar this later accounting against the succession. The domestic-relations-order exception does not itself turn probate transfers into prohibited alienation; the availability of a comparable division on divorce reinforces his objection. Travelers' rejection of indiscriminate remote-effect preemption is consistent with this position but does not decide the case. He relies on the text and purposes of §§1055 and 1056(d), not on a holding of Hisquierdo. Beyond the survivor demand resolved here, he decides no additional §1055 question, including whether another asset falls outside that section or was waived. Protecting Sandra's annuity does not extinguish the three separate claims, and he adopts no rule that every community-property claim survives ERISA. He agrees with reaching the federal controversy and leaving ancillary fees for proceedings below without adding grounds for either disposition.

## National Association of Home Builders / terms\OT1997\output\OT_1997CHUNK10.md

#### Separate Writings

**Kennedy, concurring, joined by O'Connor.** They join both routes, the identification-and-fit rule and the affirmance in full. Lopez requires a meaningful distinction between commerce among the States and a general police power. Hale demonstrates one way a local act may be necessary to interstate regulation; American Life League, Robinson and Bird identify other concrete interstate relationships from a prohibition's actual operation. No one mechanism is an exhaustive taxonomy. The necessary-inclusion rule keeps that distinction because a court independently identifies a valid interstate regulation and the actual function the local prohibition performs within it; a declared national objective or a decorative interstate provision does not suffice. The Act's regulation of interstate trade in listed wildlife and its protection of the populations those provisions govern supply that function from the statute's own operation, not from a hospital label, construction expenditure or the national importance of biodiversity, so no further record about this fly is needed. A substantial burden that escapes ordinary territorial control may also have the interstate character the Constitution requires even though the source activity is local and noneconomic.

The territorial limit does real work. A problem's existence in many States, uneven State policy or the desirability of national uniformity does not alone show that States cannot reasonably contain or internalize the asserted effect. The Court must identify the effect that crosses their authority and test its fit to the regulated class. Because Part II-A establishes power here, that route is not applied to this fly. They do not demand categorical invalidation or an added commercial-transaction element.

**Breyer, concurring in Parts II, III and IV and the judgment, and dissenting from Part I, joined by Ginsburg.** They would regard the actual habitat-altering construction and conversion as a sufficient economic-application ground under Sweet Home, Hinds, Wickard, Perez and Hodel. They therefore do not join Part I's rejection of that ground. The construction is the injury-causing activity, not simply the owner's collateral purchases.

Part I nevertheless has five votes independently of theirs and supplies the Court's governing classification. Part II-A sustains these applications on that classification as well, and they join it, its application, the identification-and-fit rule in Part III and the affirmance in Part IV. The ESA's integrated regulation of interstate trade in listed wildlife and its protection of the populations that trade concerns form one practical regulatory system, and Congress may reasonably judge that protecting those populations against unauthorized depletion is a necessary part of it. Their judgment vote therefore rests on two sufficient grounds: their own economic-application view and the Court's necessary-inclusion rule.

The two routes recognize practical interstate relationships without categorically immunizing noneconomic local conduct. The Government must identify a genuine relationship, but that showing does not require de novo judicial environmental science, universal formal findings, a fixed quantity of interstate effects, clear-and-convincing proof or proof that every exemption would defeat the regime. Congress retains substantial latitude over regulatory necessity and practical enforcement. They join Part II-B's independent externality route as an available alternative that this case does not require. Their economic-application view remains a disagreement with Part I, not a competing holding.

**Stevens, concurring in Parts II-A, III and IV and the judgment, and dissenting from Part I, joined by Souter.** They join the Court's necessary-inclusion rule, its application to the fly, the identification-and-fit rule and the affirmance. They would also affirm on a further ground. The habitat-altering construction and conversion are themselves economic activities operating in actual land, construction, financing, labor and materials markets. Sweet Home identifies that modification as the injury-causing conduct, while Hinds directs attention to actual function rather than an owner's unrelated economic relationships. Their as-applied inquiry therefore identifies the activity section 9 actually reaches here; it does not insert a new element or declare every taking economic. Wickard and Perez permit class-wide aggregation after this economic connection, and Hodel confirms that environmental purpose does not disqualify regulation of economic activity.

The Court's enacted-class approach, in their view, treats the breadth of the taking prohibition as a reason to disregard the narrower economic application before the Court. Lopez's separate payment fact and Hinds's owner purchases differ from the physical development that itself causes the habitat injury here. They need no speculative biodiversity benefits. The necessary-inclusion ground independently sustains the judgment, because the taking prohibition protects the populations whose interstate exploitation the Act regulates and requires no showing that this fly is traded. Because Part II-A suffices, they do not reach the independent externality route, reserving it for a case that requires it.

**Scalia, concurring in Part I, and dissenting from Parts II-A, II-B, III and IV and the judgment, joined by Thomas.** They would reverse and remand for relief confined to the challenged applications. They join the Court's ruling that the taking class cannot be reconstructed from these projects' economic attributes. Lopez's no-insertion rule and the Hale line require a connection grounded in the enacted prohibition, not the actor's expenditures, motive or the national importance of the subject. They accept necessary inclusion of local conduct when a demonstrated relationship to a valid interstate regime exists, as in the machinegun cases, where excluding possession would have defeated the transfer-and-stock restriction. They find no such relationship here. Calling a statute comprehensive does not establish congressional power, and the record shows no trade in this fly and no evasion or material impairment of the Act's trade provisions from its local taking. A functional relationship identified at the level of the whole statute, without that showing, makes the national statute that includes a local prohibition its own constitutional justification.

They also reject the independent externality route. A substantial ecological burden that States cannot reasonably confine does not, for them, establish commerce or necessary protection of an existing commerce regime. The territorial-control inquiry risks substituting the need for national regulation for the enumerated power to undertake it. The Court's evidentiary requirements narrow that concern but do not resolve it. They would grant confined relief against the reviewed applications on the grounds offered. They obey Sweet Home's statutory construction, do not overrule the entering Lopez/Hale architecture, and propose no facial invalidation of the ESA, categorical intrastate-species immunity or blanket development permit.

## United States v. Morrison / terms\OT1999\output\OT_1999CHUNK6.md

#### Separate Writings

**Breyer, concurring, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg.** The four join the Court's Section 5 opinion and its judgment in full. They would also uphold the action under the Commerce Clause. Congress could rationally find that the regulated class of gender-motivated violence substantially obstructs participation in interstate labor, travel, education and service markets, and choose reasonable means against the conduct creating that obstruction. The inquiry would retain judicial review, substantiality, a rational class-wide relationship and reasonable means; it would not make a congressional label, national importance or any conceivable economic consequence conclusive.

This is a proposed change to Lopez's connection-before-magnitude prerequisite, not an application that leaves that prerequisite intact. The concurring Justices maintain the broader class-effects view expressed in their Lopez dissent. In their judgment, requiring an independently eligible mechanism before considering the supported class-wide effects excludes a reasonable congressional response to this demonstrated economic barrier. They would displace that restriction to the extent it forecloses the rational substantial-class-effects ground stated here. Their proposal would preserve market, necessary-regime and externality holdings as sufficient grounds without making them exclusive prerequisites to this broader approach.

Their joins in Condon, Gillespie and other market or supply cases do not withdraw the broader position merely because those cases had an additional sufficient connection. Their Home Builders joins in Parts II-A, III and IV, and Ginsburg and Breyer's join in Part II-B, likewise rested on sufficient grounds while expressly preserving their disagreement with Part I's restriction. The concurrence does not relabel national prevalence as proof of a noninternalizable externality, invent a necessary-inclusion relationship, or assume new legislative facts. It rejects the Commerce opinion's treatment of connection and magnitude on the supported record.

Because the Section 5 holding sustains the action and requires reversal, the Commerce proposal does not affect the judgment. The concurrence adopts no additional legislative-process test. Reversal returns the statutory claim to ordinary adjudication, without establishing assault, animus, causation or damages and without guaranteeing a particular hearing, trial or award.

**Kennedy, dissenting as to Section 5 and the judgment, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas.** In the part of his opinion that does not speak for the Court, Kennedy would affirm. Section 5 does not sustain the presented nationwide action against a private assailant for gender-motivated violence because its defendant, triggering private wrong and relief lack a sufficient remedial relationship to the unconstitutional state denial invoked to support it. A private assailant's gender animus does not itself establish the State's constitutional denial, and Congress's account of discriminatory state justice does not, without that fit, convert liability for the private assault into enforcement of equal state protection.

The dissenters accept that state action includes official administration, judicial action and customs carrying legal force, that the Civil Rights Cases and Harris do not categorically forbid every corrective remedy involving a private defendant, and that Boerne and Florida Prepaid permit prevention beyond individually proven violations without a findings quota. They acknowledge Congress's documented state-justice concerns, the serious violence, the animus requirement and the civil relief. Their objection is fit: liability and relief follow the private assault whether the State denies equal protection or faithfully provides it, and in every State alike. The assailant's animus does not identify discriminatory official administration, and the action neither replaces a specified constitutionally defective procedure nor defines a supported class of official practices to which its coverage and relief are fitted. In their view the same kind of gap defeated the undifferentiated patent liability in Florida Prepaid. Scalia joins while preserving the possibility of genuinely corrective private-party remedies, and the dissenters reserve hypothetical color-of-law applications.
