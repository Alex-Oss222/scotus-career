QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Whether the Court should deny the petition when the 
record does not support a key assumption that underlies both 
the petition and the Court of Appeal’s dissent—viz., that, as a 
general matter, the State and local school districts provide: 
special educatiou services on site to students at private secular 
schools. 


2. Whether the Court should deny the petition when, con- 
trary to the view of petitioner and the dissent, the Ninth Cir- 
cuit’s opinion is narrow in scope and expressly limited by its 
facts to situations, such as the one presented here, in which 
petitioner has failed to demonstrate that any burden has been 
placed on his free exercise of religion, and when petitioner 
and the dissent also exaggerate the extent of the possible con- 
flict between the circuits—a conflict that largely disappears 
when the facts of the cases are considered. 


3. Whether the Court should deny the petition when the 
state Board of Education is in the process of repealing the 
portion of the administrative rule that petitioner challenges, 
and the case, therefore, will soon be moot.

As explained below in the discussion of the first reason 
why this petition should be denied, a key assertion made in 
petitioner’s statement of the case—viz., that the Reedsport 
School District’s (District’s) practice and policy is to provide

REASONS WHY THE PETITION 
SHOULD BE DENIED 
I. Both petitioner and the Court of Appeal’s dissent rely 
on the erroneous assumption that the State generally 
provides special education services on site at secular 
private schools. 


Both petitioner and the Ninth Circuit dissenters insist that 
the State and the District generally provide special education 
services on site at non-religious private schools. Petitioner 
asserts that the District’s practice and policy “is to provide on- 
site special education services to children with disabilities at- 
tending public schools and non-religious private schools, but 
withhold such services from children with disabilities attend- 
ing religious schools.” (Pet 5; emphasis in original). Petitioner 
also contends that the receipt of “on-site special education 
services” is “a benefit generally available to a disabled stu- 
dent under Oregon law[.]” (Pet 20). In arguing why the ques- 
tions presented are of national importance, petitioner again 
asserts that the benefits at issue here “would be available [un- 
der Oregon law] on-site to a child in a non-religious private 
school.” (Pet 21). | 


The Court of Appeal’s dissent in based on the same as- 
sumption as the one made by petitioner. The dissent emphati- 
cally insists that Oregon has “decided to provide [special edu- 


3 


cation] services in private schools” and that, in Oregon, 
“[hJandicapped children at secular private schools get special 
education in their schools[.]” (Pet, App B, 16a, 17a). The dis- 
sent also contends that “[uJnder Oregon’s regulation, if a 
blind child with cerebral palsy goes to a secular private 
school, he gets special education in his school,” and that the 
state provides special education services “generally to all 
children in their public or private schools.” (Pet, App B, 20a, 
22a). 


However, petitioner’s and the dissent’s repeated assertion 
that the District specifically and school districts in the State 
generally provide special education services on site at private 
non-sectarian schools is not supported by the record in this 
case or by the applicable law. As a factual matter, the record 
does not support petitioner’s and the dissent’s claim that the 
practice and policy of the District specifically or of Oregon 
school districts generally is to provide on-site special educa- 
tion services at private non-religious schools.” Instead, as the 
Ninth Circuit majority found, “[t]he record does not disclose 
whether Reedsport or other school districts provide services at 
nonsectarian private schools,” although admittedly the rule 
“does not preclude them from doing so.” (Pet, App B, 7a n 2). 
Petitioner does not dispute that finding. 


Moreover, it is not reasonable to assume that, absent the 
“religiously-neutral setting” requirement, school districts gen- 
erally would provide special education services on site at pri- 


? In support of the assertion, petitioner cites only the District Court’s 
order observing that the state administrative rule “has the effect of allow- 
ing in-class services to disabled students at non-religious schools while 
prohibiting in-class services to disabled students at religious schools, both 
public and private.” (Pet, App C, 33a; emphasis added). Although the rule 
may allow the provision of on-site services at secular private schools, the 
record does not demonstrate that such services are in fact provided on site 
at any such school. 


4 


vate schools. A number of factors might enter into a school 
district’s discretionary choice of whether to provide services 
on site, such as: cost, the number of students involved, the 
number of private schools involved, the distance between . 
them, proximity to a public school, whether the services could 
be provided in a group setting or are necessarily one-on-one 
in nature, and the facilities available at a particular private 
school. An evaluation of those factors would not necessarily 
lead a school district to decide to provide on-site services. 
Thus, as a matter of fact, petitioner’s and the dissent’s asser- 
tion that special education services generally are provided on 
site at private, non-religious schools is not supported. 


As a legal matter, the provision of on-site services also is 
not required. Under a portion of the Board’s rule that is not 
challenged in this case, a disabled child does not have a right 
to be provided with on-site services at any private school. 
That portion of the rule provides that if a child with a disabil- 
ity “is offered a free appropriate public education by the 
child’s resident school district and the parent of the child uni- 
laterally enrolls the child in a private school,” the child has no 
“individual entitlement to receive special education and re- 
lated services from the child’s resident school district.” OAR 
581-015-0166(2) (Pet, App F, 53a). Instead, the question of 
whether services should be provided at all, and if so, how and 
where, is left to the school district’s sole discretion: in such 
situations, the district “shall determine which children with 
disabilities shall receive services, what services shall be pro- 
vided and how those services shall be provided, including the 
location for the delivery of those services.” OAR 581-015- 
0166(2) (Pet, App F, 53a; emphasis added).°

Because the unchallenged portion of the state administra- 
tive rule makes it clear that no child has a right to on-site 
services at any private school, when the District Court upheld 
petitioner’s constitutional challenges to the disputed “relig- 
iously-neutral setting” provision, the court nevertheless did 
not order that KDM or any other student was entitled to on- 
site services at a private school. Instead, the court simply or- 
dered that the District could not rely on the “religiously neu- 
tral” requirement to deny on-site services. The District re- 
tained discretion as to whether to provide such services at all, 
and, if so, how and where to provide them. In sum, striking 
down the “religiously-neutral setting” provision does not en- 
title any child to on-site services at a private school or lead 
inexorably to the conclusion that such services will be pro- 
vided on site. 


Admittedly, under the disputed portion of the state rule, 
special education benefits are not available on site except in a 
religiously-neutral setting. However, it does not follow and, 
as noted, the record does not establish that benefits therefore 
are available and provided on site at private, non-sectarian 
schools. On the contrary, the unchallenged portion of the rule 
clearly states that no child at any private school has the right 
to receive benefits at all, and that whether and, if so, how and 
where such benefits are provided, is instead a matter entrusted 
to the discretion of the individual school district. Thus, peti- 
tioner’s argument, and the difference in treatment of which 
petitioner complains, are based on an incorrect assumption: 
viz., that students in private non-sectarian schools receive on- 
site services, while those in religious schools do not. Because 
that assumption, which is reiterated throughout the Ninth Cir- 
cuit dissent and the petition, appears to underlie much of the 
dissent’s and the petitioner’s analysis, and the assumption is 
at odds with the text of the unchallenged portion of the state’s

rule and is not supported by the record, this court should deny 
the petition. 


II. Petitioner and the Ninth Circuit dissent exaggerate 
both the sweep of the majority’s opinion and the pos- 
sible disagreement amongst the circuits. 


Petitioner, and the dissent in the Ninth Circuit, seek to 
portray the majority’s decision as one sweeping in its scope. 
Both petitioner and the dissent also suggest that the circuits 
are split and that this Court, therefore, should grant the peti- 
tion.” Both of those claims are exaggerated. 


First, the Ninth Circuit majority stressed that its decision 
was based on, and limited to, the facts of this case. The ma- 
jority emphasized that: 


The narrow question before us is whether the free 
exercise rights of KDM and his parents were imper- 
missibly burdened by the application of Oregon’s 
regulation, which precludes the District from provid- 
ing special education services to KDM at the sectarian 
school he attended. In deciding that question we are 
guided by the distinction the Supreme Court has rec- 
ognized in the Establishment Clause context between a 
statute ’s invalidity on its face and its invalidity in par- 
ticular applications. See Bowen v. Kendrik, 487 U.S. 
589, 602 (1988); see also Hunt v. McNair, 413 U.S. 
734, 742 (1973) (examining only “the transaction 
presently before [the court]” and not the statute as a 
whole”); Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills Sch. Dist., 963 
F.2d 1190, 1194 n. 3 (9th Cir. 1992) (considering 


* Petitioner asserts that “the failure of lower courts to reach consistent 
conclusions regarding the constitutionality of structurally similar educa- 
tional benefit programs impedes parents’ free exercise of their religious 
education choices for their children[.]” (Pet 21). 


7 


“only the validity of one very specific proposed appli- 
cation of the statutes at issue”); rev'd on other 
grounds, 509 U.S. 1 (1993). Whatever the impact of 
the [Oregon] regulation might be in other factually 
distinct situations, cf Zobrest, 963 F.2d at 1192 (fail- 
ure to provide a sign-language interpreter in the class- 
room for plaintiff effectively forced his parents to 
choose between foregoing a sectarian education or 
paying for the cost of the interpreter themselves) and 
Peter v. Wedl, 155 F.3d 992 (8th Cir. 1998) (failure to 
provide a spastic quadriplegic student with needed 
fulltime paraprofessional while in school forced 
choice between forgoing sectarian education or paying 
for the paraprofessional), this is not a case in which 
the regulation impinges on plaintiffs’ free exercise 
rights. 


(Pet, App B, 9a-10a; emphasis added). 


Having stressed that it was dealing with an as-applied 
challenge and that its decision, therefore, was limited to the 
facts of this case, the majority then turned to those facts. 


The parties stipulated that the service provided to 
KDM at the fire hall down the street from the school 
twice a week for ninety minutes is in compliance with 
KDM’s statutory individualized education plan, the 
adequacy of which is not in dispute, and that he could 
safely travel there—indeed, the vision specialist 
comes to KDM’s school, picks him up and them re- 
turns him to his school. Moreover, plaintiffs have 
stipulated that the vision specialist’s services would 
not be provided in-class at Harbor Baptist but in a 
separate room. Thus, there is no support for the district 
court’s finding that the regulation forces KDM and his 
parents to choose between enrolling at Harbor Baptist 


8 


and receiving special education at the fire hall or en- 
rolling at a nonreligious school and receiving in-class 
services. In sum, there is no showing that application 
of the regulation to KDM’s case burdens KDM’s or 
his parents’ free exercise of their religion. 


(Pet, App B, 10a). Thus, the majority held only that, in this 
case where no burden was shown, petitioner had not sustained 
his free exercise claim. That conclusion was derived from the 
facts of this case, which include: petitioner’s agreement that 
KDM’s individualized education plan is adequate, that trav- 
eling to the fire hall is safe, and that, even if the vision spe- 
cialist’s services were provided at Harbor Baptist, they would 
not be provided in KDM’s regular classroom, but instead in a 
separate room. The majority’s decision is not a broad or 
sweeping one, but is instead fact-bound, case-specific, and 
limited. 


As noted, petitioner and the dissent in the Ninth Circuit 
also suggest that the circuits have reached disparate results 
and have failed “to reach consistent conclusions regarding the 
constitutionality of structurally similar educational benefit 
programs[.]” (Pet 21). For that proposition, petitioner and the 
dissent cite Strout v. Albanese, 178 F.3d 57 (ist Cir. 1999), 
cert. denied, 120 S.Ct. 329 (1999), as being consistent with 
KDM, and decisions from the Third, Sixth and Eighth Circuits 
as being inconsistent with the Ninth Circuit’s decision in this 
case. Brown v. Borough of Mahaffey, Pa., 35 F.3d 846 (3d 
Cir. 1994); Hartmann vy. Stone, 68 F.3d 973 (6th Cir. 1995); 
Peter v. Wedl, 155 F.3d 992 (8th Cir. 1998). 


An examination of those decisions, admittedly, may re- 
veal some analytical tension between them. Compare, e.g., 
Strout, 178 F.3d at 63 (holding, in a case involving a chal- 
lenge to a state statute providing that the state will pay for 
some private school education “provided the institutions are 


9 

‘non-sectarian’ in nature,” that the question is “whether gov- 
ernment has placed a substantial burden on the observation of 
a central [religious] belief or practice;” internal quotation 
marks and emphasis omitted); with Hartmann, 68 F.3d at 979 
n. 3 (“substantial burden” test is “designed to judge [only] 
those situations in which a neutral and generally applicable 
regulation imposes an indirect, albeit substantial, burden on 
the practice of religion”). On closer examination, however, 
the differing results and the apparent analytical tension appear 
to be largely a result of radically differing fact patterns. Cer- 
tainly nothing in the Ninth Circuit’s opinion suggests that the 
results in these cases would be different were the cases ana- 
lyzed under the framework the court utilized in this case. 


In Peter v. Wedl—the case on which petitioner principally 
relies for his claim that there is a circuit conflict (Pet 11-13)— 
the state school district refused to provide the services of a 
full-time paraprofessional to a “severely disabled child” at the 
child’s religious school even though the child required such 
assistance in order to be able “to function in a school class- 
room.” 155 F.3d at 994. The school district’s refusal was 
based on a state statute allowing the provision of special edu- 
cation services only at a “neutral site,” which was defined to 
include “a nonsectarian private school.” Jd. 


In contrast to this case, where the burden imposed by the 
state’s rule is at most de minimis, the burden imposed in Peter 
was substantial. The full-time paraprofessional cost about 
$10,000 per year and, ultimately, the child’s parents were 
forced to choose between placing their son at a sectarian 
school and providing him with the assistance he needed to 
attend school at all. Jd. at 994. Moreover, the record demon- 
strated that the district in fact provided assistance to students 
at nonsectarian private schools—a showing not made in this 
case. Id. at 997 


10 


Hartmann v. Stone dealt with an Army regulation prohib- 
iting on-base day-care providers “from having any religious 
practices, such as saying grace or reading Bible stories, during 
their day-care program.” 68 F.3d at 975. One of the plaintiffs 
had been a day-care provider under the Army program “until 
she discovered Army regulations prohibited her from provid- 
ing any religious activities for her children or those children 
whose parents had requested such activities.” /d. The other 
plaintiffs were active duty Army personnel or their spouses 
who had been forced to choose between obedience to the 
Army regulations and selecting the child care they wanted. 
Again, in contrast to the at most de minimis burden shown 
here, the burden in Hartmann was substantial, and the Army 
in fact treated day-care providers who included some religious 
practices differently from those who did not. 


Brown v. Borough of Mahaffey, Pa., also is strikingly dis- 
similar to this case. There, in the midst of a situation marked 
by extreme animosity toward the plaintiff, a Pentecostal min- 
ister conducting revival meetings, the borough repeatedly 
locked a gate giving access to the site where the revival 
meetings were to be held. 35 F.3d at 848-49. Both the impact 
and the religious animus shown in that case are unlike any- 
thing disclosed in this record, where the Ninth Circuit major- 
ity noted that “the District’s solicitousness in accommodating 
KDM could hardly be said to reflect a purpose to ‘suppress[] 
religion or religious conduct.’” (Pet, App B, 1 1a). 


Similarly, both Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. 
City of Hialeah, 508 U.S. 520 (1993), and McDaniel v. Paty, 
435 U.S. 618 (1978)—both relied on by petitioner and said to 
be contrary to the Ninth Circuit’s decision in this case—in- 
volve notably different facts. In Lukumi, regulations that were 
ostensibly facially neutral were in fact “targeted” against a 
central religious practice of the Santeria religion—animal sac- 
rifice. This Court held that “[t]he Free Exercise Clause pro- 


11 


tects against governmental hostility which is masked as well 
as overt.” 508 U.S. at 534. In McDaniel v. Paty, state law for- 
bade a minister from serving as a delegate to the state’s lim- 
, ited constitutional convention. 435 U.S. at 620. The minister 
was forced to choose between his vocation and his desire to 
exercise his political rights “because the State ha[d] condi- 
tioned the exercise of one on the surrender of the other.” Jd. at 
626. 


In sum, in Lukumi and in McDaniel, as in the circuit court 
cases on which petitioner relies, substantial burdens were im- 
posed by the challenged laws. Therefore, the suggestions in 
some of those circuit court cases that the substantial burden 
test does not or should not apply are mere dicta. See, e.g., 
Hartmann, 68 F.3d at 979 n. 3. But most importantly, nothing 
in the Ninth Circuit opinion suggests that those cases would 
come out differently under the majority’s analysis in this case. 
Thus, when viewed in the light of their facts, the cases peti- 
tioner cites are readily reconcilable. In those cases where a 
substantial burden was created, a free exercise violation was 
found. See Peter v. Wedl; Hartmann; Brown. In contrast, in 
this case no burden, other than perhaps a de minimis one, is 
present. Petitioner has pointed to no case, and the state is 
aware of none, where no burden, or at most a de minimis one, 
has been held to amount to a free exercise violation. Simply 
put, the circuit conflict petitioner identifies is more apparent 
than real. Because the Ninth Circuit’s decision is carefully 
and explicitly limited to its facts, and because the cases can be 
reconciled in light of the facts on which they appear to de- 
pend, this Court should deny the petition. 


III. This case will soon be moot. 


As noted in the questions presented, the state Board is in 
the process of repealing the portion of its administrative rule 
that is at issue here. Petitioner’s attack is aimed at the subsec- 


12 


tion of the rule that states that special education and related 
services “shall be provided in a religiously-neutral setting.” 
OAR 581-015-0166(3) (Pet, App F, 53a). The state Board in- 
tends to repeal that portion of the rule. Appended to this brief 
is the proposed rule amendment, which would delete that por- 
tion of the rule. (App B, App-11). A hearing on the proposed 
rule amendment is scheduled for October 24, 2000. (App B, 
App-7). The Board next meets on December 7, 2000, and the 
rule amendment should become final and effective soon after 
that meeting. 


In light of the impending rule repeal, this case is on the 
verge of becoming moot. The repeal of the provision that is at 
issue here would moot this case. 


It has long been settled that a federal court has no 
authority “to give opinions upon moot questions or ab- 
stract propositions, or to declare principles or rules of 
law which cannot affect the matter in issue in the case 
before it.” Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895). 
See also Preiser v. Newkirk, 422 U.S. 395, 401 (1975); 
North Carolina vy. Rice, 404 U.S. 244, 246 (1971). For 
that reason, if an event occurs while a case is pending 
on appeal that makes it impossible for the court to 
grant “any effectual relief whatever” to a prevailing 
party, the appeal must be dismissed. Mills, 159 U.S. at 
653. 


Church of Scientology v. United States, 506 U.S. 9, 12 (1992). 
For that reason, this Court should deny the petition. The is- 
sues presented soon will be hypothetical, and the Court “does 
not sit to decide hypothetical issues or to give advisory opin- 
ions about issues as to which there are not adverse parties be- 
fore” the Court. Princeton University v. Schmid, 455 U.S. 
100, 102 (1982) (citations omitted). 


13 


Moreover, the state Board will have little incentive to de- 
fend a rule that it no longer believes is necessary or desirable. 


Constitutional adjudication being a matter of 
“great gravity and delicacy,” see Ashwander v. TVA, 
297 U.S. 288, 345 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring), 
we base our refusal to pass on the merits on “the pol- 
icy rules often invoked by this Court ‘to avoid passing 
prematurely on constitutional questions. * * *’” 


Kremens v. Bartley, 431 U.S. 119, 128 (1977), quoting 
Franks v. Bowman Transportation Co., 424 U.S. 747, 756n 8 
(1976). Because, with the amendment of the rule, no actual, 
live controversy will be presented here, this Court should 
avoid passing prematurely on the constitutional questions pre- 
sented. Kremens, 431 U.S. at 129 (passage of new statute 
“clearly moots” claims presented). See also Department of 
Treasury v. Galioto, 477 U.S. 556, 559-60 (1986) (same).° 


Admittedly, in some rare cases this Court has considered 
the validity of legal provisions despite their subsequent re- 
peal, invoking “the ‘well-settled’ rule that ‘a defendant’s vol- 
untary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a 
federal court of its power to determine the legality of the 
practice.” Fla. Gen. Contractors v. Jacksonville, 508 U.S. 


* Although claims about the validity of a statute or rule can be mooted 
by the enactment of new provisions, a case nevertheless may not be moot 
if a claim for damages is presented. See Richmond v. Crosen Co., 488 U.S. 
469, 478 n 1 (1989) (so holding); Memphis Light, Gas & Water Div. y. 
Craft, 436 U.S. 1, 8 (1978) (same). That exception does not apply here. In 
his complaint, petitioner sought an award of damages, but that request was 
based solely on his claim brought under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. The District 
Court ruled against that claim “[bJecause there [was] no evidence that [the 
superintendent] intentionally or recklessly violated plaintiff's clearly es- 
tablished constitutional rights,” (Pet, App C, 34a), and petitioner has never 
renewed the claim or challenged the District Court’s ruling. Aside from 
that claim for damages, petitioner has sought only prospective relief. 


14 


656, 662 (1993), quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle, 
Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982). In those cases, the Court has 
observed that “the defendant’s ‘repeal of the objectionable 
language would not preclude it from re-enacting precisely the 
same provision if the District Court’s judgment were va- 
cated.’”’ Jd. The Court appears to have considered the validity 
of subsequently repealed laws, however, only when it had 
some reason, grounded in the record, to suspect that the “ob- 
jectionable language” would be reenacted if the case were 
dismissed. In City of Mesquite, for example, the city “ha[{d] 
announced just such an intention.” 455 U.S. at 289. 


Here, in contrast, the State has asserted that it intends to 
repeal the disputed rule and not to enact any similar provision 
in its place. The state Board’s “statement of need” for the 
proposed rule amendment explains that, although the rule 
“was adopted in the belief that the state and federal law re- 
quired this provision,” “[rJecent legal opinions have called 
this position into question” and the current understanding “‘is 
that this provision is no longer constitutionally required.” 
(App B, App-9). See, e.g., Agostini v. Felton, 521 U.S. 203 
(1997) (placing public school teachers in parochial schools 
does not violate the First Amendment). Absent a constitu- 
tional mandate for the rule, the Board “does not believe that 
other policy reasons support the continuation of this rule[.]” 
(App B, App-9). This Court has no reason to suspect or to dis- 
regard the State’s good faith assertion that it intends to repeal 
the portion of the rule in dispute and not to enact any similar 
provision in its place. 


In sum, because of the imminent repeal of the rule provi- 
sion challenged here, this case will soon be moot. Therefore, 
this Court should deny the petition.

CONCLUSION 


For the reasons stated above, the petition for certiorari 
should be denied.

this potential litigation.

Public Agency Responsibility for Private School 
Children with Disabilities and Limitation on Services 

581-015-0166(1) No private school child with a 
disability has an individual right to receive some or all of 
the special education and related services that the child 
would receive if enrolled in a public school. 

(2) Decisions about the services that will be provided 
to private school children with disabilities must be made 
in accordance with OAR 581-015-0171 and -0151. 

[(3) Such special education and related services shall 
be provided in a religiously-neutral setting.) 

(3) [(4)] The services provided to private school 
children with disabilities must be provided by personnel 
meeting the same standards as personnel providing 
services in the public schools or ECSE program. 

(4) [(5)] (Private school children with disabilities may 
receive a different amount of services than children with 
disabilities in public schools. 

(5) [(6)] No private school child with a disability is 
entitled to any service or to any amount of a service the 
child would receive if enrolled in a public school. 

(6) [(7)] Public agencies are not required to develop 
an IEP or IFSP that assumes a public agency placement 
for each private school child each year. 

(7) [(8)] If a parent of a private school child with a 
disability requests an IEP or IFSP meeting, the public 
agency shall either: 

(a) hold an IEP meeting within a reasonable time; or 

(b) provide the parent with prior written notice of the 
public agency’s refusal to hold an IEP or IFSP meeting.
