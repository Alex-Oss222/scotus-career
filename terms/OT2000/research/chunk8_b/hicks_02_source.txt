QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


(1) Does either the sovereign immunity of the State of 
Nevada, or the qualified immunity of its officers, preclude tribal 
court jurisdiction in actions stating tort and constitutional claims 
against individually-named state Officials, for official actions 
taken in Indian country with express permission of a tribal 
judge? 


(2) Is decision on the question of tribal court jurisdiction 
over state officials properly bifurcated, with consideration of the 
tribal court’s jurisdiction separated from, and antecedent to, 
decision on the officials’ claims of sovereign and qualified 
immunity? 


(3) Is the rule of Montana v. United States, 450 U.S. 544 
(1981), creating a presumption against tribal court jurisdiction 
over non-members, limited to cases in which a cause of action 
against a non-member arises on lands within a reservation 
which are not controlled by the tribe?

JURISDICTION 


The opinion of the Ninth Circuit was entered on 
November 9, 1999. The State’s petition for rehearing and 
suggestion for rehearing en banc were denied by order dated 
March 9, 2000. App. D-1. 

In the federal district court, the jurisdictional basis for 
challenging tribal court jurisdiction over state officials was 
federal question jurisdiction. 28 U.S.C.A. § 1331. Jurisdiction 
of the Ninth Circuit was based on 28 U.S.C. § 1291. The 
jurisdiction of this Court is invoked pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 
1254(1). 

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 

The decision of the Ninth Circuit in this case holds, for 
the first time, that tribal courts may exercise jurisdiction in 
civil actions filed against state officials. The following facts 
give rise to this exceptional decision. 

This case began with official actions taken by Nevada 
game wardens in 1990 and 1991, on the Fallon Paiute- 
Shoshone Reservation (the “Reservation”), located sixty miles 
east of Reno, and wholly within Nevada. The Reservation was 
established by authority of the Act of April 30, 1908, 35 Stat. 
85, and subsequent enactments, and no treaties pertain to its 
existence. 


Significantly, the actions of the game wardens were 
approved by a tribal judge. On two occasions separated by a 
year’s time, one or more wardens seized different mounted 
bighorn sheep’s heads from the on-reservation residence of 
tribal member Floyd Hicks. The wardens believed the 
property to be evidence of off-reservation poaching of bighorn 
sheep, in violation of state law. Before seizing the heads, 
warden Michael Spencer swore out search warrants, see 
Affidavits of Michael Spencer, App. E-1 to E-2 and F-3 to F- 
4, in a state justice court, then obtained approval from a tribal 
judge for execution of the warrants on the reservation. See 
App. at F-1 and G-1 (search warrants approved by Tribal 
Judge Rebecca Harold). The wardens also obtained 
accompaniment to the Hicks residence from tribal police 
officers. In each case, the seized property proved not to be 
evidence of crime, and was returned. Subsequently Floyd 
Hicks sued Warden Spencer in two separate actions, and 
named two other wardens and the administrator! of the Nevada 
Division of Wildlife (NDOW) in one of them.? The suits were 
brought in the same tribal court that had approved the 
warrants. Hicks named the State of Nevada as well as the 
Officials in both their individual and official Capacities. His 
complaints alleged that the wardens had not followed tribal 
code provisions pertaining to search and seizure. The original 
claims sounded in tribal and common law tort, and also 
purported to rely on the Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. §§ 
1301 to 1341. App. at H-1 to H-17.> Mr. Hicks amended the

The State appealed to the Ninth Circuit on October 29, 
1996. On October 7, 1997, the case was argued and 
submitted. Three days later, the case was withdrawn from 
submission. For two years thereafter, the case languished, 
being resubmitted on November 4, 1999, and decided on 
November 9, 1999. 


In its decision affirming the lower court, the Ninth 
Circuit concluded that the, State had exhausted its remedies on 
the issue of tribal court jurisdiction, thus making it “ripe for 
federal review. We conclude that the tribal court has subject 
matter jurisdiction over the claims brought by Hicks against 
the state officials.” 196 F.3d at 1029 (App. at A-12). 


The Circuit also ruled that the immunity questions 
were not exhausted in tribal court and therefore were not 
properly before the district court or the appeals court. Jd. 
(App. at A-3). 

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 

The State urgently suggests that its petition be granted 
for two reasons. First, the decision whose review is sought 
presents to this Court for direct review, for the first time, the 
question of tribal civil adjudicative jurisdiction over state 
Officials. In deciding this case, the Ninth Circuit creates new 
federal law, which implicates fundamental federalism 
principles. It does this by elevating tribal courts to a new, 
constitutional status not warranted by the text or history of the 
Constitution; and it achieves this result by diminishing state 
sovereignty in a manner also unjustified by a faithful reading 
of the Constitution and decisions of this Court, which construe 
it. In addition, the decision sets out an onerous procedure for 
federal court review of claims of state immunity in tribal 


court. Ultimately, all of these issues are peculiarly within the 
province of this Court to evaluate and decide. _ 


The second reason for granting the petition is to 
reconcile inconsistent circuit court decisions addressing the 
import of this Court's opinions in Montana v. United States, 
450 U.S. 544 (1981), and subsequent cases. 


I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT COURT OF APPEALS 
HAS DECIDED IMPORTANT FEDERAL QUESTIONS 
ABOUT FEDERALISM, STATES, AND TRIBES IN A 
MANNER THAT CONFLICTS WITH RELEVANT 
DECISIONS OF THIS COURT, AND WHICH IN ANY 
EVENT SHOULD BE RESOLVED BY THIS COURT 
DUE TO THEIR SIGNIFICANCE. 

Both the federal district court and the Ninth Circuit 
decided the issue of the tribal court’s jurisdiction as if the state 
officials were run-of-the-mill tort feasors. Virtually all 
analysis was committed to the subject of tribal sovereignty, 
and none to the aspects of state sovereignty which were 
equally present and affected. In their disregard for the status 
of the state officials, the courts have set the law upon a new 
and misbegotten course. 

A. THE SUPREME COURT SHOULD 
EXAMINE THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S TREATMENT OF 
STATE SOVEREIGNTY, AND DEFINE THE EFFECT 
OF STATE IMMUNITY ON TRIBAL JURISDICTION. 

1. Sovereign immunity. 

The Circuit’s holding in this case is that tribal courts 
have jurisdiction to hear actions against state officials. This 
holding recognizes no distinction among the tort claims, tribal 
claims, and federal civil rights claims attempted against the 
state officials. In so deciding, the Circuit necessarily deter- 
mined that state sovereign immunity is not a jurisdictional bar 
in the tribal forum as to any claim. This is a remarkable 
holding in two regards, both of which deserve this Court’s 
review. First, as addressed in this section, the decision imparts

states which this Court disparaged in Alden v. Maine, 527 U.S. 
706, 727 (1999), quoting from Hans v. Louisiana, 134 U.S. 1, 
18 (1890). The Ninth Circuit’s holding--that a tribal court 
may have jurisdiction over state officials for actions taken in 
their official capacities--is directly at odds with ‘history and 
experience, and the established order of things,” id, which 
define state immunity. Only this Court may determine 
whether the Ninth Circuit’s view is the correct one. 


This Court has dealt over the years with the issue of 
states’ susceptibility to suit in different circumstances. The 
law has developed along several branches. 


Suit against states qua states in federal courts has been 
addressed in a continually developing line of decision, 
beginning with the vintage opinions in Chisholm v. Georgia, 2 
Dall. 419 (1792) and Hans v. Louisiana, 134 U.S. 1 (1890), 
through to such recent decisions as Blatchford v. Native 
Village of Noatak, 501 U.S. 775 (1991), Seminole Tribe of 
Fla. v. Florida, 517 U.S. 44 (1996), and Kimel v. Florida 
Board of Regents, 120 S. Ct. 631 (2000), to cite but a few. 

To avoid the general bar against federal citizen suits 
naming states as defendants, the Court has developed a 
doctrine to permit suit against state officials when the officials 
allegedly act in violation of federal law. This branch of 
decision is well-known as the Ex Parte Young doctrine, after 
the decision in Ex Parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908). 


Yet another branch of decision, an apparent extension 
of the Ex Parte Young doctrine, see Hafer v. Melo, 502 U.S. 
21, 30-31 (1991), recognizes federal court jurisdiction to hear 
damages claims against individually-named state officials who 


7 


allegedly violate individuals’ civil rights. This doctrine, 
embodied in Monroe v. Pape, 365 U.S. 167 (1961), has been 
referred to as “the Ex Parte Young of retrospective relief.” 


John C. Jeffries, Jr., “In Praise of the Eleventh Amendment 
and Section 1983,” 84 Virginia L. Rev. 47, 59 (1998). 


Finally, suit against a state in another state’s courts was 
addressed in Nevada v. Hall, 440 U.S. 410 (1979), and suit 
against states in state courts, pursuant to Congressional 
mandate, was reached recently in Alden v. Maine, 527 U.S. 
706 (1999). 


Thus in all instances in which the sovereignty of states 
has been interposed to defeat a court’s claim of jurisdiction, 
this Court has taken up the issue. 


This case presents yet another challenge to states’ 
_ sovereignty, this time in a tribe’s courts. The Court again 
should accept and hear the case, even if the issue presented is 
daunting. Cf Ex Parte Young, 209 U.S. at 143. 


The conundrum in the instant matter is whether the 
immunity of a state, within its own legal borders, is 
susceptible to breach by the courts of another sovereign, 
whose role--if there is one--in “our federalism,” Alden v. 
Maine, 527 U.S. 706, 748 (1999), is extra-constitutional. The - 
Ninth Circuit in this matter answered this question in the 
affirmative. Respondents will add that the effect of immunity 
awaits decision at trial, but nonetheless it is clear the Circuit 
has expressly ruled the state’s immunity does not bear upon 
the issue of jurisdiction. This aspect of the decision requires 
examination. 

The State has maintained throughout that its officials 
may not, in any capacity, under any circumstances, be called 
to answer in a tribal forum. This assertion is based upon the 
historical understanding of state immunity. “The contours of 
sovereign immunity are determined by the founders’ 
understanding. .. .” Alden v. Maine, 527 U.S. at 734. It is 
simply not within the contemplation of the framers of the 


8 


constitution, who devised “our federalism,” that such would be 
so. 


“It is incontestible that the Constitution established a 
system of ‘dual sovereignty.’” Printz v. U.S., 521 U.S. 898, 
918 (1997), quoting from Gregory v. Ashcroft, 501 U.S. 452, 
457 (1991). The Circuit’s decision deeply wounds “our 
federalism” by giving over to a third tier of sovereigns the 
adjudicative power to reach state officials. The decision 
redefines the dual sovereignty of constitutional federalism into 
a tripartite system, which has no textual basis in the 
Constitution. 


The State does not quarrel with the wisdom of modern 
federal policies towards tribes, which have driven the 
expansion of tribal courts’ authority. Instead it maintains that 
those policies cannot influence the definition of tribal court 
jurisdiction, if the result permits tribes to pierce the historical 
immunity of states and their officials. 

In Seminole Tribe of Fla. v. Florida, 517 U.S. at 54, 
this Court stated that, “For over a century we have reaffirmed 
that federal jurisdiction over suits against unconsenting States 
‘was not contemplated by the Constitution when establishing 
the judicial power of the United States’” (quoting from Hans 
v. Louisiana, 134 U.S. 1, 15 (1890). It was no more 
contemplated by the founders, the State would maintain, that 
tribal jurisdiction would exist over unconsenting States or 
their officials. 


Not only was it uncontemplated, but uncontemplatable. 
The possibility of tribal jurisdiction over state officials, in the 
historical context, which is necessarily referenced to construe 
the states’ immunity, is self-evidently implausible. There 
historically were no tribal courts. “Tribal courts in the 
Anglo-American mold were virtually unknown in 1789," Jowa 
Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9 (1987). "[M]ost Indian 
tribes were characterized by a ‘want of fixed laws [and] of 
competent tribunals of justice.’ H.R.Rep. No. 474, 23d Cong., 


9 


Ist Sess., 18 (1834)." Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe, 435 
U.S. 191 (1978). Slowly tribal courts developed only in the 
twentieth century, as an outgrowth of the federal statutory 
scheme. See generally Duro v. Reina, 495 U.S. 676 (1990). 

It is thus a certainty that the notion of state immunity 
never comprehended within it an exception for tribal court 
actions. The invention of such exception by the Ninth Circuit in 
this case deserves careful review, which the State respectfully 
submits may suitably be done at this time, by this Court. As the 
Court has been quick to review and condemn congressional 
acts which would “compromise the structural framework of 
dual sovereignty,” Printz, 521 U.S. at 932, it should be equally 
quick to act when subordinate federal courts similarly 
compromise that framework, as was done in this case by the 
district and appellate courts.

The Ninth Circuit held that the state officials’ 
assertions of qualified immunity were also not jurisdictional. 
“The law is clear that qualified immunity, rather than serving 
as a jurisdictional bar, ‘is an affirmative defense that must be 
pleaded by a defendant official.’” 196 F.3d at 1031 (App. at 
A-14) (citation omitted). But this presumes, without authority, 
that individual capacity lawsuits operate against state officials 
in tribal court in the same manner as in federal court. The 
Circuit’s conclusion that state officials’ qualified immunity is 
not jurisdictional crosses a significant threshold in federal law, 
which the Supreme Court should scrutinize. 

The State suggests that individual immunity of state 
officials is qualified in tribal courts, if at all, in a very different 
sense than it is in federal court. This suggestion is based upon 
the unique evolution of the qualified immunity in federal 
court. 

It has taken more than a century to develop the 
principle that individual-capacity suits against state officials in 
federal court is not suit against a state. Inability to enforce the 


10 


federal civil rights law drove the necessity for its development. 
Until qualified immunity was articulated and refined as 
doctrine, the law now codified at 42 U.S.C. § 1983 was 
“remarkable for its insignificance.” Peter W. Low and John C. 
Jeffries, Jr., Civil Rights Actions: § 1983 and Related Statutes 
II, n.1 (2d ed. 1994). The decision in Wyatt v. Cole, 504 U.S. 
158 (1992) expressly traces development of qualified 
immunity in conjunction with development of § 1983 
jurisprudence in federal court. 

Thus qualified immunity was a necessary artifice. It is 
contrary to the reality that “generally, a suit against a state 
officer is functionally a suit against the state, for the state 
defends the action and pays any adverse judgment. ... In the 
generality of cases, constitutional tort actions against 
government officers are functional substitutes for direct access 
to government treasuries.” Jeffries, supra, 84 Virginia L. Rev. 
at 50. 


Because as a practical matter it trenches closely on 
states’ sovereignty, qualified immunity should be confined to 
operation in federal courts, to serve its original purposes in 
connection with § 1983. From the legislative history of 
§1983, “[i]t is clear that one reason the legislation was passed 
was to afford a federal right in federal courts... .” Monroe v. 
Pape, 365 U.S. 167, 180 (1961). It is not, however, 
abundantly clear that the purpose of § 1983 was to afford a 
federal right in tribal courts. 


In fact it is quite clear that Congress never intended § 
1983 to operate in tribal courts. While the legislative history 
of § 1983 establishes that state courts might also enforce its 
provisions, Patsy v. Florida Board of Regents, 457 U.S. 496, 
506 (1982) (“many legisiators interpreted the bill to provide 
dual or concurrent forums in the state and federal system”’), 
there is no equivalent history to demonstrate that tribal courts 
were intended enforcers. Silence in the congressional debates 
about tribal court’s authority under the measure can only 
signify absence of such intent. Cf Alden v. Maine, 527 U.S. at 

11 


741 (silence in the legislative history for Eleventh Amendment 
on the subject of citizen suits against states in state courts 
“suggests the sovereign’s right to assert immunity from suit in 
its own courts was a principle so well established that no one 
conceived it would be altered by the new Constitution”). 

Sovereign immunity “strikes the proper balance 
between the supremacy of federal law and the separate 
sovereignty of the States.” Jd. at 757. Qualified immunity is 
part of this jurisprudence, and the Ninth Circuit in this case 
has deposited the delicate equilibrium of qualified immunity 
into the wholly dissimilar landscape of state-tribal relations. 
The State asserts that qualified immunity could and should be 
given a jurisdictional import instead, in the same manner as 
have individual immunity defenses of tribal officials in state 
and federal courts. See e.g. Hardin v. White Mountain Apache 
Tribe, 779 F.2d 476, 479 (9th Cir. 1985) (Ninth Circuit 
affirming dismissal of individually named tribal members in 
Title VII and § 1983 action, holding that “tribal immunity 
extends to individual tribal officials acting in their 
representative capacity and within the scope of their 
authority”), Miller v. Coyhis, 877 F. Supp. 1262 (E.D. Wis. 
1995), Suarez v. Newquist, 855 P.2d 1200, 1203 (Wash. App. 
1993), Romanella v. Hayward, 933 F. Supp. 163, 167 (D. Conn. 
1996), Cameron v. Bay Mills Indian Community, 843 F. Supp. 
334, 336 (W.D. Mich. 1994). On this topic, see generally 
Timothy W. Joranko, Tribal Self-Determination Unfettered: 
Toward a Rule of Absolute Tribal Official Immunity from 
Damages in Federal Court, 26 Ariz. St. L. J. 987, 1009-1017 
(1994). Analogously, if state officials are acting as officials 
when acts complained of occur, the tribal court should have no 
jurisdiction over them. 

Even if the Ninth Circuit is correct in holding that 
individual immunity is not jurisdictional, review of the 
correctness of its treatment in this case remains essential. The 
qualified immunity enjoyed by an official in federal court is 
immunity from suit, and it is lost if not decided at the earliest 

12 


stages of litigation. Hunter v. Bryant, 502 U.S. 224, 227 (1991). 
In this case, as the Ninth Circuit would have it, the officials’ 
ardent assertion of the defense stirred no response from the 
tribal court or the tribal court plaintiff. The Ninth Circuit would 
tender the entire matter back to the tribal court. This result now 
requires additional exhaustion after nine years of litigation. 

This renders the immunity, if not worthless, at least a very pale 
protection. 

The question poised in this context is how much 
solicitude will the exhaustion doctrine create for tribal courts 
and tribal court plaintiffs. The consequence for a plaintiff in 
federal court who does not meet a challenge based upon a 
qualified immunity defense is dismissal of the action. See e.g. 
Wegener v. City of Covington, 933 F.2d 390, 392 (6" Cir. 1991), 
Scott v. Clay County, 205 F.3d 867, 874 (6" Cir. 2000), Erwin v. 
Daley, 92 F.3d 521, 525 (7 Cir. 1996), Dixon v. Richer, 922 
F.2d 1456, 1460 (10" Cir. 1991), Salas v. Carpenter, 980 F.2d 
299, 306 (5" Cir. 1992), Bailey v. Board of Cty, Commrs. of 
Alachua Cty., 956 F.2d 1112, 1123 (11" Cir. 1992). Yet here— 
using the most clear-cut example—the Circuit Court refuses to 
dispose of a case against the head of Nevada’s wildlife agency, 
even when all federal judges seem to concur that no claim, nor 
even a single allegation, is made against him. See 196 F.3d at 
1031, 944 F. Supp. at 1469. 

The Ninth Circuit’s suggestion that qualified immunity 
was not broached in the tribal court (“we have never required a 
party to anticipate another party’s qualified immunity defense _ 
before the question of subject matter jurisdiction had been 
decided,” 196 F.3d at 1031) is flatly contradicted by the record. 
Anticipation was unnecessary, because the issue was expressly 
addressed and argued, see App. at J-5 to J-6 (Motion to Quash), 
and at K-8 to K-13, and it was decided, App. at L-1 and C-4 to 
C-7. 

Finally, but not least, the Circuit’s undifferentiated 
treatment of tort claims and constitutional tort claims alike 
under the qualified immunity doctrine makes an immense

expansion of the doctrine. The State has steadfastly maintained 
that an individual capacity lawsuit for common law tort claims 
is inconsistent with a pleading alleging officer actions taken 
under color of state law. Even if the matter is allowed to go 
ferward in tribal court on the constitutional tort claims, it ought 
not go forward on the simple tort claims. The State 
acknowledges development of law to the contrary in the Ninth 
Circuit, see Ashker v. California Dept. of Corrections, 112 F.3d

The effect of state officials’ qualified immunity in tribal

they know they will be subjected to personal exposure in tribal

The State therefore respectfully submits that involvement by

B. THE SUPREME COURT SHOULD 
EXAMINE THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S EXPANSIVE 
DEFINITION OF TRIBAL SOVEREIGNTY AND 
ADJUDICATIVE JURISDICTION. 


This matter is properly reviewed by this Court for the 
additional reason that it involves a substantial redefinition of 
tribal sovereignty. In absence of congressional action, this 
Court possesses ultimate authority to define tribal sovereignty, 
and thus tribal adjudicative jurisdiction. It is clearly not 
within the states’ power to affect this law. It is, foremost, the 
United States Supreme Court which “has frequently been 

14 


required to decide questions concerning the extent to which 
Indian tribes have retained the power to regulate the affairs of 
non-Indians.” National Farmers Union Ins. Cos. v. Crow 
Tribe of Indians, 471 U.S. 845, 851 (1985). 


Thus this Court has directly supervised the 
development of tribal courts into the modern age. In their 
early development, tribes and their courts had authority: 


to make their own substantive law in internal matters, 
see Roff v. Burney, 168 U.S. 218 (1897) (membership); 
Jones v. Meehan, 175 U.S. 1, 29 (1899) (inheritance 
rules); United States v. Quiver, 241 U.S. 602 (1916) 
(domestic relations), and to enforce that law in their 
own forums, see, e. g., Williams v. Lee, 358 U.S. 217 
(1959). 
Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 55-56 (1977). 
To the same effect, see Montana v. United States, 450 U.S. 
544, 564 (1981) (“Indian tribes retain their inherent power [to 
punish tribal offenders,] to determine tribal membership, to 
regulate domestic relations among members, and to prescribe 
rules of inheritance for members. . . . “). 


In their nascent form, tribal courts thus served as a 
shield for tribes against outside influences. “The Court has 
been careful to protect the tribes from interference with tribal 
control over their own membe:s.” Merrion v. Jicarilla Apache 
Tribe, 455 U.S. 130, 170 (1982) (Justice Stevens dissenting). 
The shield has, however, been gradually transformed into a 
sword. From truly internal matters such as “membership,” 
“inheritance rules,” and “domestic relations,” the tribal fora 
have burgeoned into other subject areas, acquiring virtually 
plenary authority, and assuming jurisdiction coextensive with 
the general governmental powers which states possess. 

In the course of this evolutionary development, this 
Court has also frequently been called upon as referee, to define 
the limits of tribal jurisdiction, and to stay the sword of tribal 
jurisdiction. See e.g. El Paso Natural Gas Co. v. Neztsosie, 


15 


526 U.S. 473 (1999), Strate v. A-] Contractors, 520 U.S. 438 
(1997), Burlington Northern Railroad Co. v. Estate of Red 
Wolf, 522 U.S. 801 (1997), Duro v. Reina, 495 U.S. 676 
(1990), Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe, 435 U.S. 191 
(1978). 

So it is now fitting and consistent with past practice 
that the Court opine upon this pioneering pronouncement by 
the Ninth Circuit, that a tribal court may exercise jurisdiction 
over state officials. 

As an initial matter, this Court should review the 
interesting but arbitrary line drawn by the Ninth Circuit to 
explain its inconsistent result in County of Lewis v. Allen, 163 
F.3d 509 (9th Cir. 1998) (en banc). In both cases, the tribe 
expressly authorized the presence of non-member peace 
officers. The Circuit pronounced in County of Lewis v. Allen 
that tribal consent by written agreement to non-tribal peace 
officer presence deprives the tribal court of jurisdiction, 163 
F.3d at 515, but in the present matter held that a tribal judge’s 
consent to the same by signing a search warrant does not. 196 
F.3d at 1027-1028 (App. at A-10 to A-11). 

The Court should also review the Ninth Circuit’s 
creation of a bright-line test to replace the well-known formula 
for determining tribal jurisdiction. That formula requires 
careful examination of “tribal sovereignty, the extent to which 
that sovereignty has been altered, divested, or diminished, as 
well as a detailed study of relevant statutes, Executive Branch 
policy as embodied in treaties and elsewhere, and 
administrative or judicial decisions.” Nat’ Farmers Union 
Ins. Co. v. Crow Tribe of Indians, 471 U.S. at 855-856. 

In this case, the lower courts have departed from the 
formula. The Ninth Circuit has indulged the tribal court with a 
bright-line rule based on territorial control, rather than perform 
the necessary, careful inquiry. The Circuit professes to find 
authority for abandonment of the more inquiring analysis, 


16 


relying on the decision in Strate v. A-] Contractors, 520 U.S. 
438 (1997). 196 F.3d at 1025-1027 (App. at A-7 to A-8). 

It is the State’s simple contention that Strate provides 
no basis to establish a bright-line proxy for the Nat’! Farmers 
Union formula. Rather, as the State has always argued, that 
formula still applies. Under it, proper analysis would include 
examination of the extent to which tribal sovereignty has been 
necessarily divested, and would disclose that one of the 
necessarily divested tribal powers, see United States v. 
Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313, 323 (1978), is the power to call state 
officials to answer in tribal courts. 


This limitation may be deduced from various lines of 
Supreme Court decision. One is that which confirms the 
nature and vigor of state sovereignty and immunity as a 
constitutional precept, as described in the earlier sections. 
Another is that line of cases describing tribes as limited 
sovereigns, United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313, 323 
(1978), whose sovereignty—unlike that of the states—is subject 
to defeasance. Compare, e.g., Escondido Mut. Water Co. v. 
La Jolla Band of Mission Indians, 466 U.S. 765, 787 n.30 
(1984) and New York v. United States, 505 U.S. 144, 
183(1992) (even with state consent, Congress may not enlarge 
its powers under the constitution vis-a-vis the state). 

Yet another path exists to arrive at the necessary 
conclusion that tribal courts are divested of jurisdiction over - 
state officials. It is the one embarked upon already by this 
Court in its previous decisions in Blatchford v. Native Village 
of Noatak, 501 U.S. 775 (1991), Seminole Tribe of Fla. v. 
Florida, 116 S. Ct. 1114 (1996), and Idaho v. Coeur d’Alene 
Tribe of Idaho, 521 U.S. 261 (1997). The Court’s general 
paradigm in these decisions bespeaks a clear barrier to tribal 
authority over states and their officials. The Jdaho decision 
even ventured into tribes’ authority over officials under the 
doctrine of Ex Parte Young.

The instant case represents yet one more increment in 
the development of federal law which, so far, this Court has 
deemed significant enough to address, and should address here 
to conclude the analysis.

Besides being inconsistent with its own decisions

The opponents of the broad reading, and the Ninth

CONCLUSION 


This petition presents legal issues that are of the first 
order of significance. The Ninth Circuit has unequivocally 
held that a tribal court has jurisdiction over civil actions 
naming state officials. This radically redefines the 
relationship between tribes and states. In doing so, it also 
redefines state and tribal sovereignty, cutting to core principles 
of federal law. — 


The appeals court has also redefined the legal analysis 
used for determining jurisdiction of tribal courts over 
nonmembers, and in the process creates a conflict among the 
circuits on this important subject. 

Finally, the Ninth Circuit has made new law on the 
subject of tribal exhaustion, requiring an inordinately 
prolonged process whenever immunity is present as an issue. 

Beyond its purely legal and academic importance, the 
decision of the Ninth Circuit will constrict the constructive 
interplay between tribes and states. For Nevada at least, a 
state with twenty-six tribes and in which tribal-state relations 
have been remarkably amicable, this means cooperative 
endeavors will become more difficult in areas such as law 
enforcement, fire protection, transportation, education, and 
environmental protection, to name but a few. Concern for 
these real consequences, as much as the more esoteric legal 
disputes, animate this-petition. It should likewise bear on this 
Court’s decision whether to entertain the petition. 

Review by this Court at this point in the litigation is 
critical. Review of questions of law concerning tribal court 
jurisdiction is de novo. Later review challenging tribal court 
findings on immunity defenses, after jurisdiction is already 
established, will be much more deferential and limited. Cf 
FMC v. Shoshone-Bannock Tribes, 905 F.2d 1311, 1313 (9 
Cir. 1990). Respectfully, therefore, the State’s petition should 
be granted now in order for this Court to exercise its full 
authority on these vital questions of federal law.

For all of the foregoing reasons, the petition for a writ 
of certiorari should be granted.

OPINION: 
AMENDED OPINION 
B. FLETCHER, Circuit Judge: 


This case concerns the jurisdiction of a tribal court over claims 
against state officials for tribal common law torts and federal and 
tribal civil rights violations. The events giving rise to these claims 
took place on Indian-owned land on a reservation. 


The State of Nevada and named state officials appeal the 
decision of the district court denying them summary judgment and 
granting summary judgment to Floyd Hicks and the tribal court. 
The district court held that the tribal court had jurisdiction to hear 
the suit brought by Hicks against state officials for tribal common 
law torts and federal and tribal civil rights violations occurring on 
Indian-owned land. It also held that the tribal court action against 
the state officials in their individual capacities was not barred by 
sovereign immunity. It declined to review on the merits the 
officials’ claims of qualified immunity from suit because they had 
not been exhausted before the tribal court. We affirm the district 
court's holding that the tribal court has jurisdiction, and we affinn 
its holding that the issue of qualified immunity was not exhausted 
before the tribal court and therefore was not properly before the 
district court or this court. We conclude the district court similarly 
should have refrained from addressing sovereign immunity, leaving 
the issue to the tribal court in the first instance. 

I. 

Floyd Hicks is an enrolled member of the Fallon Paiute- 
Shoshone Tribe ("Tribe"), a federally recognized Indian tribe with 
over 900 members. The Tribe's reservation in western Nevada 
consists of about 8,000 acres of land heid by the federal 
government in trust for the Tribe and for individual tribal members. 
Hicks lives within the Tribe's reservation on allotted land held by 
the government in trust for him. 

On August 30, 1990, Michael Spencer, a Nevada state game 
warden, obtained a search warrant from the New River Justice 
Court to search Hicks’ property for evidence of the possession or 
killing of a big horn sheep of the California subspecies, a gross 
misdemeanor under Nev. Rev. Stat. § 501.376. The warrant 


A-3 


provided, however, that the state court lacked jurisdiction over the 
Fallon Paiute-Shoshone reservation and that the warrant was valid 
only if approved by the Fallon Tribal Court. 


That same day, a tribal judge approved the warrant but limited 
the search to the "exterior premises and any vehicles thereon." 
Spencer, accompanied by a tribal police officer, allegedly executed 
the warrant and removed at least one mounted big horn sheep head 
trophy from Hicks' residence. However, the trophy was apparently 
of the Rocky Mountain subspecies, and it was later returned to 
Hicks. Hicks asserts that the trophy was damaged when returned. 


On June 12, 1991, Spencer applied for and received another 
state search warrant to seek evidence for the same state offense. 
Spencer, along with state game wardens Rich Ellington and Bill 
Fitzmorris, executed the warrant that same day, again accompanied 
by tribal police and with tribal court approval. One or more big 
horn sheep head trophies belonging to Hicks were removed from 
Hicks' residence. Once again, it was ultimately determined that the 
trophies were not evidence of any state crime or game violation and 
they were returned to Hicks. 


Hicks filed two complaints in Fallon Tribal Court for money 
damages alleging damages resulting from the actions of the state 
and tribal officials on August 30, 1990, and June 12, 1991. The 
complaints named William Molini, Director of the Nevada 
Department of Wildlife, Michael Spencer, Rick [sic] Ellington, and 
Bill Fitzmorris as defendants in both their official and individual 
capacities, and alleged a variety of claims under the Indian Civil 
Rights Act ("ICRA"), 25 U.S.C. § 1302, as well as tort claims under 
tribal common law. By amended complaint, Hicks claimed 
violation of unspecified federal and tribal civil rights. n1 


The tribal court held, in a written order, that it had jurisdiction 
over the actions. Following a challenge to Hicks’ service of process 
by publication, the tribal court quashed the service as ineffective. 
Following cross-appeals, the Intertribal Appellate Court reversed 
and remanded for trial, upholding both the service of process and 
the jurisdiction of the tribal court. Two weeks later, the State of 
Nevada and the named state officials (hereinafter "Nevada") filed 
the present action in federal district court against Hicks, the tribal 
court and tribal judge (hereinafter "tribal appellees"), for 
declaratory relief regarding the issue of tribal court jurisdiction. n2 


A-4 


Before the district court, the parties presented cross-motions for 
summary judgment on whether the tribal court had jurisdiction over 
the claims against the state officials. Meanwhile, the tribal court 
granted Hicks’ motions voluntarily to dismiss the claims agzinst the 
state officials in their official capacity. The district court then held 
that the dismissal mooted the issue of tribal court jurisdiction over 
the state officials in their official Capacities. 


After oral argument and supplemental briefing, the district 
court issued an order denying Nevada's motion for summary 
judgment and granting the motion for summary judgment made by 
Hicks and the tribal appellees. In its order, the district court held 
that the Intertribal Court of Appeals did not err in holding that 
service of process was in accordance with tribal law. n3 The district 
court further held that the tribal court had subject matter 
jurisdiction over the claims brought by Hicks against the state 
Officials in their individual capacities, and that the claims were not 
barred by sovereign immunity. Additionally, the district court held 
that two issues, the claims of qualified immunity by the state 
officials and whether any claims lie against William Molini, had 
not been exhausted in the tribal courts. 

II. 

As a threshold issue, the district court correctly held that it had 
federal question jurisdiction to determine whether the tribal court 
had jurisdiction. See 28 U.S.C. § 1331; see also National Farmers 
Union Ins. Cos. v. Crow Tribe of Indians, 471 U.S. 845, 85 L. Ed 
2d 818, 105 S. Ct. 2447 (1985). We review determinations of 
federal law regarding the extent of tribal court jurisdiction de novo. 
See FMC v. Shoshone-Bannock Tribes, 905 F.2d 13] 1, 1313-14 
(9th Cir. 1990), cert. denied, 499 U.S. 943, 113 L. Ed. 2d 459, 11] 

_ S.Ct. 1404 (1991). 

In determining the tribal court's jurisdiction, the district court 
first noted that no federal statutes provide guidance on the extent of 
tribal court jurisdiction over civil matters. The district court then 
adopted as basic guiding principles the distinction between civil 
and criminal jurisdiction, and the recognition by the courts of "a 
strong geographic component" distinguishing incidents occurring 
on Indian-owned land from those on non-Indian owned land. The 
district court also acknowledged the "general proposition" of 
Montana v. United States, 450 U.S. 544, 565, 67 L. Ed. 2d 493, 101 


A-5 


S. Ct. 1245 (1981), that on non-Indian owned land "the inherent 
sovereign powers of an Indian tribe do not extend to the activities 
of nonmembers of the tribe," n4 but doubted its applicability to the 
instant case after finding Hicks’ allotment to be Indian-owned land. 


The district court suggested instead that the applicable rule on 
the facts before it was that of Williams v. Lee: where the underlying 
incidents occur on Indian-owned land, tribal court jurisdiction is 
presumed unless affirmatively limited by an act of Congress. See 
Williams v. Lee, 358 U.S. 217, 222, 3 L. Ed. 2d 251, 79S. Ct. 269 
(1959) ("This court has consistently guarded the authority of Indian 
governments over their reservations . . . . If this power is to be taken 
away from them, it is for Congress to do it."). However, the district 
court expressed concern that the Supreme Court had not yet made 
clear the circumstances in which the Montana rule should be 
applied and assumed without deciding for the purposes of this case 
that it would apply. It ultimately concluded that, even were 
Montana to apply, the tribal court would still have jurisdiction 
because the underlying facts feli within the exceptions to Montana's 
general presumption against jurisdiction. 

Subsequent to the district court's decision, the Supreme Court 
decided Strate v. A-] Contractors, 520 U.S. 438 (1997). Nevada 
argues on appeal that Strate interprets Montana as precluding tribal 
court jurisdiction over civil actions involving non-Indians 
regardless of whether the underlying incidents occurred on Indian- 
owned land or non-Indian owned land. However, the Strate Court 
made no such determination, a fact we recently noted in County of 
Lewis v. Allen, 163 F.3d 509, 514 (9th Cir. 1998). 


We now affirm the district court's determination that the tribal 
court has jurisdiction over the actions underlying the instant case. 
We also affirm the district court's rulings on the issues of sovereign 
immunity and qualified immunity. 

A. 

The Court in Strate held that a tribal court lacked jurisdiction to 
hear civil tort claims against a non-Indian brought by the widow of 
a tribal member, and her five adult tribal member children, for 
injuries occurring on a public highway maintained by the State of 
North Dakota under a federally granted right-of-way over tribal 
reservation land. 520 U.S. at 442. In Strate, the Court clarified its 
interpretation of Montana, which dealt with tribal regulation of 


A-6 


hunting and fishing in a state-owned river running through a 
reservation. Strate makes clear that the Court's holding in Montana 
is equally applicable to tribal court jurisdiction over incidents 
occurring on a highway running through a reservation over which 
the tribe had ceded all right to control access. However, the Strate 
Court carefully expressed "no view on the governing law or proper 
forum when an accident occurs on a tribal road within a 
reservation." Jd. at 442 (emphasis added). 


The Court emphasized in Strate that the decision in Montana 
related to "reservation land acquired in fee simple by non-Indian 
owners." Jd. at 446. By contrast, "the Montana Court recognized 
that the Crow Tribe retained power to limit or forbid hunting or 
fishing by non-members on land still owned by or held in trust for 
the Tribe." Jd. The Court also reiterated that it "can readily agree,’ 
in accord with Montana, . . . that tribes retain considerable control 
over non-member conduct on tribal land." Jd. (quoting Montana, 
450 U.S. at 557). However, finding that the stretch of highway at 
issue was "aligned . . . for the purpose at hand, with land alienated 
to non-Indians," the Court in Strate opined that the "decision in 
Montana . . . governs this case," 520 U.S. at 456, and further 
decided that neither of the two Montana exceptions applied to the 
facts in that case. n5 

Again in E/ Paso Natural Gas Co. v. Neztsosie, 526 U.S. 473, 
119 S. Ct. 1430, 143 L. Ed. 2d 635 (1999), the Court emphasized 
the significance of land-ownership to the jurisdictional inquiry. 
That case examined the extent of the doctrine requiring tribal court 
exhaustion for claims arising under the Price Anderson Act. 119 S. 
Ct. at 1433. Members of the Navajo Nation brought suit against 
mining companies for injuries arising from uranium mining and 
processing on the Navajo Nation Reservation by the defendants. Jd. 
at 1434. The Court found for El Paso on the ground that the Price- 
Anderson Act preempted the injury claims, requiring that actions 
for liability for nuclear accidents be brought only in federal court. 
The Court, however, rejected the mining companies’ argument that 
under Strate, a tribal court has jurisdiction over nonmembers only 
where the tribe has regulatory jurisdiction with respect to the matter 
at issue. Jd. at 1436 n.4. The Court found petitioners’ reliance on 
Strate was misplaced, because, in contrast to the claims in Strate, 


A-7 


ae 


which arose on a state highway, the events at issue in E/ Paso 
occurred on tribal lands. Jd. 


Our post-Strate opinions are consistent with evolving Supreme 
Court precedent that stresses membership and rights of land 
ownership as sources of tribal power. See, e.g., William C. Canby, 
Jr., American Indian Law 72-78, 128-41 (1998); n6 Allison M. 
Dussias, Geographically-Based and Membership-Based Views of 
Indian Tribal Sovereignty: The Supreme Court's Changing Vision, 
55 U.Pitt. L. Rev. 1 (1994). In Wilson v. Marchington, 127 F.3d 805 
(9th Cir. 1997), cert. denied, 523 U.S. 1074, 118 S. Ct. 1516, 140 L. 
Ed. 2d 669 (1998), we held that a tribal court has no jurisdiction 
over a suit by a tribal member against a nonmember arising from an 
accident on a state highway that runs through the reservation. The 
facts of Wilson mirrored the facts of Strate almost precisely: the 
highway at issue in Wilson was a state highway constructed on a 
right of way granted pursuant to a federal statute with the consent 
of the Blackfeet Nation. Jd. at 8/3-14. The general public had 
unrestricted access to the road, and no statute or treaty authorized 
the tribe to govern the conduct of nonmembers on the highway. /d. 
at 814. 


In County of Lewis v. Allen, 163 F.3d 509, 514 (9th Cir. 1998), 
we looked to "the landowner's right to exclude” certain 
nonmembers from the reservation, rather than land ownership per 
se, as the source of tribal power. Central to that case is a law 
enforcement agreement between the Nez Perce Tribe and the State 
of Idaho that granted jurisdiction to the state to enforce 
misdemeanor laws. Acting under the authority of the agreement, a 
deputy county sheriff arrested a tribal member, Allen, on the 
reservation for disturbing the peace within the reservation. Allen 
brought suit in tribal court against the county and its law 
enforcement officers for torts arising out of the arrest. Allen's 
residence was on fee land not owned by the tribe or the federal 
government, but the record does not reflect whether the fee was 
owned by an Indian or non-Indian. Jd. at 512 n.1. The court stated 
that, "from the standpoint of the exercise of adjudicative authority 
over nonmember county law enforcement officers, it does not 
matter how the land was owned because the consent to criminal 
jurisdiction was tantamount to alienation of the land to non-Indians 


A-8 


for the limited purpose [of patrolling the reservation, investigating 
minor crimes and making arrests].” Jd. at 514. 

We saw the Agreement as "a significant alienation of tribal 
sovereignty and control." /d. Our analogizing to Strate was explicit: 
"Like the tribes in Strate, which consented to and received payment 
for a highway easement, the Nez Perce Tribe ceded its ‘gatekeeping 
right,’ by consenting to and receiving the benefits of state law 
enforcement protection." Jd. We found that the tribal court, having 
surrendered its exclusive criminal jurisdiction over Indians for 
minor criminal acts committed on the reservation, had surrendered 
its civil jurisdiction over the conduct of county officials acting 
pursuant to the Agreement. Jd. 

Finally, in State of Montana v. King, 191 F.3d 1108, 1999 WL 
694953 (9th Cir. 1999), we held that an Indian tribe lacked 
authority to enforce its employment ordinance against a state for 
work done on a state owned right-of-way. Our decision in King is in 
line with the Strate rule: tribes lack authority to regulate, and thus 
power to adjudicate, activities on land alienated to non-Indians. 

Unlike Montana, Strate, Wilson, County of Lewis, and King, the 
incidents underlying the instant case occurred on Indian-owned, 
Indian-controlled land, over which the Tribe retained its. right to 
exclude non-members. n7 In the absence of federal statutes limiting 
it, the Tribe has exclusive criminal jurisdiction in Indian country 
over minor crimes committed by Indians. See Ex parte Crow Dog, 
109 U.S. 556, 27 L. Ed. 1030, 3 S. Ct. 396 (1883). State officers 
have no jurisdiction over such crimes. Outside of Indian country, 
the state has general criminal jurisdiction over all persons, 
including Indians, see, e.g., Hagen v. Utah, 510 U.S. 399, 127 L. 
Ed. 2d 252, 114 S. Ct. 958 (1994). Within Indian country, however, 
the state's jurisdiction is generally limited to those crimes that do 
not concern Indians or indian interests. See id. Before a state 
official can conduct a search and seizure on the reservation to 
investigate a state crime, a tribal search warrant must be issued bya 
judge of the tribal court. See Fallon Tribe Law & Order Code, Title 
1 § 4-40-010, 4-40-030(a)); see also United States v. Anderson, 857 
F. Supp. 52 (D.S.D. 1994) (holding that a state officer lacks power 
to search the reservation residence of an Indian parolee from state 
prison, even though the parolee consented to searches as a condition 


of parole). 
A-9 


In this case, as part of his investigation into a suspected 
violation of a state game law, Warden Spencer filed an affidavit of 
probable cause in the New River Township, County of Churchill on 
August 30, 1990. The state court issued a warrant that stated: 


YOU ARE HEREBY ORDERED, SUBJECT TO OBTAINING 
APPROVAL FROM THE FALLON TRIBAL COURT IN AND 
FOR THE FALLON PAIUTE-SHOSHONE TRIBES, to search the 
said premises .. .. 

This Court has no jurisdiction on the Fallon Paiute-Shoshone 
Indian Reservation and, before any search is conducted in 
furtherance hereof, an approval authorizing same must be obtained 
from the Fallon Tribal Court in and for the Fallon Paiute-Shoshone 
Tribes of the Fallon, Churchill County, Nevada. 


The same application and approval procedure was followed on June 
12, 1991. On both occasions, Spencer was accompanied by a tribal 
police officer when he executed the warrant at Hicks' residence. 


Unlike the Agreement in County of Lewis, the warrant in this 
case bestows no broad grant of authority upon the State of Nevada. 
The Tribe retained sovereignty over the land upon which the search 
and seizure took place. The land on which Hicks' residence stood 
was neither open to the public, nor controlled or maintained by any 
entity other than the Tribe. When the tribal court agreed to grant the 
state warden's request for a warrant, it was exercising its 
"gatekeeping right." The tribal court was free to exclude state 
officials engaged in law enforcement activities on the reservation. 
The tribal court was the sole authority to which the state warden 
could apply - and to which it in fact had to apply - for permission to 
execute a search warrant on the reservation. n8 Further 
underscoring the exclusivity of tribal jurisdiction is the fact that the 
state warden was accompanied by a tribal officer upon the 
execution of each warrant. 


Each authorization of the execution of the warrants marked an 
isolated incident wherein the tribal court granted a state official 
permission to enter the reservation as part of a state investigation 
into an alleged violation of state law. There was no cession of 
criminal law jurisdiction, or indeed of any law enforcement 
jurisdiction at all. Unlike in County of Lewis, no benefit was 
conferred on the Tribe. Law enforcement on the reservation 
remained in the hands of the Tribe. The tribal court merely granted 


A-10 


a state official's request to come onto the reservation for a limited, 
clearly delineated purpose under circumstances where all inherent 
jurisdictional authority lay with the Tribe. We emphasize that the 
tribal court modified the original search warrant by restricting the 
authorized search to “exterior premises only and to vehicles 
thereon," demonstrating that the Tribe retained authority to direct 
the state officers’ activities on tribal land. 


This case then, involves no "significant alienation of tribal 
sovereignty and control" over law enforcement or over Indian- 
owned land within the reservation. See County of Lewis, 163 F.3d 
at 514. Cf. Strate, 520 U.S. at 442 (holding that the grant of the 
right-of-way to the state, which precluded the tribe from exercising 
proprietary rights of exclusion, rendered the highway the equivalent 
of ron-Indian fee land); Montana, 450 U.S. 544, 67 L. Ed. 2d 493, 
101 S. Ct. 1245 (holding that an Indian tribe had no authority to 
regulate hunting and fishing by non-Indians on non-Indian owned 
fee land within the reservation); County of Lewis, 163 F.3d at 514 
("The consent [by the tribe] to criminal jurisdiction [of the state] 
was tantamount to alienation of the land to non-Indians . . . ."); 
Wilson, 127 F.3d at 813-15 (same as Strate, where plaintiff was a 
tribal member). Instead, it concerns the effects of state officers’ 
actions that allegedly exceeded the permission given by the Tribe in 
the search warrants, and alleged damages to property that possibly 
should not have been seized at all. 

We find that the Montana presumption against tribal court 
jurisdiction does not apply in this case. Instead, in iine with Strate 
and County of Lewis, we look to the tribe's power to exclude state 
officers from the land at issue. The Tribe's unfettered power to 
exclude state officers from its land implies its authority to regulate 
the behavior of non-members on that land. See Strate, 520 U.S. at 
456 (land treated as non-Indian fee land because Tribe "cannot 
assert a landowner's right to occupy and exclude"); South Dakota v. 
Bourland, 508 U.S. 679, 692, 124 L. Ed. 2d 606, 113 S. Ct. 2309 
(1993) (“when Congress has broadly opened up such land to non- 
Indians [i.e., abrogated the tribe's power to exclude], the effect . . . 
is the destruction of pre-existing Indian rights to regulatory 
conirol") (emphasis added); Brendale v. Confederated Tribes, 492 
U.S. 408, 433-448, 106 L. Ed. 2d 343, 109 S. Ct. 2994 (1989) 
(opinion of Stevens and O'Connor, JJ.) (tribe can zone non-member 


A-11 


fee land as long as tribe retains power to control access to the land); 
n9 Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes v. Namen, 665 F.2d 
951 (9th Cir. 1982) (tribe has power to regulate structures built by 
non-members which extend over the lake bed held in trust for 
tribe). n10 

Once a tribe's authority to regulate activities on its land has 
been demonstrated, civil jurisdiction regarding those activities 
follows. The Supreme Court made clear in Strate that "where the 
tribes possess authority to regulate the activities of nonmembers, 
‘civil jurisdiction over [disputes arising out of] such activities 
presumptively lies in the tribal courts." Strate, 520 U.S. at 453. 
Any sovereign, even a limited sovereign, must have the power to 
adjudicate whatever it has the power to legislate. A sovereign who 
must depend on the courts of another sovereign to adjudicate 
violations of its own rules is little more than a landowner. 


In this case, the Tribe clearly had the power to exclude state 
officers from its land and to regulate the behavior of state officials 
present on its land pursuant to limited tribal permission. There was 
no general cession of jurisdiction by the Tribe; instead there was a 
controlled, limited permission for state officials to come onto tribal 
land and comport themselves within to the parameters of that 
permission. Disputes regarding the officials' behavior under this 
permission are within the jurisdiction of the Tribe. 

Because the tribal court has already ruled on the issue of tribal 
court jurisdiction, Nevada exhausted its tribal remedies on that 
issue. The question of tribal court jurisdiction is therefore ripe for 
federal review. We conclude that the tribal court has subject matter 
jurisdiction over the claims brought by Hicks against the state 
officials. n11 

B. 

Nevada suggests that the actions of the state officials in this 
case were undertaken as part of their official duties and that 
therefore the officials should be shielded from liability under a 
derivative of the doctrine of sovereign immunity. This argument 
concerns an affirmative defense and is not properly before the 
court, since the state did not exhaust its remedies regarding the 
sovereign immunity defense before the tribal court. n12 While the 
tribal court did find that "it is not prevented by doctrines of 
sovereign or qualified immunity from lawfully exerting personal 


A-12 


jurisdiction over specially appearing State defendants," this finding 
was Clearly restricted to the issue of personal jurisdiction and did 
not include any findings regarding the affirmative defense of 
sovereign immunity. In addition, the tribal court's ruling came 
before Hicks voluntarily dismissed the State of Nevada and all state 
defendants named in their official capacities. n13 Therefore, the 
issue of sovereign immunity as an affirmative defense for state 
officials named individually in this action has not been considered 
on the merits by the tribal court. We therefore do not reach the 
issue. 


Nevada also argues that the district court erred in holding that it 
was precluded by considerations of comity from ruling on the state 
officials' claims of qualified immunity. Nevada contends that the 
state officials sued by Hicks were entitled to summary judgment on 
the issue of tribal court jurisdiction because Hicks failed to 
overcome their claims of qualified immunity from suit. However, 
Nevada does not argue that its claim fits into any of the exceptions 
to the exhaustion requirement. Suggesting that no case has yet 
determined the existence or nature of the qualified immunity 
available to state officials in tribal court, Nevada instead invites this 
court to define the applicable test. We decline to do so. 


The Supreme Court in Strate reaffirmed its earlier decisions 
requiring, as a matter of comity and with very narrow exceptions, 
n14 the exhaustion of tribal remedies before the federal courts rule 
on the jurisdiction of the tribal courts. See Strate, 520 U.S. 438, at 
448-454, 137 L. Ed. 2d 661, 117 S. Ct. 1404 (discussing Jowa 
Mutual Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9, 94 L. Ed. 2d 10, 107 S. Ct. 
971 (1987), and National Farmers Union Ins. Cos. v. Crow Tribe of 
Indians, 471 U.S. 845, 85 L. Ed. 2d 818, 105 S. Ct. 2447 (1985)); 
see also Burlington Northern R. Co. v. Crow Tribal Council, 940 
F.2d 1239, 1245 (9th Cir. 1991) (emphasizing the exhaustion 
requirement). 

The district court correctly applied this exhaustion requirement 
to the issue of qualified immunity. See, e.g., Stock West Corp. v. 
Taylor, 964 F.2d 912, 920 (9th Cir. 1992) (en banc) (holding that 
the district court abused its discretion in addressing a qualified 
immunity defense on the merits, and explaining that "because a 
determination of this issue will require a careful study of the 
application of tribal laws, and tribal court decisions, the district 


A-13 


court should have stayed its hand until after the Colville Tribal 
Courts have the opportunity to resolve the question"). 


As a preliminary matter, we note that Nevada makes the same 
mistake with regard to qualified immunity that it made with 
sovereign immunity, by contending that it is an issue that impacts 
the subject matter jurisdiction of the tribal courts. The law is clear 
that qualified immunity, rather than serving as a jurisdictional bar, 
"is an affirmative defense that must be pleaded by a defendant 
official." Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 815, 73 L. Ed. 2d 396, 
102 S. Ct. 2727 (1982); see also Blatchford v. Native Village of 
Noatak, 501 U.S. 775, 786 n.4, 115 L. Ed. 2d 686, 111 S. Ct. 2578 
(1991) (noting qualified immunity is an affirmative defense that is 
"wholly distinct" from issues of jurisdiction). We reject Nevada's 
contention to the contrary. 


Alternatively, Nevada argues that the issue of qualified 
immunity was in fact exhausted before the tribal court. To this end, 
Nevada quotes the ruling by the Inter-Tribal Appellate Court 
affirming the decision of the tribal judge that "the doctrine of . . . 
qualified immunity does not prevent the Fallon Tribal Court from 
exerting personal jurisdiction over the State Defendants," and 
argues that this constitutes a decision on the merits and thus 
satisfies the exhaustion requirement. However, this argument 
likewise disregards the fact that qualified immunity is an 
affirmative defense and not a jurisdictional bar. A holding by the 
tribal court that it has jurisdiction cannot be construed as a ruling 
on the merits. The district court did not err in finding that the 
exhaustion requirement had not been satisfied. 


Nevada also argues that, even if the issue of qualified immunity 
was not exhausted in the tribal courts, it should be treated as having 
been since Hicks had ample opportunity to respond to the claims of 
qualified immunity. Nevada suggests that if the tribal court record 
is bereft of supportive evidence for Hicks' effort to overcome the 
state officials' individual immunity, it is his own fault. 


However, we have never required a party to anticipate another 
party's qualified immunity defense before the question of subject 
matter jurisdiction had been decided. See, e.g., McDonald v. United 
States, 102 F.3d 1009, 1011 (9th Cir. 1996) (holding that "there is 
‘no basis for imposing on the plaintiff an obligation to anticipate 
such a defense" of qualified immunity) (quoting Gomez v. Toledo, 


A-14 


446 U.S. 635, 640, 64 L. Ed. 2d 572, 100 S. Ct. 1920 (1980)). The 
argument by Nevada here again conflates the affirmative defense of 
qualified immunity with issues relating to the tribal courts’ subject 
matter jurisdiction. The district court did not err in declining to 
reach the merits of the qualified immunity issue. 

Lastly, Nevada argues that Hicks failed to state a claim against 
William Molini, Director of the Nevada Department of Wildlife, 
because Molini was not alleged to have participated personally in 
the acts of the game wardens in obtaining and executing the search 
warrants and seizing Hicks’ property. 

The district court noted that "the voluntary dismissal of the 
official capacity claims by the tribal court may well have made 
personal and subject matter jurisdiction over Molini . . . 
inappropriate." However, since "this issue was never before the 
tribal court because when the motion to quash rulings were made, 
claims against the state defendants in their official capacities had 
not yet been voluntarily dismissed," the district court declined to 
ru’ on whether a claim had been stated against Molini, "leaving 
this unexhausted issue to the tribal court." The analysis of the 
exhaustion requirement set out above applies with equal force here. 
The district court did not err. 


Il. 


The tribal common law torts and federal and tribal civil rights 
violations underlying this case occurred on Indian-owned land, the 
allotment on the Fallon Paiute-Shoshone Reservation held in trust 
by the federal government for Floyd Hicks. The tribal court has 
civil jurisdiction in this case based on the Tribe's right to adjudicate 
disputes arising out of actions within tribal regulatory authority that 
take place on Indian land. The district court did not err in holding 
that the tribal court has jurisdiction. 

Nevada's claims of sovereign and qualified immunity on behalf 
of the state officials and of Hicks’ failure to state a claim against 
William Molini have not been exhausted in tribal court. Although 
exhaustioa is not a jurisdictional prerequisite, it is required as a 
matter of comity. Strate, 520 U.S. at 453. The district court did not 
err in declining to reach the merits of those claims. 


AFFIRMED.

DISSENT: 
RYMER, Circuit Judge, dissenting: 

I part company on subject matter jurisdiction, because I board 
the train from a different station. As I see it, we have to start with 
Yellowstone County v. Pease, 96 F.3d 1169 (9th Cir. 1996), which 
applies the framework established in Montana v. United States, 450 
U.S. 544, 67 L. Ed. 2d 493, 101 S. Ct. 1245 (1981), to determine 
whether there is tribal jurisdiction over civil disputes involving an 
Indian tribe and non-Indians. Under Montana, the Fallon Tribal 
Court lacks subject matter jurisdiction over Hicks's civil actions 
because in Pease we rejected the proposition that Montana is 
limited to cases involving fee lands owned by non-Indians. As 
there is no consensual relationship between the Tribe and Nevada 
law enforcement officials, and subjecting them to suit in tribal court 
is not necessary to protect the tribes or their members, neither of the 
Montana exceptions to this rule is applicable. As the district court 
held otherwise, I would reverse. 


Along with Strate v. A-] Contractors, 520 U.S. 438 (1997), 
Montana is the leading opinion discussing tribal sovereignty over 
non-members of a tribe. In Montana, the Court noted that Indian 
tribes have lost many ofthe attributes of sovereignty and retain 
only limited powers, such as the power to punish tribal offenders, to 
determine tribal membership, to regulate domestic relations among 
members, and to prescribe rules of inheritance for members. 450 
U.S. at 564. As the Court explained, the "exercise of tribal power 
beyond what is necessary to protect tribal self-government or to 
contro! internal relations is inconsistent with the dependent status of 
the tribes, and so cannot survive without express congressional 
delegation," unless the non-members have entered "consensual 
relationships" with the tribe or its members, or the conduct of the 
non-Indians on fee land "threatens or has some direct effect on the 
political integrity, the economic security, or the health and welfare 
of the tribe." Jd. at 564-66. 


Pease rejects the argument Hicks makes here, that Montana 
only imposes restrictions on a tribe's jurisdiction over fee lands 
owned by or aligned with non-Indians. In Pease we held that a 
tribal court lacked jurisdiction to entertain a civil action brought by 
a tribe member against Yellowstone County seeking to enjoin the 
County's imposition of state property taxes on his (Indian-owned) 


A-16 


reservation property. 96 F.3d at 1173-76. Pease contended "that 
Montana does not apply to the County's action because Montana 
only imposed restriction on a tribe's jurisdiction over fee land 
owned by non-Indians." Jd. at 1174. However, we were 
unpersuaded "because the issue presented here is whether the tribal 
court may assert jurisdiction over a non-Indian party (the County), 
and this court has called Montana 'the leading case on tribal civil 
jurisdiction over non-Indians.'" Jd. (quoting FMC v. Shoshone- 
Bannock Tribes, 905 F.2d 1311, 1314 (9th Cir. 1990)). [**36] 
Applying Montana and finding neither exception triggered, we 
upheld summary judgment for the County. As Pease is the law of 
this circuit, we are bound by its directive to apply Montana to 
decide "whether the tribal court may assert jurisdiction over a non- 
Indian party . . . ." 96 F.3d at 1174. See also William C. Canby, Jr., 
American Indian Law 193 (3d ed. 1998) ("The Eighth Circuit, in 
the decision that the Supreme Court reviewed in Strate, and the 
Ninth [*1033] Circuit have both stated that the tribe's sovereign 
powers simply do not permit exercise of jurisdiction over 
nonmembers unless tribal interests are affected, so as to bring the 
exercise within one of the two Montana exceptions." (citing A-/ 
Contractors v. Strate, 76 F.3d 930, 939 (8th Cir. 1996) (en banc) 
and Pease, 96 F.3d at 1175-76)). 

Strate further bolsters this view. Strate called Montana "the 
pathmarking case concerning tribal civil authority over 
nonmembers," and reiterated that Montana "establishes that, absent 
express authorization by federal statute or treaty, tribal jurisdiction 
over the conduct of nonmembers exists only in limited 
circumstances. " 520 U.S. at 445. Although Strate's holding that a 
tribal court lacks jurisdiction over a tort action against a non- 
member who had an accident with a tribe member on a state right- 
of-way running through a reservation appears to turn on the fact 
that the accident there occurred on the state's easement (thus not on 
Indian land, but on land "aligned" with non-Indians), the Court 
unambiguously makes Montana the relevant law. Finally, even if 
Strate may be construed to signal that Montana does not apply to 
conduct by non-Indians on Indian land, the Court did not expressly 
say so and until it does, we must continue to follow Pease and 
apply Montana. 


A-17 


County of Lewis v. Allen, 163 F.3d 509 (9th Cir. 1998) (en 
banc), also supports the conclusion that there is no tribal civil 
jurisdiction in this case. In Allen, we held that the Nez Perce tribe 
lacked civil jurisdiction over a member's § 1983 action (very 
similar to Hicks's action here) against Lewis County, the sheriff and 
a deputy sheriff because the tribe ceded its right "to exclude state 
officials engaged in law enforcement activities on the reservation." 
Id. at 514. The Nez Perce had entered into an agreement with 
Lewis County giving its officers the authority to enter the 
reservation, investigate minor crimes and make arrests. See id. 
Applying Strate and Montana, we concluded that "it does not 
matter how the land was owned because the [tribe's] consent to 
criminal jurisdiction was tantamount to alienation of the land to 
non-Indians for this limited purpose." Jd. We then determined that 
neither Montana exception properly applied to extend tribal 
jurisdiction over the case. See id. at 515-16. 


In Hicks's action, Spencer, Ellington and Fitzmorris had 
obtained Fallon Tribal Court permission to execute the search 
warrant of Hicks's residence (Spencer receiving permission twice), 
and thus they had a right to be on the reservation that was no less 
absolute than Deputy Myers's right in Al/en and the non-member 
defendants’ in Strate. The fact that the Fallon Paiute-Shoshone 
Tribe granted permission to Nevada law enforcement personnel to 
come onto the reservation on a case-by-case basis instead of in a 
blanket agreement does not materially change the analysis. Just as 
the Nez Perce formally consented to Deputy Myers's presence on 
their reservation through a prior agreement establishing concurrent 
criminal jurisdiction within the reservation, see Allen, 163 F.3d at 
514, the Fallon Paiute-Shoshone formally consented to the presence 
of Spencer, Ellington and Fitzmorris on their reservation through 
the Fallon Tribal Court's express approval of their search warrants. 


So, the Fallon Tribal Court's consent to execution of the 
Nevada search warrants "aligned" Hicks's property, albeit 
temporarily, with the state of Nevada. The Tribe had surrendered its 
right to exclude Spencer, Ellington and Fitzmorris (at least for the 
time being), giving them the unqualified right to be on Hicks's 
property. This case is thus indistinguishable from Allen in all 
material respects. Therefore, | would apply Montana and Allen and 
hold that Spencer, Ellington and Fitzmorris's conduct did not 


A-18 


imperil the tribe's power to control its internal affairs, and no 
applicable exception exists. Spencer only sought to execute the 
search warrants after obtaining the tribe's consent and assistance. 

Accordingly, I would reverse and hold that the tribal court lacks 
subject matter jurisdiction to entertain Hicks's civil rights actions 
against the Nevada officials. n1

As a preliminary matter, the court agrees with the analysis of

The Intertribal Court found service by publication to be proper 
and effective under both tribal and federal constitutional law. The 
court reasoned that Judge Van Walraven was authorized by the 
tribal code to adopt provisions of NRCP as a guide, he had in fact 
adopted NRCP 4 in the Hicks case, he had judicial power to 
interpret tribal law as codified in the Law and Order Code and as 
adopted pursuant to the Code in his court, and the Nevada courts’ 
interpretations of NRCP 4 are not binding on the tribal court 
because the "meld" of adopted Nevada procedural rule in 
combination with the Law and Order Code and other sources of

tribal court determinations of tribal law unless they implicate

the service context, Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 
the Supreme Court stated, 

An elementary and fundamental requirement of due 

process in any proceeding which is to be accorded 

finality is notice reasonably calculated, under all the 

circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the 

pendency of the action and afford them an opportunity to 

present their objections. The notice must be of such 

nature as reasonably to convey the required information

practicalities and peculiarities of the case these

Exhaustion of Tribal Remedies 


In National Farmers Union Insurance Cos. v. Crow Tribe of 
Indians n8, the Court held that "the question whether an Indian tribe 
retains the power to compel a non-Indian property owner to submit 
to the jurisdiction of a tribal court is one that must be answered by 
reference to federal law." 47] U.S. 845, 852 (1985). However, the 
Court, emphasizing the Congressional "policy of supporting tribal 
self-government and self-determination", the desirability of 
developing a full record prior to federal review, and the goal of 
enhancing the legitimacy of tribal courts, required exhaustion of 
tribal court remedies before a federal court could hear a challenge 
to "either the merits or any question concerning appropriate relief." 
Id., 856. 


In Jowa Mutual Insurance Co. v. LaPlante n9, citing Merrion v. 
Jicarilla Apache Tribe n10, the Court emphasized that a tribe 
retains all the inherent attributes of sovereignty not previously 
invalidated by federal law. The Court stated, "civil jurisdiction over 
[activities of non-Indians on reservation lands] presumptively lies 
in the tribal courts unless affirmatively limited by a specific treaty 
provision or federal statute." 480 U.S. 9, 18 (1987). 


National Farmers and Iowa Mutual "place a strict premium on 
precise and rigorous tribal court analysis of its own jurisdictional 
parameters." Frank R. Pommersheim, The Crucible of Sovereignty: 
Analyzing Issues of Tribal Jurisdiction, 31 Ariz. L. Rev. 329, 331 
(1989). The crucial questions for the tribal court are (1) whether the 
tribal court is empowered by tribal law to hear a particular kind of 
case; and (2) whether tribal court authority is limited by federal 


B-7 


law. The Court in National Farmers described the necessary 
analysis as requiring "a careful examination of tribal sovereignty, 
the extent to which that sovereignty has been altered, divested or 
diminished, as well as a detailed study of relevant statutes, 
Executive Branch policy as embodied in treaties and elsewhere, and 
administrative or judicial decisions." National Farmers, 471 U.S. 
845, 855-56, 85 L. Ed. 2d 818, 105 S. Ct. 2447. 


Thus, the extent to which tribal law authorizes jurisdiction is a 
question which should be answered by the tribal court in the first 
instance. In this case, the Intertribal Court of Appeals definitively 
answered this question. While admittedly the Court did not 
memorialize its reasoning in the historical depth and legal detail 
contemplated by National Farmers, the parties have exhausted their 
tribal remedies and the matter is now properly before this court. 
Tribal Civil Adjudicatory Jurisdiction, Generally 

The contours of tribal civil adjudicatory jurisdiction over in 
Indian country are unclear for several reasons. No federal statutes 
provide guidance. The federal caselaw, while clearly mandating 
exhaustion of tribal remedies in the first instance, "fails to establish 
clear-cut guidelines for deciding in what circumstances tribal court 
jurisdiction is appropriate.” Pommersheim, 331. However, the 
Supreme Court's opinions as far back as Worcester v. Georgia n11 
suggest that, in contrast to criminal jurisdiction, which the Court 
views as purely membership-based, tribal court civil jurisdiction 
has a strong geographic component n12. The Ninth Circuit 
explicitly recognized this principle in United States v. Plainbull, 
stating "tribal courts are appropriate forums for the exclusive 
adjudication of disputes taking place within the boundaries of a 
reservation." 957 F.2d 724, 727 (9th Cir. 1992) n13. 


Felix Cohen, an oft-cited expert on federal Indian law, has 
described the backdrop against which the tribal jurisdiction analysis 
is conducted as follows: 


The whole course of judicial decision on the nature of Indian 
tribal powers is marked by an adherence to three fundamental 
principles: (1) An Indian tribe possesses, in the first instance, 
all the power of any sovereign state. (2) Conquest renders the 
tribe subject to the legislative power of the United States and, 
in substance, terminates the external powers of the sovereignty 
of the tribe, e.g., its power to enter into treaties with foreign 
B-8 


nations, but does not by itself affect the internal sovereignty of 
the tribe, i.e., its powers of local self-government. (3) These 
powers are subject to qualification by treaties and by express 
legislation of Congress, but save as thus expressly qualified, 
full powers of internal sovereignty are vested in the Indian 
tribes and in their duly constituted organs of government. 


F. Cohen, Handbook of Federal Indian Law 123 (1942 ed.). 


The continuing application of these overriding principles is 
readily gleaned from subsequent Supreme Court caselaw. In 
Williams v. Lee, a suit brought against a Navajo defendant by a 
non-Indian owner of a store on the Navajc reservation, the Court 
held that the state court lacked jurisdiction over the dispute. 358 
U.S. 217 (1959). The Court reasoned that allowing state court 
jurisdiction over disputes arising on the reservation and involving 
significant tribal interests would "undermine the authority of the 
tribal courts over Reservation affairs and hence would infringe 
upon the right of the Indians to govern themselves." Jd, 223. 


In Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 56 L. Ed. 2d 
106, 98 S. Ct. 1670 (1978), the Court held the Indian Civil Rights 
Act (ICRA) does not provide a civil cause of action in federal court 
n14, reasoning that because "tribal forums are available to vindicate 
rights created by the ICRA", an implied federal right of action was 
unnecessary. The Court cited William v. Lee for the proposition that 
"tribal courts have been recognized as appropriate forums for the 
exclusive adjudication of disputes affecting important personal and 
property rights of both Indians and non-Indians." Jd., 66. 
Jurisdiction Over State Defendants In This Case 

Here, the tribal court has asserted jurisdiction, and the 
Intertribal Court of Appeals has affirmed. A tribal court's 
interpretation of tribal law is binding on this court. Hinshaw v. 
Mahler, 42 F.3d 1178 (9th Cir.1994). However, the extent of tribal 
court jurisdiction is a federal question. National Farmers, 471 U.S. 
845, 85 L. Ed. 2d 818, 105 S. Ct. 2447 (1985). Hinshaw precludes a 
federal court from second-guessing a tribal court's own reading of 
tribal law, while National Farmers reserves the ultimate decision 
on tribal jurisdiction to federal courts. These two principles require 
federal courts to determine de novo whether the tribal court has 
asserted its jurisdiction beyond lawful limits; the applicable limits 
are those set by federal law. National Farmers, 471 U.S. 845, 85 L. 

B-9 


Ed. 2d 818, 105 S. Ct. 2447 (1985). Thus, the questions for this 
court are whether the Intertribal Court of Appeals erred, as a matter 
of federal Indian law, in holding that the tribal court properly 
exercised jurisdiction over the underlying litigation in the first 
instance. 


Montana v. United States, the Presumption of Retained 
Sovereignty, and the Rule of Implicit Divestiture 


Defendants argue that because the underlying events occurred 
on tribal territory, this court must presume the tribe possesses 
inherent sovereign jurisdiction until plaintiffs rebut the presumption 
with a showing of express divestiture by Congress; they further 
argue that plaintiffs have not and cannot make that showing. In 
contrast, plaintiffs argue the presumption urged by defendants does 
not apply because under Montana v. United States, the power of 
civil adjudication over a person not a member of the tribe has been 
implicitly divested as inconsistent with the tribe's status as a 
domestic dependent nation. The resolution of this dispute requires 
an examination of the nature and applicability of the Supreme 
Court's holding in Montana v. United States. 450 U.S. 544, 67 L. 
Ed. 2d 493, 101 S. Ct. 1245 (1981). 


In Montana, the Supreme Court held the dependent status of 
Indian tribes implicitly divested them of power to regulate the 
conduct of non-members on fee land no longer owned by the tribe. 
However, the opinion carefully reiterated that tribes retain 
significant civil jurisdiction over non-Indians on their reservations: 


A tribe may regulate, through taxation, licensing, or other means, 
the activities of nonmembers who enter consensual relationships 
with the tribe or its members, through commercial dealing, 
contracts, leases, or other arrangements. A tribe may also retain 
inherent power to exercise civil authority over the conduct of non- 
Indians on fee lands within its reservation when that conduct 
threatens or has some direct effect on the political integrity, the 
economic security, or the health and welfare of the tribe. Jd. 


Although Montana recognized the existence of retained n15 
sovereignty, it appeared to sharply curtail the scope of that 
sovereignty. The Court set forth a "general proposition that the 
inherent sovereign powers of an Indian tribe do not extend to the 
activities of nonmembers of the tribe" and further stated that tribal 
sovereignty over nonmembers "cannot survive without express 


B-10 


congressional delegation." 450 U.S. at 564. This broad language 
seemed to inexplicably reverse the traditional presumption set forth 
in Williams v. Lee, which stated "this court has consistently guarded 
the authority of Indian governments over their reservations . . . if 
this power is to be taken away from them, it is for Congress to do 
it." 358 U.S. 217, 222 (1959). In other words, whereas Williams 
presumes tribal power unless divested nl16, Montana appears to 
presume power over nonmembers has been lost, unless one of the 
two specified exceptions applies. 

No Supreme Court case explains the reversal of the Williams 
presumption, and post-Montana cases have followed the Montana 
rule inconsistently, if at all. An opinion handed down shortly before 
Montana, Washington v. Confederated Tribes of the Colville Indian 
Reservation, stated "tribal powers are not implicitly divested by 
virtue of the tribes' dependent status." 447 U.S. 134, 153-154 
(1980). In a case decided one year after the Montana decision, 
Merrion v. Jicarilla Apache Tribe, the Court, expressly rejecting an 
implicit divestiture argument, held that the power to impose a 
severance tax on oil and gas extracted from tribal lands by 
nonmember lessees was inherent in the tribe's power as a sovereign 
nation. 455 U.S. 130, 144, 149-150 (1982). National Farmers and 
Iowa Mutual, both post-Montana cases, reiterate the presumption of 
retained sovereignty, without any attempt to reconcile these 
formulations with Montana's apparent reversal of the Williams 
presumption. However, more recent cases have returned to 
Montana. See Brendale v. Confederated Tribes and Bands of the 
Yakima Indian Nation, 492 U.S. 408, 425, 106 L. Ed. 2d 343, 109. 
Ct. 2994 (1989); South Dakota v. Bourland, 508 U.S. 679, 113 S. 
Ct. 2309, 2315 n.6, 124 L. Ed. 2d 606 (1993). 


Because Montana, Brendale, and Bourland involved challenges 
to a tribe's power to regulate, and National Farmers and Iowa 
Mutual involved challenges to a tribe's power to adjudicate tort 
claims against a non-member, the different formulations of the 
"general rule" may be a result of the Supreme Court's implicit 
recognition of a principled distinction between tribal adjudicatory 
jurisdiction and tribal regulatory jurisdiction n17. The Ninth Circuit 
made this distinction in its National Farmers opinion, but the 
Supreme Court did not address it. Compare National Farmers 
Union Ins. v. Crow Tribe, 736 F.2d 1320, 1321 n.3, 1323 andn.4 


B-1] 


(9th Cir. 1984) with National Farmers Union Ins. v. Crow Tribe, 
471 U.S. 845, 85 L. Ed. 2d 818, 105 S. Ct. 2447 (1985). Several 
commentators have argued persuasively that a distinction between 
tribal regulatory and adjudicatory jurisdiction has been reflected, 
albeit implicitly, in the Supreme Court's opinions n18. The 
recognition of this distinction would resolve the apparent 
inconsistency between the Supreme Court's formulation of the rule 
in National Farmer's and Iowa Mutual, which trumpeted a vital 
presumption of retained jurisdiction unless a challenger proved 
divestment, and the arguably narrower Montana view of 
sovereignty. 

Yellowstone County v. Pease 


In Yellowstone County v. Pease n19, the Ninth Circuit cited 
with approval a portion of the Eighth Circuit's en banc opinion in 
A-1 Contractors v. Strate n20, integrating the Montana rule with 
the presumption of retained sovereignty as reiterated in Jowa 
Mutual and other cases, into one coherent rule: 

A valid tribal interest must be at issue before a tribal court may 
exercise civil jurisdiction over a . .. nonmember, but once the tribal 
interest is established, a presumption arises that tribal courts have 
jurisdiction over the . . . nonmember unless that jurisdiction is 
affirmatively limited by federal law. Pease, 1996 WL 512363, 
512366 (9th Cir.), quoting from A-/ Contractors, 76 F.3d 930, 938- 
39 (8th Cir. 1996) (en banc). 

Montana — 

If the rule in A-/ Contractors is now indeed the law of the 
Ninth Circuit, it certainly provides [**29] a helpful framework. 
However, it does not significantly alter this court's analysis in this 
case. Under either the A-] Contractors formulation or under 
Montana, the court finds tribal jurisdiction properly asserted. 
Assuming, arguendo, the Montana rule controls, the court finds 
Montana does not preclude tribal jurisdiction in this case. 

As a threshold matter, the court notes that the Montana decision 
involved the tribe's jurisdiction over fee lands within reservation 
boundaries. A finding of implicit divestiture of tribal authority over 
conduct occurring on land no longer owned by the tribe n21 and 
which has not been demonstrated to "threaten or have some direct 
effect on the political integrity, the economic security, or the health 


B-12 


and welfare of the tribe" has limited application to the issue 


presented here. 

Providing a forum for reservation-based tort actions, even 
where constitutional torts are alleged, falls within both Montana 
exceptions. Mr. Hicks, a member of the Tribe, has alleged his 
federal civil rights were violated on tribal land. The tribal code 
provides for jurisdiction over ali civil causes of action arising 
within the territorial jurisdiction of the court, and specifies that 
entry upon such territory constitutes consent to the jurisdiction of 
the court. Furthermore, tribal law provided very specific restrictions 
on the execution of extraterritorial search warrants on the 
reservation. These tribal law provisions are directly applicable to 
the facts in this case and clearly show that the tribe viewed the 
execution of state search warrants on the reservation as bearing 
directly on the political integrity of the tribe. See Montana, 450 
U.S. 544, 67 L. Ed. 2d 493, 101 S. Ct. 1245 (1981). By enacting 
such provisions, the tribe has asserted its interest in adjudicating 
such disputes. 

Even without tribal statutory law in this area, this court would 
presuine that the Tribe has an interest in providing Mr. Hicks, and 
any other tribal member similarly situated, a forum in which to 
vindicate these rights. As recognized by the Supreme Court in 
National Farmers, tribal self-government and self-determination 
are inextricably linked to the continuing enhancement of the 
legitimacy of the tribal courts. 47] U.S. 845, 856 (1985). 
Furthermore, the tribal courts’ legitimacy should not be measured 
solely by how closely it approximates the operation of the federa’ 
courts n22, or how exactly its adjudications correspond with federal 
or state caselaw. "If the word "sovereign" has any meaning in 
contemporary federal courts’ jurisprudence, its meaning comes 
from a state's or tribe's ability to maintain different modes from the 
federal government." Judith Resnik, Dependent Sovereigns: Indian 
Tribes, States, and the Federal Courts, 56 U.Chi. L.Rev. 671, 749 
(1989). Tribal courts must be permitted to be "jurisgenerative" if 
they are to thrive. See id., 751 (citing Robert Cover, Nomos and 
Narrative, 97 Harv.L.Rev. 4, 28). "If justice is a product of 
conversation rather than unilateral declaration, it is more likely to 
be achieved in the context of respectful dialogue rather than 


B-13 


majoritarian conclusions about the 'other’." Frank Pommersheim, 
Braid of Feathers 193 (1995). 

Although the issue presented here is one of first impression, the 
court is satisfied that this ruling is consistent with the federal 
caselaw. Ninth Circuit law has not found Montana to restrict tribes' 
adjudicatory jurisdiction over non-members. To the contrary, the 
Ninth Circuit cases reaffirm the concept of retained sovereignty as 
restated in Jowa Mutual and National Farmers. See, e.g., Hinshaw 
v. Mahler, 42 F.3d 1178 (9th Cir. 1994); FMC v. Shoshone- 
Bannock Tribes, 905 F.2d 1311 (9th Cir. 1990), cert. denied, 499 
U.S. 943, 113 L. Ed. 2d 459, 111 S. Ct. 1404 (1991); Wellman v. 
Chevron, 815 F.2d 577 (9th Cir. 1987); Yellowstone County v. 
Pease, 1996 WL 512363 (9th Cir.). 


The presence of federal law issues in the tribal court litigation 
does not compel the conclusion that tribal court jurisdiction may 
not lie. See Middlemist v. Lujan, 824 F. Supp. 940 (D. Mont. 1993), 
affd without opinion, 19 F.3d 1318 (9th Cir. 1994), cert. denied, 
115 S. Ct. 420 (1994). See also Iowa Mutual Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 
480 U.S. 9, 16, 94 L. Ed. 2d 10, 107 S. Ct. 971 (1986) 
("Unconditional access to the federal forum would place it in direct 
competition with the tribal courts, thereby impairing the latter's 
authority over reservation affairs."); Altheimer & Gray v. Sioux 
Mfg. Co., 983 F.2d 803, 814 (7th Cir. 1993), cert. denied, 510 U.S. 
1019, 114.8. Ct. 621, 126 L. Ed. 2d 585 (1993) ("{a] tribal court, 
presumably, is as competent to interpret federal law as it is state 
law"). Nor does the non-Indian status of the defendant. Williams v. 
Lee, 358 U.S. 217, 223, 3 L. Ed. 2d 251, 79 S. Ct. 269 (1959) ("It is 
immaterial that respondent is not an Indian. He was on the 
Reservation and the transaction took place there. The cases in this 
Court have consistently guarded the authority of Indian 
governments over their reservations . . . If this power is to be taken 
away from them, it is for Congress to do it."); Hinshaw v. Mahler; 
see also Cardin v. Cruz, 671 F.2d 363, 366 (9th Cir. 1982), cert. 
denied, 459 U.S. 967, 74 L. Ed. 2d 277, 103 S. Ct. 293 (9182) (to 
hold otherwise would "reduce to a nullity the Supreme Court's 
repeated assertions that Indian tribes retain attributes of sovereignty 
over their territory, not just their members" [internal citations 
omitted]). 


B-14 


Nor does the status of the state defendants as state actors argue 
persuasively against jurisdiction. The Supreme Court has 
consistently recognized that Indian tribes retain "attributes of 
sovereignty over both their members and their territory", United 
States v. Mazurie n23, and that "tribal sovereignty is dependent on, 
and subordinate to, only the federal government, not the States." 
Washington Confederated Tribes of Colville Indian Reservation, 
447 U.S. 134, 154, 65 L. Ed. 2d 10, 100 S. Ct. 2069 (1980). 


Conclusion 


Mr. Hicks seeks a remedy for the violation of his constitutional 
and common law rights. He chose to seek those remedies in the 
Fallon Tribal Court. The Tribal Court has asserted jurisdiction over 
his lawsuit. State defendants point to no law, statutory or 
decisional, which persuades this court he cannot proceed in tribal 
court. 


As the Supreme Court has clearly stated, it is not the role of the 
federal courts to abrogate tribal sovereignty n24. Both National 
Farmers and Jowa Mutual contemplated post-exhaustion federal 
court review of tribal jurisdictional holdings. This court reads that 
mandate conservatively; in the court's view, the task before it is to 
evaluate the tribal court's assertion of jurisdiction in light of federal 
Indian law as it stands today, not to create new federal common law 
further restricting tribal jurisdiction. The presumption is in favor of 
tribal court jurisdiction. Jowa Mutual, 480 U.S. 9, 18, 94 L. Ed. 2d 
10, 107 S. Ct. 971 (1986). Implicit divestiture will not be found 
unless "the exercise of tribal sovereignty would be inconsistent with 
the overriding interests of the National Government n25", as when 
the tribes seek to engage in foreign relations, alienate their lands to 
non-Indians without federal consent, or [criminally] prosecute non- 
Indians in tribal courts which do not accord the full protections of 
the Bill of Rights." Washington v. Confederated Tribes of the 
Colville Indian Reservation, 447 U.S. 134, 1 53-154, 65 L. Ed. 2d 
10, 100 S. Ct. 2069 (1980). 


This court has found valid tribal interests to be at issue. See 
Yellowstone County v. Pease, 1996 WL 5 12363, 512366 (9th Cir.) 
(citing A-/ Contractors v. Strate, 76 F.3d at 938-39 (8th Cir. 1996) 
(en banc). Plaintiffs have failed to prove the jurisdiction asserted by 
the tribe is affirmatively limited by federal law. No express 
congressional divestment of the tribe's presumed inherent 


B-15 


jurisdiction, or "an overriding federal interest that would 
necessarily be frustrated" by the exercise of tribal jurisdiction, has 
been shown. Washington v. Confederated Tribes of the Colville 
Indian Reservation, 447 U.S. at 154 (1980). Therefore, this court 
concludes that the Fallon Tribal Court has jurisdiction to adjudicate 
Hicks's claims against the state defendants in their individual 
capacities. 

Qualified Immunity 


Plaintiffs argue for summary judgment in favor of the state 
defendants in their individual capacities on several grounds. First, 
they argue that Hicks failed to make the requisite pleading and 
showing necessary to overcome the individuals’ claims of qualified 
immunity as state officers and employees. They claim the issue was 
raised, argued and briefed in the tribal court proceedings, the tribal 
court failed to adequately address these arguments when they were 
presented, and erred as a matter of law by denying them immunity. 


The tribal trial court held that qualified immunity did not serve 
as a jurisdictional bar, and the tribal appellate court affirmed. This 
court agrees. As a matter of federal law, qualified immunity is not a 
jurisdictional bar to suits against a state official in his individual 
capacity, but rather an affirmative defense. See Harlow v. 
Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 815, 73 L. Ed. 2d 396, 102 S. Ct. 2727 
(1991). Therefore, the court affirms the Intertribal Appellate Court's 
ruling on qualified immunity as a jurisdictional issue. 


To the extent that the parties dispute whether qualified 
immunity is available to the state defendants as a defense, the court 
finds that question was not exhausted in the tribal courts n26. While 
mindful of the policy reasons behind the general rule that issues of 
qualified immunity ordinarily be decided long before trial, see 
Hunter v. Bryant, 502 U.S. 224, 116 L. Ed. 2d 589, 112 S. Ct. 534 
(1991), the court is unwilling to disregard the exhaustion rule to 
decide that issue. Although plaintiffs argue that the tribal court 
"implicitly" ruled on the defense, the court disagrees. The tribal 
court did not hold an evidentiary hearing or make definitive 
findings of fact or conclusions of law on the application of the 
qualified immunity defense to the facts of this case. The court held 
only that qualified immunity was not a jurisdictional bar to tribal 
court jurisdiction in the first instance. n27 Therefore, this court 
does not reach the issue of qualified immunity as a defense to the 


B-16 


underlying claims. See Crawford v. Genuine Parts Co., 947 F.2d 
1405, 1408 (9th Cir.1991), cert. denied, 502 U.S. 1096, 117 L. Ed 
2d 419, 112 S. Ct. 1174 (1991) ("When the dispute is a reservation 
affair . . . there is no discretion not to defer n28"). 

Claims Against Molini 

At oral argument, the court questioned whether Hicks states a 
claim against William Molini in his individual capacity, as it 
appeared no specific facts were alleged as to his personal 
participation in the allegedly unconstitutional and tortious acts of 
the wardens in obtaining and executing the search warrant and 
seizing Hicks's property. 

The voluntary dismissal of the official capacity claims by the 
tribal court may well have made personal and subject-matter 
jurisdiction over Molini in CV-FT-034 n29 inappropriate. This 
issue was never before the tribal court because when the motion to 
quash rulings were made, claims against the state defendants in 
their official capacities had not yet been voluntarily dismissed. 
Therefore, the court declines to rule on whether a claim has been 
stated against Molini, leaving this unexhausted issue to the tribal 
court. See Crawford, 947 F.2d 1405 (9th Cir. 1991). 

For the reasons set forth in this opinion, IT IS ORDERED that 
plaintiffs' motion for summary judgment (# 33) is DENIED. Floyd 
Hicks' and the tribal defendants' motions for summary judgment (# 
# 34, 35) are GRANTED.

OPINION 


BY JUDGE C. Stephen Rowley: Findings of Fact. 


The court makes the following findings of fact: That the 
plaintiff Floyd Hicks (Hicks) appeals an order quashing service of 
Process upon the State Defendants, preventing the State Defendants 
in their individual and official capacities from being sued in the 
Fallon Paiute-Shoshone Tribal Court (Fallon Tribal Court). 

That in 1986, the Business Council of the Fallon Paiute- 
Shoshone Tribes (Tribe) enacted a Law & Order Code which, 
among many other provisions, required: 

1) That Fallon Tribal Court take jurisdiction over all 
civil causes of action, over persons and things, arising or existing 


C-1 


within the territorial jurisdiction of the court (Title 1, §1-20- 
020(b)). 

2) That the act of entry upon the territory within the 
jurisdiction of the Fallon Tribal Court shall conclusively be deemed 
consent to the jurisdiction of the Fallon Tribal Court with respect to 
any civil action arising out of such entry (Title 1, §1-20-020(b)). 

3) That before any state official could conduct a 
search or make an arrest pursuant to a state court warrant on the 
Fallon Indian Reservation or Colony that there had to be a 
reciprocal agreement allowing the Tribe to officially serve its 
warrants off the reservation and colony (Title 1, §4-40-060(f)) and 
there had to be a felony investigation (Title 1, §4-04-060(c)). 

4) That before a state official could conduct a search 
and seizure, a Tribal Search Warrant, the form described in detail 
by tribal law, must be issued by a judge of the court (Title 1, §4-40- 
010), 4-40-030(a)). 

5) That before any evidence of criminal activity that 
took place off the reservation could be removed from the 
reservation by state officials there had to be a dispositional hearing 
(Title 1, §4-40-040). 

6) That the Nevada Rules of Civil Procedure and 
general Civil Laws of the State of Nevada were to be used by the 
judges of the Fallon Tribal Court, as they thought necessary, but 
“not so as to impede the administration of justice nor contravene 
the application of any tribal law or custom or federal law not to 
produce any absurd result nor impose any regulatory scheme or 
policy not contemplated by the tribe nor to interfere with the tribe’s 
laws or policies on any subject on any subject regulated by the 
tribe.” (Title 1, §1-30-040) 

That in 1990 and 1991, State Defendants repeatedly, and 
voluntarily, availed themselves of the jurisdiction of the Fallon 
Paiute-Shoshone Tribal Court, after obtaining legal counsel from 
the Churchill County District Attorney, to seek permission to 
conduct a search of Hicks’ allotted Indian lands and his residence 
on the Fallon Indian Reservation for evidence of the killing of the 
California subspecies of Big Horn Sheep, a gross misdemeanor 
under Nevada law. 

That in 1990 and 1991, State Defendants repeatedly, and 


C-2 


voluntarily, entered the Fallon Indian Reservation, after obtaining 
what may be on valid search and seizure warrant from the Justice of 
the Peace, New River Township, Churchill County, Nevada. 

In 1990 and 1991, all in contravention of tribal law, the 
chief judge of the Fallon Tribal Court repeated authorized the 
defendants to: 

1) Enter the Plaintiffs allotted Indian lands and 
residence, respectively, for the purpose of seizing evidence of 
freshly killed Big Horn Sheep of the California subspecies; 

2) Seize the Plaintiff's aged mounted heads of the 
Rocky Mountain subspecies of Big Horn Sheep; and 

3) Remove the Plaintiff's mounted heads from the 
exterior boundaries of the Fallon Indian Reservation and Colony 
without the required dispositional hearing. 

There was and is no reciprocal agreement or Tribal-State 
Compact regarding the honoring of each jurisdiction’s warrants and 
this was not a felony investigation. 

During the December 4, 1991, Status Conference Hearing 
Judge Van Walraven defined what “guide” meant in Title 1, §1-30- 
040 and adopted and applied NRCP Rule 11 to all parties, 
including State Defendants’ legal counsel demonstrating what he 
meant by his definition of the ward “guide.” 

This is the second time the Fallon Tribal Court has issued 
an order quashing service of process upon State Defendants. The 
first order, which is not before us, was issued after the Fallon Tribal 
Court, under the direction of Tribal Defendant/Chief Judge Harold, 
had effected official service of process upon the State Defendants 
by use of Certified, U.S. Mail. At that point in time, no judge of the 
Fallon Tribal Court had issued an order authorizing extraterritorial 
service of process as permitted by tribal law. (Title 1, §1-30-040) 

That on May 3, 1993 Judge Joseph Van Walraven issued an 
order “adopt[ing] an apply[ing] the Nevada Rules of Civil 
Procedure (NRCP), Rule 4, inclusive, for the remainder of th[e] 
case[s]” without modifying the language of NRCP Rule 4(f) and 
without limiting service to within the exterior boundaries of the 
Fallon Indian Reservation and Colony only. 

NRCP Rule 4(f) states, in part: 

Territorial Limits of Effective Service. All process 


C-3 


... may be served anywhere within the territorial 
limits of the State [of Nevada] and, when a statute or 
rule so provides, beyond the territorial limits of the 
State. (Emphasis added.) 


That on February 25, 1993, Judge Joseph Van Walraven 
adopted and applied NRCP Rules “26-37, inclusive, for the 
remainder of th[{e] case[s].” 


That on July 22, 1993, Chief Judge Harold, one of the 
named defendants in these cases, and Judge Van Walraven, enacted 
Title 2A of the Fallon Law & Order Code, governing “Jury Trials 
Procedures.” Title 2A was imposed on the Plaintiff and State 
Defendants during their jury trial. 


That in August 1993, the idigent [sic] Plaintiff at his own 
personal expense, and in reliance on Judge Van Walraven’s May 3, 
1993 order adopting and applying NRCP Rule 4(f), effected private 
service of process upon the State Defendants by publication in the 
Reno Gazette-Journal. (See Affidavit of Lisa A. Wakayama 
(August 25, 1993)). 


That on February 1, 1994, Judge Joseph Van Walraven 
issued a written order quashing Plaintiff's August 1993 service of 
process upon the State Defendants by private means on the basis 
that he found “no method for extraterritorial service of the 
summons and complaint beyond the boundaries of the Fallon 
Paiute-Shoshone Tribes and [that] the court further [found] that 
there is no ‘long-arm’ statute or ordinance in effect on the Fallon 
Paiute-Shoshone Tribes.” (Order Quashing Service Of Process As 
To Specially Appearing State Defendants at 1, lines 21-25). 


Conclusions of Law 


Plaintiff asserts that the Fallon Tribal Court neither 
correctly applied the relevant law nor was the court’s decision of 
fact supported by substantial evidence. (These are the sole grounds 
for appeal by tribal law. See Title 1, Fallon Paiute-Shoshone Law 
& Order Code Title 1, §1-90-030(a), (b)). We conclude not only 
the Hicks effected a valid form of private service of process upon 
the State Defendants, but because of three reasons the State 
Defendants are properly before the Fallon Tribal Court for suit. 
First, the State Defendants acts of repeated voluntary entry onto the 
Fallon Indian Reservation and the Plaintiff's allotted Indian lands; 
Second, State Defendants repeatedly availing themselves, 

C-4 


voluntarily, of the jurisdiction of the Fallon Tribal Court that State 
Defendants; and, Third, the declaration of tribal law that the act of 
entry upon the Fallon Indian Reservation and Colony “shall 
conclusively be deemed consent to the jurisdiction” of the Fallon 
Tribal Court. We therefore remand to the Fallon Tribal Court to 
immediately set a trial date and allow the Plaintiff's civil jury trials 
to be heard without further delay consistent with this opinion. 


We start our discussion with the advisement of Felix S. 
Cohen: 


Indian law is an extraordinary rich and diverse field. 
The cases, both old and new, weave a fabric with 
threads drawn from constitutional law, international 
law, federal jurisdiction, conflict of laws, real 
property, contracts, corporation, torts, domestic 
relations, procedure, trust law, intergovernmental 
relations, sovereign immunity, and taxation. 
Typically, as those fields meld into Indian law, the 
blend produces a new variation the could not have 
been predicted by analysis of the applicable law 
from those other fields. (Handbook of Federal 
Indian Law (Michie Pub. Co., 1982), the most 
recognized treatise on Indian law.) 


It is not enough that a judge of a tribal court merely looks 
to state law for the answers to the issue presented. Indian law 
analysis which includes a review of custom, tradition and tribal 
common law, requires more. It does not affect the jurisdiction of 
this court that State Defendants’ counsel admits that he lacks any 
knowledge of the customs, tradition and common law of the Fallon 
Tribes or the Northern Paiute and Western Shoshone Nations. 


The holdings and interpretations of procedure by Nevada 
and other state courts identified by State Defendants, while 
supporting this court’s decision, are not controlling on the Fallon 
Tribal Court or this court because when NRCP Rule 4(f) is melded 
into Title 1, §1-30-040, the blend produces a different result. (See 
State Defendants’ Points and Authorities In Support Of Renewed 
Motion To Quash Service Of Process at 4, lines 4-19, citing 
Crummer v. Fourth Jud. Dist., 69 Nev. 276, 283, 249 P.2d 226 
91952); Lain v. John Hancock Mut. Life Ins., 398 N.E.2d 278 


C-5 


(Ill.App.1979); Hunguenor v. Hunguenor, 420 So.2d 344 
(Fla.App.1982); Braley v. Horton, 432 So.2d 463 (Ala.1983)). 


Contrary to the opinion of the:tribal court and the State 
Defendants, there is no need for the separate “long-arm” statute for 
extraterritorial service of process and one [sic] Judge Van Walraven 
issued a rule providing for extra territorial service of process by the 
methods approved by NRCP Rule 4(f), service in accordance with 
such rule of the court is valid. 

~ Judge Van Walraven’s authority to approve and apply 
Indian law is demonstrated by his adopted of NRCP Rules 4, 
inclusive, 11, inclusive 26-37, inclusive, NRS 49.335 et seq., and 
Title 2A of the Law & Order code, and he is stopped from denying 
such authority after the indigent Plaintiff has observed repeated 
demonstration of such authority and relied upon Judge Van 
Walraven’s actions. (Judge Van Walraven’s act of ordering the 
adoption and application of these provisions of NRS clearly 
demonstrate his broad authority from the Business Council). 

The fact that the Fallon Business Council has not acted to 
reverse Judge Van Walraven’s orders adopting and apply NRCP 
Rules 4, 11, 26-37, NRS 49.335 et seq., and Title 2A of their Law 
& Order Code demonstrates that he acts with the approval of the 
tribe’s Legislature and governing body, thus Judge Van Walraven 
has not usurped the choices of the Fallon Business Council as 
suggested by State Defendants. 

As well, Judge Van Walraven’s order amending his May 3, 
1993, adoption and application of NRCP Rule 4, inclusive, fails 
under tribal law (Title 1, §1-30-040) because it: 

1) Emasculates the Fallon Tribal Court and renders it 
useless to reservation residents; impedes the administration of 
justice, 


2) Contravenes the application of tribal law; 

3) Contravenes the application of tribal custom, 

4) Contravenes the application of federal laws, 
produces an absurd result, and } 

5) Interfere’s [sic] with the tribe’s laws declaring 


jurisdiction in these very types of civil matters. 


Given the declaration of tribal law, that the “act of entry 
upon the territory within the jurisdiction of the court shall 


C-6 


SRILA GPE IAA EMI EAE OM 


LOM ER DLE LOND LORD AE SLA tie 


conclusively be deemed consent to the jurisdiction of the [Fallon 
Tribal Court] with respect to any civil action arising out of such 
entry,” the Plaintiff did not need tribal law authorization to effect 
notice of suit outside the exterior boundaries of the Fallon Indian 
Reservation. Plaintiff could have merely mailed notice of suit to 
the State Defendants since their act of entry is conclusively deemed 
as consent to suit. 


With regard to State Defendants’ argument that as mere 
Nevada game wardens they lacked authority to subject the State of 
Nevada to suit in tribal court or that they lacked authority to 
consent to suit by the act of their entry on the Fallon Indian 
Reservation, State Defendants ignore the fact that sought legal 
counsel from the Churchill County District Attorney before 
repeatedly entering the Fallon Indian Reservation and the Plaintiff s 
allotted Indian lands voluntarily. 


If a judgment is entered against the State Defendants 
following trial, they have the right to contribution by a number of 
persons, including the Churchill County District Attorney. 
Moreover, the August 1990 Search and Seizure Warrant issued by 
Justice of the Peace Marilyn Craig clearly on its fact advised State 
Defendants that they were entering a jurisdiction in which Nevada 
law did not apply. 


State Defendants would do well to recognize that they have 
not only been sued in their official capacity, but their individual 
capacity as well. 


Moreover, State Defendants’ argument appears only to be 
an extension of their unsuccessful argument in Hall v. Nevada (8 
Cal.3d 522, 105 Cal.Rptr. 355, 503 P.2d 1363, 1365-1366 (1972)), 
and Nevada v. Hall (440 U.S. 410, 99 S.Ct. 1182, 59 L.Ed.2d 416, 
reh. denied, 441 U.S. 917 (1979)). For the same reasons that 
California’s courts exist to protect their citizens so do tribal courts. 
(See Hall v. Nevada, 8 Cal.3d 522, 105 Cal.Rptr. 355, 503 P.2d 
1365 (1972) (citing People v. Streeper, 12 II].2d 204, 145 N.E.2d 
625, 629 91957) for the proposition that the sovereignty of one 
jurisdiction does not extend into the territory of another so as to 
create immunity from suite)). 


With regard to State Defendants’ objection to the method of 
private service given the practicalities and peculiarities of these 
cases. The State Defendants were given notice and it was effective 


C-7 


because they appeared to contest the service. That is all that is 
required of by federal law. See Mulane v. Central Hanover Bank & 
Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306, 70 S.Ct. 652, 94 L.Ed. 865 (1950), where 
the Court held: 


“An elementary and fundamental requirement of due 
process in any proceeding which is to be accorded 
finality is notice reasonably calculated, under all the 
circumstances, to appraise interested parties of the 
pendency of the action and afford them an 


opportunity to present their objections .... The 
notice must afford a reasonable time for those 
interested to make their appearance . . . . But if with 


due regard for the practicalities and peculiarities of 
the case these conditions are reasonably met the 
constitutional requirements are satisfied. . . .” 


339 U.S., at 314-315. 
Order 


This matter is therefore Ordered by this Court to be 
reversed & remand for full consideration with regards to all of the 
above findings of fact & conclusions of law.

POINTS AND AUTHORITIES 


Personal jurisdiction is based on a combination of two 
elements, amenability to jurisdiction, and service of process. 
Perfumer’s Workshop v. Roure Bertrand du Pont, 737 F. Supp. 785, 
787 (S.D.N.Y. 1990); Thoenes v. Tatro, 529 P.2d 912, 918 (Or. 
1974); Harvey v. Price, 603 F. Supp. 1205 (D. Ill. 1985). Because 
neither of these factors is present with regard to the defendants who 
specially appear by this motion, this court lacks personal 
jurisdiction over them and should issue its order quashing service of 
process. 

(1) The specially appearing defendants are not amendable to 
the process of the Fallon Tribal Court. 


The specially appearing defendants are the State of Nevada 
and its officers and employees. They are not amenable to the 
process of this court. The State has waived its immunity to a 
limited extent as set forth at NRS 41.0305 et seg., but has consented 
to suit only "in a court of competent jurisdiction of this state." NRS 
41.031(2). 

The Fallon Tribal Court is not a "court of this state," see 
Hubert v. City of Acworth, 267 S.E.2d 823 (Ga. App. 1980) ("court 
of this state" means a court created by the constitution and laws of 
the state), nor can plaintiff demonstrate otherwise that the Nevada 
Legislature intended to permit suit against the State in a tribal court. 
Accordingly, the immunity of the specially appearing defendants 
prevents this court from asserting its jurisdiction over them. 

Further, Plaintiff has pleaded no facts which show that the 
State has waived its immunity with regard to the claims in the 
complaint. "To state an actionable claim against the state, a pleader 
must plead facts in the complaint in avoidance of immunity."

McCann v. State Dep't of Mental Health, 247 N.W.2d 521 (Mich. 
1976) (emphasis in original). This also is the rule in Nevada. 
Taylor v. State of Nev. & Univ. of Nev., 73 Nev. 151, 311 P.2d 733 
(1957). See also 81A C.J.S. States § 322 (1977). Plaintiff 
provides no basis for finding that the State has waived its immunity, 
but relies instead on unspecified federal law, tribal law! and a 
United States Supreme Court opinion2, none of which provide any 
basis for waiver. First Amended Complaint at 3. 


The recent opinion of the United States Supreme Court in 
the case entitled Blatchford v. Native Village of Noatak, S. Ct. 
___, 1991 WL 107382 (1991), confirms this court's lack of 
jurisdiction. Although that decision dealt with a suit against the 
State of Alaska filed in federal court, the law which the court relied 
upon to reach its result logically precludes jurisdiction over a state 
in a tribal court. The Supreme Court first recognized that the 
"States entered the federal system with their sovereignty intact." Id. 
at 3. It recognized the full vitality of the states' sovereign immunity 
based on "traditional principles of immunity." Id. And it 
concluded that the establishment of a federal court system did not 
abrogate the states’ immunity, even in cases wherein a state is sued 
by an Indian tribe acting in its sovereign capacity. 

A state is no more amenable to suit by a tribe than it is to 
suit by a foreign country. Id. at 3. One reason is the absence of 
mutuality which would result by recognition of Indian jurisdiction 
over states: tribes have not surrendered their immunity from suit by

Specially appearing defendants rely upon the Eleventh

immunity for federal law enforcement officers, and held that 
whether an official may be held personally liable depends on the 
objective legal reason-ableness of the official's action, assessed in 
light of legal rules that were clearly established at the time the 
action was taken, and without regard to his personal belief in the

Applying this rule fashioned by the Supreme Court, the

The opinion of the Nevada Supreme Court in Edgar v.

affirmative showing of personal jurisdiction. Fields v. Sedwick

In Nevada v. Hall, a California resident sued the State of

which occurred in California, involving an employee of the State of

The circumstances of the Supreme Court case differ so 
- significantly from those in the instant action that the opinion in the 
former cannot be considered controlling on the issue of state 
sovereign immunity raised in the pending motion. To the extent 
that it is relevant authority, the Supreme Court's decision supports 
granting the motion. 

A. The decision in "Nevada v. Hall" did not address 
tribal-state relations. : 

The question presented in Nevada v. Hall was "whether a 
State may claim immunity from suit in the courts of another State." 
440 U.S. at 415. This presents the first clear difference between 
Nevada v. Hall and this case: Nevada v. Hall addressed the 
relationship between states, while this case concerns the 
relationship between a state and an Indian tribe, thereby raising 
significant Indian law questions. !

authority is overlapping. The differences between the sovereigns 
are numerous. "The sovereignty that the Indian tribes retain is of a

The circumstances of the present case are not even 
remotely similar to those referenced in the Court's footnote. The 
alleged injuries occurred within the State of Nevada, by wardens 
acting under the State's game laws. The action therefore has far 
stronger significance for Nevada's exercise of sovereign authority 
within its own borders. 


The regulation of game falls within a state's police powers. 
"Protection of the wild life of the State is peculiarly within the 
police power, and the State has great latitude in determining what 
means are appropriate for its protection." Lacoste v. Department of 
Conservation, 263 U.S. 545, 552 (1924), quoted in Baldwin v. 
Montana Fish & Game Comm'n, 436 U.S. 371, 392 (1978). Police 
power is the exercise of the sovereign right of a government to 
promote order, safety, health, morals, and general welfare. 
Marshall v. Kansas City, 355 S.W.2d 877, 883 (Mo. 1966). It is 
"an indispensible, essential and vital attribute of sovereignty... 
[T]he Constitution supposes the preexistence of the police power 
and must be construed with reference to that fact." 16A Am.Jur.2d 
Constitutional Law § 360 (1979). Cf. Rice v. Rehner, 463 U.S. at 
731 (recognizing state's interest in liquor regulation pursuant to 
police power as support for state liquor regulation in Indian 


country). 

This action, filed in this tribunal, is an imposition on and 
impediment to Nevada's sovereign prerogative to carry out its 
purposes with regard to its wildlife. Wardens must be allowed to 
enforce the game laws of the State without fear of being held to a 
standard of liability higher than that which the legislature of the 
State has determined is appropriate. The State's interest as a 
sovereign is therefore manifestly greater than in Nevada v. Hall. 
This distinction, too, requires a determination different from that in 
Nevada v. Hall.

It is well settled that Indian tribes possess the

Indian tribes may not be sued absent express and

(citations omitted). 

Moreover, it has been held that tribal immunity operates 
regardless of where a suit is brought. Val/Del, Inc. v. Superior Ct.

It thus defies all notions of fairness to hold Nevada liable

II. Hafer v. Melo, U.S. 112 S. Ct. 358 (1991). 


This case arose when the newly elected Auditor General of 
Pennsylvania discharged certain employees, who then filed an 
action in federal court for money damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. 
Section 1983 expressly creates a private cause of action against any 
person who, acting under color of state law, violates the civil rights 
of anyone else within United States jurisdiction. The court has 
ruled, however, that neither a state nor an officer or employee of a 
state in their official capacity is a "person" against whom an action 
may be brought. The question in Hafer v. Melo was whether such 
an officer or employee could be made liable for money damages 
under § 1983 in their capacity as an individual. The Court 
answered in the affirmative. 


A. Plaintiff's claims are not brought pursuant to statute 


expressly creating a cause of action based on violation of 
constitutional rights. 


Plaintiff's claims in this case do not rely on 42 U.S.C. § 
1983 or the United States Constitution. Instead they rely on the 
Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA) and common law tort theories. 
This makes a significant difference for the outcome. 


The Court has held that federal government officials are 
ordinarily accorded absolute immunity from liability under state 
tort theories, so long as their actions fall within their discretionary 
authority. Barr v. Mateo, 360 U.S. 564 (195 ), Westfall v. Erwin, 
484 U.S. 292, 296-97 (1988). The development of liability for 
officials in their individual capacities as discussed in Hafer has 
occurred only in the field of constitutional violations. 

If a public officer is acting with proper authority and 

the acts are "discretionary," the Court in Barr v. 

Mateo held there is no liability even though the 

officer acts willfully or maliciously. 


The advent of "constitutional torts" in 1971 
substantially reduced the scope of the immunity 
defense in suits against public officers... Put 
simply, public officials who violate constitutional 
rights might be personally liable to the injured 
person for damages. 


Ralph W. Johnson and James M. Madden, Sovereign Immunity in 
Indian Tribal Law, 12 American Indian Law Review 153, 175-75 
(1984). Thus only for certain wrongs is the immunity of 
government officials qualified. 


The protections for wrongs done under the ICRA differ 
greatly from those afforded under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. Vindication 
for wrongs done under the ICRA are extremely limited. "Congress 
has provided no private right of action to enforce the [Indian Civil 
Rights] Act, other than habeas corpus.” Imperial Granite Co. v. 
Pala Band of Indians, 940 F.2d 1269, 1271, n. 3 (9th cir. 1991). 
"The structure of the statutory scheme and the legislative history of 
Title I suggest that Congress' failure to provide remedies other than 
habeas corpus was a deliberate one." Santa Clara Pueblo v.

Martinze, 436 U.S. at . It is not even clear that the ICRA provides a 
remedy against a tribal sovereign in its own courts. 


In testimony before the Commission [on Civil 
Rights], Judge Elbridge Coochise, administrator and 
chief judge of the Northwest Intertribal Court 
System, was asked whether he thought the ICRA 
waived sovereign immunity. He responded in the 
negative and indicated that it is "something we need 
to look at and give the tribes the opportunity, like any 
other government, to say 'Yes, we waive that,' or "No, 
we don't." 


United States Civil Rights Commission, The Indian Civil Rights 
Act at 67 (June 1991). The Tribe may have waived the absolute 
immunity of tribal officials in their individual capacities, Fallon 
Tribal Code § 1-10-40, but this is not equivalent to an express 
Congressional signal such as the one in § 1983, and implies no 
waiver of the State's immunities. 


In contrast, Congress operated with a now undisputed intent 
to establish a remedy for individuals whose rights under 42 U.S.C. 
§ 1983 and the Constitution are abused. 


Through § 1983, Congress sought "to give a remedy 
to parties deprived of constitutional rights, 
privileges, and immunities by an official’s abuse of 
his position." Accordingly, it authorized suits to 
redress deprivations of civil rights by persons acting 
"under color of any [state] statute, ordinance, 
regulation, custom, or usage." 


Hafer, 112 S. Ct. at 363. 


Congress’ failure to create an express remedy for ICRA 
violations like the one it created for constitutional violations 
justifies different treatment of the immunity question under the 
ICRA. The qualified immunity exception has no place in the 
enforcement of the ICRA. Defendants should be measured under 
the standard rule established in Barr v. Mateo. Under that standard, 
the individually named defendants are entitled to absolute 
immunity because they were clearly acting within their 
discretionary authority under the State's game laws.

B. The defense of reasonable reliance on the law was 


not addressed in Hafer. 


Even if the individually named defendants are not entitled 
to immunity under the Barr standard, they may still make personal 
defenses at the earliest stages of litigation, and the court must 
determine the validity of the defenses even where they involve 
questions of fact. 

Because the entitlement is an immunity from suit 

rather than a mere defense to liability, we 

repeatedly have stressed the importance of 

resolving immunity questions at the earliest 

possible stage in litigation. 


Immunity ordinarily should be decided by the court 
[instead of a jury] long before trial. 


Hunter v. Bryant, 52 S. Ct. Bull. (CCH) B469 (Dec. 16, i991) 
(citations omitted) (emphasis in original). The Hafer opinion is 
does not speak to the validity of these defenses. 

The government official's argument in Hafer was that she 
could not be made personally liable since she acted within her 
official capacity when she terminated the plaintiff employees. She 
did not admit to being an individual defendant, and the Court 
therefore did not explore "personal immunity defenses such as 
objectively reasonable reliance on existing law." 112 S. Ct. at 362. 
Specially appearing individual defendants have made that defense; 
Hafer offers no guidance on its determination. 


The Hafer decision thus did not address the test to be 
applied when determining whether immunity should be recognized. 
In Romero v. Kitsap County, 931 F.2d 624 (9th Cir. 1991), the 
court enunciated the test when it found two peace officers immune 
from claims they had violated Indian treaty rights: 


[T]he qualified immunity test necessitates three 
inquiries: (1) the identification of the specific right 
allegedly violated; (2) the determination of whether 
that right was so ‘clearly established’ as to alert a 
reasonable officer to its constitutional parameters; 
and (3) the ultimate determination of whether a 


K-11 


reasonable officer could have believed lawful the 
particular conduct at issue. 


931 F.2d at 627. Immunity should be given and discovery should 
not be permitted where the pleadings fail to claim violation of a 
clearly established right. Id., 931 F.2d at 628, n.6. The pleadings 
and papers in this case establish no such violation. The law for 
issuance and execution of search warrants on the Fallon 
Reservation is not at all clear, as the pleadings indicate; and rights 
and remedies under the ICRA are unclear, in view of Santa Clara 
Pueblo v. Martinez. 


Nor has plaintiff alleged violations of law with sufficient 
specificity. "Mere allegations of malice do not destroy qualified 
immunity.... The complaint must... allege specific facts 
demonstrating that the actions of the individual state defendants 
were objectively unreasonable." Bellamy v. Borders, 727 F. Supp. 
247 (D.S.C. 1989). The only specific allegation in plaintiff's 
complaint is that the officers failed to leave a copy of the warrant 
with the plaintiff. However: 


[A] search and seizure is not illegal if irregularities in 
the procedure do not affect the substantial rights of the 
accused.... As a general rule, statutes which require 
peace officers to exhibit or deliver a copy of the 
search warrant at the place to be searched are viewed 
as ministerial only and failure to comply with such 
statutes does not void an otherwise valid search. 


State v. Chatriand, 792 P.2d 1107, 1108-09 (Mont. 1990). See also 
United States v. Klapholz, 17 F.R.D. 18 (S.D.N.Y. 1955) ("if the 
search warrant was valid and the original entry lawful, we cannot 
agree with the contention that the search was rendered unlawful by 
the mere failure of the officers to leave a copy of the warrant," 
quoting Giacolone v. United States, 13 F.2d 108 (9th Cir. 1926). Cf. 
People v. Gifford, 782 P.2d 795, 797, n.2 (Colo. 1989) 
("presentment of the warrant prior to the commencement of the 
search is not an absolute constitutional requirement"). 

Moreover, defendants rely on plaintiff's own exhibits, 
attached to the complaint, as prima facie, unrebutted evidence of 
defendants' objective good faith. It is necessary for plaintiff to put 
forth evidence establishing an issue of fact regarding the 
defendants’ alleged malicious intent or bad faith. Yet there is no 


K-12 


competent evidence in the record by way of affidavit or otherwise 
to support the claims. 

None of these principles delimiting immunity were 
addressed by the Court in Hafer, and that case does little to 
elucidate or elaborate on them. 


CONCLUSION 


The decision in Hafer stands only for the proposition that 
officials can be made personally liable in their individual capacities 
in actions brought pursuant to 42 U.S.C. § 1983. It does not 
evaluate the merits of a reasonable reliance defense. The holdings 
of the decision therefore contribute little to decision on the pending 
motion, since the case before the court does not involve a § 1983 
claim, and it raises a claim of reasonable reliance.
