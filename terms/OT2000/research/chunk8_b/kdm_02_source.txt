Ill. THE OREGON REGULATION AS APPLIED DOES 
NOT VIOLATE THE FEDERAL CONSTITUTION 


A. The Free Exercise Clause 


The narrow question before us is whether the free exercise 
rights of KDM and his parents were impermissibly burdened 
by the application of Oregon’s regulation, which precludes 
the District from providing special education services to 
KDM at the sectarian school he attended. In deciding that 
question we are guided by the distinction the Supreme Court 
has recognized in the Establishment Clause context between a 
Statute’s invalidity on its face and its invalidity in particular 
applications. See Bowen v. Kendrick, 487 U.S. 589, 602, 108 
S.Ct. 2562, 101 L.Ed.2d 520 (1988); see also Hunt vy. 
McNair, 413 U.S. 734, 742, 93 S.Ct. 2868, 37 L.Ed.2d 923 
(1973) (examining only “the transaction presently before [the 
court}]” and not “the statute as a whole”); Zobrest v. Catalina 
Foothills Sch. Dist., 963 F.2d 1190, 1194 n.3 (9th Cir. 1992) 
(considering “only the validity of one very specific proposed 
application of the statutes at issue”), rev’d on other grounds, 
509 U.S. 1, 113 S.Ct. 2462, 125 L.Ed.2d 1 (1993). Whatever 
the impact of the regulation might be in other factually 
distinct situations, cf. Zobrest, 963 F.2d at 1192 (failure to 
provide sign-language interpreter in the classroom for 
plaintiff effectively forced his parents to choose between 
foregoing a sectarian education or paying for the cost of the 
interpreter themselves) and Peter y. Wedl, 155 F.3d 992 (8th 
Cir. 1998) (failure to provide Spastic quadriplegic student 
with needed fulltime paraprofessional while in school forced

choice between foregoing sectarian education or paying for 
the paraprofessional), this is not a case in which the 
regulation impinges on plaintiffs’ free exercise rights. The 
parties stipulated that the service provided to KDM at the fire 
hall down the street from the school twice a week for ninety 
minutes is in compliance with KDM’s | statutory 
individualized education plan, the adequacy of which is not in 
dispute, and that he could safely travel there-indeed, the 
vision specialist comes to KDM’s school, picks him up and 
then returns him to his school. Moreover, plaintiffs have 
stipulated that the vision specialist’s services would not be 
provided in-class at Harbor Baptist but in a separate room. 
Thus, there is no support for the district court’s finding that 
the regulation forces KDM and his parents to choose between 
enrolling at Harbor Baptist and receiving special education at 
the fire hall or enrolling at a nonreligious school and 
receiving in-class services. In sum, there is no showing that 
application of the regulation to KDM’s case burdens KDM’s 
or his parents’ free exercise of their religion. : 


While the Oregon regulation is not “neutral” because it 
restricts the provision of services to “religiously-neutral 
settings,” cf. Employment Division v. Smith, 494 U.S. 872, 


878, 110 S.Ct. 1595, 108 L.Ed.2d 876 (1990), as applied here © 


it does not have “the object or purpose . . . [of] suppression of 
religion or religious conduct.” Church of The Lukumi Babalu 
Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah, 508 U.S. 520, 533, 113 S.Ct. 
2217, 124 L.Ed.2d 472 (1993). KDM is not subjected to 
“[o]fficial action that targets religious conduct for distinctive 
treatment.” Jd. at 534, 113 S.Ct. 2217. His case is wholly 
unlike Brown v. Borough of Mahaffey, 35 F.3d 846 (3d Cir. 
1994), involving the deliberate installation by the city of a 
gate impeding access to a particular revival meeting, or 
Hartmann v. Stone, 68 F.3d 973 (6th Cir. 1995), involving an 
Army regulation banning all religious practice by day care 
providers on the base. Nor is Peter v. Wedl analogous because 
under the facts of that case, as noted, plaintiff indeed was

forced to choose between enrolling in the sectarian school or 
receiving services essential to his ability to attend school. 155 
F.3d at 1001. 


That the regulation, standing alone, “discriminates” against 
Students in religious schools, i.e., treats them differently by 
denying them state services on the school grounds, does not 
result in a burden on the free exercise of religion by someone 
in the position of KDM or his parents. We agree with the 
court in Strout v. Albanese, 178 F.3d 57 (Ist Cir. 1999), cert. 
denied, USS. . cae SAX. Sy. L.Ed.2d 
(1999), upholding, against a Free Exercise challenge, a Maine 
Statute funding grants to private schools for students in 
communities without public education facilities, provided the 
schools are nonsectarian. The court distinguished Lukumi 
because there was no evidence of “a substantial animus . . . 
that motivated the law in question.” Jd. at 65. Here, the 
District’s solicitousness in accommodating KDM could 
hardly be said to reflect a purpose to “suppress[] religion or 
religious conduct.” The mere fact that the District makes its 
service to KDM available in the fire hall down the street from 
his school does not amount to Suppression of religion or 
religious conduct. 


We conclude that Oregon’s regulation as applied to KDM 
and his parents does not impose an impermissible burden on 
their free exercise of religion. 


B. The Establishment Clause 


The district court also found that the Oregon regulation 
violates the Establishment Clause because it requires the State 
Superintendent of Education to decide on a case-by-case basis 
whether particular settings are religious. Since Zobrest, the 
mere presence of a public employee on religious premises 
clearly is not enough to invoke the Establishment Clause. See 
Zobrest, 509 U.S. at 13, 113 S.Ct. 2462: see also Agostini v. 
Felton, 521 U.S. 203, 234, 117 S.Ct. 1997, 138 L.Ed.2d 391] 


12a 


(1997) (“[A]fter Zobrest we no longer presume that public 
employees will inculcate religion simply because they happen 
to be in a sectarian environment.”’). 

In Agostini, the Supreme Court held that monthly visits by 
supervisors to parochial school classrooms to ensure that 
remedial education provided by public school teachers 
remained secular did not result in excessive entanglement 
under the test of Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602, 612-13, 
91 S.Ct. 2125, 29 L.Ed.2d 745 (1971). Agostini is merely the 
latest in a line of cases rejecting an entanglement claim when 
applied to the making of judgments by officials overseeing 
regulatory schemes concerning the religious character of 
activities. See Hernandez v. CIR, 490 U.S. 680, 696-97, 109 
S.Ct. 2136, 104 L.Ed.2d 766 (1989) (“[RJoutine regulatory 
interaction which involves no inquiries into religious 
doctrine, . . . and no ‘detailed monitoring and close 
administrative contact’ between secular and religious bodies, 
does not of itself violate the nonentanglement command.” 
(citations omitted)); Mueller v. Allen, 463 U.S. 388, 403, 103 
S.Ct. 3062, 77 L.Ed.2d 721 (1983) (finding no entanglement 
despite the fact that state officials required to determine 
whether textbooks were religious); Board of Educ. v. Allen, 
392 U.S. 236, 245, 88 S.Ct. 1923, 20 L.Ed.2d 1060 (1968) 
(same). . 


We conclude that the regulation does not offend the 
entanglement prong of the Lemon test. We need go no further 
because the district court did not determine that it offended 
the first (secular purpose), or second (primary effect to 
advance or inhibit religion), prong of the test.

against people based on their exercise of religion, so long as 
the discriminatory burdens it imposes are not more substantial 
than requiring a blind child with cerebral palsy to leave his 
school building and go down the street to a fire hall. That is 
quite a surprise. Fortunately, that is not the law in most of the

Defendants have not suggested any justification aside from Oregon’s 
anti-establishment concerns. 


34a 


benefits on such a basis, however, neither the IDEA nor the 
United States or Oregon constitutions impose an obligation to 
provide a vision specialist to K.D.M. While the IDEA 
mandates a regular educational environment “to the 
maximum extent appropriate,” its recent amendments make 
clear that a district’s provision of services is not required if a 
parent opts out of a free and appropriate public education. 
Thus, a district may refuse to provide special education and 
related services to private school students whose parents opt 
out of free and appropriate public education as long as the 
district does not create a distinction based on religious 
settings and otherwise complies with the IDEA. 


The court need not address plaintiff's claims under the 
Oregon Constitution or the Religious Freedom Restoration 
Act. Because there is no evidence that defendant Paulus 
intentionally or recklessly violated plaintiff's clearly 
established constitutional rights, Act Up!/Portland v. Bagley, 
988 F.2d 868 (9th Cir. 1993), plaintiff is not entitled to relief 
on his claim under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. 


CONCLUSION 


The decision of the Superintendent is reversed and 
judgment entered in favor of plaintiff on counts three and four 
of his Complaint (#1). As the prevailing party, plaintiff is 
entitled to his reasonable attorney fees. 20 U.S.C. § 1415 (i) 
(3) (B) (1997)

CONCLUSIONS AND REASONS: 


The governing statute applicable to the facts is the Individuals 
with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) (20 U.S.C. §§ 1400 et 
seq.), which was implemented by the U.S. Department of 
Education through regulations published at 34 C.F.R. 
§§ 300.1 et seg. The IDEA requires that all children with 
disabilities have available to them a free appropriate public 
education which emphasizes special education and related 
services designed to meet their unique needs (§ 1400(c)). 
Further, § 1412 (2) (B) requires any State desiring to qualify 
under this statute to demonstrate, among other things, that “a 
free appropriate education will be provided for all children 
with disabilities between the ages of three and eighteen 
within the State not later than September 1, 1978... .” 34 
C.F.R. §300.452 makes it clear that “[e]Jach LEA [Local 
Educational Agency] shall provide special education and 
related services designed to meet the needs of private school 
children with disabilities in the jurisdiction of the agency,” 
and cites 20 U.S.C. 1413(a) (4) (A) and 1414(a)(6) as 
authority. 


All disabled children must be provided services under the 
IDEA. This is true even if the parents of the child choose to 
send the child to a private school. Title 34 C.F.R. § 300.403 
provides that if a child with a disability has a free and 
appropriate public education available and the parents choose 
to place the child in a private school or facility, the public 
agency is not required to pay for the child’s education at the 


4la 


private school facility. “However, the public agency shall 
make services available to the child as provided under 
§§ 300.450-300.452”. 34 C.F.R. § 300.403. 


Claimant argues that the Individuals with Disabilities 
Education Act requires that K.D.M.’s vision specialist and 
any other aid be provided in the regular educational 
environment. He argues that parents have a fundamental right 
to direct the upbringing of their children, including the right 
to choose the type of education they receive. Section 300.452 
requires each LEA to “provide special education and related 
services designed to meet the needs of private school children 
with disabilities residing in the jurisdiction of the agency.” 


In Oregon, school districts must make available special 
education designed to meet the needs of resident handicapped 
children in private schools. The regulation requires special 
education and related services be provided in a religiously 
neutral setting. OAR 581-15-166(3). 


The U.S. Department of Education’s administrative reg- 
ulations for the IDEA are found in 34 C.F.R. §§300.1 ef seq. 
According to section 300.533, Placement Procedures, each 
public agency is to 


“(4) Ensure that the placement decision is made in 
conformity with the LRE rules in §§300.550- 
300.554.” 


Section 300.550 gives the general requirements for ensuring 
that a child is placed in the least restrictive environment, and 
states: 


“(a) Each SEA shall ensure that each public agency 
establishes and implements procedures that meet 
the requirements of §§ 300.550-300.556. 


“(b) Each public agency shall ensure- 


“(1) That to the maximum extent appropriate, 
children with disabilities, including children in 


42a 


public or private institutions or other care facilities, 
are educated with children who are nondisabled; 
and 


“(2) That special classes, separate schooling or 
other removal of children with disabilities from the 
regular educational environment occurs only when 
the nature or severity of the disability is such that 
education in regular classes with the use of 
supplementary aids and services cannot be achieved 
satisfactorily.” 


20 U.S.C. 1412(5)(B); 1414(a)1)(C)(iv). 


K.D.M. is attending a private school with nondisabled 
students. K.D.M. requires vision services. He is being 
educated in a separate classroom at his school. His parents 
want the vision services provided in the separate classroom. 
The school district alleges they cannot provide the services at 
the private school. The services are provided at a nearby fire 
hall because Oregon law prohibits the services from being 
provided in the school. In this instance, the school district is 
providing the student with special education services to the 
maximum extent possible. 34 CFR 300.550. There is no 
requirement that IDEA requires services to be provided in a 
religious school. 34 C.F.R. 300.403. See also Goodall v. 
Stafford County School, 930 F2d 363, cert den, 502 U.S. 
112 SCt 188 (1991). 


In K.D.M.’s circumstances, it cannot be argued that the visual 
therapy services provided in the fire hall are preventing his 
education with other students when if that service were 
provided in the private school in a separate room from other 
students, it would be no more beneficial. 


Claimant argues that the Establishment Clause of the U.S. 
Constitution’s First Amendment does not prohibit the 
placement of K.D.M.’s vision — in Harbor Baptist 
Church School. 


43a 


In Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills School District, 125 L.Ed. 2d 
1 (1993), the parents of a disabled child chose to send the 
child to a Roman Catholic sectarian high school. When they 
requested that the school district provide a sign-language 
interpreter, their request was denied on the basis that to do so 
would be a violation of the Federal Constitution. The United 
States Supreme Court reversed the U.S. Ninth Circuit Court 
of appeals and held that, pursuant to the Individuals with 
Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), it is constitutionally 
permissible to allow a public school district to provide a sign- 
language interpreter to accompany a deaf student to classes in 
a sectarian religious school. The court reasoned that the IDEA 
benefits went to the child who qualifies as “handicapped” 
under the IDEA, not to the sectarian organization, and thus 
were not impermissible. 


It does not require placement of the vision specialist in the 
private school nor does it support claimant’s contention that 
the OAR is contrary to law. 


Claimant argues that Article I, Section 5, of the Oregon State 
Constitution does not prohibit the placement of K.D.M.’s 
vision specialist in Harbor Baptist Church School. 


States must comply with Federal requirements if they accept 
special education payments. The law accommodated the 
States in allowing some flexibility in administering the 
program where the requirements of special education would 
come into conflict with issues of church and state or the 
nature of the disability. It was important that special 
education be provided to individuals in need of it regardless 
of religious affiliation. The federal regulations allow school 
districts to make modifications in what is provided as well as 
where the services are provided, keeping mindful of the “least 
restrictive environment” and the “maximum extent 
appropriate” requirements, assuring they are educated with 
children who are not disabled. 


44a 


The School District argues that Article I, Section 5 should not 
be interpreted identically to the federal Establishment Clause. 
In Cooper v. Eugene School District, 723 P2d 298, 306 Or 
1986, the court held that only “sometimes” is_ the 
interpretation the same and traditionally the provision of the 
State constitution has been construed more strictly than the 
federal Establishment Clause. 


The SEA School districts must walk a fine balance between 
the requirements of 20 U.S.C. and the Oregon Constitution. 
School districts may provide the services at public schools or 
at a neutral site and the Reedsport School District provides 
the service at a neutral site. 


The reality of the circumstances is that vision services are 
provided to a student who is eligible for special education. 
The services are provided at a religiously neutral setting 
across the street from the parochial school. The services are 
paid regardless of the location of the provided service. The 
student benefits and the school benefits. Where the benefit is 
a function of education, both benefit. 


Claimant argues that refusal to place the vision specialist in 
his school is impermissibly hostile toward religion. The First 
Amendment to the U.S. Constitution “affirmatively mandates 
accommodation, not merely tolerance, of all religions, and 
forbids hostility toward any.” Lynch v. Donnelly, 465 U.S. 
668, 673 (1984). The IDEA, 20 U.S.C. § 1401 (a), promotes 
accommodation. The purpose of 20 U.S.C., Section 1400 was 
to assure that all children have available to them a free and 
appropriate education. The school district is reasonably 
accommodating K.D.M. by providing him with a vision 
specialist within the constraints of the regulations adopted by 
the State of Oregon as it relates to students attending private 
religious schools. The parents have elected to place K.D.M. 
in a private school. The State has complied with the IDEA 
and with State regulations by making services available to 
K.D.M. as provided under §§ 300.450-300.452. There is no 


j 
/ 


45a 


evidence that the action taken by the school district prevents 
K.D.M from practicing his religion or that the action prevents 
access to the vision specialist. 


In interpreting the Equal Access Act, 20 U.S.C. §§ 4071-74 
(1988), the United States Supreme Court held in Board of 
Education v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226, 248 (1990) that, “(t]he 
Establishment Clause does not license government to treat 
religion and those who teach or practice it, simply by virtue 
of their status as such, as subversive of American ideals and 
therefore subject to unique disabilities.” Claimant argues that 
to deny the visual specialist in his school setting merely 
because that school is religious subjects him to unique 
disabilities not faced by similarly situated students and as 
such it denies K.D.M. equal Participation in the IDEA 
program. The facts differed substantially from the facts of the 
instant case. In Mergens the issue involved participation in a 
private school club that was not related to a specific 
curriculum. Additionally, the age of the students was 
considered. The court considered that secondary school 
Students were mature enough to understand that a school does 
not endorse or support student speech that it merely permits 
on a nondiscriminatory basis. The courts determined that the 
Equal Access Act did not on its face contravene the 
Establishment Clause. 


Claimant alleges that the failure to provide a vision specialist 
at Harbor Baptist Church School is a violation of 42 U.S.C. 
§ 1983. It provides in part that “[evJery person who, under 
color of any statute, ordinance, regulation, custom or usage, 
of any State or Territory or the District.of Columbia, subjects, 
or causes to be subjected, any citizen of the United States or 
other person within the jurisdiction thereof to the deprivation 
of any rights, privileges, or immunities secured by the 
Constitutions and the laws, shall be liable to the party injury 
in an action at law, suit in equity, or other proceeding for 
redress.” 


46a 


Zobrest does not require districts to provide special education 
services in a parochial school. In Oregon, the State has taken 
a position of neutrality consistent with the Oregon 
Constitution and adopted Administrative Rules which 
prohibit the provision of special education in a religious 
setting. The school district is providing special education to 
K.D.M., in compliance with the Oregon Administrative Rule, 
at a religiously neutral site. 


The courts have been consistent in interpreting Article I, 
Section 5 of the Oregon Constitution regarding religious 
neutrality. The court contended in Dickman v. School District 
62C, 232 Or. 238 (October 1962) that the wall of separation 
in the state of Oregon must be kept “high and impregnable” 
to meet the demands of Article I, Section 5. 


The State is required to be neutral. Zobrest does not mandate 
provision of services in the private school. It allows it in some 
circumstances. Nothing in Zobrest, IDEA or the federal 
regulations makes provision of on-site services mandatory. 
The District is in compliance with IDEA, federal regulations, 
the Oregon Constitution and the Oregon Administrative 
Rules. 


The hearing officer affirms for the school district.

Types of Service through Consultation Process 


(1) For purposes of this rule, “public educational agencies” 
means: 


(a) The Department of Education for children who age 
three through the age of eligibility for kindergarten; and 


(b) School districts for children who are at the age of 
eligibility for kindergarten through age 21. 


(2) Public educational agencies shall consult with 
appropriate representatives of children with disabilities 
enrolled by their parents in private schools during all phases 
of the development and design of the special education 
services to be provided to private school children with 
disabilities, including consideration of: 


(a) Which children shall receive services; 
(b) How the needs of children shall be identified; 
(c) What services shall be provided; 


(d) How services shall be provided, including the location 
for the delivery of those services; and 


(e) How the services shall be evaluated.
