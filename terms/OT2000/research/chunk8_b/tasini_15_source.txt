SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


Publishers have the right to reproduce and distribute 
electronic copies of their collective works under Section 201(c) 
of the Copyright Act. Petitioners’ opening brief demonstrated 
why it makes no difference under the Act’s media-neutral 
provisions that the revised collective works are fixed in electronic 
media — i.e., on disks rather than paper copies. Relying on those 
provisions and the relevant legislative history, petitioners refuted 
the rationale underlying the Second Circuit’s contrary holding. 
It is irrelevant that electronic copies in some instances do not 
retain all of the selection and arrangement of the paper editions 
and that users seek to retrieve individual articles. 
The former is precisely what makes the electronic copies 
“revisions,” and the latter rests on a theory of contributory 
infringement affirmatively waived by respondents and 
foreclosed by Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios, 
Inc., 464 U.S. 417, 446 (1984). Publishers are privileged to create 
electronic copies, just as they may publish microfilm copies. 
Both are fixed in a different medium from print editions, contain 
multiple editions on a single reel or disk, and are used to identify 
and retrieve individual articles. 


Respondents do not seriously defend the court of appeals’ 
reasoning and focus instead on the manner in which electronic 
copies are fixed and may be retrieved by third parties. 
These newfound arguments, however, are equally erroneous, 
as this reply demonstrates in five parts. First, respondents’ 
attempt to narrow the scope of Section 201(c) is flawed: The 
electronic versions of print periodicals are permitted by the broad 
“any revision” privilege because they substantially retain the 
editorial selection of the collective work. Indeed, respondents’ 
position would overturn the compromise struck by Section 
201(c) between the right of publishers to engage in the 
reproduction and distribution of their collective works in the 
aggregate in any medium and the right of freelance authors to 
exploit their individual contributions apart from the collective 
work. Second, respondents’ claim that individual articles in the 


2 


electronic copies are published apart from the collective work 
misstates the record; the district court found precisely the contrary 
on the undisputed summary judgment record. Third, respondents’ 
claim that when periodical copies are fixed electronically they 
constitute either a “new anthology” or an entirely different collective 
work, because individual files of other articles also may be fixed 
on the same disk or tape, conflicts with the Act’s media-neutral 
provisions. If accepted, respondents’ argument would make it 
impossible to continue to use computer storage in ways that have 
become familiar to countless researchers, publishers, libraries and 
others. Fourth, the remaining alternative arguments advanced only 
by the Garson respondents — that a publisher may not license 
reproduction and distribution of its collective work and that 
electronic publication by petitioners infringes upon freelance 
authors’ “display rights” — are contradicted by the plain language 
of the Act. Finally, even if the right of publishers, in which 
freelancers have long acquiesced, to publish their works 
electronically were open to question, it would be for Congress to 
overturn this long-settled publishing practice, particularly given 
that the Second Circuit’s judgment will result in wholesale deletions 
of freelance contributions, with correspondingly grave 
consequences for research. 


ARGUMENT 


I. The Electronic Copies At Issue In This Case Qualify As 
“Revisions” Under Section 201(c). 


1. Section 201(c) grants publishers the right to include 
freelance contributions in “any revision” of their collective 
works. Respondents have no effective answer to precedents of 
this Court that emphasize the great breadth of the term “any.” 
See Pet. Br. 21 (citing cases). They argue only that, depending 
on context, some limitations on that term may exist. See Garson 
Br. 21-25. Section 201(c), however, does not suggest any 
contextual limitation. The Second Circuit’s “floor-to-ceiling” 
approach is entirely artificial, see Pet. Br. 25-27, and the Tasini 
respondents understandably do not defend it. They also appear 
to acknowledge that a proper Section 201(c) “revision” can be 


3 


an “amended, improved or updated” version, see Tasini Br. 25, 
terms that aptly describe the collective works as transmitted by 
the publishers to NEXIS and fixed on CD-ROMs. 


The Tasini respondents assert, without further explanation, that 
a revision must be of the same “type” as the original, contain 
contributions of the same “kind,” and retain “the same ‘selection, 
coordination, or arrangement.’” Tasini Br. 16, 26 (emphasis 
added).' That proposal, however, directly conflicts with the text 
of Section 201(c). If the revision must be the “same” in all these 
respects, it can hardly be called a “revision.” The district court 
was correct to conclude instead that a revision must simply be a 
“recognizable version” of the original. See Cert. App. 61a, 73a.” 


Significantly, respondents do not defend the Second 
Circuit’s conclusion that the differences between the print and 
electronic versions in this case — particularly the failure to retain 
the print-only aspects of arrangement (such as placement 
above-the-fold) or selection (such as advertising and similar 
materials) — disqualify the electronic copies from being 
“revisions.”’ Those differences properly are characterized as 
creating a revised collective work but one that plainly is 
recognizable as an “amended” version of the original. This is 
particularly so given, as the district court concluded and 
respondents do not dispute, that the defining characteristic of a 
periodical is its editorial selection. See Cert. App. 70a, 73a;

II. Petitioners Publish Each Of Their Collective Works As 
An Aggregate Whole, Not As Individual Articles.

are reproduced and accessed individually. The Tasini 
respondents thus assert that petitioners “‘select[] out of the day’s 
edition the news and feature articles and . . . prepare{] a series 
of individual electronic files,” Tasini Br. 7, while the Garson 
respondents argue that petitioners “disassemble their collective 
works,” Garson Br. 14. In support of those and related 
statements, respondents consistently cite not to actual evidence 
in this case, but instead to an affidavit submitted by respondents’ 
counsel in the district court. See J.A. 65a-115a.

to the contrary: Petitioners exploit their periodicals only as a

Cert. App. 29a-32a. The publishers send precisely the same files

editorial contents of each collective work. Cert. App. 70a n.13

512a (parties’ statements of undisputed material facts);

III. Respondents’ Arguments Conflict With The Media- 
Neutral Terms Of The Copyright Act. 


Respondents argue at length that a collective work is 
changed in some fundamental way from the print copy when it 
is stored electronically. Most extreme is the argument that a 
work never can be fixed electronically because it will be stored 
as separate bits and bytes. See Tasini Br. 8 n.2 (“[OJne cannot 
Open up a computer and ‘draw a circle’ around the group of 
files that contain, say, the text of articles constituting a particular 
issue of Sports Illustrated.”). Alternatively, respondents contend 
that, once placed in a database, all the articles in a collective 
work take on an independent status. E.g., id. at 34-36. Finally, 
respondents claim that the NEXIS database is itself a new 
collective work that contains only numeyous individual articles, 
and not collective works published originally in hard copy. 
E.g., id. at 43-46. 


These theories are contradicted by the Copyright Act’s 
media-neutral provisions.'° The Act’s basic definitional 
provisions carefully distinguish among a “work,” the “medium” 
in which it is “fixed,” and the “copy” that results upon fixation. 
A “collective work,” such as the periodicals in this case, is “a 
work formed by the collection of preexisting materials 
[i.e., individual articles] .. . that are selected, coordinated, or 
arranged in such a way that the resulting work as a whole 
constitutes an original work of authorship.” 17 U.S.C. § 101. 
A hard copy, print edition of the periodical is one type of “copy,” 
which is defined as a “material object[] . . . in which a work is 
fixed and from which the work can be perceived, reproduced, 
or otherwise communicated, either directly or with the aid of a 
machine or device.” The work becomes “fixed” “when its 
embodiment in a copy . . . is sufficiently permanent or stable to

and distribution, licensing arrangements are forbidden. 
See Garson Br. 26-29. This argument rests on an artificial

particularly because the legislative history of Section 201(c) 
uses the terms “rights” and “privileges” interchangeably. 
Pet. Br. 20 n.12; J.A. 556a, 585a, 595a, 657a. When Congress 
intended “privileges” to be limited, it said so expressly.
