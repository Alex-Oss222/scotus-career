1. The Irreparable Harm Threatened By The Decision 
Below. The Second Circuit’s judgment, as a practical matter, 
requires the destruction of hundreds of thousands of 
electronically stored articles, spanning decades. Whether the 
deletions occur voluntarily, or eventually are court-ordered, they 
will occur on a massive scale. Respondent Tasini, of the National 
Writer’s Union (“NWU”), himself guaranteed this response 
when he promised a “tidal wave of lawsuits” and warned 
virtually every newspaper in the country that it sits on a 
“financial ticking time bomb.” See Pet. 14-15. In fact, at least 6 
Tasini-type actions already are pending, Garson Br. 14, including 
a nationwide class action composed of freelancers holding 
copyrights since 1977. See Complaint in Posner v. Gale Group, 
Inc., 00-CIV-7376 (S.D.N.Y., filed Sept. 28, 2000). 

The inevitability of these deletions, unless the Second 
Circuit’s erroneous decision is reversed, is a direct consequence 
of the substantial remedies available to plaintiffs under the 
Copyright Act! and the even more substantial remedies available 
for willful infringement.” No rational publisher can assume risks 
of such magnitude, as noted by: (1) an amicus brief by five 
Pulitzer Prize winning historians (some of them freelance 
writers), who find the completeness and integrity of electronic 
archives vital to their research and have urged the Court to review 
the Second Circuit’s decision; (2) an amicus brief by publishers

4. The Judgment Below Conflicts With Decisions Of 
This Court And Lower Courts. Petitioners do not seek 
“different rules” for the electronic medium. Garson Br. 7; 
Tasini Br. 12-13. Rather, petitioners seek to apply familiar 
copyright principles to electronic databases in a medium-neutral 
way. The Second Circuit did not do that. It defined the collective 
work by examining how the electronic copies might be used by 
third parties (to search for and retrieve individual articles), not 
by examining whether the electronic copies retained the editorial 
selection of the printed version. This conflicts directly with Sony, 
Feist and all of the Circuits correctly applying those cases. Sony 
unequivocally held that “[t]hird-party conduct [is] wholly 
irrelevant in an action for direct infringement.” 464 U.S. at 446. 
Yet the Second Circuit’s decision turns entirely on the ability 
of end users to access particular articles according to 
individualized and unknowable search criteria. That respondents 
did not even plead a contributory infringement claim based on 
end-user conduct, Garson Br. 3 & n.6, 22; Tasini Br. 11, makes 
the Second Circuit’s decision even more baffling.'° 

Similarly, the holding that “new” works are created by the 
mechanical process of transferring content from a print to 
electronic medium is a direct attack on the originality 
requirement addressed in Feist, 499 U.S. at 362-64, and 
inconsistent with the practice of the U.S. Copyright Office in
