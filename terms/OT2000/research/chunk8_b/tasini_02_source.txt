QUESTION PRESENTED 


The Copyright Act expressly grants periodical publishers 
the right to reproduce and distribute their publications, 
including articles contributed and separately copyrighted by 
freelance authors, in, inter alia, “any revision” of the 
publication. 17 U.S.C. § 201(c). For decades, publishers have 
relied on that provision to make complete editions of their 
periodicals available in both paper copies and copies stored 
electronically (such as in the NEXIS database and on 
CD-ROMs). The Second Circuit nonetheless held in this case 
that such electronic publication infringes upon the copyrights 
of freelance authors, a ruling that will require both the 
massive deletion of freelance articles published in certain 
electronic copies and the wholesale destruction of other 
electronic editions of many of: the nation’s leading 
periodicals. 


The question presented is whether a publisher’s 
reproduction and distribution of its entire periodical not only 
in print, but also electronically, is privileged under the 
Copyright Act or instead infringes upon the copyrights held 
by contributing freelance authors.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Petitioners publish a number of periodicals in both print 
and electronic copies. Some include articles contributed by 
respondents, who are freelance authors that own the copyrights 
to their individual contributions. The district court held that the 
electronic copies were privileged under Section 201(c) of the 
Copyright Act, which, in the absence of an express agreement, 


2 


permits a publisher to include individually copyrighted articles 
contributed by freelancers in copies of a periodical and “any 
revision” of that periodical. 17 U.S.C. § 201(c). The Second 
Circuit reversed, holding that publication in electronic copies 
infringes the copyrights held by freelance authors. 


The court of appeals’ holding merits this Court’s immediate 
review because it will require publishers and electronic database 
companies, nationwide, irreversibly to delete tens of thousands 
of freelance contributions currently stored in electronic archives. 
It also will force the wholesale destruction of CD-ROMs 
containing periodicals that include only a handful of such 
contributions. In addition, the decision below conflicts with 
decisions of this Court and of other circuits holding that the 
conduct of third parties is irrelevant to a claim of direct copyright 
infringement and that “originality” is the sine qua non of a “new” 
work. 


1. Petitioners publish a number of the nation’s most 
prominent newspapers and special interest magazines, including 
The New York Times (“the Times”), Newsday, and Sports 
Illustrated (collectively, ‘the Publications”), in both print and 
electronic copies. The electronic copies appear in databases such 
as NEXIS, which contains essentially the complete editorial 
content of the Publications dating from the early 1980s, as well 
as CD-ROM products, which contain the same content of the 
Times, as well as complete photographic copies of The New 
York Times Sunday Magazine and The New York Times Book 
Review. It is undisputed that, in every instance, the electronic 
copies are created from complete editions of the periodicals, 
not on an article-by-article basis. 


These electronic copies have revolutionized research in 
ways directly anticipated by Congress as far back as the early 
1960s. They function as the modern equivalent of national 
library shelves, combined with computerized search tools that 
serve as indices with awesome power. This makes it possible to 
complete in seconds research that previously would have 
required months. For example, prior to the “information age,” a 


3 


researcher would locate articles in back issues of the Times by 
retrieving and studying a hard-copy index of each year to be 
researched, then retrieving each individual article from either 
hard copies or microfilm reproductions of the newspaper. Now, 
the identical research can be completed instantly and remotely 
by entering a command into the search engine associated with 
a database; indeed, the electronic search generally produces far 
more accurate results because it retrieves articles based on their 
text rather than on the general subject headings set forth in the 
hard copy indices. 


2. Respondents are a number of freelance authors, led by 
the president of an organization (the National Writers Union) 
dedicated to advancing the interests of freelance authors. 
Between 1990 and 1993, respondents contributed articles, in 
which they retained separate copyrights,' to petitioners’ 
publications (collectively, “the Articles”). It is undisputed that 
respondents provided the Articles to petitioners precisely for 
the purpose of having them included in petitioners’ Publications 
and that no agreement between petitioners and respondents 
limits distribution of the Articles to print editions only. 


Nonetheless, respondents contend that electronic 
publication of entire periodical issues that contain their articles 
constitutes direct copyright infringement. Specifically, they 
maintain that the Copyright Act, as a matter of law, limits 
publishers to reproducing and distributing the full contents of 
their periodicals only in paper copies absent separate contractual 
agreements with the freelance contributors. (Plaintiffs’ Amended 
Compl. { 1). Of particular note, respondents do not assert that 
petitioners contribute in any way to copyright infringement by 
third parties who retrieve articles from the databases, a claim 
that automatically would be defeated under this Court’s decision 
in Sony Corporation of America v. Universal City Studios, 464 


-1. Respondents’ contributions thus differ from articles written by 
the Publications’ employees, which the Copyright Act deems 
“works for hire” and for which the Publications hold the copyright. 
17 U.S.C. § 101 (defining “works made for hire’’).

3. The district court rejected respondents’ claim, holding 
that petitioners’ publishing activities are contemplated 
and privileged by Section 201(c) of the Copyright Act. 
Pet. App. 23a-80a. The Act defines the Publications as 
“collective works” (a type of “compilation’”’) and the Articles 
as “contributions” to those collective works. 17 U.S.C. § 101. 
Section 201(c) grants a publisher, absent an agreement to the 
contrary, “the privilege of reproducing and distributing the 
contribution as part of that particular collective work, any 
revision of that collective work, and any later collective work 
in the same series.” Jd. at § 201(c). The district court held that, 
because a print edition of a periodical is a “collective work,” 
the electronic copy of the same periodical, whether distributed 
alone or stored in a larger database comprised of a number of 
such editions and other works, falls within the phrase 
“any revision of that collective work.” 


The district court explained that respondents were wrong 
to assert that electronic publication results in a work 
fundamentally different from the print edition. The databases 
indisputably “permit users to consult defendants’ periodicals 
in new ways and with new efficiency, but for the same purposes 
that they might otherwise review the hard copy versions of those 
periodicals.” Pet. App. 75a (emphasis added). Although some 
(but not all) of the electronic copies differ slightly in appearance 
from the original print editions — because in the ASCII text 
copies no graphics, agate-type and paper page layouts are present 
— that is precisely what makes them “revisions” rather than 
originals. Pet. App. 72a-73a. In particular, the failure to retain 


5 


the exact arrangement of a particular periodical is “of only 
peripheral concern” when petitioners retain their single most 
important original contribution: the selection of the individual 
articles to be published. Jd. The commitment of the Times 
editorial staff to “[i]dentifying ‘all the news that’s fit to print,’ ” 
for example, is “a highly subjective undertaking, one that 
different editors and different periodicals undoubtedly perform 
with varying degrees of success.” Id. 69a. That “selection,” in 
turn, “remains evident online” because petitioners uniformly 
include, with each article in the databases, information 
identifying the publication, edition, and even pagination of the 
hard copy edition in which that article was originally published. 
Id. 71a. 


Moreover, as the district court also noted, the basic, 
definitional concepts of the Copyright Act expressly state that 
“revisions,” including the “any revision” incorporated into 
Section 201(c), can embrace substantial changes from the 
original edition of a publication. The Act provides that “editorial 
revisions” to a preexisting work can be so substantial that 
they create a new work with its own copyright protections. 
17 U.S.C. § 101 (providing that “editorial revisions” can create 
new “derivative work’). As the district court succinctly 
explained, “If ‘editorial revisions’ can transform a work to this 
extent, the broader ‘any revisions’ language of Section 201(c) 
Suggests the promise of even greater change.” Pet. App. 58a. 


Fundamental to the district court’s conclusion was its 
recognition that Congress intentionally drafted the terms of the 
Copyright Act to be “media neutral.” Jd. 57a. Section 101 of 
the Act states that a “literary work,” such as a periodical, 
remains the same “regardless of” how it is embodied, whether 
in paper or instead on “tapes, disks, or cards.” 17 U.S.C. § 101; 
see also id. (a“*copy” of a work is the same whatever the medium 
— whether “now known or later developed” — in which it is 
embodied). These provisions are set forth in the very first section 
of the Act, because Congress intentionally sought “to 
accommodate developing technologies,” Pet. App. 57a, 


6 
specifically employing broad terms so “ ‘that they can be 
adapted as time goes by to each of new advancing media.’ ” Jd. 
(quoting Copyright Law Revision: Hearing on H.R. 4347, 4680, 
6831, 6835 Before Subcomm. No. 3 of the House Comm. on 
the Judiciary, 89th Cong., Ist Sess. 57 (1965) (testimony of 
George D. Cary, Deputy Register of Copyrights)). 

The district court also recognized that Congress enacted 
Section 201(c) principally to overturn those decisions under 
the predecessor Copyright Act of 1909 holding that publishers 
could exploit freelance authors’ contributions apart from the 
rest of the collective work. Pet. App. 60a. Electronic copies do 
not implicate that concern because regardless of how such copies 
may be accessed and read, the entire editorial content of the 
collective work is published together. Thus, as the district court 
concluded, Congress intended Section 201(c) to “avoid the 
exploitation of individual articles,” without “prevent[ing] 
publishers from reworking their collective works in significant 
ways.” Id. 72a.? 


Finally, the district court rejected respondents’ claim that 
copies published electronically result in a “new” collective work 
rather than in a permissible “revision” under Section 201(c). 
Under this Court’s decision in Feist Publications v. Rural 
Telephone Service Co., 499 U.S. 340, 359-61 (1991), a “new” 
work is created only if it fails to retain the “original” elements 
— whether “selection, coordination, or arrangement” — of the 
prior work. 499 U.S. 340, 359-61 (1991). When the original 
element of a periodical (the “compilation” copyright 
contemplated by Section 101) is retained in the electronic copy

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


The petition should be granted for two reasons. First, the 
Second Circuit’s judgment, as a practical matter, sets a national 
rule requiring the destruction of decades’ worth of articles 
currently stored in electronic archives. Consistent with this 
Court’s practice of immediately reviewing decisions under the 
Copyright Act that overturn settled practices in publishing — 
an inherently national industry closely tied to the constitutional 
values underlying the First Amendment — review should be 
granted to prevent this irreversible, nationwide harm. 


Second, the Second Circuit’s decision conflicts with 
holdings of this Court and of other circuits in two important 
ways. It espouses the legal principle that a publisher may be 
held liable for direct copyright infringement because of the 
conduct of the third parties who retrieve articles from the 
databases. That holding is contrary to this Court’s decision in 
Sony and Sony’s progeny in other circuits, which hold that third- 
party conduct is irrelevant to a direct infringement claim. The 
Second Circuit’s judgment also holds that electronic publication 
results in a “new” work. That holding is contrary to this Court’s 
decision in Feist that the “mechanical” reproduction of a work 
(as opposed to one adding some element of “originality”) cannot 
create a new, copyrightable work, as well as Feist’s progeny in 
other circuits. In both respects, the Second Circuit’s decision 
also conflicts with this Court’s holding that, in cases of doubt, 
the Copyright Act should be construed to encourage the 
development of new technologies.

I. The Decision Below Sets A National Rule Meriting This 
Court’s Immediate Intervention Because It Will Cause 
Irreparable Harm To Electronic Archives Nationwide. 


One consistent theme emerges from this Court’s leading 
copyright decisions: the Court has taken care to review rulings 
of the courts of appeals that disrupt settled practices in the 
publishing and related mdusines, even absent a direct circuit 
conflict.° The Court’s prompt intervention in such cases is 
sound: the publishing industry is virtually unique in that a

B. The Decision Below Conflicts With Decisions Of 
This Court And Of Other Circuits Holding That 
“Originality” Is An Essential Element Of The

Act. 


The Second Circuit held in this case that the reproduction 
and distribution of electronic copies of periodical issues resulted 
in a new work outside the privilege accorded to publishers to 
create “any revision” of their periodicals. According to the 
Second Circuit, this new work was either “at best a new 
anthology of innumerable editions of * * * the [Publications], 
[or] at worst a new anthoiogy of innumerable articles from these 
editions.” Pet. App. 17a. It is undisputed, however, that the 
differences in the periodicals as fixed in print and as fixed in 
electronic copies are dictated by the different media involved, 
with the “revision” in this case characterized only by the 
omission of graphics, advertisements and agate-type entries.
