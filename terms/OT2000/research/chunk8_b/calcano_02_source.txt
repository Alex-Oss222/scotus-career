QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


The Board of Immigration Appeals found that peti- 
tioners, legal permanent residents of the United States, 
were deportable on the basis of their prior criminal con- 
victions and were barred from applying for a discre- 
tionary waiver of deportation under 8 U.S.C. 1182(c) 
(1994) by a recent amendment to the Immigration and 
Nationality Act (INA). Petitioners filed petitions for 
review in the court of appeals seeking review of the 
Board’s ruling on the ground that the amendment did not 
apply retroactively to their cases and that, if applicable, 
it would violate due process and equal protection. The 
court of appeals consolidated the three cases and dis- 
missed all of petitioners’ claims for lack of jurisdiction 
in light of 8 U.S.C. 1252(a)(2)(C), one of the permanent 
judicial review amendments to the INA enacted by the 
I}legal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsi- 
bility Act (IIRIRA). The court of appeals held, however, 
that petitioners could obtain review of their claims in a 
district court habeas corpus action under 28 U.S.C. 
2241. The questions presented are: 


1. Whether the court of appeals correctly concluded 
that, under 8 U.S.C. 1252(a)(2)(C), it lacked jurisdiction 
on direct petition for review over petitioners’ statutory 
and constitutional challenges to their final removal 
orders, but that the district courts had habeas corpus 
jurisdiction to entertain those challenges under 28 
U.S.C. 2241. 


2. Whether IIRIRA’s amendments to the Immigration 
and Nationality Act violate the Suspension of Habeas 
Corpus Clause, Article III, or the Due Process Clause of 
the Fifth Amendment if they preclude all review of peti- 
tioners’ constitutional or statutory claims?

A. Introduction 


1. This case raises questions concerning the right of 
longtime legal permanent resident immigrants to obtain 
judicial review of a final order of removal in light of 
the permanent jurisdictional amendments to the Immi- 
gration and Nationality Act (INA). Those amendments 
were enacted by the Illegal Immigration Reform and 
Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 (IIRIRA), Pub. L. 
No. 104-208, Div. C., 110 Stat. 3009-546. 


Petitioners assert that IIRIRA’s jurisdictional amend- 
ments do not preclude review of their challenges to their 
final removal orders, and that if they do preclude review 
of their claims, they violate the Suspension of Habeas 
Corpus Clause, Article III, and due process. The gov- 
ernment contends that Congress has dictated that no court 
may hear their challenges because of the nature of the 
claims. The government further contends that the com- 
plete preclusion of review over petitioners’ claims raises 
no constitutional concerns. 


a government's petition in St. Cyr also seeks review of the 
Second Circuit’s merits ruling in that case. Petitioners in this case 
-raised the same or similar statutory and constitutional claims as those 
raised in St. Cyr, but the Second Circuit did not reach the merits in 
this case. Accordingly, this petition addresses only the jurisdictional 
question decided by the Second Circuit.

More specifically, the jurisdictional issue raised in this 
case is whether immigrants with certain criminal con- 
victions may judicially challenge the Board of Immigra- 
tion Appeals’ ruling that they are statutorily ineligible to 
apply for a discretionary waiver of deportation. That issue 
raises the questions (1) whether the proper forum to raise 
this type of challenge is in the court of appeals by means 
of a petition for review or in the district court by means of 
a habeas corpus action, and (2) whether irrespective of the 
forum for review, the scope of jurisdiction is limited 
to reviewing only substantial constitutional claims, so 
that all non-constitutionai errors (including errors of statu- 
tory construction, such as the statutory retroactivity 
claims advanced by petitioners in this case) are wholly 
unreviewable. 


The three petitioners in this case are all lawful perma- 
nent residents of the United States who are subject to 
administratively final orders of removal (formerly called 
orders of deportation). The Board of Immigration Appeals 
(BIA or Board) determined that they were deportable 
based on their past criminal convictions and further held 
that a substantive amendment enacted by IIRIRA rendered 
them statutorily ineligible to apply for a discretionary 
waiver of deportation. Each petitioner sought review by 
filing both a district court habeas action under, inter alia, 
28 U.S.C. 2241, and a petition for review in the Second 
Circuit pursuant to 8 U.S.C. 1252(a)(1), which provides 
a general grant of jurisdiction to the courts of appeals to 
review challenges to final orders of removal by means of 
a petition for review. Petitioners did not contest the BIA’s 
determination that they were deportable in light of their 
prior criminal convictions. Rather, they challenged on sta- 
tutory and constitutional grounds the BIA’s determina- 
tion that they were ineligible to apply for a waiver of 
deportation under former 8 U.S.C. 1182(c), INA § 212(c) 
(1994) (granting Attorney General discretionary author-

B. Statutory Framework 


IIRIRA was enacted on September 30, 1996, and 
revised both the substantive and jurisdictional provisions 
of the INA. The jurisdictional amendments of IIRIRA that 
are at issue here are the permanent provisions codified at 
8 U.S.C. 1252 et seq. They apply to all cases in which the 
administrative immigration “removal” proceedings com- 
menced on or after April 1, 1997. See IIRIRA § 309(a), 
110 Stat. 3009-625. 


The jurisdictional issue presented by this case initially 
arose, however, under two prior sets of amendments to the 
INA enacted by Congress in 1996. The first set was 
enacted on April 24, 1996, as part of the Antiterrorism 
and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996 (AEDPA), Pub. 
L. No. 104-132, 110 Stat. 1214. The second was the tran- 
sitional rules enacted by IIRIRA. See IIRIRA § 309(c), 
110 Stat. 3009-626. Although these prior jurisdictional 
amendments are not directly at issue here, the decisions 
from the courts of appeals construing the prior amend- 
ments have had a significant impact on the manner in 
which the circuits have interpreted the permanent juris- 
dictional amendments. 


1. AEDPA and IIRIRA’s Transitional 
Amendments 


Historically, deportation orders were reviewable in dis- 
trict court, primarily through habeas corpus, and for a 
brief period, through both habeas and APA declaratory 


9 


actions. See Shaughnessy v. Pedreiro, 349 U.S. 48, 
52 (1955). In 1961, Congress amended the INA and 
created a new judicial review scheme, formerly codified 
at 8 U.S.C. 1105a et seq. (1994), INA § 106 et seq. 


The 1961 amendments gave the courts of appeals “sole 
and exclusive” jurisdiction to review final deportation 
orders by means of a petition for review. See former 
8 U.S.C. 1105a(a) (1994), INA § 106(a); Foti v. INS, 
375 U.S. 217, 221 (1963). The 1961 amendments also 
contained a provision that expressly stated that habeas 
review of deportation orders remained available, 8 U.S.C. 
1105a(a)(10), INA § 106(a)(10), although there was con- 
fusion regarding precisely when aliens could invoke such 
jurisdiction given that the 1961 amendments simultane- 
ously vested the courts of appeals with “sole and exclu- 
sive” jurisdiction over final deportation orders. See, e.g., 
Nakaranurack v. INS, 68 F.3d 290, 293 (9th Cir. 1995); 
United States ex rel. Marcello v. INS, 634 F.2d. 964, 969 
(5th Cir. 1981). 


AEDPA was enacted on April 24, 1996, and contained 
two relevant changes to the judicial review scheme cre- 
ated in 1961.° First, in Subtitle A of Title IV, AEDPA 
§ 401(e) repealed the habeas provision in 8 U.S.C. 
1105a(a)(10). 110 Stat. 1268. Second, in Subtitle B, 
AEDPA § 440(a) amended former 8 U.S.C. 1105a(a)(10) 
to provide that a final order of deportation entered against 
immigrants deportable on the basis of certain enumerated 
criminal offenses “shall not be subject to review by any 
court.” 110 Stat. 1276-1277. AEDPA’s amendments did 
not mention 28 U.S.C. 2241. 


3 The courts of appeals held thet AEDPA’s jurisdictional provi- 


sions went into effect immediately and applied to all cases pending on the 
date of enactment. See, e.g., Hincapie-Nieto v. INS, 92 F.3d 27 (2d Cir. 
1996). 


10 


IIRIRA became law four months later and enacted addi- 
tional changes to judicial review, including both transi- 
tional and permanent jurisdictional rules. The transitional 
rules applied to immigration proceedings commenced 
before April 1, 1997, in which the deportation order 
became administratively final on or after October 31, 
1996. IIRIRA §§ 309(a), (c)(1), (c)(4). Like AEDPA, 
IIRIRA’s transitional amendments contained a provision 
that restricted review in cases involving aliens deportable 
on the basis of certain criminal offenses. That provision, 
IIRIRA § 309(c)(4)(G), provided that “there shall be no 
appeal permitted in the case of an alien who is inadmis- 
sible or deportable by reason of having committed a crim- 
inal offense covered in [enumerated INA sections].” 110 
Stat. 3009-626. ITRIRA’s transitional rules, like AEDPA’s 
amendments, did not mention Section 2241 habeas juris- 
diction. 


The jurisdictional amendments enacted by AEDPA and 
IIRIRA’s transitional rules resulted in a 10-1 division in 
the courts of appeals on the question of whether aliens 
who are deportable on the basis of a criminal offense enu- 
merated in AEDPA § 440(a) or JIRIRA § 309(c)(4)(G) 
could obtain review of the BIA’s determination that they 
are statutorily ineligible to apply for a waiver of depor- 
tation (the question at issue here under IIRIRA’s perma-

2. IIRIRA’s Permanent Jurisdictional 
Amendments 


The permanent IIRIRA amendments repealed the prior 
judicial review scheme (i.e., the 1961 scheme as amended 
by AEDPA and IIRIRA’s transitional rules) and substi- 
tuted a new statute, codified at 8 U.S.C. 1252 et seq. 
Although the relevant permanent amendments are worded 
differently, the basic structure of the new provisions is the 
same. The permanent jurisdictional rules enacted by 
IIRIRA contain provisions vesting the courts of appeals 
with sole jurisdiction to review removal orders by petition 
for review (see 8 U.S.C. 1252(a)(1); 1252(b)(9)) and, as 
noted, include a provision restricting review for aliens 
removable on the basis of specified criminal offenses. 
codified at 8 U.S.C. 1252(a)(2)(C). Like the prior sets of 
amendments, the permanent IIRIRA amendments do not 
mention Section 2241 habeas jurisdiction. 


As with the prior amendments, IIRIRA’s permanent 
jurisdictional amendments have resulted in litigation 
regarding the availability of judicial review over chal- 
lenges to the denial of a waiver of deportation. Four cir- 
cuits, including the court below, have held that aliens may 
not challenge the denial of a waiver in the courts of 
appeals by means of a petition for review, but may bring 
both constitutional and statutory challenges to the denial 
of a waiver in a Section 2241 habeas action in district 
court. See Pet. App. la (opinion below); Mahadeo vy. 
Reno, 226 F.3d 3, 13 (1st Cir. 2000), petition for certio- 
rari pending, No. 00-962; Liang v. INS, 206 F.3d 308. 
317-23 (3d Cir. 2000), petition for certiorari pending sub 
nom. Rodriguez v. INS, No. 00-753; Flores-Miramontes v. 
INS, 212 F.3d 1133, 1137 (9th Cir. 2000); Richards-Diaz 
v. Fasano, No. 99-56530, 2000 WL 1715956 (9th Cir. 
Nov. 17, 2000).

Three Circuits (the Fifth, Seventh and Eleventh) have 
stated that there is generally no longer any habeas juris- 
diction over final orders and that any review that remains 
for aliens removable on the basis of a criminal conviction 
enumerated in Section 1252(a)(2)(C) must be sought in 
the court of appeals by a petition for review. These courts 
have acknowledged that the permanent rules, like the 
prior amendments, nowhere mention Section 2241, but 
have concluded that the language and overall structure of 
the permanent amendments are sufficient to repeal habeas 
jurisdiction. Max-George v. Reno, 205 F.3d 194, 198 (5th 
Cir. 2000) (challenge to threshold finding of deportabil-

D. Decision of the Court of Appeals 


All three petitioners filed petitions for review in the 
court of appeals seeking review of the BIA’s ruling on two 
main grounds. The first is a statutory claim: that IIRIRA’s 
repeal of the INA’s former Section 1182(c) waiver pro- 
vision does not apply retroactively to cases where the 
relevant criminal conduct triggering deportation occurred 
before passage of the 1996 amendments. The second is a 
constitutional claim: that the retroactive repeal of Section 
1182(c) relief under the circumstances here would be arbi- 
trary and irrational in violation of due process and equal 
protection. Because of the jurisdictional controversy sur- 
rounding the 1996 amendments, all three petitioners also 
filed district court habeas corpus actions raising the same 
Statutory and constitutional claims, which the district 
courts have declined to adjudicate pending a final dispo- 
sition on the petitions for review. 


The court of appeals consolidated the three petitions for 
review for purposes of argument and ruling and issued a 
consolidated decision on September 1, 2000. In light of 
the jurisdictional bar in 8 U.S.C. 1252(a)(2)(C), the court 
of appeals held that it could not entertain petitioners’ con- 
stitutional or statutory claims challenging the denial of a 
Section 1182(c) waiver because the jurisdictional bar in 
Section 1252(a)(2)(C) repealed the court’s petition-for- 
review jurisdiction over such challenges. The court stated 
that it was unnecessary for it to create exceptions to the 
jurisdictional bar because the permanent rules left open 
district court habeas review under 28 U.S.C. 2241 as an 
adequate alternative avenue of review. By finding that 
habeas jurisdiction remained available to challenge the 
denial of Section 1182(c) relief, the court avoided the 


18 


“profound constitutional questions” that would have been 
triggered by a repeal of habeas corpus in the absence of 
some other avenue of review over petitioners’ constitu- 
tional and statutory claims. Pet. App. 31a. 


The court of appeals’ conclusion that Section 2241 
habeas review remained available was based on its prior 
decisions interpreting the jurisdictional provisions of 
AEDPA and IIRIRA’s transitional rules. The court of 
appeals stated that under those decisions Section 2241 
could only be repealed by an express directive. Noting 
that none of the permanent provisions mentioned Section 
2241, the Court held that the amendments did not repeal 
that grant of jurisdiction. Pet. App. 22a-23a. The court of 
appeals thus held that Section 2241 jurisdiction remains 
available and instructed petitioners that they could pursue 
their claims by means of their previously-filed habeas 
actions.’ ;

ernment’s jurisdictional contentions are identical in 
St. Cyr and this case, and that the alien in St. Cyr did not 
file a petition for review, this case is the better vehicle for 
the Court to address the Second Circuit’s interpretation of 
ITRIRA’s jurisdictional amendments (assuming the Court 
concludes that the issue warrants its attention in the first 
place). If, however, the Court were to decide to grant 
review on the jurisdictional question in St. Cyr, peti- 
tioners respectfully request that the Court also grant this 
petition.
