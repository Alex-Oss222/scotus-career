STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This case presents the question whether a public school 
district may refuse to provide on-site special education 
services to a child with disabilities solely on the basis of his 
_ attendance at a religious rather than a non-religious private 
school. 


1. Petitioner Kai Moore is a 14-year-old boy, who lives 
with his parents, William and Gwen Moore, in an area of 
rural Oregon served by the Reedsport School District. 
Department of Public Instruction Order (hereinafter “DPI 
Order”), Finding of Fact No. | (hereinafter “FF”) (App. D at 
37a). Kai is blind and suffers from cerebral palsy, but 
otherwise possesses full cognitive abilities. Kai’s disabilities 
identify him as a “child with disabilities” under the 
_ Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), 20 
U.S.C. §§ 1400 et seg. DPI Order, FF 2 (App. D at 37a). By 
law, he is eligible for a broad range of special education 
benefits, to be determined by his public school district. DPI 
Order, FF 3 and 5 (App. D at 37a). 


Since 1993, Kai has been enrolled in Harbor Baptist 
Church School, a school that teaches the religious values and 
ideals the Moores wish Kai to learn as part of his education 
and upbringing. DPI Order, FF 6 (App. D at 38a). Under the 
IDEA, as administered by the State of Oregon, Kai is entitled 
to receive special education services as provided by his public 
school district, even though he attends private school. DPI 
Order, FF 3 (App. D at 37a). See also OR. ADMIN. R. 581- 
015-0166(2) (1995) (App. F at 53a). Federal law permits, 
but does not require, IDEA services to be provided on 
the premises of a private school. See 20 U.S.C. 
§ 1412(a)(10)(A)(i)CID (1998) (App. E at 50a). 

The Reedsport School District’s policy is to provide special 
education services, whenever possible, in the child’s regular 


school setting, including on the premises of a child’s private 
school. DPI Order, FF 3, 4, 5 and 8 (App. D at 37a, 38a). 


5 


However, an Oregon administrative rule forbids the provision 
of special education services at a private school if the private 
school is a religious school. Or. ADMIN. R. 581-015-0166(3) 
(1995) (App. F at 53a). As a consequence, the policy and 
practice of the Reedsport School District is to provide on-site 
special education services to children with disabilities 
attending public schools and non-religious private schools, 
but withhold such services from children with disabilities 
attending religious private schools. See KDM ex rel. v. 
Reedsport Sch. Dist., No. 96-6075-HO (D. Or. filed January 
5, 1998) (App. C at 33a).' 


The parties stipulated that if Kai attended either a public 
school or a non-religious private school, his special education 
services would be provided on-site. It is only because Kai 
attends a religious private school that respondent school 
district will not provide services on-site. DPI Order, FF 8 
(App. D at 38a). As the court below recognized, the 
operation of the Oregon administrative rule “ ‘discriminates’ 
against students in religious schools, i.e., treats them 
differently by denying them state services on the school 
grounds” of a private religious school, but not on the grounds 
of a private non-religious school. KDM y. Reedsport Sch. 
Dist., 196 F.3d 1046, 1051 (1999) (App. B at 1 1a). 


2. The Moore family first sought relief in an 
administrative hearing. The hearing officer found as facts, 


Under IDEA, children with disabilities are to be provided special 
services in the “[l]east restrictive environment.” 20 U.S.C. § 1412(a)(5) 
(App. E at 49a-Sla). Removal from the child’s “regular educational 
environment” is discouraged under the IDEA. 20 U.S.C. § 1412(a)(5)(A) 
(App. E at 49a-50a). See Board of Educ. of Kiryas Joel v. Grumet, 512 
U.S. 687, 717 (1994) (O'Connor, J., concurring in part and concurring in 
the judgment) (“If the government provides this education on-site at - 
public schools and at nonsectarian private schools,it is only fair that it 
provide it on-site at sectarian schools as well.”).

consistent with the parties’ stipulation, that Kai is legally 
blind and has cerebral palsy, is entitled to receive special 
education assistance on the basis of his disability, that the 
Moores enrolled Kai in Harbor Baptist Church School “based 
on their sincerely-held religious conviction and beliefs,” and 
that “[Kai] is not being provided the services of a vision 
specialist in his regular classroom at the Harbor Baptist 
Church School by the District because of the religious nature 
of the school. This is the sole issue in the dispute.” DPI 
Order, FF 2-6 and 8 (emphasis added) (App. D at 38a). 


The hearing officer noted that the services “are provided at 
a nearby fire hall because Oregon law prohibits the services 
from being provided in the school.” DPI Order at 6 (App. D. 
at 42a). See also OR. ADMIN. R. 581-015-0166(3) (1995) 
(App. F at 53a). The hearing officer concluded that the denial 
of on-site services to Kai was both appropriate and required. 
See DPI Order (App. D at 46a). 


3. Having exhausted administrative remedies, the Moores 
brought this action in the United States District Court for the 
District of Oregon. Following a bench trial on stipulated 
facts, the district court entered judgment holding that the 
IDEA did not require the District to provide services at a 
private school, but that the Oregon Administrative Rule 
which permits services to be provided to private school 
students only in a non-religious setting violates the Free 
Exercise, Establishment, and Equal Protection Clauses of the 
United States Constitution. 


The district court held that after the District made a 
decision to provide on-site disability services in private 
schools, the State could not then deny them to a student 
merely because he attended a private religious school. The 
district court held that such a denial imposes a burden on Kai 
and his parents’ “free exercise rights by forcing them to 
choose between enrolling at Harbor Baptist and receiving 
special. education at the fire hall or enrolling at a non-

religious school and receiving in-class services.” KDM ex 
rel. v. Reedsport Sch. Dist., No. 96-6075-HO, slip op at 8. (D. 
Or. filed January 5, 1998) (App. C at 32a). 


The district court concluded that Oregon’s Rule violates 
the Equal Protection Clause because it lacks either a 
compelling justification or a rational basis and “has the effect 
of allowing in-class services to disabled students at non- 
religious schools, both public and private” while prohibiting 
in-class services to disabled students at religious schools, 
DPI Order at 6 (App. C at 33a). The court then enjoined the 
District “from following a policy of providing special 
education and related services to disabled students at religious 
schools on different terms than provided to disabled students 
at non-religious schools.” DPI Order at 6 (App. C at 33a). 


4. A panel of the United States Court of Appeals for the 
Ninth Circuit reversed the district court by a divided vote (2- 
1), Judge Kleinfeld dissenting. KDM ex rel. vy. Reedsport 
Sch. Dist., 196 F.3d 1046 (9th Cir. 1999) (App. B. at 4a-26a). 
The majority acknowledged that the “regulation 
‘discriminates’ against students in religious schools, i.e.. 
treats them differently by denying them state services on the 
school grounds.” /d. at 1051 (App. B at Ila). Nonetheless. 
the panel majority concluded, “Oregon’s regulation as applied 
to KDM and his parents does not impose an impermissible 
burden on their free exercise of religion.” /d. (App. B at I 1a). 
Kai could still receive special education services “at the fire 
hall down the street.” /d. (App. B at 10a). The panel held 
that official discrimination on the basis of religion in a 
government program does not itself violate the Free Exercise 
Clause in the absence of “evidence of a ‘substantial animus - . 
. . that motivated the law in question.’ ” /d. at 1051 (quoting 
Strout v. Albanese, 178 F.3d 57 (Ast Cir. 1999), cert. denied, 
120 S.Ct. 329 (1999) (App. B. at Ila). The panel believed 
that “the Oregon regulation is not ‘neutral’ ” with respect to 
religion. KDM, 196 F.3d at 1050 (App. B. at 10a). 


8 


Nonetheless, the panel concluded the Free Exercise Clause 
requires a further showing that even a facially discriminatory 
statute has the object or “purpose” of suppression of “religion 
. or religious conduct.” Jd. at 1051 (quoting Church of the 
Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah, 508 U.S. 520 
(1993)) (App. B at Ila). The panel concluded that “[t]he 
mere fact that the District makes its service to KDM available 
in the fire hall down the street from his school does not 
amount to suppression of religion or religious conduct.” 
KDM, 196 F.3d at 1051 (App. B at 10a). 


The panel stated that Peter v. Wedl, 155 F.3d 992 (8th Cir. 
1998), which reached the opposite result on similar facts, was 
distinguishable. The panel did not suggest that the result in 
Peter was based on a showing of religious animus, however. 
Rather, the panel thought the case distinguishable on the 
ground that the plaintiff in Peter “was forced to choose” 


between attending religious school and “receiving services. 


essential to his ability to attend school.” KDM, 196 F.3d at 
1051 (App. B at 9a-10a). The panel did not, however, dispute 
findings of the hearing officer that KDM could not receive 
on-site disability services at his religious school. 


The panel also rejected the Equal Protection Clause claim, 
on the ground that “parochial school students are not a 
suspect class.” Jd. at 1052 (App. B at 13a). The panel 
concluded that the State of Oregon’s interpretation of its state 
constitution was a_ sufficiently “rational basis” for 
discriminating against a child with disabilities in provision of 
special education benefits on the basis of the child’s 
attendance at a religious school. /d. (App. B. at 13a). 


Judge Kleinfeld vigorously dissented, citing conflicting 
rulings by this Court and by three other circuits. Judge 
Kleinfeld wrote: 


Under today’s decision the government may dis- 
criminate against people based on their exercise of

religion, so long as the discriminatory burdens it 
imposes are not more substantial than requiring a blind 
child with cerebral palsy to leave his school building and 
go down the street to a fire hall. That is quite a surprise. 
Fortunately, that is not the law in most of the rest of the 
country. The Supreme Court and the Third, Sixth, and 
Eighth Circuits have all held to the contrary—the Eighth 
Circuit in a case materially identical to this one. 


KDM, 196 F.3d at 1052 (Kleinfeld, J., dissenting) (footnotes 
omitted) (citing, inter alia, Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, 
Inc. v. City of Hialeah, 508 U.S. 520 (1993); Brown v. 
Borough of Mahaffey, 35 F.3d 846 (3rd Cir. 1994); Hartmann 
v. Stone, 68 F.3d 973 (6th Cir. 1995); and Peter v. Wedl, 155 
F.3d 992 (8th Cir. 1998)) (App. B. at 15a). 


Judge Kleinfeld identified the error in the panel’s Free 
Exercise Clause analysis as its reliance on the test used when 
laws are neutral with respect to religion, rather than the test 
used when a law or rule is religiously discriminatory on its 
face. See KDM, 196 F.3d at 1053 (Kleinfeld, J., dissenting) 
(App. B at 16a). Judge Kleinfeld noted that, by the majority’s 
own admission, this was a case of the latter type. “When the 
government, by a law not neutral on its face, treats people of 
one or all religions better or worse than others, the 
constitutional question is traditionally formulated so that the 
answer has to be ‘No!’” /d. (Kleinfeld, J., dissenting) (App. 
B at 16a). In the instant case, “[hJandicapped children at 
secular private schools get special education in their schools, 
but handicapped children at religious private schools must 
leave school to get the same education. This law violates the 
Constitution because it distinguishes between people and 
burdens some of them on account of their religious practices.” 
Id. (Kleinfeld, J., dissenting) (App. B at 17a). 


Judge Kleinfeld noted that the majority’s opinion “puts us 
at odds with three other circuits to have considered analogous 
issues.” /d. (Kleinfeld, J., dissenting) (App. B. at 17a). In 


10 


particular, he emphasized that the Eighth Circuit’s decision in 
“Peter v. Wedl is materially identical to the case we decide 
today, yet the Eighth Circuit reache[d] the opposite 
conclusion.” /d. at 1054 (Kleinfeld, J., dissenting) (App. B at 
19a). The Eighth Circuit held that “[glovernment dis- 
crimination based on religion violates the Free Exercise 
Clause of the First Amendment.” /d. (Kleinfeld, J., 
dissenting) (quoting Peter, 155 F.3d at 995, where the Eighth 
Circuit struck down a Minnesota regulation essentially 
identical to the Oregon regulation as a violation of the Free 
Exercise, Equal Protection, and Free Speech Clauses) (App. 
B at 19a). Judge Kleinfeld found that “[t]he majority’s 
attempt to distinguish Peter makes no sense because, there, as 
here, the regulation allowed special education services only at 
a religiously ‘neutral site.’” /d. at 1055 (Kleinfeld, J., 
dissenting) (citation omitted) (App. B at 21a). “That case and 
this one are, for analytic purposes, carbon copies.” /d. 
(Kieinfeld, J., dissenting) (App. B at 21a).

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


I. The Decision Below Conflicts Directly with the 
Eighth Circuit’s Decision in Peter v. Wedl, 155 F.3d 
992 (8th Cir. 1998). 


The Ninth Circuit conceded that the regulation at issue 
below “ ‘discriminates’ against students in religious schools, 
i.e., treats them differently by denying them state services on 
the school grounds.” KDM, 196 F.2d 1046, 1051 (9th Cir. 
1999) (App. B at Ila). This, then, is a case about admitted 
discrimination—discrimination against “religious ideas, 
religious people, [and] religious schools.” Board of Educ. of 
Kiryas Joel v. Grumet, 512 U.S. 687, 717 (1994) (O’Connor, 
J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment) (“The 
Religion Clauses prohibit the government from favoring 
religion, but they provide no warrant for discriminating 
against religion.”). Oregon provides an immensely valuable 
benefit—special education services—to every eligible “child 
with disabilities.” Petitioner has been denied this educational 
benefit solely because his parents have chosen a religious 
school whose purpose it is to impart religious tenets of faith 
and values to its students by means of religious instruction in 
a religious environment and setting. Respondent’s 
Administrative Rule 581-015-0166 violates the “equal 
treatment” principle at the heart of the Free Exercise and 
Equal Protection Clauses of the First and Fourteenth 
Amendments to the United States Constitution and conflicts 
with the Eighth Circuit’s decision in Peter v. Wedl, 155 F.3d 
992 (8th Cir. 1998). 


12 


In Peter, two families with disabled children challenged 
their school districts’ refusal to provide on-site special 
education services at the families’ chosen religious schools. 
The school districts relied in part on a Minnesota State Rule 
that required districts to provide on-premises services to 
children who needed them at a public school or at a private 
non-religious school, but forbade provision of such services 
at a private religious school. Minnesota’s distinction tracked 
exactly the line drawn by Oregon’s Rule and the Ninth 
Circuit in this case. The Minnesota Rule, in the Eighth 
Circuit’s words, “explicitly discriminated against children 
who attended private religious schools.” Peter, 155 F.3d at 
996. 


The Eighth Circuit concluded that such “[glovernment 
discrimination based on religion violates the Free Exercise 
Clause of the First Amendment, . . . the Free Speech Clause 
of the First Amendment, . . . and the Equal Protection Clause 
of the Fourteenth Amendment.” /d. at 996 (citing Church of 
the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah, 508 U.S. 520, 
532 (1993); Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of University 
of Virginia, 515 U.S. 819, 830 (1995); and Romer v. Evans, 
517 U.S. 620, 633 (1996)). 


The court in Peter held that Establishment Clause 
principles could not justify the discriminatory exclusion from 
an education benefit program. See Peter, 155 F.3d at 997. 
Minnesota’s Rule, therefore, “violated the plaintiffs’ rights to 
free exercise of religion, free speech, and equal protection, 
and the district court properly enjoined its enforcement.” Jd. 
The Eighth Circuit noted that one school district’s stated 
policy of religious neutrality appeared to be a “mere pretext 
for religious discrimination.” Jd. On remand, the district 
court granted full relief to the families. Westendorp v. ISD 
No. 273, 35 F. Supp. 2d 1134 (D. Minn. 1998). 


The conflict between Peter and the decision of the Ninth 
Circuit below against KDM is stark, Both cases involve on-

students attending private schools. Both cases involve 
governmental discrimination by withholding benefits from 
those disabled students who attend religious private schools, 
while providing the benefits to those students who attend 
non-religious private schools. 


The Ninth Circuit held that it is permissible to discriminate 
on the basis of religion. The Eighth Circuit, on nearly

Free Exercise Clause and the Equal Protection Clause. The 
Ninth Circuit said religious discrimination is permissible as

In Peter, the Eighth Circuit found such discrimination

III. The Decision Below Conflicts with This Court’s 
Precedents Holding That the First Amendment 
Forbids Discriminatory Exclusion from a 
Government Program, Benefit, or Forum on the 
Basis of Religious Belief, Exercise, Affiliation, or 
Expression. | 


It is the bedrock principle of this Court’s First Amendment 
jurisprudence that the “government may not . . . impose 
special disabilities on the basis of religious views or religious 
status.” Employment Div. of Oregon v. Smith, 494 U.S. 872, 
877 (1990); see also, e.g., McDaniel v. Paty, 435 US. 
618, 639 (1978) (Brennan, J., concurring in the judgment) 
(“government may not use religion as a_ basis for 
classification for the imposition of duties, penalties, 
privileges or benefits”). If the Free Exercise Clause means 
anything at all, it means that government may not 
“discriminate[] against some or all religious beliefs or 
regulate[] or prohibit{] conduct because it is undertaken for 
religious reasons.” Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. 
City of Hialeah, 508 U.S. 520, 532 (1993). As this Court 
said, long ago in Everson, this non-discrimination rule is no 
less fundamental than the Establishment Clause’s command 
that the government not- require or subsidize religious 
observance: 


{The First] Amendment requires the state to be a neutral 
in its relations with groups of religious believers and 
non-believers; it does not require the state to be their 


st a 


17 


adversary. State power is no more to be used so as to 
handicap religions than it is to favor them. 


Everson v. Board of Educ., 330 U.S. 1, 18 (1947). 


Religious exercise and expression, including choosing to 
educate one’s child in a religious tradition, has long been 
valued, not feared, in our constitutional tradition. Directing 
and supervising a child’s education, especially a disabled 
child, is perhaps the most important and personal 
constitutionally protected activity most people ever exercise. 
Penalizing and stigmatizing that precious activity is contrary 
to the principles that undergird the First and Fourteenth 
Amendments. 


A. The Decision Below Conflicts with This Court’s 
Decision in Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, 
Inc. v. City of Hialeah, 508 U.S. 520 (1993). 


In Lukumi, this Court invalidated city ordinances that 
prohibited religiously motivated animal sacrifice by adherents 
of the Santeria religion. The Court said that “[a]t a minimum, 
the protections of the Free Exercise Clause pertain if the law 
at issue discriminates against some or all religious beliefs or 
regulates or prohibits conduct because it is undertaken for 
religious reasons.” Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc., 
508 U.S. 520, 532 (1993); accord Employment Div. v. Smith, 
494 U.S. 872, 877 (1990) (“government may not . . . impose 
special disabilities on the basis of religious views or religious 
status’’). 


Contrary to the requirement imposed by the court below, | 
Lukumi does not require a litigant who challenges a facially 
discriminatory regulation to prove a “substantial animus” 
motivation for the government. See KDM, 196 F.3d at 1051. 
(App. B at 11a). Nor does it require a litigant to show that a 
facially discriminatory law substantially burdens his religion 
as does the court below. See id. (App. B at 11a) In Lukumi,

this Court expressly reaffirmed its intention that laws which 
are “not neutral or not of general application must undergo 
the most rigorous of scrutiny, . . . must advance ‘interests of

those interests.” Lukumi, 508 U.S. at 546 (quoting McDaniel 
v. Paty, 435 U.S. 618, 628 (1978). Yet because the Ninth 
Circuit could find no impermissible burden on KDM’s or his 
parents’ free exercise of religion in a state regulation that 
facially discriminates against them, the Ninth Circuit saw no 
need to apply strict scrutiny and judged Oregon’s Rule “under 
the rational basis test.” KDM, 196 F.3d at 1051-52 (App. B at 
11a). 


It is rare indeed that facially discriminatory laws could 
survive strict scrutiny. At a minimum, the free exercise of

Employment Div. v. Smith, 494 U.S. at 877. The Oregon

The First Amendment does not permit such religious 
discrimination absent a compelling interest, which is not

In this case, no less than in Lukumi, the government action

B. The Decision Below Conflicts with This Court’s 
Decision in McDaniel v. Paty, 435 U.S. 618 (1978). 


McDaniel presented the question whether a Tennessee 
Statute barring “Minister[s] of the Gospel, or priest[s] of atiy 
denomination whatever” from serving as delegates to the 
State’s constitutional convention violated the Free Exercise 
Clause. McDaniel, 435 U.S. at 620. This Court noted that 
Tennessee extended to its adult citizens generally the right to 
seek and hold office as delegates, and observed that by 
denying that right to clergy “the State ha[d] conditioned the 
exercise of one [right] on the surrender of the other.” Jd. at 
626. The Court stated emphatically: “In so doing, Tennessee 
has encroached upon McDaniel’s right to the free exercise of 
religion.” Jd. 


Justice Brennan’s concurrence drove home the clash 
between this case and McDaniel even more forcefully. He 
highlighted the fact that the Tennessee disqualification 
“imposes a unique disability upon those who exhibit a 
defined level of intensity of involvement in protected 
religious activity.” Jd. at 632 (Brennan, J., concurring). He 
noted that the Tennessee statute “requires appellant’s 
disqualification solely because he is a minister of a religious 
faith. If appellant were to renounce his ministry, presumably 
he could regain eligibility for elective office, but if he does 
not, he must forgo an opportunity for political participation he 
otherwise would enjoy.” Jd. at 634 (Brennan, J., concurring). 
After emphasizing the neutrality principle that is at the heart 
of the First Amendment’s Religion Clauses, Justice Brennan 
insisted that “government may not use religion as a basis of 
classification for the imposition of duties, penalties, 
privileges or benefits.” /d. at 639 (Brennan, J., concurring).

The decision below is inconsistent with this Court’s 
reasoning and holding in McDaniel and with the equality 
principles expounded in Justice Brennan’s concurrence. 
Oregon has done precisely what Justice Brennan observed 
government may not do. Having decided to provide special 
education services to private school students, it has “use[d] 
religion as a basis of classification” in the administration of 
that program and “impose[d]-a unique disability” on 
otherwise eligible beneficiaries who choose religious schools. 
Id. at 632, 639. Even worse, Oregon has, like Tennessee did 
in McDaniel, communicated to this blind boy with cerebral 
palsy and his parents that the price of enjoying a benefit 
generally available to a disabled student under Oregon law, 
on-site special education services, is giving up the right to an 
uninterrupted, unstigmatized education at his own school with 
his own classmates.

confusion as to whether special education services may be 
provided on-site to parochial school students without running 
afoul of the Establishment Clause.
