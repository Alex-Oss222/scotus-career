## Event and chronology

**Bartnicki v. Vopper / United States v. Vopper**, Nos. 99-1687 and 99-1728, 532 U.S. 514. Merits decision, May 21, 2001. Argued December 5, 2000. On writs of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit.

Both petitions concern the same judgment. The question is whether the First Amendment permits civil damages for disclosure of an accurate recording about a matter of public concern when an unknown third party intercepted the conversation unlawfully, the defendants neither participated in that interception nor obtained the recording unlawfully, and they knew or had reason to know of the interception's illegality.

The recording concerned contentious public-school collective bargaining. Union negotiators discussed the dispute and pressure on school-board members, including language suggesting physical harm. Jack Yocum said he found the recording in his mailbox, played it for board members and delivered it to broadcaster Frederick Vopper. Vopper broadcast it in reporting about the settlement, and other media disclosed its contents. The accuracy of the recording is undisputed.

The District Court denied cross-motions for summary judgment. It rejected an interceptor-only statutory construction and the First Amendment defense, while identifying a factual dispute over intentional interception. On a certified interlocutory appeal under 28 U.S.C. §1292(b), the Third Circuit held the federal and Pennsylvania disclosure provisions unconstitutional as applied and directed summary judgment for respondents. The United States intervened to defend the federal enactment. Petitioners and the United States seek reversal; respondents seek affirmance.

The lawful review channel is certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) from the Third Circuit's certified interlocutory review under §1292(b). The United States' statutory-defense intervention does not create a separate damages action. ## Holdings

The two holdings below are adopted by the Opinion of the Court, **Kennedy**, with the complete seven-Justice coalition identified in Opinion Topology. They control by direct majority agreement; no narrower-rationale inference is required.

### The disclosure provisions reach noninterceptors, but their neutrality does not end constitutional review

**Holding and operative rule:** For these claims, 18 U.S.C. §2511(1)(c) reaches intentional disclosure or attempted disclosure of a communication's contents by a person who knows or has reason to know that the information came from an interception violating the subsection; neither that provision nor the parallel Pennsylvania disclosure provision is confined to the interceptor. Their selection by unlawful source rather than message is content neutral, but their direct burden on communicating information requires constitutional examination even when enforced through civil damages against a nonmedia speaker.

**Authority:** The complete seven-Justice Opinion of the Court adopts the statutory construction, classification and requirement of constitutional review.

**Controlling explanation:** The disclosure prohibition addresses the conduct and knowledge of a discloser. It does not require that the same person also made the interception. Constitutional avoidance therefore cannot replace the enacted coverage with an interceptor-only rule. The certified constitutional question proceeds on assumed intentional illegality and knowledge or reason to know; lawful receipt is consistent with the statute's separate prohibition on later disclosure.

Turner requires attention to what a restriction selects and why. These provisions select an unlawful interception as the source, not a viewpoint or bargaining position. Yet disclosure communicates the recorded words, and liability burdens that expression directly. Neither neutrality nor the use of damages removes the First Amendment question.

Cohen and Branzburg reject a categorical press exemption from valid general law; they do not make every direct restriction on publication valid. Yocum's communication to board members and delivery to a broadcaster are expression even though he is an intermediary rather than professional media. The Court therefore examines the justification for all the challenged disclosures under the same relevant constitutional principles.

**Precedent treatment:** Turner supplies source-sensitive classification and substantive review; Cohen and Branzburg retain their rejection of special press immunity without dispensing with review of this publication burden.

### The First Amendment defeats these disclosure claims on the certified premises and public-bargaining record

**Holding and operative rule:** The First Amendment bars the requested federal and Pennsylvania disclosure damages against these respondents, who lawfully received an accurate recording without participating in its unlawful interception and disclosed this conversation concerning the contentious public-school bargaining controversy and apparent physical pressure against its participants. Their knowledge or reason to know that a stranger intercepted the conversation unlawfully does not defeat that defense on these premises; the rule protects Yocum's communications as well as the media disclosures and requires affirmance of the direction to enter summary judgment.

**Authority:** The complete seven-Justice Opinion of the Court adopts this bounded rule and its application, including both the deterrence analysis and the independent conversational-privacy analysis.

**Controlling explanation:** Daily Mail, Florida Star and Landmark protect truthful information of public significance lawfully obtained by its speaker. They left open the unlawful-source problem. The Court extends their protection to this application, where recipients lawfully obtained the recording and took no part in interception.

Deterring unlawful interception is legitimate. Turner nevertheless requires examination of the actual expressive burden and adequate fit while respecting supported legislative predictions. The ordinary target is the person responsible for interception. General incentive reasoning does not sufficiently justify suppressing these nonparticipants' disclosures to discourage an unknown stranger. This conclusion neither requires an empirical study in every case nor makes deterrence irrelevant whenever a recipient is innocent of interception.

Dissemination also causes a distinct privacy injury. Protecting conversation encourages candid private speech, and that interest remains substantial even when later liability adds little deterrence. But the actual information here bears directly on public-school negotiations and apparent pressure involving physical harm against board members. The participants directed that public dispute; the public has a strong interest in their actual words. Settlement does not erase that significance. The Court need not find a punishable threat or a continuing danger at publication to recognize this public interest. In these circumstances, the asserted privacy protection does not justify the burden imposed on the disclosures.

Rice preserves responsibility for purposeful, materially effective assistance to crime, while distinguishing awareness and ordinary publication. Knowing the completed interception's unlawful origin does not establish that assistance. The rule neither excuses independently unlawful acquisition nor turns all information concerning a public participant into public property. Sheets, Denius and Sterling protect confined intimate interests against unjustified official dissemination, compulsory access or coercive surrender; they do not decide these private recipients' disclosure liability. Because the defense succeeds even assuming intentional illegality, the disputed interception intent cannot defeat summary judgment on the disclosure claims. Interception, procurement, independently unlawful acquisition, purely private or intimate material, trade secrets and separately asserted use restrictions lie outside the accepted disclosure question and are not decided.

**Precedent treatment:** Daily Mail, Florida Star and Landmark are extended to the described unlawful-source application; Turner and Rice retain their justification and participation distinctions. Sheets, Denius and Sterling remain confined to their official-conduct holdings and are not extended into a private-publication damages rule.

## Public Projection

## Event

**Bartnicki v. Vopper / United States v. Vopper**, Nos. 99-1687 and 99-1728, 532 U.S. 514. Merits decision, May 21, 2001. Argued December 5, 2000. On writs of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit.

Both petitions concern the same judgment. The question is whether the First Amendment permits civil damages for disclosure of an accurate recording about a matter of public concern when an unknown third party intercepted the conversation unlawfully, the defendants neither participated in that interception nor obtained the recording unlawfully, and they knew or had reason to know of the interception's illegality.

The recording concerned contentious public-school collective bargaining. Union negotiators discussed the dispute and pressure on school-board members, including language suggesting physical harm. Jack Yocum said he found the recording in his mailbox, played it for board members and delivered it to broadcaster Frederick Vopper. Vopper broadcast it in reporting about the settlement, and other media disclosed its contents. The accuracy of the recording is undisputed.

The District Court denied cross-motions for summary judgment. It rejected an interceptor-only statutory construction and the First Amendment defense, while identifying a factual dispute over intentional interception. On a certified interlocutory appeal under 28 U.S.C. §1292(b), the Third Circuit held the federal and Pennsylvania disclosure provisions unconstitutional as applied and directed summary judgment for respondents. The United States intervened to defend the federal enactment. Petitioners and the United States seek reversal; respondents seek affirmance.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

## Holdings

The two holdings below are adopted by the Opinion of the Court, **Kennedy**, with the complete seven-Justice coalition identified in Opinion Topology. They control by direct majority agreement; no narrower-rationale inference is required.

### The disclosure provisions reach noninterceptors, but their neutrality does not end constitutional review

**Holding and operative rule:** For these claims, 18 U.S.C. §2511(1)(c) reaches intentional disclosure or attempted disclosure of a communication's contents by a person who knows or has reason to know that the information came from an interception violating the subsection; neither that provision nor the parallel Pennsylvania disclosure provision is confined to the interceptor. Their selection by unlawful source rather than message is content neutral, but their direct burden on communicating information requires constitutional examination even when enforced through civil damages against a nonmedia speaker.

**Authority:** The complete seven-Justice Opinion of the Court adopts the statutory construction, classification and requirement of constitutional review.

**Controlling explanation:** The disclosure prohibition addresses the conduct and knowledge of a discloser. It does not require that the same person also made the interception. Constitutional avoidance therefore cannot replace the enacted coverage with an interceptor-only rule. The certified constitutional question proceeds on assumed intentional illegality and knowledge or reason to know; lawful receipt is consistent with the statute's separate prohibition on later disclosure.

Turner requires attention to what a restriction selects and why. These provisions select an unlawful interception as the source, not a viewpoint or bargaining position. Yet disclosure communicates the recorded words, and liability burdens that expression directly. Neither neutrality nor the use of damages removes the First Amendment question.

Cohen and Branzburg reject a categorical press exemption from valid general law; they do not make every direct restriction on publication valid. Yocum's communication to board members and delivery to a broadcaster are expression even though he is an intermediary rather than professional media. The Court therefore examines the justification for all the challenged disclosures under the same relevant constitutional principles.

**Precedent treatment:** Turner supplies source-sensitive classification and substantive review; Cohen and Branzburg retain their rejection of special press immunity without dispensing with review of this publication burden.

### The First Amendment defeats these disclosure claims on the certified premises and public-bargaining record

**Holding and operative rule:** The First Amendment bars the requested federal and Pennsylvania disclosure damages against these respondents, who lawfully received an accurate recording without participating in its unlawful interception and disclosed this conversation concerning the contentious public-school bargaining controversy and apparent physical pressure against its participants. Their knowledge or reason to know that a stranger intercepted the conversation unlawfully does not defeat that defense on these premises; the rule protects Yocum's communications as well as the media disclosures and requires affirmance of the direction to enter summary judgment.

**Authority:** The complete seven-Justice Opinion of the Court adopts this bounded rule and its application, including both the deterrence analysis and the independent conversational-privacy analysis.

**Controlling explanation:** Daily Mail, Florida Star and Landmark protect truthful information of public significance lawfully obtained by its speaker. They left open the unlawful-source problem. The Court extends their protection to this application, where recipients lawfully obtained the recording and took no part in interception.

Deterring unlawful interception is legitimate. Turner nevertheless requires examination of the actual expressive burden and adequate fit while respecting supported legislative predictions. The ordinary target is the person responsible for interception. General incentive reasoning does not sufficiently justify suppressing these nonparticipants' disclosures to discourage an unknown stranger. This conclusion neither requires an empirical study in every case nor makes deterrence irrelevant whenever a recipient is innocent of interception.

Dissemination also causes a distinct privacy injury. Protecting conversation encourages candid private speech, and that interest remains substantial even when later liability adds little deterrence. But the actual information here bears directly on public-school negotiations and apparent pressure involving physical harm against board members. The participants directed that public dispute; the public has a strong interest in their actual words. Settlement does not erase that significance. The Court need not find a punishable threat or a continuing danger at publication to recognize this public interest. In these circumstances, the asserted privacy protection does not justify the burden imposed on the disclosures.

Rice preserves responsibility for purposeful, materially effective assistance to crime, while distinguishing awareness and ordinary publication. Knowing the completed interception's unlawful origin does not establish that assistance. The rule neither excuses independently unlawful acquisition nor turns all information concerning a public participant into public property. Sheets, Denius and Sterling protect confined intimate interests against unjustified official dissemination, compulsory access or coercive surrender; they do not decide these private recipients' disclosure liability. Because the defense succeeds even assuming intentional illegality, the disputed interception intent cannot defeat summary judgment on the disclosure claims. Interception, procurement, independently unlawful acquisition, purely private or intimate material, trade secrets and separately asserted use restrictions lie outside the accepted disclosure question and are not decided.

**Precedent treatment:** Daily Mail, Florida Star and Landmark are extended to the described unlawful-source application; Turner and Rice retain their justification and participation distinctions. Sheets, Denius and Sterling remain confined to their official-conduct holdings and are not extended into a private-publication damages rule.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Smith v. Daily Mail Publishing Co.:** Extends its protection of truthful public-significance information lawfully obtained by the speaker to the nonparticipating lawful recipients and actual disclosures here; it is not treated as having already resolved unlawful-source publication.
- **Florida Star v. B. J. F.:** Applies its protection against inadequately justified liability for truthful, lawfully obtained information and resolves only this portion of its unlawful-source reservation. The decision creates no universal public-concern defense to privacy law.
- **Landmark Communications, Inc. v. Virginia:** Applies protection of public-significance disclosure and the judiciary's responsibility to examine the asserted justification; legislative identification of an interest is not conclusive.
- **Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC (1994):** Applies its content-classification inquiry and requirement of meaningful review while preserving its actual, limited majority agreement on predictive deference. The earlier four-Justice insufficiency explanation is not converted into controlling law or a universal empirical-study requirement.
- **Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC (1997):** Preserves its developed-record approval of the particular cable scheme and its adequate-fit standard; the cable result does not decide this direct disclosure burden.
- **Rice v. Paladin Enterprises, Inc.:** Distinguishes the stipulated purposeful and materially effective assistance there from lawful receipt and knowledge of another person's completed interception here. Its actual participation rule and ordinary tort requirements remain intact; it is not an exhaustive test of every valid publication restriction.
- **Sheets v. Salt Lake County:** Preserves protection against unjustified official dissemination of objectively intimate marital and family information. Its express distinction concerning the private author's speech remains; the present decision neither rejects intimate privacy nor creates a private-publication claim under Sheets.
- **Denius v. Dunlap:** Preserves its bounded acquisition protection and objectively intimate-content requirement against official employment demands. Confidentiality alone does not make the public-school bargaining conversation constitutionally intimate, and its employment inquiry is not transferred to these private recipients.
- **Sterling v. Borough of Minersville:** Distinguishes its protection against a credible official ultimatum compelling an adult to disclose suspected sexual orientation to family without a legitimate governmental purpose. Its operative-coercion holding preserves an objectively intimate interest; it supplies no rule imposing disclosure damages on lawful private recipients of the public-bargaining recording. Its conduct-date immunity and factual-appellate holdings remain separate.
- **Cohen v. Cowles Media Co.:** Preserves the absence of a general press exemption from independently binding obligations; its general-law principle does not eliminate examination of a direct speech restriction.
- **Branzburg v. Hayes:** Preserves the absence of a professional-press license to engage in unlawful conduct. Media status supplies neither the defense here nor a reason to exclude Yocum.
- **New York Times Co. v. United States:** Preserves its distinct treatment of prior restraints. Its rule is not transferred automatically to subsequent civil damages; the present result rests on the actual publication and justification analysis.

No precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

Effective May 21, 2001, the federal and parallel Pennsylvania disclosure provisions cannot support these damages claims against nonparticipating lawful recipients who communicate the accurate recording in the public-bargaining circumstances decided here. Both media publication and Yocum's nonmedia communications are protected. Source illegality and the recipient's awareness do not alone defeat the constitutional defense on these premises.

The decision adds this application to existing truthful-publication law. It leaves the statutory coverage of knowing noninterceptor disclosure intact, with a constitutional defense controlling the application before the Court. Courts must retain the actual speech and privacy context; neither an automatic public-concern exception nor a professional-press privilege follows. The fact that the recorded language strengthens the public interest does not establish that proof of a criminal threat is required for constitutional protection.

Interception and procurement remain distinct from lawful receipt and disclosure. Rice's actual purposeful-assistance rule continues to govern its predicates. Official acquisition, disclosure and operative compelled surrender of objectively intimate information remain governed by Sheets, Denius and Sterling within their decided scopes. The Court neither decides the constitutional treatment of purely private or intimate material, trade secrets or separate use restrictions outside the accepted question nor imposes an additional test for those subjects. There is no Act-wide invalidation, new mandatory scrutiny formula or special transition.

## Separate Writings

**Breyer, concurring, joined by O'Connor.** They join the Court's opinion in full because it resolves this application without making truthful publication automatically superior to every privacy interest. The statutes protect private speech as well as privacy: people may speak less freely when an illegal interception can be disseminated without consequence. The competing public-speech interest is therefore assessed against a real constitutional and practical value, rather than against a mere dislike of publicity.

The recipients acted lawfully in obtaining the recording and did not cause its interception. The speakers were directing a public-school labor dispute, and the recorded discussion concerned apparent physical harm against board members. Those circumstances create an unusually strong public interest and reduce the legitimate claim to secrecy for this particular discussion. They do not establish a criminal true threat, a plan of violence, a continuing danger when broadcast, or surrender of every private conversation by people who take public roles.

Their Turner, Rice, Sheets, Denius and Sterling merits positions support actual review, lawful responsibility boundaries and serious protection of genuine confidentiality. They preserve legislative latitude to protect private conversation and to prevent the distinct harm of dissemination in materially different settings. They do not adopt automatic strict scrutiny whenever substantial public-speech and private-speech interests conflict, a closed balancing checklist, or a rule that deterrence can never justify liability absent complicity. Their additional explanation qualifies the reach of their agreement while remaining consistent with every proposition of the bounded Court opinion.

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** They would reverse the constitutional ruling and summary-judgment direction in both dockets, then return the claims for ordinary adjudication of statutory elements and preserved defenses. The law regulates the defendant's knowing disclosure as a separately harmful act; it does not merely attribute a stranger's interception to an innocent recipient. Dissemination enlarges the intrusion and undermines the privacy that permits candid conversation.

The source criterion does not select a viewpoint or message. Under the content-neutral framework, the dissent finds adequate justification in preventing the additional disclosure injury, reinforced by the reasonable legislative judgment that limiting downstream dissemination can reduce the reward for interception. They would not require lawmakers to accept a route around the prohibition through recipients who did not themselves conduct the tap. That argument is a judgment about permissible prevention, not a finding of a quantified deterrent effect or respondent participation.

They distinguish Daily Mail, Florida Star and Landmark because the information here remained private until unlawful interception and the knowing disclosure itself is prohibited. Cohen and Branzburg preserve application of valid general law to the press; Rice supplies no universal requirement that a discloser must have assisted the antecedent crime before any publication restriction can be justified. Their objections to constitutional extensions in Sheets, Denius and Sterling do not deny legislative authority to protect conversation. They accept the need for First Amendment review and the significance of lawful receipt and public relevance, but find those considerations insufficient to invalidate this application. Reversal would not establish intentional interception, the remaining statutory elements, damages or any criminal threat.
