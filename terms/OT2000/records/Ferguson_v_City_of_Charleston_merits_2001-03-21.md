## Event and chronology

Ferguson v. City of Charleston, No. 99-936. Argued October 4, 2000; decided March 21, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, 186 F.3d 469. The question is whether the public hospital's nonconsensual drug-testing program, coordinated with police to obtain prosecution evidence, is justified by special needs. Ten patients challenge the appellate special-needs ruling; hospital, municipal and law-enforcement defendants seek affirmance.

Hospital staff perceived increased prenatal cocaine use in fall 1988. In April 1989 they began testing suspected maternity patients and referring positive results for counseling and treatment. Use did not appear to change, an observation rather than quantified proof of failure. About four months later hospital personnel initiated cooperation with prosecution. The challenged policy supplied collection and testing procedures, chain of custody, police disclosure and arrest criteria. Four plaintiffs were arrested under the initial policy without a treatment alternative; the others were arrested after its 1990 modification for noncompliance or another positive result.

The district court found a genuine maternal and infant health goal in deciding the later-abandoned discrimination claim, but separately rejected a special-needs justification for the coordinated police operation. The jury found for defendants under an instruction placing on them a preponderance burden to establish consent to collection, testing and possible police disclosure. The patients challenged the sufficiency of that consent evidence; the Fourth Circuit instead affirmed on special needs without deciding the challenge. These are distinct findings, instructions and procedural events.

Review proceeds under 28 U.S.C. section 1254 on the supported argument and decision dates. No companion or nonparticipation is established. The faithful grant formulation is used without a claim that exact wording was independently recovered. The present civil action supplies no suppression ruling in a separate criminal case.

## Holdings

### The coordinated prosecution program lacks an adequate special-needs justification

**Holding and operative rule:** Assuming legally insufficient consent, the public hospital's systematic collection and testing of patient urine under the arranged disclosure, evidence-preservation and arrest program is not justified by the special-needs exception. Genuine clinical work and a treatment objective do not themselves supply an independently adequate justification for this program's designed prosecution operation; the special-needs affirmance must be reversed, with the preserved consent issue and remaining claim-specific consequences returned for ordinary adjudication.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. All seven directly adopt this confined rule, its application and the reversal/remand. No general immediate-versus-ultimate-purpose rule is adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the actual operation must have an adequate justification for dispensing with ordinary Fourth Amendment protection. Chandler requires a substantial operational need, and Edmond prevents an ordinary narcotics-evidence program from obtaining an exception merely through additional legitimate functions. Vernonia recognizes urine collection and analysis as searches despite participation in a public program; its confined athletic safety arrangements and nonpolice use do not authorize the present operation.

The hospital began clinical screening and referrals before police cooperation. Its concern for maternal and infant health was genuine, and the district court's health-goal finding is not erased. But the later policy prescribed chain of custody, police disclosure and arrest criteria. Four plaintiffs were arrested without a treatment alternative; later arrests followed noncompliance or another positive. Those designed means require justification at their actual scope. Beneficial ends alone cannot distinguish this evidence program from ordinary prosecution intended to prevent harm.

Griffin establishes that police cooperation and later criminal use do not invariably defeat special needs. It sustained a probation search under a valid supervisory regulation, conditional liberty and the institutional need for prompt action on reasonable grounds. Ordinary patients here are not subject to that lawful supervisory relationship, and the record establishes no independently adequate medical justification for the challenged penal implementation. This distinguishes Griffin without requiring custody, emergency, quantified treatment failure or exhaustion of alternatives in every special-needs case. Skinner and Von Raab likewise rest on their operative protective arrangements.

The assumed absence of adequate consent resolves only the exception question. The defense jury verdict was not reviewed for evidentiary sufficiency on appeal. The Court therefore returns that preserved challenge rather than overturning the verdict on unreviewed facts. Medical attendance alone is no waiver, but neither a universal three-permission rule nor a final patient-specific consent finding follows.

**Limits and matters not reached:** Consent to medical care and the scope of consent to the challenged evidence operation require distinct, fact-sensitive analysis. The trial's collection/testing/disclosure instruction is not adopted as a universal constitutional formula, and disclosure alone is not held to be a separate search. The preserved consent challenge and live individual responsibility, municipal attribution, immunity, causation, injury and relief questions remain for their proper adjudication. Private-hospital conduct, independent mandatory-reporting duties, genuine emergent treatment, materially different authorized clinical programs and suppression in separate criminal prosecutions lie outside this case. No damages, universal testing injunction, automatic new trial or new evidentiary hearing is ordered.
