## Controlling holdings

### I. The distributor has no statutory medical-necessity exemption

**Holding and operative rule:** The Controlled Substances Act forecloses the Cooperative's medical-necessity defense to its prohibited manufacture, distribution and associated possession with intent to distribute marijuana. Medical recommendations and state permission do not supply federal authorization, and even satisfaction of the lower court's medical criteria does not create this distributor's exception.

**Authority:** Scalia's Opinion of the Court is joined throughout by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Those eight directly adopt both bounded holdings and their remedy; Breyer does not participate.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 841(a)(1) prohibits the identified conduct except as authorized by the subchapter. Schedule I treatment and the specified research route give operative content to that authorization structure; the Cooperative claims no research authorization. The conclusion rests on Congress's enacted classification, not a judicial finding that marijuana can never benefit a patient. Rutherford supports adherence to the chosen medical-regulatory scheme. Bailey supplies necessity's ordinary background and attention to lawful alternatives, but does not permit a court to override the particular medical-distribution policy Congress prescribed.

The Cooperative's strongest argument is the need of seriously ill patients after other treatment fails. The district modification required four conjunctive conditions: a serious medical condition; imminent harm without cannabis; need for cannabis to treat or alleviate the condition or associated symptoms; and no reasonable legal effective alternative because all legal alternatives tried were ineffective or caused side effects the patient could not reasonably tolerate. Those conditions must be accurately understood, and the order does not establish that every recipient satisfied them. Even assuming satisfaction, however, medical need does not furnish the statutory authorization for this distributor. Neither the absence of express necessity-defense words nor sympathy decides the issue; the prohibition, classification and actual authorization channels together do so.

### II. Equitable discretion does not supply the same substantive exception

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 882(a) preserves lawful discretion whether and how to enjoin violations, but a court may not grant the same prohibited medical-distribution exemption by recharacterizing its own balance of medical benefits and statutory policy as injunction modification. The court may tailor authorized enforcement means consistently with the Act; it may not displace the substantive distribution rule.

**Authority:** The same eight-Justice Opinion of the Court directly controls the independent equitable ground and its preserved discretion.

**Controlling explanation:** Hecht and Romero-Barcelo preserve traditional choice and tailoring in equitable enforcement. Meghrig requires that this discretion operate within the enacted remedial scheme. Grupo Mexicano likewise distinguishes an actual source of remedial authority from a desired result; its disputed general-equity question does not decide every statutory injunction. Section 882(a) authorizes relief without commanding an injunction in every case or its broadest possible form. A court may therefore consider whether relief is needed, whether its wording exceeds a violation, and how lawful compliance should be ordered. It may not authorize continued prohibited distribution because it gives the medical advantages of nonenforcement greater weight than Congress did. That would supply the statutory exception rejected above through the remedy alone. These limits resolve the independently offered equitable ground for affirmance without deciding all injunctions affecting individual patients.

## Event

United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers' Cooperative, No. 00-151. Merits decision, May 14, 2001; argued March 28, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit. The live judgment reversed the refusal to modify a federal injunction and directed consideration of a medical-necessity exemption. The questions concern the Cooperative's asserted statutory defense for manufacture and distribution and its alternative reliance on equitable discretion.

California law removed specified state prohibitions for patients and primary caregivers possessing or cultivating marijuana on a physician's recommendation or approval. The Cooperative operated a dispensary with medical screening and membership identification. The United States obtained an injunction against manufacture, distribution and associated possession with intent; the Cooperative did not appeal the initial order. The separate contempt appeal later became moot. After the appellate ruling, the district court adopted medical criteria for exempted distribution, and a separate government appeal followed. State permission, physician recommendations and federal authorization remain distinct.
