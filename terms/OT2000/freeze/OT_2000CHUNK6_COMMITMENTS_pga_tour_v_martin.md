# Frozen independent commitments: PGA TOUR, Inc. v. Martin

Event: merits, May 29, 2001. Argument: January 17, 2001. Research cutoff: May 29, 2001. All nine Justices participate at both milestones. Inputs are the approved Section I packet, neutral validation C, mechanically copied enacted text and the entering-law files named there. Neither Stone's position nor the present historical Supreme Court decision was received. No author assignment is made.

## Legal paths and sufficient grounds

Coverage and fundamental alteration are independently assessed. The six Associates Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer favor affirmance on both. The leased and operated golf courses are enumerated public accommodations; qualifying and tournament play are services and privileges offered to eligible entrants. A participant does not cease receiving those opportunities because his performance also attracts spectators. Even if the customer language governs the relevant relationship, the paid qualifying opportunity makes Martin a recipient; subsection (b)(1)(A)(iv) cannot be transplanted beyond its expressly named clauses as a blanket exclusion.

Necessary reasonable modification remains separate. A requested change can be fundamental if it changes an essential competitive feature or, in its application to the individual, confers a material competitive advantage. The latter inquiry cannot be replaced by the fact that all ordinary competitors obey the rule. The supported findings identify shot-making as the essential contest, walking as nonessential in this tournament setting, cart use at related stages and Martin's remaining fatigue as greater than the ordinary walking fatigue. On those findings the cart changes neither the essential task nor the competitive burden to Martin's advantage. The injunction therefore stands subject to ordinary skill-based qualification and all other valid rules.

Scalia and Thomas favor reversal on the coverage ground. In their construction the relevant professional tournament service is the competition provided to spectators, with competitors producing it; paying for admission to a qualifying process does not turn employment-like entry into consumption of the professional contest. Assuming coverage, they also would hold that removing a defining rule for one competitor alters that contest. They do not dispute the disability or convert necessity into entitlement; their disagreement concerns the statutory service and what counts as its fundamental alteration.

The strongest objection to the affirmance path is that courts would define a sport's essence and compare inherently different fatigue levels, substituting judgment for the competition's rules. The six affirmers answer that Congress expressly enacted a modification obligation and a demonstrated-alteration defense. Treating every existing rule as essential would nullify the distinction. The supported trial findings address this actual rule and claimant, rather than requiring courts to equalize every player's talents or fatigue.

The strongest objection to reversal is the statute's breadth, the express golf-course inclusion and the entitlement to modifications of policies and practices. Scalia and Thomas answer that broad protection within a received service does not create a new service or rewrite its defining conditions. Their reading must preserve the expressly limited scope of the clients-or-customers subsection; they rely on the professional relationship in context, not a fictitious universal statutory definition. Their alternative alteration position treats uniform rule-defined competition as the relevant product even where walking is not the main athletic skill.

## Available topology, limits and remedy

The six affirmers directly join coverage, the distinct modification/alteration rule, the application to the supported findings and affirmance of the injunction. Their grounds are compatible at the stated level of generality; no narrower-ground aggregation is needed. Scalia and Thomas share the two specified reversal grounds, which should be preserved separately if published. Assignment and final writing structure belong to assembly.

The Court would not hold that every sport participant is covered in every legal relationship, that walking never matters to a competition, that disability guarantees tournament admission, that every cart request must be granted, or that a federal safety standard is equivalent to a private sporting rule. No different burden is imported from auxiliary-aid undue burden or direct threat. Martin must satisfy normal competitive qualifications; the entity bears the enacted fundamental-alteration defense after a necessary reasonable modification is established. The trial findings remain record-specific, not statutory rules.

No new damages or factual trial is directed. Affirmance preserves the supported cart injunction; reversal would remove its Title III basis while deciding no employment claim. No current-term disposition materially changes these grounds: Garrett concerns constitutional enforcement against state employers, not the private coverage or alteration questions here.
