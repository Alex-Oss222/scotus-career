## Completed source predicate

The authenticated recitations establish separate annual-base provisions in sections 31.3121(a)(1)-1(a)(2) and 31.3306(b)(1)-1(a)(2). They distinguish remuneration received or paid within the calendar year from remuneration for employment performed during that year. This supplies a ground concerning the annual bases independently of the already quoted attachment/rate provisions. The statute supplies the applicable FUTA ceiling of $7,000; the older printed regulatory figure of $3,000 cannot replace it. The quoted FICA provision's earlier employment boundary is preserved as an attributed quotation; compensation for 1986 and 1987 makes no question about that earlier boundary material here.

The public administrative-history recitation identifies Revenue Rulings 78-336 and 89-35 and describes an established interpretation applying payment-year taxation to back wages regardless of when earned or due. The Government's attributed brief additionally identifies the earlier rulings and regulatory ancestry. Those materials establish the described interpretation's existence and prelitigation character without a categorical demand for every original ruling. They do not confer independent binding statutory force on a ruling merely because it was published, establish every ruling-specific fact or exception, or prove an unquoted authorization instrument. The ground below uses the actual established interpretation of the identified Treasury regulations, not a fictional new litigation policy.

Section 31.3121(a)-2(a), as recited, preserves actual or constructive payment and the express reference to specified payments deemed subsequently paid. The Government identifies the minor-cash category as outside this case; that remains an attributed recitation. No evidence establishes constructive payment in 1986 or 1987. The earlier due date and the salary period repaired by the settlement do not themselves establish that event. Sections 3121(v)(2) and 3306(r)(2) separately govern specified nonqualified deferred compensation; no fact puts these awards within such a plan. This decision neither makes all wage timing exceptions disappear nor invents one for these stipulated salary awards.

## Final common judgment and two distinct sufficient rationales

All eight Associates vote to reverse the Sixth Circuit refund judgment and remand for application of the 1994 employer FICA and FUTA rates and annual wage-base rules to the stipulated non-interest salary awards. Individual benefit accounts are not adjudicated. Seven adopt the regulation-interpretation rationale below. Scalia reaches the same judgment through his independent direct statutory construction and does not join the seven's finding of interpretive room or necessary reliance on the administrative construction. Judgment agreement is not eight votes for a single interpretive rationale.

### I. Seven-Associate ground: a fair, considered interpretation within the separate regulations

**Operative rule:** Under the applicable annual employer-tax provisions and the responsible Treasury interpretation of their implementing regulations, these back wages enter the payment year's tax calculation even though they replace salary attributable to earlier years. The respective rate provisions use the rate at payment, and the separate annual-base provisions aggregate remuneration paid or received in that year; the legal character of these salary damages as stipulated wages does not itself decide their temporal allocation, but the existing administrative construction permissibly resolves it in favor of 1994 on the actual record.

The statutory language strongly supports payment-year treatment. It does not specifically address a damages substitute for earlier wages, however, and Nierotko makes earlier-period legal attribution a substantial competing construction rather than a semantic impossibility. The seven therefore recognize interpretive room in applying the statutory and regulatory payment concepts to this category. They do not adopt an unambiguous-statute holding merely by observing that actual money changed hands in 1994.

The relevant regulations separately address attachment, rates and annual bases. They choose a payment/receipt framework rather than an employment-performed framework, but do not expressly mention backpay. The established Treasury interpretation applies that framework to back wages. Auer's actual rule, joined by all eight Associates in that case, gives controlling weight to a responsible agency's fair and considered construction of its own regulation unless plainly erroneous or inconsistent with it. The sourced preexisting backpay rulings identify an agency interpretation with history beyond this litigation; it is not treated as authoritative merely because an advocate now requests that result.

The construction is textually permissible for each component. Treating the award as wages when actually paid respects the attachment and rate clauses. It also gives independent effect to the annual-base clauses' distinction between the year of remuneration and the year of performed employment. The IRS does not change a permissive provision into a prohibition, invent current employment, or define a nontaxable payment into wages; inclusion is stipulated, and the actual dispute concerns attribution of those wages. Auer therefore supplies controlling interpretive weight within the rules, while Christensen's separate restriction on an advisory statutory opinion does not make these existing own-regulation interpretations irrelevant or confer independent statutory force on every revenue ruling.

The seven also examine the interpretation against the tax statutes rather than letting the agency define its own powers. The annual rates and bases permit this payment-based treatment; no supported statutory direction requires attribution of these damages to their earning periods. The defined deferred-compensation exception is different, and no constructive-payment fact changes the year. The statutes' actual annual collection mechanism gives a reason for treating taxes separately from benefit restoration. Avoiding repeated reconstruction of prior employer tax accounts supports the reasonableness of this permitted choice; administrative convenience does not create the interpretive authority, establish ambiguity or override contrary law.

**The strongest competing ground:** Nierotko regarded backpay as remuneration for an employment relationship, allocated it to earlier benefit periods and expressly did so despite paid-quarter language. Its discussion of common wage definitions gives the Company's argument real force. The answer is that Nierotko's judgment restored a benefit account whose eligibility and measure depended on employment periods. It did not decide employer taxes under the later rate and annual-base provisions here. Its earlier tax language reproduced in note 8 referred to employment during the specified years, and does not establish the interpretation of these later provisions. Common wage status does not make benefit and tax allocation necessarily identical.

The seven preserve Nierotko both as a wage-classification holding and as an allocation rule at its benefit-account scope. They do not describe it as having ignored payment language. They also preserve its rejection of an agency construction that exceeded statutory bounds: had Treasury's rule contradicted an actual statutory attribution command, administrative experience would not save it. Here the identified regulations and the permissible construction fit the annual tax provisions. Neither the later benefits regulation nor the reported employee-election instruction decides this employer refund. No finding classifies these awards for that separate benefit rule or finds an employee election in 1994.

### II. Scalia's separate direct statutory ground

Scalia votes to reverse because the applicable tax statutes themselves allocate these stipulated wage payments to 1994. He treats each rate clause as selecting the rate for a wage payment in that year and each annual-base clause as aggregating remuneration paid by the employer during that year, subject to its stated exclusions. The payments' purpose of repairing earlier salary losses and the recipients' lack of current employment do not change the tax event selected by those provisions. This is a provision-specific construction, not the general claim that paid can never support legal relation back.

Nierotko therefore requires a substantive answer but does not make the later tax text ambiguous for him. It resolved benefit eligibility and measurement under its employment account; its recognition of a compensable relationship and common wage definitions does not dictate the timing of every later tax provision. Its older tax-rate wording further prevents transposing the benefit result mechanically. Nothing in the supported record establishes earlier constructive payment or the distinct deferred-compensation predicate. His holding concerns the actual 1994 awards and does not decide every possible wage timing exception.

Treasury's regulations and established treatment confirm his reading but are not necessary to it. His Christensen concurrence prevents advisory form alone from excluding an otherwise authoritative administrative construction, yet does not require deference when the statute independently answers the question. He withholds a join to the seven's declaration of interpretive room and its necessary Auer rationale. He agrees with their resulting component rules and mandate without being counted as an eighth vote for that reasoning.
