## Klamath: revised documentary coverage, unchanged affirmance commitments

### Corrected premise and complete legal question

The dispute includes six tribal-origin communications to federal recipients and one communication prepared by a Bureau official and delivered to tribal lawyers. The documents concern the operating plan, the Oregon adjudication, or both. The original description of the whole set as submissions advancing the sender's claim was incomplete: that wording cannot itself decide the Bureau-origin document. The relevant common feature is the role of the outside participant in the exchange, not authorship by a tribe on every page.

The statutory text in `OT_2000CHUNK3_LATE_STATUTES.md` separately requires an inter-agency or intra-agency communication and protection against disclosure in the relevant litigation setting. Agency burden, de novo review and segregability remain operative. Landano, effective May 24, 1993, and the Standards heading **Confidential sources and segregable information under FOIA** supply their specific Exemption 7(D) rules and no consultant holding. FLRA, effective February 23, 1994, and **Privacy Act incorporation of FOIA in federal bargaining requests** preserve the enacted inquiry despite a representational purpose and distinguish exemption permission from another law's prohibition; their Exemption 6 balance is not applied here.

### Refreshed propositions and formal judgment

**K-R1 — Participant function in both directions.** Even assuming an outside adviser can sometimes function within an agency's deliberative process, communications between an agency and an outside claimant advancing its own claim to a scarce resource against competing claimants do not become intra-agency communications merely because the agency owes that claimant trust duties. That conclusion applies to the six claimant-origin messages and to the Bureau-origin message delivered to the claimants' lawyers as part of these exchanges: neither the outside sender nor the outside recipient is functioning as a detached component of the agency's decisionmaking in the relationship supplied here. Government authorship is relevant but not sufficient to make a communication within the agency when its other participant acts in this distinct claimant capacity.

This is a category conclusion about the communications presented and the asserted consultant/trust relationship. It is not a general rule that an agency-origin document loses every exemption upon external transmission. It does not decide waiver, the status of every contractor or adviser, incidental personal interests, or an independently established internal communication later transmitted elsewhere. No independent internal circulation, mixed-content category or waiver fact is supplied for the seventh record, and none is presumed. Nor does the conclusion depend on treating every beneficiary as outside every agency function regardless of the role actually performed.

**K-R2 — All seven records and distinct privilege question.** The actual outside-claimant role defeats the Exemption 5 agency-category argument for all seven disputed communications. Whether any would otherwise satisfy deliberative-process or work-product protection cannot substitute for the failed category. No actual privilege or waiver finding is needed for this ground. It does not determine tribal water rights or excuse the Department's substantive trust duties.

**K-R3 — Disposition and implementation.** Affirm the Ninth Circuit's judgment rejecting Exemption 5 withholding of the seven records. The district court implements disclosure subject only to any independently supported, properly preserved other ground; this handoff establishes none and revives none. Reasonably segregable nonexempt material remains subject to release. No document is excepted from the affirmed category ruling merely because a Bureau official prepared it. The judgment is an affirmance of the actual withholding judgment, not an abstract declaration confined to incoming submissions.

### Adversarial alternatives

**Trustee/consultant path:** Interior's strongest argument is that it obtains and supplies candid information in a legal representation relationship, a function more integrated than ordinary outside lobbying. On that reading, the tribal lawyers' receipt of an agency-authored paper might be a particularly strong example of federal representation rather than advice offered to influence federal policy. If that statutory-category account were accepted, actual privileges and segregation would still require independent resolution; no blanket withholding could follow.

**Participant-function answer:** The federal duty does not erase the beneficiary's distinct claim against other users. The text requires an agency communication independently of litigation protection. A message to an interested claimant's lawyer is part of the same external claimant relationship as a message from that lawyer, absent a supported different function. The distinction between sender and recipient therefore does not cure the missing statutory relationship on this record. This answer preserves the trust duty while rejecting its use as an unstated FOIA category exception.

**Seventh-document distinction and its strongest objection to the chosen path:** An agency might generate a genuinely internal deliberative paper and later share it without changing its original category; its litigation privilege and any waiver could raise different questions. Agency origin therefore deserves separate analysis and cannot be dismissed as irrelevant. Here the only established seventh-document function is a Bureau communication to the tribal lawyers in the disputed exchange. No independently established internal communication or different recipient capacity is supplied. The holding classifies that exchange, rather than finding that later external transmission universally destroys internal status. A result based on unknown contents, internal circulation, mixed portions or waiver would need the actual descriptions and preserved grounds; the chosen role-based path does not posit them. Those absent propositions are not treated as proof of default or concealment.

## Shafer: corrected instructional record and revised adequacy commitments

### Corrected premise and separable components

The trial judge gave repeated until-death descriptions of life imprisonment, including a natural-life formulation. Counsel was barred from reading the express statutory parole provision but was allowed to argue that Shafer would remain in prison for his life and would not return home. The jury then asked two questions about possible parole eligibility after a murder conviction, without expressly limiting either question to a life sentence. The supplemental response again described imprisonment until death while directing the jury not to consider parole eligibility or ineligibility. These facts replace the earlier bare-ordinary-meaning and complete-exclusion shorthand.

The governing rule is the Court's actual Simmons, June 17, 1994, as preserved by Ramdass, June 12, 2000, and the Standards heading **Parole ineligibility as an answer to future-danger evidence**. The rule permits an effective accurate defense presentation or an adequate instruction; it prescribes no particular words. Its dangerousness predicate, adequacy component and Chapman consequence are distinct. Simmons's own harmful-error determination cannot decide this changed record. Ramdass's habeas restrictions do not govern this direct review.

### Supported propositions and competing legal-sufficiency grounds

**S-R1 — Statutory stage.** At the jury's actual penalty choice after the required aggravator finding, the alternatives are death and life without ordinary parole. A different judicial sentencing option if no aggravator is found is not a contemporaneous alternative at that jury choice. The State's categorical structural distinction from Simmons fails. This legal conclusion does not establish danger, inadequate information or harmful error.

**S-R2 — Unresolved danger.** The supplied prosecution description does not establish whether the State actually put future danger in issue. The general murder/parole questions establish neither that predicate nor its absence. An adequacy ruling can be made on an assumption favorable to Shafer that the predicate exists; a judgment requiring relief for a due-process error must instead have that predicate established or an independently sufficient ground. No present Justice commitment below rests on a new dangerousness finding.

**S-R3-A — Sufficient combined presentation, assuming Simmons applies.** The judge's repeated descriptions of imprisonment until death, reinforced by permitted defense argument that Shafer would remain imprisoned and would not go home, conveyed the substantive legal alternative of confinement for the rest of his life. Refusal to use the requested statutory wording does not alone deny the effective opportunity that Simmons permits by either accurate advocacy or adequate instruction. In this combined presentation, the later direction excluding parole from consideration does not affirmatively tell jurors that ordinary release is available or remove the judicially stated until-death alternative. The questions concern murder convictions generally; they do not compel the finding that the jury misunderstood the particular life verdict already explained. The complete charge and permitted argument are legally sufficient under Simmons even if future danger was raised, without a new finding about what jurors actually believed or a harmlessness determination.

This is a record-specific sufficiency ground. It does not hold that any natural-life phrase always suffices, that counsel's permission alone establishes adequacy, or that a court may mislead a jury about ordinary parole. Its force comes from the repeated judicial description, consistent permitted argument, actual question wording and the absence of an affirmative release option in the response, considered together.

**S-R3-B — Inadequate combined presentation, if Simmons applies.** The repeated judicial until-death language and permitted advocacy are substantial explanations, not a complete exclusion of every accurate answer. Nevertheless, after the jury raised parole and the defense sought an express explanation of the applicable law, the supplemental direction that eligibility and ineligibility were outside the jury's consideration left a reasonable ambiguity between the nominal duration of a life sentence and the legal possibility of ordinary release. It also prevented use of ineligibility as the legal response to a State future-danger case. The permitted natural-life argument does not remove that problem when the court's final direction takes parole status out of consideration. The general wording of the questions weakens an inference of actual misunderstanding but does not make ordinary release irrelevant to the capital jury's present choice. On this combined account, the provided opportunity is legally inadequate if the State raised future danger.

This ground does not demand the word parole, a statutory recital, a promise against clemency, or an authoritative instruction in every case. An unequivocal accepted defense explanation or equivalent adequate judicial account can satisfy the governing rule. The defect found here concerns the full communication, including the exclusion of the legal subject after questions, not a missing verbal formula. It is an error conclusion conditional on the unresolved dangerousness predicate, not a finding of constitutional harm.

**S-R4 — Independent requested-explanation ground.** Stevens and Souter's earlier Simmons Part III supplies a distinct Eighth Amendment position: when the defendant requests a judicial explanation of a legal sentencing term and there is a reasonable likelihood that jurors will misunderstand it, the judge must give an adequate explanation. They now assess the actual judicial charge as well as counsel's argument. They regard the until-death statements as meaningful but insufficient in combination with the final refusal to allow parole eligibility or ineligibility to be considered after the jury's parole questions. The unresolved relation between a life term and lawful ordinary release presents the required reasonable likelihood; no finding of the jurors' actual thoughts is made. Their position requires an effective judicial clarification of that legal relationship, without prescribing a word or denying possible clemency. It does not depend on State-raised future danger. The error's effect remains for Chapman review.

**S-R5 — Personal no-duty alternative.** Scalia and Thomas retain their published Simmons objection to a federal disclosure obligation based on future-danger relevance alone and their rejection of the independent Eighth Amendment extension. They do not deny the actual state-law alternatives or describe that personal view as the Court's governing law. In addition, they now accept S-R3-A as independently sufficient even under the governing Simmons rule. Neither ground supplies a conditional finding of harmlessness. No affirmative materially misleading assertion that ordinary release was available is established by this account; their separate concern about such an assertion is not adjudicated as an unproved new claim.

### Exact competing dispositions and remedy instructions

**Affirmance on S-R3-A:** Affirm the South Carolina Supreme Court's death-sustaining judgment against the federal parole-information claim, while rejecting its categorical statutory-stage rationale. Even assuming State-raised future danger, the actual combined instruction and permitted defense presentation satisfy the asserted protection, so neither a dangerousness finding nor a Chapman inquiry is needed to decide this claim. The decision does not find a constitutional error harmless; it finds this asserted informational error absent. The death sentence remains undisturbed by this claim, and no new hearing, noncapital sentence or guilt adjudication is ordered. The independent S-R4 rationale is not adopted.

**Vacatur/remand on S-R3-B:** Vacate the South Carolina Supreme Court's judgment insofar as it rejects this federal sentencing challenge and remand. The state court must determine whether the State actually raised future danger. If it did, the legal inadequacy of this combined presentation has been decided under S-R3-B and must not be reopened as though the Court had merely removed a structural bar. The State must then establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the choice of death. If it cannot, a new lawful penalty proceeding is required if death is pursued; a lawful noncapital disposition remains available. If the State did not raise future danger, Simmons's controlling due-process rule alone gives no relief. This disposition orders no present new penalty hearing and does not itself vacate or replace the death sentence; the conviction remains untouched.

**Additional remand supported by S-R4:** For Stevens and Souter, their independent instruction error exists on the supplied facts regardless of danger. They would remand its Chapman effect inquiry even if the State had not raised danger. If the State fails that burden, their position requires the same lawful new penalty proceeding if death is sought, with a lawful noncapital alternative. They do not find present harm, make error structural, or order an immediate new proceeding on the limited record. Their additional ground is not counted as governing law merely because they also join S-R1–S-R3-B.

### Justice-specific support and tests of the contrary path

His Ramdass opinion protects truthful legal consequences while declining to award relief on an unresolved predicate or effect. The new judicial statements and counsel's argument eliminate an important premise of the original draft: this is not a case of no accurate life-duration explanation. The affirmative sufficiency argument therefore has real weight. Stevens nevertheless treats the final direction excluding eligibility and ineligibility, after the parole questions and refused legal explanation, as leaving the relevant relationship between sentence duration and ordinary release unsettled. His position is not that a particular statutory word must be used; it is that the final account must allow the legal ineligibility to serve its constitutional function. The general murder wording does not prove actual confusion about life, but within the capital choice it remains sufficient to expose the legal ambiguity for his independent requested-explanation rule. His unchanged disposition thus rests on a revised complete-record application. He does not transplant Simmons's harmfulness finding to this record.
