## Entering law, separable components and competing paths

The unanimous 1995 Kansas judgment establishes compact pumping liability and reserves remedy. It rejects the requested pre-1985 laches bar because inexcusable delay was not established, while reserving categorical availability of laches and properly supported remedy equities. No finding of culpable delay can be inferred from the length of this litigation. Texas v. New Mexico enforces past compact obligations despite good-faith uncertainty, permits a suitable money remedy for a sovereign's own compact claim and recognizes that the State may receive and control that remedy even when private users experienced the shortages. It specifically treats postjudgment interest; its discussion does not already decide every prejudgment-interest issue here.

West Virginia v. United States requires consideration of the relevant governmental interests before applying the federal compensatory rule and finds interest necessary to preserve the underlying allocation. It rejects generalized hardship or a borrowed state sovereign-immunity exemption; an actual equitable bar such as laches remains distinct. Milwaukee treats ordinary interest as loss-of-use compensation, rejecting ordinary liability uncertainty or fault already reflected in principal as sufficient exceptions. Its maritime rule must be expressly extended by analogy, not silently treated as an interstate rule already decided.

**Sovereign ownership.** The principal path rejects immunity because Kansas owns and controls the compact claim and recovery. Its strongest objection is that agricultural loss measures conceal a suit for farmers' private debts. The reported ownership and control, and the compact's statewide allocation interest, answer that objection; no assignment or pass-through arrangement is established. A genuinely assigned private-debt suit would require different treatment. Economic evidence alone does not transform the plaintiff's legal right.

**Interest availability and commencement.** One path awards compensatory interest from the losses beginning in 1950, subject to an actual supported exception, because delayed compensation reduces the value of the agreed allocation. Its strongest objection is the extraordinary interval, uncertain violations and cumulative compounding burden. The competing path defers commencement to 1969 because of the Master's finding that Colorado then knew or should have known of the violation, or to the requested 1985 litigation year as a further accommodation to sovereign interests. Its strongest objection is that a compensatory rather than punitive remedy does not arise only after the debtor understands its obligation; the 1995 decision found no established inexcusable claimant delay. No exact filing-year selection is needed for the first path.

The provisional assessment of each Associate favors compensation from the supported losses rather than a notice-based cutoff. This is not a claim that Milwaukee compels that result or that the earlier laches ruling abolishes all remedial discretion. The reported additional grounds identify uncertainty, long elapsed time and aggregate amount, but no distinct finding of duplicative recovery, claimant-created loss of use or legally justified nonperformance. The narrower competing position remains legally available for historical reconciliation; the specific changed-law premise must be assessed there rather than assumed.

**Rates.** The report's year-specific individual-loss approach tracks the loss being valued. A mandatory State borrowing rate could undercompensate that economic loss even though Kansas owns the claim. Conversely, the individual rate must compensate actual loss of use rather than reward speculative investment or duplicate inflation already included elsewhere. The supported disposition overrules the categorical demand for State rates, preserving numerical implementation and nonduplication in the calculation proceeding. It invents no annual table or compounding interval.

**Crop model.** Kansas has affirmative support: accepted shortage attribution, crop acreage and prices; published crop-specific water/yield studies; and a 25-percent reduction for ordinary farming stresses. The noninvestment objection does not itself refute marginal yield where the Master identifies capital limits, credit access, debt aversion and imperfect cost information. Comparison studies involving additional capital can measure a different transaction from extra seasonal water without additional capital. Colorado's abandoned alternative is not affirmative proof for Kansas and does not shift the burden. The contrary path would recommit if those limitations still left the coefficients unsupported; on the reported supporting studies and adjustment, these specified exceptions do not establish that defect. No perfect-model requirement or permanent future accounting formula results.

## Remedy and remaining stage

Overrule Colorado's Eleventh Amendment exception, its categorical bar on interest for an unliquidated claim, its demand to substitute State borrowing rates solely because Kansas receives the award, and the described crop-loss objections. Sustain Kansas's commencement objection and recommit calculation of compensable interest from losses beginning in 1950 under the supported year-specific loss-of-use method. Preserve proof, actual nonduplication and any genuinely open concrete remedy issue; do not reopen settled compact liability or create a new allocation.

No final principal or interest sum, numerical rate, compounding frequency, private distribution command, future water-delivery rule or final closing decree is entered by this commitment. The unresolved 1985/1986 procedural-source discrepancy is nondecisive to this path and remains disclosed. If reconciliation selects a filing-based cutoff, obtain the complaint or docket rather than silently harmonizing the reports.
