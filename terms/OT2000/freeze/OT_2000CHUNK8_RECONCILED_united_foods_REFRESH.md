## Five-Associate sufficient rule and its limits

Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas adopt this operative proposition: the government cannot compel otherwise independent producers to finance the selected generic private advertising here merely because it expects the campaign to benefit the industry, because compulsory payment may overcome free riding, or because the economic financing lines are reasonably designed. The promotion is the very activity for which association is compelled; this program supplies no independently sufficient common function justifying that forced support. Advertising cannot become germane to an independently justified association simply by declaring itself the association's purpose.

Abood and Keller supply the need to identify the function justifying compelled association before evaluating the supported expression's relationship to it. Actual Glickman supplies the contrasting substantially collective agricultural program and its express limits; it does not itself decide the new case. Actual Southworth's broad neutral educational-expression opportunity is another sufficient and materially different function, so these Justices do not declare that every valid compulsory subsidy must accompany collective price or output regulation. United Foods' commercial objection remains protected despite lack of personal utterance or attribution and its freedom to advertise independently.

The government has actually offered a commercial-speech alternative. The five do not find it waived, deny that agricultural development can be substantial, or reject its reported evidence as nonexistent. Their sufficient legal objection remains even assuming the promotion can increase demand and the small-producer, no-credit and national-scope choices can be reasonable economic lines. That assumption is an analytical test favorable to the government, not a factual holding. The asserted effectiveness and fit answer how a desirable campaign may operate; they do not answer these Justices' objection to forcing independent dissenters to finance the selected private advocacy. No separate empirical Central Hudson disposition is required.

These five decline the government-speech alternative as an exercise of review discretion. Its legal inclusion does not eliminate the distinction between statutory oversight and actual adoption/control of the message, or require development of a materially different operational theory first raised here. They neither find the Secretary's powers nonexistent nor hold that the Council can never speak for government. The properly adjudicated private-subsidy controversy can be resolved without creating a government-speech remand. A general tax, an actual governmental message, a separate health or safety justification, unrelated research assessments and other independent purposes are not adjudicated by this ruling.

## Three-Associate independent commercial-speech ground

O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer do not obtain their result from the proposition that useful economic regulation is exempt from the First Amendment. They accept the expressive burden and consider the independent alternative on its merits: the same challenged assessment, constitutional controversy and relief are involved; United Foods has addressed that alternative at length; no affirmative waiver is established. Inclusion and adversarial presentation support this exercise of discretion but do not create a general rule admitting late evidence or requiring review of every reply argument. Government speech's conceded nonpresentation is a different issue.

For these three, the applicable commercial-speech inquiry requires a substantial governmental interest, direct advancement and reasonable fit, with government bearing the justification burden; no unlawful or misleading-message issue is supplied here. They conclude that the challenged program satisfies that inquiry. National agricultural-market maintenance, market development and the supported collective-action problem are sufficiently substantial in this commercial setting. The assessment finances the actual national generic-promotion activity directed to that objective, and the legislative/program materials and reported experience support the practical judgment that collective promotion can advance it. They reject the view that government must guarantee this particular producer's additional profits or prove a specific sales coefficient before undertaking the program.

The reported failures of voluntary campaigns bear on the need for compulsory rather than voluntary contributions. The program's national scope answers an objection based merely on the existence of geographic differences; the lack of a producer-specific regional return does not itself make a national market program disproportionate. Brand advertising seeks a firm's share and does not necessarily perform the same market-wide task, so an automatic credit is not required. The small-producer line can reasonably avoid disproportionate collection and administration; the reply's numerical illustration is arithmetic, not a verified cost study. These are the three Justices' legal assessments of the asserted objectives, actual program design and supported record, not new findings about every alleged market response or legislative motive.

The contrary arguments remain material and are answered on their actual terms: exempt beneficiaries do not by themselves make every anti-free-riding measure ineffective; regional spending need not track each payer's distribution; elastic supply, entry and intermediary capture do not establish that expanding the national market fails to serve the identified public object; brand-specific advertising and generic promotion need not be interchangeable. The inquiry calls for reasonable fit, not the least restrictive possible funding method, mandatory identical treatment of all commodities, guaranteed individual gain, universal brand credits or a prescribed empirical study.

The effectiveness review strengthens the historical evidence of these Justices' assessment of the existing record, but this reconciliation does not convert its quoted conclusion into an entered empirical finding. It adopts no numerical effect, methodology, lower-court evidentiary status or concession by United Foods. The published recitation is adequate to establish that this was an evidentiary submission actually considered in the historical judicial comparison; absence of the original study from the operator's supplied files is not a change in the Court's record. Neither that absence nor the party's contested quotation supplies a reason for these Justices to abandon their independently supported constitutional assessment and order new fact development. Ordinary record and remedial rules remain; no new admission or supplementation order is given.

A sufficient commercial-speech ground makes the separate government-speech issue unnecessary for these three. They therefore make no finding that an official authored, adopted or finally controlled the advertising. Their reversal permits lawful implementation under this constitutional ground; it does not automatically award all claimed assessments, resolve accounting or adjudicate every other program function.
