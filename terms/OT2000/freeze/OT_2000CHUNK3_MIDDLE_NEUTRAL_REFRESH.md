## Velazquez: three textual components and separate remedial choices

**Restriction and operation.** The copied section 504(a)(16) contains three separate components: the broad restriction on recipient participation in litigation, lobbying, or rulemaking involving welfare reform; permission for representation of an eligible individual seeking specific relief from a welfare agency; and the condition that the relief not involve an effort to amend or otherwise challenge existing law in effect when representation begins. The present challenge to the last component is not a challenge to every broad activity restriction.

The recipient restrictions extend to activities financed with non-LSC as well as LSC money under reported sections 504(d)(1)–(2). Characterizing this solely as a rule for expenditure of the federal dollars is incomplete. Entity-level restrictions and permitted affiliate arrangements are distinct. LSC interpreted the broad welfare-reform litigation restriction to exclude both challenges to and defenses of existing reform law; respondents reportedly did not deny that interpretation. This is not an agreement that the condition on individual-benefits representation treats all viewpoints alike.

Under LSC's reported interpretation an individual claimant could contest erroneous facts, misreading or misapplication of an existing statutory term, or an agency policy inconsistent with existing law. The recipient could not undertake representation designed to change welfare law or challenge constitutional or statutory validity. Prohibited validity arguments included a state statute's conflict with federal law and constitutional objections to state or federal law on its face or as applied. Ordinary misapplication and validity therefore cannot be collapsed into one category.

If a prohibited issue arose during representation, LSC's position required withdrawal consistent with applicable professional-responsibility rules. Counsel could and must explain why representation could not continue, could discuss views of welfare law with the client, and could refer the client to another lawyer. Those permissions do not establish an available substitute, authorize continued representation of the excluded claim, or eliminate a possible representational burden. The reported oral-argument interpretation also prevented counsel from answering a judge's constitutional inquiry while remaining in the prohibited representation. The same interpretation required withdrawal from the representation even if a non-LSC lawyer entered that same case to present the validity issue while the LSC lawyer proposed to continue other arguments.
