## Alternative-path comparison

**Completed-plea protection.** Neither the general effective-date/savings structure nor §304(b)'s repeal gives a sufficiently clear direction to extinguish the former opportunity for a person who completed a guilty plea while eligible before the change. The plea exchanges trial rights for the conviction's then-existing legal consequences. Removing an existing opportunity for lawful discretionary consideration adds a disability to that completed exchange even though no favorable exercise was promised. Under Landgraf, the relevant loss need not be a vested entitlement to the ultimate benefit. Reyes-Hernandez establishes one protected surrendered-option setting; Kolster expressly does not make surrender of a colorable deportability defense or subjective reliance a universal prerequisite. His plea occurred before eligibility and lacked the protected option established here. Goncalves preserves the distinction between legal eligibility and favorable discretion while reserving this separate plea question.

**Strongest contrary case.** Deportation governs future presence; Congress may change current administrative relief. The plea resolved a state criminal case and did not bind the federal immigration authority to grant a waiver. No individualized finding shows that this respondent knew of or relied on §212(c), and new proceedings commenced after the replacement system began. The protection path answers that the rule preserves the legal opportunity existing at the completed plea, not a criminal plea promise binding the Attorney General or a permanent immunity. Clear temporal language could remove that opportunity subject to constitutional limits, but the operative provisions do not clearly say so here. Objective loss of a legal option is sufficient; an invented individual reliance finding is neither needed nor made.

**Present-relief construction.** The provisions govern the Attorney General's authority to grant relief when a new removal proceeding seeks it. On this view the relevant regulated activity is the present administrative decision; the earlier conviction supplies a statutory predicate rather than the event to which the new legal consequence attaches. The old statute supplied an opportunity for a discretionary benefit, not immunity from a later change in the power to grant it. The explicit preservation of earlier proceedings, together with the new system's commencement, supports applying current relief law in the proceeding begun April 10, 1997. This route can reach reversal without finding unmistakable language retrospectively altering the criminal plea itself.

**Strongest contrary case.** Identifying the later administrative decision cannot alone make a new consequence prospective: Landgraf rejects just that kind of relabeling when a statute changes the legal consequences of a completed act. Here trial rights were surrendered while the waiver opportunity existed, unlike mere filing or an already ineligible plea. The three proponents accept that this is a material difference, but find the particular statutes regulate a prospective executive benefit rather than criminal adjudication or the completed exchange's enforceable terms. Their disagreement concerns the relevant activity and legal consequence, not a universal demand for vested rights or a claim that legal eligibility is indistinguishable from discretionary weighing.

**Individual-reliance remand.** A further remand could require proof that St. Cyr would not have pleaded guilty but for a particular immigration expectation. No Associate adopts that route. The five preserving eligibility find no basis in the actual temporal rule to add that subjective requirement to this objective, then-eligible plea transaction. The other three would apply the present-relief rule regardless of such ordinary expectations absent a different, established legal promise or enforceable claim; this record supplies none and this judgment does not decide one. A remand merely to search for a predicate the law does not require would not resolve a genuine factual gap.

## Compatibility, operative rule and complete relief

The five can fully join this operative temporal rule: when a lawful permanent resident completed a guilty plea before the relevant 1996 eligibility change and was then eligible to seek former §212(c) relief, AEDPA §440(d) and IIRIRA's repeal do not eliminate that former-law opportunity merely because removal proceedings began after April 1, 1997. The agency must consider the application under the former applicable eligibility criteria and, if those criteria are met, exercise lawful discretion. This rule requires neither an individual finding of subjective reliance nor an immigration promise, and it supplies no favorable waiver.

The holding resolves both challenged statutory changes for St. Cyr's March 8, 1996 plea. Trials, pleas made after the relevant change, and persons not eligible at the plea present different transactions outside the question resolved. No universal rule for every old conviction follows. A later factual or discretionary determination is ordinary application under the rule, not a postponed legal answer to this plea's temporal protection.

The affirmance sustains the actual habeas eligibility judgment and requires the immigration agency to entertain the former-law request rather than applying the new categorical exclusion. The Board must exercise whatever lawful discretion the former law supplies; no judicial reweighing, waiver, release, termination of removal proceedings, automatic hearing beyond what lawful adjudication requires, stay, stay dissolution or removal direction is ordered. Current procedural law and other applicable predicates remain distinct from the protected substantive opportunity.
