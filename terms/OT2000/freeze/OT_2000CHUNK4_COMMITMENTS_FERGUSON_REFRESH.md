## Corrected factual premises

The clinical concern was real, rather than a motive invented after police cooperation. In fall 1988 hospital staff perceived increasing prenatal cocaine use. In April 1989 they began screening urine of maternity patients suspected of cocaine use and referring positives for counseling and treatment. Use did not appear to change; that is an observation, not quantified proof of failure. About four months later a nurse learned of arrests elsewhere, consulted hospital counsel, and counsel offered cooperation with prosecution. That chronology establishes actual earlier clinical screening, but does not establish that every later test had identical medical indications, that clinical functions ceased, or that the programs were legally identical.

The district court found, for the abandoned Title VI claim, that the policy operated throughout the maternity departments and sought treatment and protection of mother and child rather than arrest as its goal. That finding remains part of the record. Its separate Fourth Amendment ruling rejected independent-purpose justification because of the police arrangement and disclosure of positives. Neither finding cancels the other: genuine health objectives can coexist with a challenged means of systematic evidence collection. No Justice here invents pretext, exclusive criminal motive or a finding that all treatment stopped.

The coordinated program's chain of custody, disclosure, arrest criteria and actual arrests remain established. Four plaintiffs were arrested during the initial policy without a treatment alternative; later arrests followed noncompliance or another positive. A treatment alternative cannot be attributed to all ten. Patient-specific consent, any material plaintiff-specific implementation difference and preservation of an independent legal objection to the jury instruction remain limited to what the sources actually establish.

## The two special-needs paths

### A. Reject this exception despite genuine clinical work

This path reverses the special-needs affirmance and remands the preserved consent issue and remaining litigation. It does not hold that any police contact destroys every special need. The required inquiry is whether a distinct institutional need sufficiently justifies dispensing with ordinary protection for this search and its scope. Edmond's controlling rule rejects ordinary drug-evidence gathering supported only by ancillary legitimate functions. Chandler requires an actual sufficient need linked to the operation. Vernonia treats urine collection and chemical analysis as searches despite participation, and upholds its own confined protective program with restricted nonpolice use.

The genuine health objective and antecedent clinical program strengthen the defendants' case. They nevertheless do not establish a special supervisory authority over ordinary patients or explain why obtaining prosecution-ready evidence, systematically disclosing it and operating arrest criteria is a reasonable incident of an independently adequate warrant exception. The proper distinction is between actual clinical function and the legal sufficiency of the asserted justification for this evidentiary operation. The former is now affirmatively supported; the latter remains the issue and is rejected at the challenged scope.

Griffin is a substantial contrary precedent, not a categorical police-use prohibition. Police supplied information and accompanied the probation officers; the gun supported a new criminal conviction. Scalia's opinion sustained the search because the probationer was in legal custody under a criminal sentence and valid supervisory regulations, with conditional liberty, continuing rehabilitation and a demonstrated institutional need for prompt action on reasonable grounds. It expressly declined to authorize every probation-officer search whenever reasonable grounds exist. Its reasons for departing from warrant and probable-cause requirements were tied to that lawful supervisory arrangement.

The ordinary patient relationship does not supply the equivalent legal custody, conditional liberty or compulsory supervision. The absence of probation does not itself defeat every medical special need: school and workplace precedents demonstrate other qualifying settings. But their existence requires their own supported institutional grounds and fit, not analogy from the shared word treatment. No comparable authority, emergency or independently sufficient clinical warrant exception for the challenged prosecution operation is established here. This conclusion does not decide that every diagnostic act requires a warrant, or that a genuine clinical search otherwise authorized must lose protection merely because police later receive evidence.

### B. Sustain a genuinely independent mixed clinical function

This path would affirm if the actual clinical operation independently justifies the search at its actual scope, despite added or instrumental criminal enforcement. Its strongest support is the preexisting medical screening and treatment, the health-goal finding, the need for prompt diagnostic response, Griffin's acceptance of mixed supervision and enforcement, and Scalia and Thomas's Edmond position permitting real independent roadway functions alongside narcotics objectives. A clinician's work is not necessarily ordinary policing merely because it finds evidence of a crime.

Its strongest objection is that the asserted medical function cannot justify more intrusion or use than its independent authority permits. The record does not supply a medical emergency for each plaintiff, an institutional custody relation, legally adequate consent, or an identified lawful exception that independently authorizes this arranged evidentiary operation. The later program's chain of custody, direct transmission and arrest rules are part of what must be justified, not automatically incidental use of evidence lawfully encountered. Clinical origin and a valid health goal are necessary evidence to consider, but do not themselves establish the legal sufficiency of warrantless nonconsensual testing as operated. In particular, the four initial arrests cannot be justified by a later treatment-before-arrest option.

The present record permits a bounded decision rejecting the appellate exception. It need not be stopped to recover every historical paper or remanded for an indefinite search for a new justification. It also does not establish a separate special-needs disposition for an earlier unchallenged medical-only program. A genuinely supported different clinical exception would require its actual predicates, rather than assuming this challenged policy was identical to the earlier program.

## Consent and the scope of remand

Collection, chemical analysis and information use must be identified separately; voluntary hospital attendance is not automatic consent to all of them. The district court instructed the jury to find consent to collection, cocaine testing and possible police disclosure, with defendants carrying a preponderance burden. The verdict establishes the trial result under that instruction. It does not itself establish a constitutional rule requiring three separately expressed permissions, and this handoff does not adopt one.

For deciding special needs, assume legally inadequate consent only as the question requires. The actual scope and voluntariness of consent, and sufficiency of the evidence under the properly applicable standard, remain genuine independent issues. The patients preserved an evidentiary-sufficiency challenge, which the Fourth Circuit did not resolve. No preserved independent challenge to the instruction's legal scope is established; no such challenge is invented or declared waived. If one is actually presented, its preservation and legal authority must be identified in the ordinary proceedings. The rejection of special needs supplies no affirmative ruling that disclosure alone is always a new search or always requires a separately expressed authorization.

A consent-based final affirmance is unavailable on the present handoff because the necessary appellate evaluation has not been performed and patient-specific facts are not supplied. Conversely, final judgment for all plaintiffs is unavailable merely from the assumption used for the exception question. The appropriate consequence on Path A is reversal of the special-needs ground and remand for the preserved consent issue and otherwise live liability and remedy questions, without directing a new trial or new evidentiary hearing by default.
