## 1. Refreshed operative record

At least one single-family home can be built on existing upland without additional wetlands fill. Approximately $200,000 of retained value is supported. Neither proposition establishes the maximum permitted use or value. The State expressly rejected a one-home ceiling at argument pages 26–28 and characterized its opposition as identifying a minimum. Its admission that the opposition did not mention the additional uncertainty is not an express waiver of that issue. No bench response is treated as a judicial finding or procedural ruling.

The record includes testimony and accounts of possible additional upland sites, including an island and an access-road issue, and testimony that two, three or four lots might support building. Those statements do not issue permits or establish compliance with the regulatory conditions. Petitioner contends that additional sites cannot be reached without prohibited wetlands fill; the transcript at pages 5–8 supplies that contention, not an agreed finding that all additional sites are inaccessible. At pages 31–32 the State accepts the question's restriction on ordinary-use wetlands filling while distinguishing the remaining property. At pages 34–38 it preserves its objection that the whole parcel's available use has not been settled. It does not concede that every ancillary access-road proposal is forbidden or that $200,000 exhausts the property's value.

The corporate purchase was in 1959 and personal title passed in 1978. The 1983 and 1985 dates identify applications, not automatically the dates of denial or injury. Petitioner identifies the final denial in 1986 as the asserted taking event at argument page 16. That contention places his alleged injury after his own acquisition but does not establish when or whether a taking occurred. No finding establishes a prior accrued compensation claim or its transfer.

The complaint and trial memorandum emphasize denial of all development and Lucas. Both lower decisions and the State's supplemental post-trial memorandum discuss Penn Central. At argument pages 23–24 petitioner requests a remand including contextual review, while pages 54–59 continue his insistence on the Lucas theory. These sources neither establish that every partial-taking argument was fully preserved nor prove that the contextual issue was absent from the adjudication. The State reports a trial nuisance finding and confirms at pages 25–26 that the state supreme court did not address it. Its status and any preserved alternative defense must be distinguished from a final state appellate holding.

The whole parcel remains the property presented for review. The wetlands-only denominator was not pressed below or presented in the petition. The 74-lot subdivision underlying the $3,150,000 compensation demand was not a submitted permit application; neither its permissibility nor its value is found. A compensation proceeding does not itself confer a building permit.

### Section 130A remains complete and unchanged

An exception to an otherwise prohibited activity may be granted only if and when the applicant demonstrates all three conditions: (1) a compelling public purpose benefiting the public as a whole rather than individual or private interests, consisting of one or more of public-infrastructure activity such as utilities, energy, communications or transportation; water-dependent activity generating substantial economic gain to the State; or activity providing shore access to broad segments of the public; (2) all reasonable steps to minimize environmental impacts and/or use conflict; and (3) no reasonable alternative means or location for serving the cited compelling public purpose. Meeting the necessary conditions permits, rather than requires, an exception. The supported beach-club denial concerns failure of the public-purpose requirement; it supplies no independent finding under conditions (2) and (3). Private ownership is not itself inconsistent with every enumerated public-purpose category. No new exception, entitlement or compliance finding is added.

## 2. Components and competing lawful paths

### F — Finality

**Entering law:** Thomas's unanimous Suitum opinion requires a definitive agency account of permitted use and rejects an act incapable of changing that completed land-use decision as a ripeness prerequisite. Its application concerned off-parcel transfer rights, not potentially developable land on the claimant's own parcel. Lucas preserves ordinary finality while allowing review of an elapsed period of a categorical prohibition that could not then be lifted. Neither decision makes a valuation ceiling a universal jurisdictional requirement or establishes that any two denials suffice.

**Path F1: sufficient finality of the challenged wetlands restriction.** The regulations, actual denials and State's express distinction at argument page 31 settle the substantial private wetlands filling whose denial gives rise to this claim. Another version of the same ordinary private filling request cannot alter that answer. The Court may consider the whole parcel's actual residual uses and their value when evaluating the extent of the burden without requiring the owner first to maximize development elsewhere on the parcel. This is a conclusion about the sufficiency of the agency's settled application, not a finding that only one home is possible.

The strongest objection is that potential additional on-parcel homes and access arrangements concern the type and intensity of use, not merely valuation. F1 accepts that those possibilities remain relevant and does not declare every road proposal prohibited. It regards the established restriction as sufficiently definite to examine the existing compensation claim while leaving those factual and remedial consequences for lawful adjudication. This path extends Suitum's separation of a settled restriction from unresolved merits consequences to a partial-development setting; it does not pretend Suitum involved this same factual pattern. Its narrower formulation must preserve a genuinely unresolved application of the challenged restriction if later evidence establishes one, rather than treating every unsubmitted use as a valuation issue.

**Path F2: insufficient finality of the burden on the whole parcel.** The actual denials settled the major proposed filling uses but did not fix the materially different upland and access possibilities shown by the testimony. Because the case proceeds on the whole parcel, those alternatives may affect the actual restriction itself. A definitive maximum dollar valuation is unnecessary, but a definitive enough account of permissible development remains necessary. The State has not waived that objection, and no automatic inference of futility follows from the party's assertion about roads.

The strongest objection is that F2 makes a settled wetlands prohibition unreviewable until the owner proposes additional uses of remaining property that do not change the challenged refusal. F2 answers that the remaining question here concerns materially possible development of this same parcel, not an off-parcel market transaction or endless variations on an already rejected design. It requires no exhaustive application campaign, no guaranteed permit and no new exhaustion rule. It supports affirmance on finality without a holding that the regulation never effects a taking.

The transcript resolves the prior uncertainty about what the State actually said. It does not resolve the legal disagreement between F1 and F2 or establish either party's factual characterization as a finding. No finality commitment below depends on an agreed $200,000 ceiling, a finding that all additional sites need forbidden fill, or a State concession barring every possible access road.

### A — Acquisition notice; T — injury and claim ownership

Dodd Part III, authored by Stevens and joined by all eight Associates, requires the combined Penn Central inquiry. Retained value, acquisition under an existing restriction or a legitimate regulatory purpose alone is not dispositive. Its result rested on actual profitable timber use, the necessary-and-accessory dwelling condition, nonassuring communications and the governmental character of that restriction. It supplies no factual outcome for Palazzolo. Each Associate remains committed to rejecting an acquisition-notice bar and to allowing actual regulatory history to bear on expectations without making it conclusive.

The strongest contrary argument is that existing law defines the acquired interest and reasonable expectations. Actual background title law and actual expectations remain relevant; notice alone cannot perform both inquiries or extinguish constitutional protection. Lucas preserves genuine background property and nuisance limitations under their own requirements. The State's public-trust and historical-title assertions at argument pages 44–45 remain contested advocacy and do not establish such a limitation here.

T is separate. Dodd's rejection of a notice bar neither assigns an accrued claim nor determines when this alleged injury occurred. Petitioner's asserted 1986 injury cannot be treated as a pre-1978 taking merely because the regulation existed earlier. Conversely, acquisition of title does not establish ownership of an earlier completed-injury claim. No supplied finding or adjudicated transfer permits a final ruling on that separate question. The eight Associates would not replace the rejected notice rule with an unproved accrued-claim defect, and would not award a prior owner's compensation by assuming transfer. Any necessary timing, ownership and state-law transfer issue remains for resolution on its actual predicates. No general claim-transfer doctrine is newly announced.

### L — Complete deprivation

Meaningful retained residential use defeats the Lucas complete-deprivation category on the whole-parcel premise presented. Dodd Part II, directly joined by all eight Associates, supplies that application. At least one buildable home and supported substantial value suffice; no maximum is needed to establish that loss is not complete. The strongest contrary argument is that the alleged loss is very large compared with the asserted subdivision value, or that the wetlands themselves cannot be used. A severe percentage loss is not complete deprivation, and the separate wetlands denominator is not properly presented. No new percentage or parcel rule follows. This ground requires no present resolution of a background-law defense.

### P — Contextual review, preservation and remedy

If F permits review, the proper merits disposition rejects A's categorical acquisition rule and preserves L's rejection of complete loss. It returns the existing compensation case for proceedings consistent with those holdings, rather than ordering a new trial or directing consideration of an entirely new claim. The lower courts must identify the actual contextual issue litigated or passed upon, the effect of the invalid acquisition-only premise, any independently sufficient lawful ground, and the scope of any remaining preserved defense. They may apply ordinary preservation and sufficiency rules; no forfeiture or entitlement to a second evidentiary presentation is found here.

The affirmative support for this limited remand is that both lower decisions discussed Penn Central, the State submitted an alternative argument under it, and petitioner seeks review of the acquisition and residual-value propositions. His emphasis on Lucas does not by itself remove a federal issue the judgments addressed. The strongest contrary argument is that a developed partial-taking claim was not presented and a remand would manufacture one. The proposed instruction answers that objection by neither reviving abandoned arguments nor assuming every preservation predicate. Reconsideration of an adjudicated legal ground is distinct from adjudication of an entirely new factual theory.

The record does not establish a final contextual merits outcome. A mere reference to Penn Central does not establish that the lower judgment independently applied all required considerations free of the acquisition-only premise. Nor is the existence of such a ground declared impossible. The remand must preserve any actual independent ground or properly preserved background-law issue for its correct treatment. Dodd's record-specific summary judgment does not compel affirmance or an evidentiary remand here. No value ceiling, taking date, compensation amount, permit, transfer of a prior claim or satisfaction of section 130A is ordered.
