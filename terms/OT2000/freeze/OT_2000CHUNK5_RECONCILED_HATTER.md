## Threshold: the adopted statutory affirmance and the later judgment

### Sufficient ground and strongest contrary path

Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support this confined conclusion: the adopted no-quorum disposition does not prevent this Court from considering the two compensation questions on proper review of the later judgment. The earlier Court could consider neither the merits nor whether to undertake merits review. Pink's binding-party statement arose from an equal division after consideration of the controversy, rather than this inability to act. That distinction, together with the difficulty of an equivalent substitute for this Court's review, defeats the asserted law-of-the-case bar in this proceeding. The earlier order operated as the prescribed affirmance below; it is not relabeled an ordinary denial of certiorari and did not disappear because it supplied no precedent.

Section 2109's express equation with an equally divided affirmance supplies the strongest contrary argument. Pink, Durant and Hertz distinguish interparty force from precedent, so lack of a precedential rationale alone cannot justify present review. The five address that objection by the limited difference between a prior merits-considered disposition and the adopted inability to consider the case, in this continuing review posture. They do not decide that every statutory affirmance lacks party-binding effect, that every unreasoned order is revisitable, or that a constitutional issue automatically defeats finality. The prior liability direction's lawful operation during the remand is preserved; the question is its asserted insulation from the present review.

Garvey Part I confirms the channel for reviewing a necessary antecedent ruling through the later judgment. It does not independently overcome an earlier Supreme Court order. Christianson, 486 U.S. 800, 815–818, separates power from law-of-the-case practice but also warns that failure to explain a decision does not mean no legal rule was decided. Messenger similarly supplies same-action reconsideration principles without an intervening equal-division Supreme Court affirmance. Neither supplies a compulsory extraordinary-circumstances test for this precise setting. The five therefore withdraw the provisional characterization of the present holding as an exercise of extraordinary reconsideration justified by constitutional importance and an unfinished damages phase. Those facts were present historically too and do not establish a changed legal premise.

The historical Court's threshold reasoning is evidence of the five Justices' positions; it is not cited as existing law of this timeline. The legal distinctions rest on the adopted order, Section 2109, Pink and the established limits of the comparison, together with the current Garvey channel. No new lower-court finding or missing procedural condition is filled by inference.

## Merits: ordinary taxation and the two different extensions

### Common four-Justice constitutional ground

Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer retain their commitments to permit ordinary nondiscriminatory taxation of judicial compensation and to invalidate this discriminatory Social Security transition. The general-tax rule encompasses taxes enacted after appointment, including changes in rates or conditions, while preserving the prohibition on direct salary reductions and discriminatory indirect reductions. Evans is displaced only insofar as it prohibits such ordinary shared taxation because the judge took office first. O'Malley's civic-obligation reasoning is adopted for the incumbent question; its earlier holding is not falsely described as already having decided that question. Will's distinction between a general tax and a direct compensation reduction remains essential.

The Medicare extension applied generally to federal employees and supplies no described discriminatory transition. The four therefore reverse that liability component. The Social Security extension supplies a different comparison: current federal employees facing entry into the program. Most could remain outside it; mandatory participants in contributory retirement systems could avoid an additional payroll burden through the adjustment; the incumbent judges' noncontributory arrangement did not permit that protection. The resulting special new burden falls outside the ordinary-tax rule. A pension-coordination explanation deserves consideration, but the existence of a noncontributory judicial system does not itself justify using the transition to offset the protection attached to judicial compensation. No finding of congressional hostility is required.

This explanation rests on the transition facts in the frozen neutral materials. The historical Court's fuller discussion of individual costs, prior coverage percentages and retirement comparisons is not converted into additional current record findings or a mandatory four-prong test. The sufficient existing record identifies the affected incumbent class, the two distinct protections for other incumbents, and their absence for these judges. No new statistical or individual-benefit premise is necessary to these commitments. The combined transition, rather than any isolated difference between pension plans, supplies the discrimination conclusion.

For **Kennedy**, the historical full join and current Acker actual-operation join remain compatible; nothing changes the ordinary-tax/discriminatory-transition distinction. For **Souter**, the historical full join and current Acker reservation require a candid limited displacement of Evans without repudiating its judicial-independence protection. For **Ginsburg**, her Acker compensation-tax and comparator analysis informs method but supplies no Article III answer; the historical full join remains supported on the distinct constitutional analysis. For **Breyer**, his Acker local-function dissent concerns the ordinance's cumulative operation, not immunity from generally shared national taxes; his own historical Hatter rationale fits the described national extensions. None of those current positions creates a changed premise requiring a departure on either tax.

Their common join barriers remain: no automatic approval of everything called a tax, no permission for direct salary reductions because they also affect other officials, no identical-retirement-plan requirement, no Article III holding derived from Section 111 consent, and no hostile-purpose requirement for discriminatory diminution. Acker's independent Compensation Clause reservation remains a reservation until this event resolves the questions actually presented.

### Scalia: compensation package rather than discrimination alone

Scalia's provisional Medicare-validity vote and Social Security discrimination join are corrected. His full historical separate writing, 532 U.S. at 581–586, distinguishes government acting as sovereign in imposing a general tax from government withdrawing an employment-conditioned compensation benefit. The prior exemption from these payroll taxes was attached to federal employment. Its withdrawal from sitting judges therefore reduces their compensation package even when it leaves them paying the same tax as ordinary citizens. Generality does not save a reduction of compensation itself; Will's direct-reduction rule supports that distinction.

He agrees that Evans should be displaced insofar as it immunizes judges from general taxes imposed in the government's sovereign capacity. He does not accept the four Justices' sufficient rule that nondiscrimination alone validates the Medicare extension. Both challenged extensions withdraw an existing employment benefit, so he would affirm both liability components. His Social Security agreement is not a join in the discrimination rationale: on his ground the withdrawal would remain forbidden if every incumbent federal employee experienced the same new charge. His analysis does not decide an exchange of the exemption for a different benefit equivalent in value; his historical footnote expressly excludes that unpresented situation.

The frozen independent inference relied on his Acker compensation-tax characterization and Cleveland Indians accounting distinction. Neither changes this premise. Acker expressly leaves Article III open, and classifying an exaction as a tax does not decide whether withdrawing its employment exemption diminishes compensation. Cleveland Indians concerns allocation of employer taxes on backpay, not the constitutional employment package. No changed current law, record, relief or prior published Scalia position answers his specific objection. The correction thus follows his particular legal distinction after testing the apparent alternatives; it is not an automatic historical vote substitution.

**Join boundaries:** Scalia supports consideration of the merits, the contextual no-cure ground, and the two liability judgments on his ground. He supports limited displacement of Evans's universal general-tax immunity but not the Medicare-validity rule or the discrimination-only explanation of Social Security liability. An opinion may not count his agreement to overrule part of Evans as agreement with every ordinary-tax application.

## Later raises, limitations and remedy

Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support the contextual no-cure rule and application: a later salary increase does not automatically terminate or satisfy an earlier unconstitutional deduction merely because its amount exceeds the tax. The court must consider the relationship between that increase and the earlier diminution. The general increase described in the lower-court record did not make these judges whole for that injury; it represented independently owed compensation. Treating it as an automatic cure would leave the discriminatory reduction operating against the newly lawful salary.

Will does not establish the government's proposed automatic rule. Its pleaded Year One interval ended at a subsequent increase, but the precise continuing-tax cure question was not adjudicated there. A lawful, genuinely compensatory measure is not excluded in every circumstance. No rule requiring a particular formula or an unpresented form of appropriation is added. The record supports rejection of this proposed cure, not a holding that no later increase can ever repair a compensation violation.

The five individual grounds remain compatible after comparison: **Scalia** expressly joined the historical cure part and protects an existing compensation package; **Kennedy** preserves the distinction between new salary entitlement and satisfaction of a prior injury; **Souter** rejects unsupported netting of separate legal obligations; **Ginsburg** treats the general raise as owed compensation rather than an unexpressed payment of this liability; **Breyer** considers the raise's actual context and the continuing unequal burden. Their cited current tax-accounting positions are consistent supporting analogies, not the source of a new constitutional offset rule.

**Thomas** supports affirmance of the no-cure result, as his historical disposition expressly did, on his retained compensation-entitlement ground. His separate Evans premise does not turn a generally owed raise into satisfaction of deductions already held unlawful. He is not counted as joining the five's complete purpose-and-context explanation, and his support does not establish their broader formulation through agreement by inference. His preferred remedy includes timely Medicare as well as Social Security deductions because he would affirm both liability components.

All six preserve these implementation boundaries independently of their merits disagreements:

- The en banc limitations ruling displaced the panel's blanket Medicare bar. Within the supplied review scope, the surviving continuing-claim treatment governs recurring timely deductions and excludes deductions more than six years before the relevant filing. This handoff does not adopt a general new Supreme Court accrual rule or decide an unpresented challenge to that framework.
- A Medicare reversal eliminates compensation recovery for that component on the constitutional ground, not on the superseded limitations theory. A Justice favoring Medicare affirmance preserves that recovery only within the lawful periods and entitlement requirements.
- Social Security liability concerns the affected incumbents. It supplies neither a blanket exemption for every federal judge nor invalidation of the tax for every taxpayer.
- Remand implements the actual assembled liability judgment and the surviving lower-court limitations framework. The lower court determines individual lawful amounts; no numerical award, invented election, new evidentiary-hearing mandate or further filing condition is supplied.
- No later-appointee claim, different pension exchange, new tax classification or hypothetical corrective statute is adjudicated. The existing lawful scope of O'Malley and Will remains as expressly described, rather than being reopened through an unnecessary neighboring question.
