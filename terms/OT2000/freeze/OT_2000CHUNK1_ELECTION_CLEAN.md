## 2. Actual grounds and qualifications of the November 21 judgment

Source: [Florida Supreme Court opinion](../sources/chunk1-neutral/palm-beach-state-2000-11-21.txt), all 45 pages, including footnotes and the concluding directive, read in full. Page references below are the source's printed pages and corresponding page markers. The propositions in this section describe the lower court's decision; they do not adopt its conclusions as federal law.

| Subject | Source-grounded account |
|---|---|
| Manual-recount authority | Pages 10–14 and 19–21 distinguish an error in vote tabulation from an error confined to the tabulating system. Section 102.166(4) permits an eligible candidate, political committee, or party to request a manual recount with reasons before county certification or within 72 hours after midnight of election day, whichever is later. The board may authorize the sample; it is not automatically obliged to do so. A sample includes at least three precincts and at least one percent of the relevant votes, with precincts selected by the requester. If the sample indicates an error that could affect the outcome, subsection (5) requires one of three responses: correct the error and recount the remaining precincts using the system, request verification of the software, or manually recount all ballots. The statute does not invariably require the third alternative. |
| Voter intent and recount administration | Page 13 recites §101.5614(5)'s protection where the board finds a clear indication of voter intent and describes subsection (6) as requiring discard when the board cannot discern intent. These are the state court's statutory recitations, not a finding that every undervote is legal. Page 21 quotes §102.166(6)–(7): the recount is public; teams contain at least two electors, members of at least two parties when possible, and no candidate involved in the race; indeterminate ballots go to the board for its voter-intent determination. |
| Deadline and late-return discretion | Pages 22–29 quote the seventh-day, 5 p.m. general-election deadline in §§102.111 and 102.112. Section 102.111 directs disregard of missing counties; §102.112 permits late returns to be ignored and the returns on file to be certified. The latter also prescribes personal fines of $200 per board member per day and an appeal to the Florida Elections Commission. The state court treats §102.112 as the later and more specific provision, gives effect to the fine provision, and reads the code together with manual recounts, official returns, and overseas ballots. It retains the filing deadline while rejecting automatic exclusion of late amended returns. A manual recount creates no express statutory filing-deadline exception; the court says so at page 29. |
| Constitutional and remedial grounds | Pages 29–39 apply the Florida constitutional protection of suffrage and earlier state election decisions to constrain the Secretary's discretion. Under the state court's announced rule, returns may be ignored only if their lateness would prevent a candidate, elector, or taxpayer from contesting certification under §102.168, or prevent Florida voters from participating fully in the federal electoral process. Exclusion must be reasonable and necessary, and the Secretary must explain a legally supported reason. The court says neither circumstance was claimed to apply at that point. Page 38 also withholds fines when late certification results from compliance with the code, administrative rules, or other good cause. These limits are judicial constructions; they are not quoted exceptions enacted in §102.112. |
| Operative remedy | Pages 39–40 invoke equity because of conflicting advisory opinions and the exceptional circumstances. Amended certifications must be accepted by 5 p.m. November 26 if the office is open for receipt; if it is closed for that special purpose, they must be accepted until 9 a.m. November 27. The state court's November 17 certification stay lasts until that acceptance period expires, and the certificates must include accepted amended returns. No rehearing is allowed. The frozen packet's unconditional November 26 formulation must be read with this express contingency. The source does not establish whether the office actually opened, the later returns received, or the eventual certified totals. |

Additional precision: the five-day protest period under §102.166(1) and the 72-hour manual-recount request period under §102.166(4) are distinct; both have their stated certification alternatives. Pages 17–18 separately describe post-certification contests under §102.168. Pages 28–29 recite the overseas-ballot rule: an otherwise proper federal-office ballot from an overseas elector must be postmarked or signed and dated no later than election day and received no later than ten days afterward. The state court uses that distinct rule in construing the certification provisions; it is not an unconditional ten-day exception for every ballot or return.

The opinion consequently has identifiable statutory, constitutional, and equitable reasoning. It cannot be characterized as resting on wholly unknown grounds. The narrower uncertainty is the legal dependence of its particular limits and equitable date on the state constitution, its view of Article II's effect on those sources, and the significance it attached to federal electoral timing. Its footnote 55 cites 3 U.S.C. §§1–10 without supplying a specific §5 analysis. Its objective of preserving time for contests does not itself establish a state-law December 12 termination rule. Footnote 10's statement that no party challenged the constitutionality of Florida's election laws does not resolve preservation of a federal challenge to the judicial construction or remedy announced in this decision.

## 3. Yoshina and the independent due-process inquiry

The operative entering-law provision is **Election guidance, authoritative construction and neutral approval thresholds**, effective January 21, 1999, from Citizens for a Constitutional Convention v. Yoshina. The following rule and limits are copied mechanically from the authorized [baseline slice](../entering-law/OT_2000CHUNK1_BASELINE.md):

**Current rule:** A neutral judicial construction of an ambiguous election provision does not violate due process merely because officials previously issued a contrary advisory interpretation. The actual official inducement, dissemination, reliance and voting circumstances must be examined for fundamental unfairness; a legally possible construction does not by itself answer a supported inducement claim. A neutral referendum threshold may include blank ballots in the approval denominator without compelling political opposition when blanks remain accurately counted and reported as blanks and the rule does not discriminate among identifiable voters. The legal consequence of abstention is distinct from falsely attributing a message.

**Exceptions and limits:** Deliberate misleading directions, pervasive frustration of an effective choice and false attribution of views remain distinct questions. Federal relief does not authorize replacement of authoritative state law merely because informal advice differed.

Yoshina defeats a theory that disagreement with an earlier advisory reading, or the mere timing of an authoritative construction after voting, automatically establishes a due-process violation. It does not resolve whether this particular construction amounts to a fundamentally unfair change. Apply its factual inquiry independently of Article II: who gave what guidance, when and how it was disseminated, what conduct it induced, what reliance is supported, and what the voting circumstances show. These are the subjects the existing rule requires examination of, not a new mandatory multi-element formula.

The supplied opinion identifies post-election advice and conflict about recounts and deadlines, including advice sought by canvassing boards and the Secretary's November 13–15 positions. It does not establish a particular pre-election assurance to voters, its dissemination, or detrimental voter reliance. Do not invent those facts or infer from this limited source that no such contention exists anywhere in the case record.

Analytical possibilities, not invented filings: the challenge can argue that mandatory acceptance under newly restricted discretion and a judicially selected date altered the operative election procedures after voting, rather than merely resolving an ambiguity. The opposing argument points to the pre-existing manual-recount authorization, the competing mandatory and permissive return provisions, established state construction principles, and the limited remedial response to conflicting advice. Each must address the actual statutory scheme and Yoshina's distinction between authoritative construction and supported fundamental unfairness. A decision rejecting a categorical post-election-change theory need not decide an unsupported individualized reliance claim. An affirmative finding of deception, reliance, or fundamental unfairness requiring facts beyond this source remains unavailable without those specific facts.

## 4. Lawful federal review and neutral decisional paths

**Palm Beach review channel.** The appropriate channel is certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257(a), reviewing a final judgment of the highest state court on the supplied federal challenges. The November 21 decision reverses the two trial orders and itself gives a complete, binding certification directive, with no further merits adjudication ordered in that proceeding and no rehearing allowed. The later county work implements that directive. A possible separate statutory contest is a distinct proceeding; its existence does not make this certification judgment nonfinal. The concrete effect on the competing candidates supplies an adverse controversy. On these supplied facts there is no unresolved finality blocker requiring assumption of an extraordinary writ or a federal original election contest.

That conclusion establishes a lawful review channel, not the federal merits. Section 1257 does not authorize free-standing correction of state law. The question is whether the state judgment itself violates the identified federal constraint. Ordinary deference to the state court's construction does not answer that claim, and describing the reasoning as state law does not by itself defeat a challenge that the resulting state action is federally forbidden. Preservation and scope must remain tied to the submitted federal challenge; the source's observation about facial challenges to the statute supplies no contrary waiver finding.

**Clarification path.** Minnesota v. National Tea Co., 309 U.S. 551 (1940), supports seeking clarification when uncertainty about the basis of a state judgment prevents sound federal review. A supported clarification here would identify exactly whether, and to what extent, the state constitution supplies an independent constraint or an interpretive aid, and whether the court's remedial date rests on a particular understanding of federal electoral requirements. It must acknowledge the statutory grounds already explained above. Clarification is available only if that remaining ambiguity matters to the federal question; it is not a mandatory first outcome or a means to ask the state court to repeat an already clear holding. Vacatur and remand and a jurisdiction-retaining request have different procedural consequences; any selected order must state its authority and effect rather than treating the forms as interchangeable.

**Federal merits paths.** The judgment may be sustained if the actual construction and remedy fall within the pre-existing statutory and state lawmaking system and no separate due-process violation is shown. It may be set aside to the extent a demonstrated Article II displacement or due-process violation supports that relief. McPherson v. Blacker, 146 U.S. 1 (1892), supplies the Article II legislative-direction setting; Smiley v. Holm, 285 U.S. 355 (1932), supplies the relevance of the state lawmaking process in its distinct congressional-election setting. Neither is silently treated as already deciding the present presidential recount dispute. A merits opinion must answer the supported contrary path and identify the particular legislative rule or federal protection that does the work.

Then-effective §5 concerns a State that has provided, by laws enacted before the day appointed for choosing electors, for final determination through judicial or other methods of a controversy or contest concerning their appointment. A determination made under those laws at least six days before the electors meet receives conclusive treatment in ascertaining the electors for the federal electoral count. The packet supplies December 12 as that six-day date and December 18 as the meeting date. Loss of the conclusive-treatment benefit is not by its own force a prohibition on all subsequent counting. A different asserted state-law or federal deadline needs its own legal basis. Foster v. Love, as supplied in the [Holdings slice](../entering-law/OT_2000CHUNK1_HOLDINGS.md), concerns final congressional selection before the federal election day and expressly leaves presidential timing unresolved; it does not decide §5's remedial operation here. None of these paths establishes a winner, authorizes rejection of ascertainable lawful votes without a legal ground, or decides a later contest not yet before the Court.

Neither December 12 nor December 18 alone establishes how much lawful recounting could be completed or that a particular remedy was impossible.

**Finality must fit this different judgment.** Section 1257 is the relevant state-judgment review channel. Unlike the completed certification directive, the supplied contest judgment leaves disputed counts and additional adjudication open. Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn, 420 U.S. 469 (1975), permits review of specified classes of effectively final state judgments despite remaining proceedings; urgency alone is not one of those classes. Any reliance on a Cox exception must identify the conclusively settled federal issue, the remaining proceedings, whether later review would remain available, and how the particular exception fits. A claim that immediate reversal would end the litigation and delayed review would seriously erode a federal policy requires those conditions to be shown, not simply recited. If the actual later posture does not satisfy §1257 finality or a recognized exception, the lawful result is a threshold disposition rather than assumed merits jurisdiction. This is a conditional reviewability question for the actual follow-on record, not a finding that jurisdiction is necessarily absent.

The existing alternatives remain open: no federal violation; a correctable equality defect requiring consistent treatment under lawful standards; or a proved legislative or timing constraint requiring narrower or terminating relief. The actual record must support the classification of the ballots, material inequality, source of authority, and feasibility relevant to the selected path. No complete statewide ballot standard, indisputable deadline, infeasibility finding, or final tally is supplied by this supplement.
