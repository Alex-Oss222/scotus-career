## Shafer v. South Carolina

### Corrected account of the instructions, argument and jury questions

The following paragraphs are mechanically copied from the sanitized control artifact:

The trial judge repeatedly explained that life imprisonment continued until the defendant's death. The judge also described the life verdict as imprisonment for the balance of the defendant's natural life. This is more specific than a bare reference to the ordinary meaning of life imprisonment.

Defense counsel requested permission to read the statutory language expressly excluding parole to the jury; the judge refused. Counsel nevertheless argued that a life sentence meant Shafer would die in prison, spend his natural life there, and would not go home. The record thus contains permitted natural-life argument alongside the refusal to permit the requested express parole explanation.

The jury submitted two written questions during sentencing deliberations. One asked whether there was any remote chance that a person convicted of murder could become eligible for parole; the second asked the conditions under which a person convicted of murder could become eligible. Both questions referred to conviction of murder; neither expressly specified a sentence of life imprisonment.

After receiving the questions, the judge again defined life imprisonment as continuing until the offender's death and added that parole eligibility or ineligibility was not for the jury's consideration. The prosecutor had urged an ordinary-and-plain-meaning instruction in the discussion with the court; the reported instruction actually given used the until-death language and the direction excluding parole from consideration.

The original packet's description of an ordinary-meaning direction must therefore not be used as the actual charge. Nor may the refusal to permit the requested express parole language become a finding that every accurate defense explanation was excluded. The corrected record contains repeated judicial until-death language, permitted counsel argument, a refused express parole explanation, two questions about murder convictions generally, and a supplemental direction that parole was not for the jury. The facts do not settle whether these communications adequately explained the legally available sentencing alternative or whether the jury misunderstood it.

### Entering authority and independent components

The operative Standards entry is **Parole ineligibility as an answer to future-danger evidence**. Its present operation is effective June 12, 2000 for Ramdass's completed-adjudication and conditional-information boundary; Simmons's June 17, 1994 substantive and direct-review remedy components and O'Dell's earlier retroactivity components remain separately identified. This is direct review; the habeas gateways discussed in Ramdass and O'Dell do not replace the direct-review inquiry.

Simmons's controlling due-process rule requires an effective opportunity, through accurate defense presentation or an adequate instruction, to explain ordinary-parole ineligibility when the State raises future dangerousness and the only legally available noncapital alternative is life without ordinary parole. No fixed script or guarantee against clemency or future legislation is required. Its harmful-error conclusion concerned its own record, including complete exclusion of the legal answer; that finding cannot be transplanted to Shafer's different permitted argument and judicial charge. Its direct-review remedy rule places the burden on the State to establish beyond a reasonable doubt that a constitutional error did not contribute to death. Error is not structural, and neither harmlessness nor harmfulness is established merely by reciting this burden.

Gardner supplies the opportunity to answer material capital-sentencing information; Skipper supplies future-danger rebuttal and preserves its distinct mitigation protection. Ramos permits truthful commutation information without authorizing a misleading account of ordinary parole. Ramdass preserves accuracy about actual and conditional legal consequences. Shafer's supplied statutory account concerns actual ineligibility at the post-aggravator choice, not an expected additional conviction. The jury's general murder questions do not themselves determine the prosecution's future-danger position.

**Adequacy if the due-process rule applies.** One supported argument is that the repeated judicial until-death and natural-life descriptions, reinforced by counsel's permitted argument that Shafer would die in prison and not go home, conveyed the needed substance despite the refusal to use the requested statutory parole wording. **Independent requested-instruction ground.** Souter and Stevens's published Simmons Part III proposed an Eighth Amendment judicial explanation when a defendant requests clarification of a sentencing term reasonably likely to be misunderstood. That ground is noncontrolling and does not require a State-raised future-danger issue. Their distinction between counsel's advocacy and a judicial statement warrants separate consideration; the actual judicial until-death instructions here must also be assessed, rather than treating counsel as the only source of explanation. The request, the full charge, the question wording and the parole-not-for-jury direction bear on its trigger and satisfaction. Scalia and Thomas's published Simmons opposition distinguished parole law from individualized character, record and offense mitigation and favored state latitude absent materially misleading state assertions. O'Connor and Kennedy's narrower Simmons ground reserved a federal duty outside State-raised danger. These are date-eligible competing grounds for individual assessment, not a supplied disposition or a new majority. Any adoption, refusal or narrower application must receive its own present legal explanation and defined scope.
