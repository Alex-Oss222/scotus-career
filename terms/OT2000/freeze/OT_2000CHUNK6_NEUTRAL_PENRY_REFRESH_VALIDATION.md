## Validated additional facts and limits

All twelve eventual jurors received an instruction largely similar to the supplemental mitigation direction during the month-long voir dire. The judge then explained its operation; the prosecutor also explained it, and defense counsel gave a similar explanation with most jurors. The quoted examples expressly describe a negative answer even when the factual answers would otherwise be affirmative. They establish the nature of the explanations, not that every juror heard the identical quoted words or that the voir dire copy was identical to the final charge.

Voir dire began almost two months before penalty deliberations. In between came the remaining voir dire, a five-day guilt trial with extensive instructions, approximately two and one-half hours of guilt deliberations and a further five-day punishment trial. This interval matters to the context in which the final charge operated; it establishes neither forgotten instructions nor a formal withdrawal of the earlier explanations.

Defense counsel renewed the mitigation explanation in penalty closing, including the direction to answer a question negatively even if its literally correct answer was affirmative. Thus it would be inaccurate to say the jurors received no contemporaneous explanation of the supplemental permission. The reported prosecution argument emphasized oath, evidence and law. That summary does not establish an express prohibition on mitigation, a command to ignore the supplemental instruction or a particular effect on a juror. No commitment uses it as such a finding. Any proposed rationale depending on the precise unquoted prosecution wording would require a further neutral source extraction before reliance.

These additions must be read with the already preserved final charge and signed form: affirmative factual answers required proof beyond reasonable doubt; a negative verdict-form certification referred to reasonable doubt; the supplemental instruction directed effective consideration of mitigation and a negative answer if life fitted culpability. The entire final packet went into deliberations. The new explanations provide substantial support for the State's account of how that permission could operate; they do not alter the final printed terms. Conversely, the form alone cannot now be discussed as though no judge had explained a mitigation-based negative answer.

## Entering law and affected questions

Graham and Johnson, effective before the February 22, 1995 state merits decision, require effective use of material independent culpability mitigation and a sentencing vehicle that does not require a false factual answer. Boyde requires assessment of the charge and trial context as a whole, under reasonable likelihood rather than mere possibility. These principles require actual consideration of the new judicial explanations as well as the two sides' arguments. They do not establish a categorical rule that voir dire can never clarify a charge, that advocacy always cures a defect, or that temporal distance proves confusion.

Buchanan and Weeks remain later applications and present continuity evidence, not the clearly established substantive source at the 1995 state adjudication. Williams governs present interpretation of the independent statutory clauses and objective unreasonable-application inquiry. The new context is relevant separately to constitutional adequacy, objective reasonableness of the state court's disposition and any later actual-effect inquiry. A conclusion on one does not resolve the others.

The addendum supplies no new psychiatric-examination fact, certificate procedure, state adjudication date, participation fact, aggravating or mitigating evidence, complete penalty transcript or independent whole-record effect determination. It does not establish harmlessness or harmfulness. The original distinctions among Coleman's defined Chapman category, Weeks's open category and the present original-charge question remain necessary.
