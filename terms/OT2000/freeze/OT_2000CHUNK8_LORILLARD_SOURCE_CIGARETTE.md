QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Whether state regulations that sharply limit the public 
display of cigarette advertisements impose requirements “with 
respect to the advertising or promotion” of cigarettes within 
the meaning of the preemption provision of the Federal 
Cigarette Labeling and Advertising Act. 


2. Whether Massachusetts’ content- and viewpoint-based 
prohibition on virtually any public display of tobacco 
advertisements violates the First Amendment, either under the 
strict scrutiny standard that Petitioners contend is applicable or 
under the test set forth in Central Hudson Gas & Elec. Corp. 
v. Public Serv. Comm’n, 447 U.S. 557 (1980).

STATEMENT 


In recent years, numerous States and local jurisdictions 
have enacted diverse and increasingly severe limitations on the 
public display of tobacco advertisements. See, e.g., Pet. App. 
128a-34a. Like the Massachusetts regulations at issue in this 
case, many of these restrictions prohibit virtually a// outdoor 
advertising within a broadly defined distance of any “park,” 
“playground,” or “school.” As in this case, the resulting 
geographical sweep of these prohibitions often covers as much 
as 90% of the most populated areas in the jurisdiction. And 
again, like the regulations in this case, many of these 
regulations impose further substantial restrictions on the 
display of tobacco advertising inside retail establishments. The 
purpose of all these restrictions is unabashedly censorial: as 
Respondent acknowledged below, the challenged regulations 
are based on the premise that, for the putative purpose of 
preventing children from being exposed to “positive” images of 
tobacco products that involve significant health risks, any 
tobacco advertising aimed at adults must be banned from 
virtually all public areas. 


Petitioners (various tobacco manufacturers) brought suit, 
contending that Massachusetts’ severe restrictions on cigarette 
advertising were preempted by the Federal Cigarette Labeling 
and Advertising Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1331 et seq., and violated the 
First and Fourteenth Amendments. The court of appeals 
ultimately rejected both challenges and upheld the relevant 
provisions of the regulations. Pet. App. la-37a. It is this 
decision that is presently before the Court for review.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


1. Section 5(b) of the Federal Cigarette Labeling and 
Advertising Act (FCLAA) preempts Massachusetts’ tobacco- 
advertising regulations. The First Circuit’s conclusion that the 
regulations do not impose requirements “with respect 
to... . advertising” within the meaning of § 5(b) disregards the 
FCLAA’s text, its history, and Cipollone v. Liggett Group,

1. “If there is a bedrock principle underlying the First 
Amendment, it is that the government may not prohibit the

The Court has, however, adhered to a “limited categorical 
approach” in this area, holding that there are certain categories 
of expression in which the prohibition against content 
discrimination “applies differently” than it does “in the area of 
fully protected speech.” R.A.V., 505 U.S. at 383, 387. These 
“few limited areas” consist of certain categories of speech that 
have traditionally been thought to be wholly outside the

The more limited protection afforded to commercial speech 
arises largely from the fact that, by definition, it “is linked 
inextricably to commercial activity’ that the government has 
power to regulate. Friedman v. Rogers, 440 U.S. 1, 10 n.9 
(1979) (emphasis added); see also 44 Liquormart, Inc. v. 
Rhode Island, 517 U.S. 484, 499 (1996) (plurality) (“[T]he

the underlying transaction may give it a concomitant interest in

the expression itself.”). In particular, the government’s power

misleading” or that proposes “transactions which are 
themselves illegal.” Bates v. State Bar of Ariz., 433 U.S. 350, 
383-84 (1977); see also Ohralik v. Ohio State Bar Ass'n, 436 
U.S. 447, 457-58, 462 (1978). It is precisely because of this 
“greater potential for deception or confusion in the context of 
certain advertising messages” that “content-based restrictions 
on commercial speech may be permissible.” Bolger, 463 U.S. 
at 65 (emphasis added). 


Nonetheless, the idea that commercial speech is afforded 
more limited protection against content discrimination “must be 
taken in context.” R.A.V., 505 U.S. at 383. The power to 
proscribe particular speech “on the basis of ome content

basis of other content elements.” /d. at 386 (emphasis in 
original). Thus, the lesser protection for commercial speech

distinctions it wants, but only that it may regulate content

regulation of commercial speech “for reasons unrelated to the 
preservation of a fair bargaining process, there is far less reason 
to depart from the rigorous review that the First Amendment 
generally demands.” 44 Liquormart, 517 U.S. at 501

authority when its commercial speech restrictions strike at ‘the 
substance of the information communicated,’ rather than the 
‘commercial aspect of [it]—with offerors communicating offers 
to offerees.”” Jd. at 499 (quoting Linmark Assoc., Inc. v. 
Willingboro, 431 U.S. 85, 96 (1977)).

Accordingly, the government has greater power to regulate

disclosure of beneficial consumer information.” 44

the government’s content discrimination is “directed not at any 
commercial aspect of the prohibited advertising but at the ideas 
conveyed and form of expression,” the regulation strikes at 
“the core of First Amendment values” and is presumptively 
unconstitutional. Carey v. Population Servs. Int’l, 431 U.S. 
678, 701 n.28 (1977); see also Rubin v. Coors Brewing Co., 
514 US. 476, 497 (1995) (Stevens, J., concurring) (“Any 
‘interest’ in restricting the flow of accurate information because 
of the perceived danger of that knowledge is anathema to the 
First Amendment{.]”). 


The fundamental First Amendment principles at work here 
are well illustrated by R.A. V., in which the Court addressed a 
city ordinance banning “fighting words’ that insult, or provoke 
violence, ‘on the basis of race, color, creed, religion or 
gender.”” 505 U.S. at 391. The Court invalidated the ordi-

specified disfavored topics.” /d. In response to the argument 
that the unprotected status of “fighting words” meant “that the 
government ‘may regulate [them] freely,” id. at 384, the Court 
held that “the power to proscribe [speech] on the basis of one 
content element . . . does not entail the power to proscribe it 
on the basis of other content elements.” Jd. at 386 (emphasis 
in original). Thus, for example, “the government may 
proscribe libel; but it may not make the further content 
discrimination of proscribing only libel critical of the 
government.” Jd. at 384. Of particular note here is the fact 
that the Court explicitly observed that the same limitation 
would apply in the commercial speech context:

If the basis of the government’s content-based regulation of

these regulations.” 491 U.S. at 798 n6. It is therefore 
obvious that intermediate scrutiny is neither designed, nor is it 
well-suited, to detect impermissibly content-based speech 
restrictions.

505 US. at 390... The transparent objective of the 
Massachusetts regulations is not just to regulate a category of — 
speech but to “drive . . . viewpoints from the marketplace,” 
id. at 387 (quotation omitted), that are inconsistent with the 
State’s. That makes the violation of the First Amendment all 
the more “egregious,” Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of 
Univ. of Va., 515 US. 819, 829, 830-31 (1995), and makes the 
case for declining to apply Central Hudson to this setting all 
the more compelling. 


One “freedom of speech” principle surpasses every other: 
government may not “favor some viewpoints or ideas at the 
expense of others.” City Council v. Taxpayers for Vincent, 
466 U.S. 789, 804 (1984). “When the government targets not 
subject matter, but particular views taken by speakers on a 
subject, the violation of the First Amendment is all the more 
blatant.” Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 829. 


This case presents not just the risk, but the fact, of 
viewpoint discrimination. Doubtless, the State is free to 
publicize its own views about cigarettes, but in doing so it may 
not “prohibit, edit, or restrain the distribution of advocacy 
materials” offering a different view. Meese v. Keene, 481 US. 
465, 480 (1987). And that is precisely what the State has 
done. 


The facts of this case make clear that the State’s actions are 
designed in part to suppress a viewpoint with which the State 
disagrees. In banning advertisements that promote the sale of 
tobacco products, for example, these regulations represent the 


40 


culmination of one State’s “war against Big Tobacco.” JA 
248. Even the most aloof observer would find it difficult to 
discern where the political nature of such speech restrictions 
ends.and the commercial nature of the regulations begins. 


Making matters worse, the Massachusetts regulations 
silence not just one alternative viewpoint but a multiplicity of 
viewpoints. Whether it be large tobacco companies or small 
ones, companies with new products or companies with old 
ones, or the smallest retailers or the largest distributors, each. 
has a different interest in a free flow of truthful information on 
this subject. Yet because each of these different viewpoints 
expresses something favorable about a tobacco product 
(whether it be taste, price, etc.), each differs from the 
orthodoxy prescribed by the State, and they are all substantially 
suppressed. And it is of course too late in the day to argue 
“that no viewpoint discrimination occurs because the 
[regulations] discriminate against an entire class of 
viewpoints.” Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 831-32. That position 
“reflects an insupportable assumption that all debate is bipolar” 
and constitutes “a contrived description of the marketplace of 
ideas.” Jd. at 831. 


The court of appeals gave insufficient weight to the 
viewpoint-discriminatory nature of Massachusetts’ regime. In 
finding no “danger of viewpoint discrimination,” the lower 
court noted that the regulations apply to all tobacco products 
“regardless of brand or manufacturer”; that they “permit 
nonpromotional speech relating to tobacco products”; and that 
“{t]he companies make no allegation that the Attorney General 
has disparately, much less discriminatorily, applied these 
provisions.” Pet. App. 15a-16a. But it is hardly reassuring to 
observe that Massachusetts has not applied the regulations in 
a discriminatory manner: who needs to apply provisions 
discriminatorily when the provisions aiready discriminate on 
their face? That the regulations prohibit only speech that 
“promotes” tobacco consumption, see 940 C.M.R. § 21.03

41 


(Pet. App. 107a), while permitting both “nonpromotional” and 
commercial speech that discourages tobacco consumption, see 
Pet. App. at 14a (allowing “tobacco-related speech that has a 
purpose or effect other than promotion, such as public health 
campaigns”), is precisely why they are viewpoint-based. The 
Court has never rejected the argument that anything less than 
strict scrutiny applies to such transparent censorship. It should 
not do so here. 


C. Massachusetts’ Near-Total Ban On Outdoor and 
Indoor Tobacco Advertising Fails Central Hudson. 


Even if the Court were to apply intermediate scrutiny under 
Central Hudson, the Massachusetts regulations still violate the 
First Amendment. More particularly, Massachusetts has failed 
to satisfy the last two elements of the Central Hudson 
test—i.e., that the regulatory measure “directly advances” the 
government’s interests and that it be no “more extensive than 
is necessary.” 447 US. at 566. 


1. The State bears the burden of demonstrating that its 
speech ban “advances [its] interests in a direct and material 
way.” Edenfield, 507 U.S. at 767; Central Hudson, 447 U.S. 
at 564. “{MlJere speculation or conjecture,” the Court has 
emphasized, will not suffice. Edenfield, 507 U.S. at 770. Nor 
will a justification that “provides only the most limited 
incremental support for the interest asserted.” Bolger, 463 
US. at 73. Rather, the State “must demonstrate that the harms 
it recites are real and that its restriction will in fact alleviate 
them to a material degree.” Edenfield, 507 US. at 771. 
Otherwise, ‘“‘a State could with ease restrict commercial 
speech in the service of other objectives that could not 
themselves justify a burden on commercial expression.”” 
Rubin, 514 U.S. at 487 (quoting Edenfield, 507 U.S. at 771); 
accord Greater New Orleans Broad. Ass'n v. United States, 
527 U.S. 173, 189 (1999).

Measured against this test, the State’s showing plainly fails. 
In arguing that its ban directly advances the objective of 
curbing underage smoking, it points principally to a 1994 
Surgeon General’s report and the background statement

simply announcing, without amplification, that they are 
“sufficient to satisfy the Attorney General’s burden.” Pet. App.

the initiation of illegal youth smoking. JA 204 (“To

is sufficient to . . . [e]stablish that advertising as an effect of 
directly causing minors to use tobacco products.”). See also 
JA 135-44 (Morse Aff.), 145-61 (Henke Rep.). Nor can 
Massachusetts overcome this deficiency merely by pointing to 
studies purporting to establish that advertising generally 
increases demand for tobacco products. Even “[a]ssuming the 
accuracy of [a] causal chain” between advertising and the

2. In addition to violating Central Hudson’s third prong, 
the Massachusetts regulations also violate its fourth. They 
simply do not reflect the precision that this aspect of the test 
requires. Instead of demonstrating that the State “‘carefully 
calculated’ the costs and benefits associated with the burden on 
speech imposed by its prohibition,” Discovery Network, 507 
U.S. at 417, the regulations amount to exactly the kind of 
scattershot legislation that the Court has not hesitated to 
invalidate in the past. 


Central Hudson places the burden on the government to 
establish a “close fit” between the ends and means of its 
speech-restrictive regulations. 447 U.S. at 418. “[I]f the 
governmental interest could be served as well by a more limited 
restriction on commercial speech,” Central Hudson instructs, 
“the excessive restrictions cannot survive.” Jd. at 564-65, 569. 
Because “the First Amendment directs that government may 
not suppress speech as easily as it may suppress conduct,” the 
Court has alerted States that “speech restrictions cannot be 
treated as simply another means that the government may use 
to achieve its ends.” 44 Liquormart, 517 U.S. at 512 
(plurality). Thus, where options are “availabl[e] . . . , all of 
which could advance the [g]overnment’s asserted interest in a 
manner less intrusive to . . . First Amendment rights,” a speech 
restriction is “more extensive than necessary” and more 
extensive than is constitutionally permissible. Rubin, 514 U.S. 
at 491; Discovery Network, 507 U.S. at 418 & n.13 (“[I]f there 
are numerous and obvious less-burdensome alternatives to the 
restriction on commercial speech, that is certainly a relevant 


45 


consideration in determining whether the ‘fit’ between ends and 
means is reasonable.”). 


In applying this requirement, the Court has upheld 
restrictions that left open “many alternative channels for 
communicating necessary information” about the product or 
service. Went For It, 515 U.S. at 633-34. But it repeatedly 
has invalidated restrictions where no such “alternative 
channels” were readily available. See, e.g., 44 Liquormart, 517 
U.S. at 501-02 (plurality); id at 529-30 (O’Connor, J., 
concurring in the judgment); Linmark, 431 U.S. at 92-94. 


These requirements cannot be satisfied here. For the 
ostensible purpose of discouraging underage smoking, the 
State has enacted what is tantamount to a complete ban on 
outdoor advertising of cigarettes. The regulations ultimately 
prohibit outdoor advertising (and indoor advertising that is 
visible to passers-by) in 90% of the three most heavily- 
populated areas of Massachusetts. See 940 C.M.R. § 21.04(S), 
Pet. App. 11a; id., § 22.06(5), Pet. App. 123a. 


Far from being “narrowly drawn,” the regulations overlook 
numerous “practical and nonspeech-related forms of 
regulation” that “could more directly and effectively” 
accomplish the professed goal of reducing underage smoking. 
Greater New Orleans Broad., 527 U.S. at 192. The State did 
not have “to choose suppression over a less speech-restrictive 
policy.” 44 Liquormart, 517 U.S. at 509. It could have, inter 
alia, (1) strictly enforced its laws prohibiting retailers from 
selling cigarettes to underage buyers; (2) enacted laws making 
underage purchases of cigarettes illegal; (3) imposed a licensing 
requirement on retailers as a means of supervising compliance 
with existing tobacco sales laws, and (4) enforced the 
prohibition against smoking on school grounds. 


Moreover, as noted, the MSA provides that no cigarette 
manufacturer “may take any action, directly or indirectly, to 
target Youth within any Settling State in the advertising,

promotion or marketing of Tobacco Products, or take any 
action the primary purpose of which is to initiate, maintain or

cigarette manufacturer had failed to comply with these require-

Disciplinary Counsel of S. Ct. of Ohio, 471 U.S. 626, 648 
(1985).

judgment); Rubin, 514 U.S. at 491. Each of these alternatives

clos[ing] them.” Virginia State Bd., 425 U.S. at 770; see also 
44 Liquormart, 517 U.S. at 498 (plurality). Cf Carey, 431 
US. at 700 (statute that “completely suppress[es]” information 
about a product is unconstitutional) (citation omitted).’

That leaves the final ground for the court of appeals’ 
decision—the view that it could sidestep these draconian 
requirements by claiming that they are necessary to protect 
children. Despite the importance of insulating minors from 
certain products, the Court has specifically held that 
commercial speech restrictions may not “‘reduce the adult 
population . . . to reading only what is fit for children.’” 
Bolger, 463 U.S. at 73-74 (quoting Butler v. Michigan, 352 
U.S. 380, 383 (1957)). In Bolger, the Court invalidated a 
federal statute prohibiting unsolicited mailed advertisements for 
contraceptives. | Acknowledging the importance of the 
governmental interest in protecting children from these 
advertisements, the Court nonetheless concluded that “[t]he 
level of discourse reaching a mailbox simply cannot be limited 
to that which would be suitable for a sandbox.” Jd. at 74. 
Quoting Justice Frankfurter, the Court reasoned that banning

to protect children from exposure to the product, would surely 
“burn the house to roast the pig.” Jd. at 74 n.27 (quoting 
Butler, 352 U.S. at 383). Similarly, Carey v. Population Servs.

children, the Court responded that this was “classically not [a]

by a determined child.” Jd at 759. And in Sable

the Court relied on Bolger to invalidate a federal statute 
prohibiting indecent telephone communications, despite

disobedient young people would manage to secure access to 
such messages.” Jd. at 130. See also Playboy Entertainment

In rejecting Petitioners’ claim that commercial speech 
restrictions cannot “deny communication to a large number of 
adults for the sake of protecting children,” the court of appeals 
reasoned that this principle applies only to what it termed 
“expressive” speech, but not to “commercial” speech,” Pet. 
App. at 34a. That analysis, however, squarely conflicts with 
the holdings of Bolger and Carey (which each involved 
commercial speech), and with the logic of the Court’s recent 
indecency decisions “(which each rely on Bolger for the 
proposition that government may not “reduce the adult 
population to only what is fit for children,” 463 U.S. at 73). 
Neither was the court of appeals correct in claiming that this

principle did not apply here because the advertising restrictions 
were not “equivalent to a ‘blanket ban’ on speech.” Pet. App. 
35a. Under that reasoning, Bolger would apply to a complete 
ban on advertising a disfavored product, but the interest in 
protecting children would support an a/most-complete ban on 
such advertising. A 90% restriction on advertising in the end 
warrants the same First Amendment scrutiny that a 100% 
restriction receives. The lower court’s contrary analysis 
demands reversal.
