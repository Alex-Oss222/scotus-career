## Brewer — factual and remedial precision

The January 14, 1999 district order required transfer of fourth-grader Jessica Haak to Iroquois no later than the second semester or February 1, 1999. Haak had first been accepted, in writing even after an administrator saw her, and acceptance was revoked after another administrator verified that she was white. This is not merely an unsuccessful initial application. The program's racial direction restrictions are its actual administration: minority city pupils may transfer outward; nonminority suburban pupils may transfer inward only when the transfer does not negatively affect the receiving school's racial balance. The regulation defines minority pupils to include Black or Hispanic pupils and other racial minorities historically subjected to discrimination; defendants included Asian and American Indian pupils. Race is absent from the printed application and brochures; defendants described telephone advice and parental self-screening, which Haak's mother disputed receiving. Opinion paragraphs 1–9.

The controlling state funding regulation required participating districts to demonstrate annually that their voluntary transfers would reduce racial isolation. The statutory and regulatory text does not itself state a categorical legislative prohibition on every white city pupil's outward transfer. The appellate majority's statement that allowing such transfers would necessarily cost program funding is its characterization of application and funding consequences. Keep those sources of propositions distinct. The statutory criterion is a commissioner-approved program designed to reduce racial isolation; the regulation permits minority pupils, nonminority pupils, or both to be transferred voluntarily. It also forbids selecting transfers solely for receiving-school nonacademic pursuits, or solely because of disability, irregular attendance, low performance, or discipline. Annual reports are required. Majority paragraphs 3, 6–7, 14; Miner paragraphs 51–54.

The appellate court found irreparable harm on the record but vacated because the heightened showing for a mandatory preliminary injunction had not been made. The order was mandatory because it changed the existing placement and required tuition-free attendance outside the student's home district. It did not finally uphold the program. The majority required a fuller factual record concerning de facto segregation, the precise objective, and fit. Parker's doubts about effectiveness and Miner's findings-like statements about failure and alternatives are their separate assessments, not new adjudicated facts accepted by the whole panel. The reported increase in minority population, transfers, and program duration does not itself establish the absence of educational benefits.

The appellate protection extended only to completion of **1999–2000**, and only **assuming Haak had begun attending Irondequoit**. It urged resolution before 2000–2001. The source does not establish January 2001 enrollment, current grade, a later injunction, or current seat availability. A Supreme Court disposition may decide the challenged preliminary-relief framework and remand current equitable details without pretending that the expired protection remains operative. An order granting present placement would need current circumstances rather than an assumption derived from the old order.

The complaint also included Title VI, §1983, state statutory discrimination, contract, and promissory-estoppel claims. The contract and estoppel grounds were rejected as bases for preliminary relief, and plaintiffs did not challenge that rejection on appeal. These claims do not silently enlarge the supplied equal-protection grant. Majority notes 2–4.

## Denius — material corrections required before modeling

**Correct judgment below:** The Seventh Circuit **affirmed monetary qualified immunity on the First Amendment attorney-client claim and the Fourteenth Amendment financial-information claim**. It **reversed the monetary-immunity judgment on the medical-information claim and reversed dismissal of the equitable claims**, remanding for further proceedings. It did not reverse attorney-client damages immunity. This appears expressly in paragraph 36 and the final paragraph 54, independently confirmed at 209 F.3d 954–55, 959–60 in the parallel reporter text. `denius-verified-extracts.md` mechanically copies the pertinent passages.

**Medical coverage is disputed, not established:** Footnote 8 expressly reserves whether the authorization reaches medical records or communications for district-court determination. The medical discussion proceeds only **to the extent** it does. Neither the proposed constitutional question nor the blanket wording turns that reserved factual issue into a finding. This qualification materially affects breach, immunity, and relief.

The new authorization lasted **one year from execution**. It covered all public, private, and confidential records concerning Denius and enumerated educational, financial, military/veterans, law-enforcement, and employment records, together with attorneys' records and recollections concerning civil or criminal matters in which he had or previously had an interest. It also released providers and the program from liability. The enumerated authorization passage does not separately name medical records. The 1994 criminal-background/educational release had **no time limit** and remained unchallenged insofar as the new form duplicated it. The live dispute concerned additional information. Paragraphs 5–10; notes 2 and 6.

Denius signed the employment contract but refused the new authorization. On August 2, 1996, Dunlap told him he could not work without signing and required him to leave. Refusal was the sole reason for nonrenewal. Dunlap intended only a routine criminal check because funds were limited, would have done a broader check if funds permitted, and did not tell Denius of the intended limitation. No justification for the additional categories had been advanced at this stage. These are the opinion's summary-judgment record recitations, not findings after trial. Paragraphs 11–14, 21–22.

Denius had never faced criminal prosecution, so the Sixth Amendment counsel right had not attached. The appellate court rejected a standing objection based on the absence of a prior or current attorney matter: the employment condition itself could burden future consultation, and Denius lost employment for refusing it. That reasoning does not establish that any confidential communication was actually collected. No specific protected financial item had been identified; the appellate analysis addressed breadth, justification, and missing safeguards. Paragraphs 29, 47; notes 4–5.

Only medical and financial privacy were sufficiently developed on appeal among the additional informational categories. Separate arguments that the liability release impaired court access or a property interest in causes of action had not been presented below and were deemed waived. Do not convert the presence of a release into a preserved additional holding. Paragraph 40 and note 10.

Gary Sadler, the successor director, was added under Appellate Rule 43(c)(2) because the action included prospective relief. The court rejected an asserted requirement to seek Rule 60(b) relief before appealing the legal dismissal of equitable claims. Reversal restored those claims for adjudication; it neither awarded reinstatement nor declared damages due. Damages immunity and equitable entitlement remain distinct. Paragraphs 49–54 and note 1.

## Kansas — statutory qualifications and pleading posture

The action sought declaratory and injunctive relief and was dismissed for failure to state a claim. Fiscal-year 1996 amounts of $29.3 million IV-D support and $101.9 million welfare funding were undisputed as recited by the court; they are historical funding figures, not present audited losses. The opinion describes federal coverage of 66 percent of program operating costs and 80 percent of computer expenditures. It identifies conditional participation rather than an order directing state employees regardless of participation. It does not report an adjudicated cessation of all payments, an actual exhaustion of state resources, or an evidentiary trial on fiscal inability to withdraw.

The opinion's general account of enforcement mechanisms must not replace each statute's operative conditions. In particular, its shorthand that states revoke passports is not a basis for assigning the Secretary of State's passport function to Kansas. Its paragraph 4 cross-references also should not be copied as statutory identifiers: the State Directory of New Hires is under §653a, while the State Case Registry is part of §654a. A general Spending Clause judgment need not adjudicate every underlying collection mechanism.

Section 602(a)(2) requires certification that the State will operate a child-support program under an approved part-D plan. **Do not treat every implementation defect as automatically forfeiting every dollar.** Section 609(a)(8) addresses specified performance, data, or substantial-compliance findings, an ensuing corrective year, and graduated TANF reductions of 1–2 percent, 2–3 percent for a second consecutive finding, and 3–5 percent thereafter. Its substantial-compliance branch excepts §654(24), (27)(A), and (27)(B)(i); it also addresses technical defects and the Secretary's determinations. Complete refusal to operate the required program and audited deficiencies within a continuing program are distinct statutory situations. The court's statement that Kansas risks both funding streams is not a finding that the latter penalty process does not exist.

Section 666(d)'s exemption is **discretionary**. The State must satisfy the Secretary, with caseload, processing-time, administrative-cost, average-collection, and other requested data or estimates, that the law or procedure required **by or pursuant to §666** will not improve program effectiveness and efficiency. The Secretary **may** exempt that requirement, subject to continuing review and termination if circumstances change. It is not an automatic exemption from all IV-D requirements. The opinion's note 8 uses it as a qualification to the State's burden argument; the full terms are copied in the statutory handoff.

Section 654(26) requires safeguards for all confidential information, including unauthorized-use restrictions, protective-order and risk-of-harm restrictions on whereabouts disclosures, notice to the Secretary in domestic-violence or child-abuse circumstances, and court screening before further disclosure of flagged information. Kansas's privacy and procedural-due-process objections were described by the appellate court as cursory and undeveloped. Their rejection does not establish the legality of every possible application or remove statutory safeguards.

## Urofsky — exact scope and qualifications

The amended §§2.1-804 to -806 were before the en banc court, rather than the broader original definition. Covered content must have as its **dominant theme** a **lascivious** description or depiction of the listed sexual subjects. The statute still covers some nonobscene material. Section 2.1-805 permits access to the extent required for a **bona fide, agency-approved research project or other agency-approved undertaking**; approvals must be written by agency heads and publicly available under the state public-records law. The second branch is not limited to research projects. The definition of agency includes institutions of higher education but **excludes the Department of State Police**. All those terms, the infrastructure definition, and incorporated sexual-subject definitions appear in the full opinion, majority paragraphs 2–8 and notes 2–3.

The professors challenged work-related use and disclaimed a personal-use entitlement. None of the six had requested and been denied approval. The record disclosed no denied request; other professors had obtained waivers. A machine-shop supervisor's disability inquiry was treated as not needing advance approval. Absence of denial does not establish that every proposed project would be approved, and it does not eliminate an argument aimed at the approval requirement itself. Majority paragraph 9 and note 4; Wilkinson paragraph 94.

The complaint alleged specific present effects: Urofsky abandoned an online indecency-law assignment because he feared he could not check student work; Meyers's Victorian-poetry research access was restricted; Smith's website had been censored; Heller, Levin, and Delaney hesitated to continue sexual-subject research. These are complaint allegations as identified by the majority, not findings of unlawful denials under the statutory approval process. Majority note 9.

The statutory reference to agency heads and the record-based statement that colleges delegated primary approval to deans or department heads must be kept distinct. The latter is Wilkinson's account of university administration, not statutory language requiring every agency to use academic peer review. Conversely, treating this record as necessarily assigning approvals to an outside nonacademic state officer would also be inaccurate. The majority's note 17 expressly left questions concerning a future refusal to approve a particular university project unanswered. Private-citizen expression, private universities, and primary/secondary schools were not the presented entitlement.

The judgment reversed the district court's summary judgment for the professors. It did not decide a damages demand, a disciplinary dismissal, or an individual denied project. Wilkins's seven-judge majority rejected the general and individual academic-freedom claims; Wilkinson agreed only in judgment after balancing the narrowed restriction and university approval system; Hamilton joined the majority while identifying circuit precedent as decisive; Murnaghan and three joiners would affirm, criticizing both the categorical employee-speech premise and the restriction's fit and discretion. These descriptions record the lower opinions only and prescribe no Supreme Court result.
