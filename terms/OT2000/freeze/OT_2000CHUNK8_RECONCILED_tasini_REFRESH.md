## The six's complete statutory ground and important limits

The six can join the rule that a publisher's default privilege under §201(c) does not cover these separately offered article copies merely because the database identifies the publication in which each first appeared. Sections 103(b), 106 and 201 preserve the contributor's separate rights and distinguish the compiler's creative contribution from ownership of the article. Media neutrality permits revised electronic editions; it does not answer whether the article remains part of the statutory collective work. A revision need not preserve every item or the original typography, but source provenance alone is insufficient.

NEXIS and NYTO present individually retrieved text across editions without the edition's surrounding contents and layout. NYTO's single-publisher identity does not make every delivered article part of a recognizable revision of its particular source issue. GPO retains source-page images and related page material, yet delivers the article pages detached from the rest of that issue. That distinct fact does not satisfy the contextual requirement. The six do not require physical page-turning as a universal legal test; the way these products present the contribution is the relevant application. A possible user search assembling an edition is different from the database itself perceptibly presenting a revision.

Microform preserves the article within the continuously reproduced issue even if a reader later focuses on one item. That difference answers the strongest media-neutrality argument without privileging paper as a medium. Intact intermediate transmission from a print publisher would not itself resolve the downstream reproduction and distribution at issue. The databases' usefulness and possible future licensing cannot expand the statutory permission. The right claimed here is reproduction and distribution; the statutory fair-use defense was not asserted, and public display is not decided. Transferability under §201(d) is unnecessary because these uses fail the privilege's substantive scope in the first place.

The complete historical source also discusses direct sale of unauthorized article copies rather than merely sale of copying equipment, the narrow separate library exceptions, and an alternative lower-court observation about compilation creativity. These are not permission to add new unmodeled holdings. No general library status, independent §108 entitlement, general contributory-infringement rule, display-right holding or new compilation-originality rule is supplied by this reconciled handoff. The already supported reproduction/distribution and §201(c) ground resolves the question without them.

## The two's exact contrary statutory rule

Stevens and Breyer may not be summarized as finding provenance alone sufficient. Their historical contrary position requires **both** that each article expressly identify its source collective work **and** that substantially the rest of that collective work be readily accessible to the reader of the individual file at the same time. The publisher-originated editorial selection and issue association must remain available; a detached article carrying only a bibliographic label does not qualify. This gives precise terms to the refreshed model's recorded insistence that the actual archival relationship do real limiting work. It is not a new historical fact, new interface or circulation bargain.

Their analysis separates two questions: whether the collection of electronic files representing one issue is a revision, and whether putting that revision alongside other editions and publications destroys its status. They answer the first yes on the retained issue content and source identification, and the second no where that relationship remains accessible. Individual retrieval and altered layout are consistent with a media-neutral revision; aggregating issues resembles placing periodicals together in a library or on a common microfilm roll. GPO's preserved page images add context but are not the sole basis of their rule. They distinguish an unrelated anthology or a separately sold article without that archival relationship. Author ownership, control of unrelated exploitation and the ban on revising the contribution itself remain intact.

The strongest opposing argument is that the database, as users receive it, sells distinct articles rather than a revised issue. The two regard the remaining accessible editorial selection as enough statutory context; the six require the stronger perceptible collective presentation that these interfaces lack. That is the actual legal disagreement. No party is assigned a newly invented factual concession that every archive has complete contents or that every search produces the original edition.

The separate writing must also preserve the narrow transferability view verified at 533 U.S. 509 n.4. Stevens and Breyer reject the proposition that the privilege is completely nontransferable: a publisher may farm out printing or distribution of its own collective work or qualifying revision. That does not authorize assignment permitting the contribution to appear in a wholly different collective work. Because the majority and the court of appeals did not decide the question, they decline further resolution. The refreshed model's bounded statutory remand remains compatible with this limited position; it cannot be described as an affirmative endorsement of complete nontransferability, a general license-transfer holding or complete silence about the elementary printing/distribution distinction.

This statutory construction and its limits explain why the two would reverse. Archive completeness, clearance costs and dissemination policy reinforce their reading of the asserted ambiguity but do not create an independent exception or a factual prediction of inevitable deletion. Their proposed disposition leaves the unpressed contract defenses untouched and gives no automatic final judgment against every other properly preserved statutory contention.
