## Full comparator verification

Historically Kennedy wrote for Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas throughout; Souter joined Parts I and II but withheld the immunity application and would remand. Ginsburg, joined by Stevens and Breyer, concurred in the judgment. Their judgment did not merely rest on a conclusion that every alleged use of force was reasonable. It specifically depended on their view that Katz had not supplied a triable basis for attributing the van shove to Saucier rather than Parker (pages 211-212 and notes 1-3). They separately rejected an additional reasonableness screen after Graham in this excessive-force setting (pages 210, 213-217). The historical majority assumed a possible constitutional violation because the granted review was confined to immunity (page 207), notwithstanding its ordinary merits-first instruction. It then granted immunity on the particular security/removal circumstances, not on a factual finding that Saucier did not participate. These separate grounds must not be aggregated merely because eight historically supported the judgment.

## Present record and distinct legal questions

The frozen neutral account remains controlling. Katz's supported version describes sudden two-officer removal of an unresisting demonstrator and a forceful van entry that nearly caused his head to strike the interior. He avoided injury. Visibility of the leg brace is supported; Saucier's actual awareness is not conclusively established. The nearby Vice President and protected event are genuine context, but no weapon, resistance, concerted attack or continuing physical threat is invented. The petition's Parker-only reading of the video is advocacy, not the trial court's operative account.

The district judge actually reviewed the news video and found plaintiff-favorable two-officer involvement and disputed force/threat/resistance. The appellate opinion likewise used that account. Under Johnson, Behrens and the now unanimous Sterling Part III, a participation-sufficiency attack cannot be decided on this interlocutory review; a genuinely separable legal defense uses the supported adverse facts. The historical concurrence's reappraisal of participation proof is consequently unavailable under the concrete same-term rule to which all three concurring Justices joined.

Force legality, sufficiently particularized 1994 notice, personal participation and the permissible appellate record remain separate questions. The grant does not reopen probable-cause, First Amendment, supervisory or prospective-relief branches. No new federal-officer cause of action is adjudicated.

## Rule components and sequencing

**Separate legal-mistake protection:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support the proposition that Fourth Amendment merits reasonableness and Harlow/Anderson qualified immunity are distinct: an officer may correctly perceive the facts yet reasonably misunderstand the particular constitutional force limit. A general statement that excessive force is forbidden is insufficient by itself, but an identical factual precedent is not indispensable. The assumed record must be preserved. Souter agrees with this rule without agreeing that it disposes of this record for Saucier.

**The force-specific objection:** Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer accept the continued ordinary Harlow defense, conduct-date notice and lawful summary-judgment dispositions. In this Graham excessive-force setting, however, they consider the merits' reasonable-officer inquiry sufficient to account for the officer's practical choices and mistakes. They withhold the proposition that objectively unreasonable force under that standard gains a second, overlapping reasonableness shelter. This is neither abolition of qualified immunity in every constitutional setting nor an actual-reliance-only replacement. Denius's different acquisition claims and Wilson's uncertain media-entry law do not negate that particular objection. Their refusal must not be converted into a join merely because they accept the ordinary defense elsewhere.

**Ordinary sequence:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support a merits-first sequence for ordinary lower-court adjudication of a properly presented immunity defense: first ask whether the supported plaintiff-favorable facts could establish a constitutional violation; if they could, ask whether that particular right was sufficiently established at the time. Developing the actual right supplies future legal guidance that would disappear if every case ended on notice alone. The sequence is newly adopted here, not falsely attributed to entering law. Elder and Conn declined to impose such a categorical sequence, and Wilson used its own order without establishing an across-the-board rule; those reservations do not prohibit this later adoption. No concrete changed premise supports treating the five historical supporters as refusing the rule merely because an earlier mandatory command is absent.

The actual grant's limited scope still permits this Court to assume a possible violation before deciding the immunity application; the historical opinion itself did so. No exceptionless instruction to decide a constitutional question outside review scope, no new factual finding, and no general license to create advisory constitutional opinions is attributed to the five. Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer refuse the duplicative sequence in this force setting. A final broader formulation must undergo assembly compatibility rather than be inferred from these bounded commitments.

## Application and adversarial tests

The four direct-immunity Justices accept, rather than deny, the serious adverse shove account. Their legal ground is that moving an arrestee from the active protected event into a van was one continuous, urgent security removal; Graham allowed some physical coercion and did not make the particular boundary of this brief nondeadly force sufficiently apparent in September 1994. Absence of injury is context, never a dispositive requirement. Their conclusion assumes a possible violation and grants personal damages protection; it does not declare the force lawful or resolve Saucier's actual role. The strongest objection is that a controlled, unresisting person's violent van entry has no additional demonstrated need. They regard that account as presenting a force-degree boundary rather than an unmistakable separate assault after the seizure was complete.

The three affirm-denial Justices consider the actual adverse account indispensable. Security can justify taking control and removing Katz without justifying every additional degree of force. Under their historical Graham framework, a jury-eligible finding that this force was objectively unreasonable cannot be bypassed by relabeling the same conduct reasonably believed lawful. A permissible early merits judgment requires an adequately supported no-violation account, as in Plakas; the lower courts have identified a contrary material account here. They do not require least-intrusive tactics or conclude after trial that the officer acted unlawfully. The strongest counterargument is the continuity and urgency of the move into the van; those features permit proper merits assessment but do not remove the material assumed unnecessary force.

Souter supports correction of the legal merger and return for the lower court's application, rather than a present final legal notice answer. The strongest objection is that the Court can decide the legal defense on assumed facts immediately. His historical remand choice, together with the current Hunter/Johnson/Behrens approach, supports leaving the first distinct application below; no source supplies a concrete changed premise that warrants the provisional contrary direct-immunity result. His position neither endorses the three-Justice force-specific merger nor establishes their conclusion that the existing denial should stand on their ground.

Sterling's intervening unanimous Part III, which each joined, specifically excludes interlocutory reappraisal of participation sufficiency; the frozen district-court account already includes the judge's own video review and supported two-officer force. That concrete legal and procedural premise removes the historical concurrence's sufficient evidentiary route. This is not a new finding that Saucier actually shoved Katz.
