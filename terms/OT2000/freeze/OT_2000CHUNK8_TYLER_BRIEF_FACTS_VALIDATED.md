## Petitioner position on newness
Source: sources/chunk8-c/Tyler_Archive_06.txt, merits brief 11.

There is no question but that Cage v. Louisiana 
announced a “new rule” within the meaning of Teague v. 
Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), and the AEDPA. As Teague 
makes clear, “a case announces a new rule when it breaks 
new ground or imposes a new obligation on the States or 
the Federal Government” — that is, when “the result was 
not dictated by precedent existing at the time the defen- 
dant’s conviction became final.” Teague, 489 U.S. at 301. 
This definition of a new rule has been repeatedly reaf- 
firmed by the Court. See O'Dell v. Netherland, 521 U.S. 151, 
156 (1997); Graham v. Collins, 506 U.S. 461, 466-467 (1993). 
As the Court put it in Goeke v. Branch, 514 U.S. 115 (1995), 
the question is “whether a state court considering [the 
defendant's] claim at the time his conviction became final 
would have felt compelled by existing precedent to con- 
clude that the rule {he] seeks was required by the Consti- 
tution.” Jd. at 118 (quoting Saffle v. Parks, 494 U.S. 484, 488 
(1990)). If an issue was “susceptible to debate among 
reasonable minds,” the resolution of the issue constitutes 
a new rule. Butler v. McKellar, 494 U.S. 407, 415 (1990).

## Respondent position on newness
Source: sources/chunk8-c/Tyler_Archive_07.txt, merits brief 12.

Respondent concurs with petitioner that the rule 
announced in Cage v. Louisiana is a new rule of constitu- 
tional law within the meaning of Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 
288 (1989). The issue was one of first impression in Skel- 
ton v. Whitley, 950 F.2d 1037 (5th Cir. 1992) where the 
court considered that “ ‘a case announces a new rule if 
the result was not dictated by precedent existing at the 
time the defendant’s conviction became final,’” and 
stated, “This is emphatically such a case,” Id. at 1043, 
quoting Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. at 301. The Louisiana 
Supreme Court, addressing the retroactivity of Cage, like- 
wise determined that Cage announced a new rule. State ex 
rel. Taylor v. Whitley, 606 So. 2d 1292 (La. 1992) In Adams v. 
Aiken, 965 F.2d 1306 (4th Cir. 1992) the court stated, 
“Whether a trial court’s unconstitutional misdescription 
of the burden of proof in a criminal case violates the Due 
Process Clause was certainly an open question before 
Cage.” The Fifth Circuit’s opinion remained unchanged 
following Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275 (1993), which 
prompted the court to state, “It is undisputed that Cage 
announced a new rule of constitutional law.” In re Smith, 
142 F.3d 832, 835 (5th Cir. 1998). Indeed every court to 
reach the issue has determined that Cage announced a 
new rule. See Brief for Petitioner 13.
