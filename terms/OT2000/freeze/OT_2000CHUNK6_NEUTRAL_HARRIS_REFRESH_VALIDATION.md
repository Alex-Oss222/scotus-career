## Objective source and completeness correction

The addendum accurately distinguishes three lower-court writings: the majority's commencement rule, Anderson's special concurrence and Tjoflat's partial concurrence and dissent. Anderson's full paragraphs 49-51 supply a distinct combination of legal scope and judgment that the original two-path handoff did not separately address. This refresh adds that alternative before further adjudicative stages.

Anderson agrees that the statute ordinarily calls for dismissal of the covered action even after release. He nevertheless recognizes some Rule 15(d) discretion to permit a supplemental pleading recording release in unusual circumstances. His example is a colorable physical-injury claim tried to a jury where dismissal would manifestly waste judicial resources. That is expressly an example, not an exhaustive set of necessary conditions. He concludes that remand would be futile in this case and concurs in affirmance.

His present-case futility assessment is a lower appellate legal judgment. It does not establish a trial finding, a universally applicable threshold, actual Rule 15 compliance, waiver, prejudice or a Supreme Court instruction. His recognition of discretion also does not mean a district court must grant supplementation, even in the example he gives. Neither side may change permission into obligation or an illustrative circumstance into an exclusive exception.

## Three independently relevant paths

1. The original filing-time path affirms because section 1997e(e)'s historical commencement condition cannot be cured by subsequent release in the existing action. Rule 15(d) can record later facts but cannot alter that statutory trigger.
2. The original ordinary-supplementation path reverses the categorical legal foreclosure and remands so the district court may address proper motion, notice, just terms and permission under Rule 15(d). It does not require the supplement to be allowed or guarantee damages.
3. Anderson's path recognizes a narrow discretionary capacity but affirms because exceptional exercise would be unwarranted here and remand would be futile. Its substantive premise differs from the categorical filing-time path, and its remedy differs from the ordinary-supplementation path. Judgment agreement with path 1 would not itself establish a join in path 1's categorical rule; agreement that some discretion exists would not itself establish path 2's ordinary scope or remand.

The third path has a substantial argument: it gives effect to the statutory filing restriction while allowing an unusual procedural cure where dismissal and refiling would waste work already performed. Its principal difficulties are the legal source of a rarity restriction within Rule 15(d), the relationship of that exception to a supposedly controlling historical filing condition, and the basis for deciding futility before a lower court has exercised the legally correct discretion. The existing dispute about the actual supplemental paper remains material to the remedy. An example involving trial cannot alone establish that this record lacks every unusual circumstance.

McNeil and Mathews remain the central competing statutory analogies; the new concurrence changes neither holding. Semtek supplies the procedural/substantive boundary without itself choosing among these constructions. Artuz requires classification of this actual provision. Randell does not create an automatic release exception or establish any facts about this case. The same-day Booth matter remains outside Harris's entering law.
