## Freedom From Religion Foundation v. City of Marshfield

1. In December 1998 the parties stipulated that the City provided neither maintenance nor electrical service to the Fund's parcel. The Fund maintains the statue and property and pays for the electrical service lighting the statue. The City had separated that electrical service from the park's street-lighting system. These are affirmative reported facts, not merely an absence of evidence of public spending. Source: [reported lower opinion](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/203/203.F3d.487.99-1639.html), paragraphs 4, 6–7 and 14.
2. The parties stipulated that no material facts were disputed and that summary judgment was appropriate; the appellate dispute concerned conclusions of law on that record. This does not supply unproduced details about a future remedial design. Source: same opinion, paragraph 9.
3. The Fund paid fair market value, and the parties agreed that the sale complied with the applicable Wisconsin municipal-land-sale requirements. The City did not solicit alternative bids. The warranty deed dated July 2, 1998 restricted use to public park purposes. The plaintiffs did not contend that the City had tried to enforce that covenant. Absence of that contention is not a finding about every possible future enforcement. Source: same opinion, paragraphs 4 and 14–15.
4. The City's existing disclaimer was situated on Fund property. The Fund parcel was not visually differentiated from the surrounding city park, including from Highway 13. No installed replacement disclaimer or new boundary structure is established. Source: same opinion, paragraphs 7 and 22, note 1.
5. The reported appellate argument occurred October 1, 1999; the lower decision was February 4, 2000 and was amended on denial of rehearing and rehearing en banc March 20, 2000. Those are lower-court dates. They do not establish an argument date for the scheduled February 27, 2001 merits event. Source: same opinion, heading.

## Whitman / American Trucking

### Administrative position and review posture

1. The 1996 proposed interim implementation policy expressly described its interpretations as preliminary and not legally binding on States or the public. After receiving comments, EPA published an explanation with the final ozone standard under the heading concerning its final decision on the primary standard. The explanation said Subpart 1 would apply immediately to implementation of the new eight-hour standard, while Subpart 2 would continue as a matter of law while an area failed the old one-hour standard; after attainment of the old standard, EPA's position was that Subpart 2 would no longer apply. Preserve this transition rather than describing EPA as abolishing Subpart 2 everywhere immediately. Sources: 61 Fed. Reg. 65752 (1996); 62 Fed. Reg. 38873, 38884–38885 (1997), recited in `sources/chunk3-control/99-1257.ZO.txt`, Part IV.A, paragraphs describing the proposed policy and final explanation; [source page](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-1257.ZO.html).
2. A same-day White House memorandum prescribed implementation procedures; EPA separately published its explanation. Later EPA rulemaking responses treated the earlier implementation interpretation as settled and declined to reconsider it. The memorandum and EPA action are distinct instruments. Source: 62 Fed. Reg. 38421 (1997); 63 Fed. Reg. 31014, 31018–31019 (1998), recited in the same source, Part IV.A. These are reported administrative acts, not a stipulation that every published policy has legal effect.
3. In the appellate litigation, industry petitioners argued that EPA could not revise the ozone standard because revision would trigger Subpart 1 even though Subpart 2 had supplanted it for ozone. EPA answered that Subpart 2 supplemented rather than supplanted Subpart 1, and that Subpart 1 continued to apply to nonattainment areas under revised ozone standards. EPA also argued that its interpretation harmonized Subpart 2 with its authority to revise a national standard. These are the reported litigating positions, not concessions about their legal correctness. Source: Industry Petitioners and Intervenors brief in No. 97-1441 and consolidated cases, pages 32–34; EPA final brief, pages 67–68 and 71, recited in the same source, Part IV.A, final paragraph.
4. The parties agreed that the final ozone rule was final agency action ripe for review. EPA separately contested review of the implementation policy, arguing it was not agency action, not final, and not ripe. The agreement about the ozone rule does not concede those separate implementation objections. Source: same source, Part IV.A, opening and final paragraphs.
5. The cross-petition raises implementation costs in national standard setting under section 109. Section 109(b)(2)'s secondary-standard text is already included in the authorized text supplement; the source reports that respondents discussed secondary standards only briefly. Primary health standards and secondary welfare standards are distinct statutory commands. Source: same source, Part I, final paragraph, and Part II, note 3; `entering-law/OT_2000CHUNK3_MIDDLE_TEXT.md`, section 109(b)–(d).

## Cook v. Gralike

1. The district court permanently enjoined enforcement of sections 15 through 19 of Article VIII. The court of appeals declined the proposed severance that would have removed sections 17 through 19 while leaving the other provisions intact. The State did not request severance in this Court if its constitutional defense failed; its reply sought to sustain the provision in its entirety. This reports the actual relief and scope of the parties' requests; it does not itself decide severability or expand the presented constitutional issue. Source: `sources/chunk3-control/99-929.ZO.txt`, Part I and note 9; 996 F. Supp. 917, 920; 191 F.3d 911, 926 n.12; Reply Brief for Petitioner, pages 1–2 as recited there; [source page](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-929.ZO.html).
2. Gralike withdrew from the 1998 election while the appeal was pending. Harmon, a nonincumbent candidate in the 2000 Republican congressional primary in Missouri's Seventh District, intervened as an appellee. In light of that participation, no party contended the case was moot. Source: same source, note 8. This is a reported procedural fact, not a general exception to mootness.
3. Both parties accepted for this litigation the premise that States have no authority to regulate congressional elections beyond authority the Constitution delegates. The source identifies Brief for Petitioner, pages 25–26, and Brief for Respondents, pages 12–13. That reported concession concerns the source of congressional-election authority; it is not a concession that the challenged labels fall outside an otherwise applicable grant. Source: `sources/chunk3-control/99-929.ZC1.txt`, sole substantive paragraph; [source page](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-929.ZC1.html). The original briefs have not been independently recovered in this pass.
4. Article VIII's section 19 allowed a voter to challenge a decision omitting a label and a candidate to challenge a decision requiring one, with review in the Missouri Supreme Court and a clear-and-convincing-evidence requirement for showing that the label did not belong. Section 20 provided for automatic repeal upon ratification of the specified federal amendment; section 21 concerned the forum for challenges; section 22 contained severability language. These are the source's reported provisions, not a conclusion that the clause requires a particular severance. Source: `sources/chunk3-control/99-929.ZO.txt`, note 5.
5. The parties also disputed whether the Elections Clause's reference to a state legislature covered this referendum enactment. That issue was raised at the margins; the source cites Brief for Petitioner, page 38 n.46, and Brief for Respondents, pages 12–13 n.8. The presence of the competing arguments is reported without supplying their answer. Source: same source, note 20.

The argument date is November 6, 2000. Source: `sources/chunk3-control/99-929.ZS.txt`, caption, cross-checked against [official Reports](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-531/pdf/USREPORTS-531-510.pdf), page 510.

## Legal Services Corp. / United States v. Velazquez

### Restriction, interpretation, and procedural scope

1. The recipient restrictions apply to activities paid for with non-LSC funds as well as LSC funds. The reported statutory source is sections 504(d)(1)–(2). That entity-level scope must be kept separate from any permitted organizational affiliate arrangement. Source: `sources/chunk3-control/99-603.ZO.txt`, Part I, paragraph following the full section 504(a)(16) quotation; [source page](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-603.ZO.html).
2. Under LSC's interpretation, an eligible individual benefits claimant could dispute an erroneous factual determination, a misreading or misapplication of an existing statutory term, or an agency policy inconsistent with existing law. The recipient could not undertake representation designed to change welfare law or challenge its constitutional or statutory validity. Validity includes an argument that a state statute conflicts with a federal statute or that state or federal law violates the Constitution by its terms or application. Source: same source, introductory discussion and Part I, paragraphs describing 45 CFR part 1639 and section 1639.4; Brief for Petitioner in No. 99-603, page 7, as recited there.
3. LSC represented that the broad welfare-reform litigation restriction excludes both challenges to and defenses of existing welfare reform law; the source states that respondents did not deny that interpretation. Keep this reported position about the broader litigation ban separate from the individual-benefits exception and its challenged condition. It is not a stipulation that the condition is constitutionally neutral. Source: `sources/chunk3-control/99-603.ZD.txt`, Part I, paragraph citing Brief for Petitioner in No. 99-603, page 29; [source page](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-603.ZD.html).
4. LSC's litigating position required withdrawal when a prohibited validity issue arose after representation began. The source quotes its qualification that discontinuation be consistent with applicable professional-responsibility rules. The reported rules allowed and required explaining why representation could not continue, allowed expression of views about welfare law to the client, and permitted referral to another attorney. These permissions do not establish availability of another attorney or permit continued LSC-funded representation of the excluded claim. Source: same source, Part I, final paragraph; Brief for Petitioner, page 7 n.4; 164 F.3d 757, 765; 985 F. Supp. 323, 335–336, as recited there.
5. Regulations permitted recipients to establish affiliate organizations for litigation and other activities outside the LSC program. The identified source is 45 CFR part 1610 (2000). This establishes the reported permission, not that a particular affiliate existed, had resources, could take a client's case, or could cure loss of a current representation. Source: same source, Part II, paragraph discussing affiliate organizations and part 1610.
6. LSC's oral-argument explanation included the situation in which a judge asked counsel whether there was a constitutional concern: counsel could not answer while continuing the prohibited representation. The identified transcript pages are 8–9. Treat this as the reported agency litigating interpretation, not an independently recovered transcript or a newly hypothesized courtroom event. Source: `sources/chunk3-control/99-603.ZO.txt`, Part II, paragraph addressing the question arising during litigation.
7. The lower court excised only the condition attached to the individual-benefits exception, after finding legislative intent on severability unclear. Severability was outside the limited question granted and was not briefed or argued in this Court. Its absence from the parties' presentation does not itself establish whether a court must, may, or may not address the issue. Source: `sources/chunk3-control/99-603.ZO.txt`, Part I, severability paragraph and last paragraph; `sources/chunk3-control/99-603.ZD.txt`, Part III, first paragraph.
8. The underlying statute states both the legal-assistance purpose and the concern that the program be free of political pressures. These are section 2996's enacted findings; their operation and significance must be considered from the text and actual program rather than treated as proof that any specific representation is partisan. Source: 42 U.S.C. sections 2996(2), (5), (6), as reproduced in `sources/chunk3-control/99-603.ZD.txt`, Parts I and III. The broad restriction, the individual-benefits permission, and the permission's condition remain distinct textual provisions.
9. Under the reported withdrawal interpretation, bringing in a non-LSC lawyer for the validity challenge would not permit the LSC lawyer to remain in the same case advancing the other, permitted arguments: the validity issue required withdrawal from that representation. This concerns joint representation of that case, not a ban on every later referral or separate affiliate activity. Source: `sources/chunk3-control/99-603.ZO.txt`, Part II, paragraph comparing withdrawal and alternative channels; that paragraph expressly addresses proposed joint representation.

The argument date for the consolidated petitions is October 4, 2000. Source: `sources/chunk3-control/99-603.ZS.txt`, caption, cross-checked against [official Reports](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-531/pdf/USREPORTS-531-533.pdf), page 533.
