## Reconciled operative propositions

All seven participating Associates support reversal and remand on two bounded grounds:

1. The Act's prohibition, Schedule I treatment and specified research authorization foreclose the Cooperative's asserted medical-necessity defense to manufacture, distribution and associated possession with that intent. The Cooperative claims no authorization under the research route. State permission and medical recommendations do not provide federal statutory authorization. Congress's chosen regulatory treatment is the legal premise; the Court makes no independent scientific finding that marijuana can never benefit a patient.
2. The Cooperative cannot obtain the same distribution exemption by relabeling it an injunction modification based on the court's own weighing of the medical advantages of statutory nonenforcement. Section 882(a) authorizes equitable relief but does not command an injunction in every case or its broadest form. A court may choose lawful enforcement means and tailor authorized relief consistently with the statutory scheme. It may not use this rejected medical-necessity balance to displace that scheme. This ruling addresses the distributor's requested modification, not every injunction concerning an individual patient.

The statutory conclusion does not depend on proving that the lower-order criteria were unmet. They are four conjunctive conditions in the modification: a serious medical condition; imminent harm without access to cannabis; need for cannabis to treat or alleviate the condition or associated symptoms; and no reasonable legal effective alternative because all legal alternatives tried were ineffective or caused side effects the patient could not reasonably tolerate. Even their satisfaction does not create this distributor's statutory authorization. Their appearance in the order establishes no universal factual finding about recipients.

## Alternatives, precedent and nonreach

The necessity route receives its strongest support from the common-law background and alleged extreme need with no legal alternative. That background does not authorize a judicial balance contrary to this particular enacted medical-distribution scheme. Bailey remains relevant to the necessity framework and its ordinary limits; this case neither announces universal authority to create defenses nor casts a new general doubt on the defense's existence. Rutherford supports respecting the medical-regulatory scheme, rather than judicial creation of an exemption.

The equitable route receives its strongest support from Hecht and Romero-Barcelo's traditional discretion. Those cases preserve choice and tailoring of lawful enforcement means, not authority to contradict Congress's chosen substantive restriction on this distribution. Meghrig's statutory limit is shared by all seven despite the division in Grupo Mexicano over general equitable preservation. Neither actual precedent establishes a universal ban on modifying injunctions, and the Grupo Mexicano dissenters need not adopt that case's disputed general-equity rationale here.

The lack of statutory ambiguity defeats use of constitutional avoidance to insert this distribution exception; that conclusion does not decide the underlying constitutional claims. The Commerce Clause, substantive due-process and other asserted patient-liberty challenges were not resolved below and remain outside this decision's adjudication. Individual patient possession, patient-specific necessity or injunction questions and necessity under unrelated federal statutes likewise are not presented. These are scope limits, not permissions or predictions of later results. The source's additional self-created-emergency observation need not become a separate current holding; the shared statutory ground suffices.
