## Components and distinct rules

**J — Review.** All eight accept jurisdiction over the actual habeas legality claims under section 2241 and the controlling channel decisions. Effective relief is available if custody is unlawful, without disturbing the removal order or converting habeas into ordinary appellate review of removal merits.

**A — Purpose-bound statutory authority, the primary rule of Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.** For the previously admitted persons presented here, paragraph (6) permits detention beyond the removal period for a period reasonably necessary to accomplish removal. Once removal has no reasonable prospect within a reasonably foreseeable future, that provision does not authorize continuing imprisonment solely because a former offender may be dangerous or might fail to appear for a removal that has no realistic prospect. The agency retains a reasonable opportunity to arrange removal; uncertainty and ordinary diplomatic delay are not themselves disqualifying. Continuing flight and safety concerns may affect detention while removal remains realistically attainable and the lawful conditions of release. They do not supply a new general civil-commitment power after the removal ground ceases to justify confinement.

This construction rests on the statute's removal setting, discretionary rather than mandatory extension, and express supervised-release alternative, reinforced by the serious liberty problem of potentially lifelong imprisonment without a closely limited independent preventive ground. It uses constitutional avoidance; it does not announce a categorical constitutional ban on every preventive detention regime. The criminal and risk categories retain work in determining who may be detained beyond 90 days while removal remains a genuine prospect. The competing construction fairly invokes those categories, the words permitting detention beyond the period and the absence of an endpoint; it is not dismissed as textually impossible. Its difficulty is converting this removal authority into an unrestricted substitute for a separately justified civil commitment.

**A's implementation.** Ninety days is not a release deadline. A detainee who has remained confined beyond that period must present concrete grounds, considered with elapsed time, showing that the reasonable removal opportunity has run out and removal lacks a realistic foreseeable prospect. The government must answer that supported showing with facts supporting such a prospect, rather than the bare order, assertions of good faith, danger alone, or the theoretical possibility that a country may someday change its policy. The court assesses the actual attempts, refusals, negotiations, likely receiving arrangements, the detainee's conduct and time already spent; it gives appropriate weight to supported executive assessments of diplomatic prospects without treating them as conclusive. Increasing elapsed time makes a nonspecific prediction less persuasive. No requirement of permanent impossibility and no requirement of a formal repatriation agreement applies. A sufficient current countershowing permits continued custody; otherwise the court must provide supervised release under the statute. This is an operative legal standard with factual application left below, not permission to postpone the authority question indefinitely.

No fixed number of months, automatic release date, new periodic-review calendar or universal evidentiary-hearing command is adopted on these supplied sources. A numerical presumption could aid uniform administration, but would be a new judicial rule requiring a reason for its chosen interval. The old statute's six-month removal period and the new regulation's usual transition to headquarters after the 90-day period and an additional three months are real reference points. Neither purports to establish a substantive custody ceiling or proves that the same interval is appropriate for every receiving-country problem. The primary commitments favor the stated individualized standard and do not condition relief on adoption of a number. A new proposed interval cannot be attributed to these Justices without a grounded join test.

**B — Express discretionary detention authority, the primary statutory rule of Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** Paragraph (6) permits continued custody of the covered persons without making a judicial prediction of foreseeable removal an additional statutory prerequisite. Its distinct criminal, danger and noncompliance categories and its conditional supervision language do more than extend a fixed transportation window. The authority serves execution of the valid removal order and the statutory protective purposes while the government genuinely pursues removal. Lack of a treaty, unsuccessful attempts or inability to predict a date does not alone terminate that authority. The three reject a court-created duration or foreseeability limitation as statutory construction. Their agreement on B does not establish agreement on the constitutional safeguards or the Fifth Circuit disposition.

**K — Kennedy's additional constitutional limit.** For an admitted person held beyond the ordinary period, the government must supply an individualized, current justification connected to the statutory protective or removal purposes, with a meaningful opportunity to contest it and effective habeas review. A final order and the proposition that removal is not forever impossible do not alone justify prolonged custody. A substantial current individual danger, or a concrete compliance concern connected to a genuine removal process, can justify continued nonpunitive detention even though a removal date cannot reasonably be forecast. The justification must actually persist; stale criminal conduct and a paper review cannot be treated as irrebuttable. The court must assess whether the supported reason remains sufficient in light of the duration and actual circumstances, including the practical ability of lawful conditions to address that reason. This rule does not require an ordinary psychiatric diagnosis, does not transplant a particular civil-commitment burden or a universal least-restrictive-means test, and does not make every unsuccessful negotiation require release. The new regulation supplies meaningful mechanisms and possible evidence of compliance; it is not facially invalidated. Its alien satisfaction wording cannot insulate a prolonged detention from habeas determination of the government's actual current reason. This resolves the substantive need for a continuing reason and the necessary effectiveness of review; remand supplies the individual facts and any tailored procedural remedy. Zadvydas's intervening years at liberty particularly require a present decision rather than assumed persistence of the old custody rationale.

The Fifth Circuit's actual rule did require danger or flight, continuing good-faith efforts and reasonable parole and periodic-review procedures; it was not a bare-order authorization. Kennedy's narrower refusal concerns its very high impossibility threshold and the sufficiency of that framework to resolve the physical-liberty claim. Under K, inability to prove harmlessness plus a theoretically possible future removal cannot make an executive danger conclusion conclusive. The habeas court must independently assess the supported current justification with the duration and effective conditions in view, even though it does not substitute its own general immigration policy. This is the additional legal instruction supporting his vacatur, rather than a fictitious omission of all review below or the mere later adoption of a new regulation.

**S/T — Scalia and Thomas's narrower constitutional disposition.** The presented general challenge does not establish that continued detention under B, with genuine removal efforts, a reasonable individual administrative foundation and available release review, is unconstitutional merely because its endpoint cannot be predicted. Their statutory conclusion does not validate punitive confinement, sham removal efforts, arbitrary administration, denial of access to review, or a refusal ever to reconsider release. They do not impose K's additional government-justification formulation or A's foreseeability trigger. Actual Seling remains binding if a supported punishment claim is made; it supplies no finding of punishment here. The current general duration claim is rejected, rather than remanded for an unspecified constitutional theory.

**RZ/RM — Companion remedies.** Under A, vacate the Fifth Circuit judgment concerning Zadvydas and remand because permanent impossibility plus executive good faith and review is too permissive. Vacate the Ninth Circuit judgment concerning Ma and remand because the absence of an agreement cannot replace an individualized assessment of actual foreseeable removal. Neither vacatur itself orders release or renewed detention. The habeas courts apply A and order or preserve supervised release when detention lacks its required ground; if a genuine foreseeable removal prospect supports custody, they may deny the release claim, without the Supreme Court itself directing a new detention. Zadvydas's effective release order and the stayed appellate mandate must be handled in their actual posture; the judgment does not recast his years at liberty as custody or require an unnecessary new release event. Properly presented cooperation allegations require findings and application of the distinct statutory provision. Under K, Kennedy also vacates both judgments, but for a distinct individualized-justification inquiry: danger or concrete noncompliance may sustain custody even without A's foreseeable-removal showing. Under S/T, affirm the Fifth Circuit against the presented general duration challenge; reverse the Ninth Circuit's categorical release basis and remand under B and the retained limits. Even S/T would require the agency to decide any renewed Zadvydas custody under current standards rather than order his automatic return. No position infers that Ma must automatically be rearrested or that an existing release condition or stay changes without a proper order.

**P — Review-process issue and completeness.** A's five supporters decide that the new administrative machinery does not cure the statutory defect if it permits detention after A's removal-purpose basis ends. The current rule may consider travel prospects and other circumstances, but consideration alone is not A's required substantive consequence. The satisfaction showing and safety criteria cannot operate as an additional obstacle to relief from detention already unauthorized under A. No independent facial procedural invalidation, automatic hearing schedule or universal official-compliance finding is needed for that complete statutory resolution. Kennedy gives the current review machinery full weight but requires its actual use to satisfy K; he finds no categorical failure solely because the first review is by an immigration official, and mandates no replacement court hearing absent a material defect. Scalia and Thomas regard the present regulatory safeguards and retained habeas as sufficient to defeat the general duration/process objection presented, without declaring every application compliant. None of the eight uses superseded clear-and-convincing wording to decide the current regulatory question.

**I — Consequences shared by all eight.** Supervision preserves the order and the prospect of later removal. Paragraph (3)'s periodic identification, necessary government-funded medical or psychiatric examination, sworn information and reasonable written restrictions apply on release. Conditions must remain within lawful authority. Release does not grant admission anew, lawful permanent status, asylum, a waiver, cancellation or immunity from removal. A later detention or alternative custody decision requires an actual lawful source and satisfied predicates; neither an unlimited recommitment power nor immunity from otherwise lawful custody is announced. No contempt, new crime, obstruction or dangerousness finding is made.

His joins of Foucha's Parts II-A, II-B and II-C (May 18, 1992) require continuing grounds, distinguish substantive authority from hearings and reject that indefinite dangerousness substitute. His actual Hendricks Parts II–III join and Seling join support scrutiny of what custody does, without converting duration alone into punishment. His present-term St. Cyr opinion treats a legality judgment as requiring effective relief within the governing channel.

**Strongest competing path:** B gives genuine effect to Congress's criminal and danger categories, and his own National Center opinion rejects a claim that every immigration restriction serves appearance alone. He nevertheless distinguishes a supervised-release work condition from potentially lifelong imprisonment. A permits extended removal detention and risk-sensitive lawful conditions; B would let the removal provision carry a much larger independent commitment function without corresponding substantive limits.

**Barriers and conditional joins:** He will not join an impossibility test, a civil-label answer, or a process-only response after statutory authority ends. He can join a narrower fact-specific disposition if it applies A to both records and requires effective relief when removal is not realistically foreseeable. ### O'Connor

**Components and remedy:** J; A; RZ/RM vacatur under A; I. Her concurrence-level reservation concerns carefully delimited preventive regimes, not an exception that swallows A for anyone deemed risky. She would directly join A's application to these admitted persons and its bounded remedy.

**Own date-eligible grounds:** Her Foucha concurrence and Parts II joins reserve narrower detention while rejecting the indefinite regime actually before the Court. Her Flores concurrence, joined by Souter, recognizes a substantial liberty interest and rests on genuine protective care, the conditions agreement and temporary custody. Her actual Hendricks dissent (June 23, 1997) regards indefinite duration alone as insufficient proof of punishment but emphasizes current qualifying conditions and real release routes; it does not approve every preventive confinement statute. Her Seling join keeps those predicates and the separate unlawful-custody remedy in view. Her Calcano Court opinion supplies the actual review channel.

**Strongest competing path:** B plus K could preserve express congressional protective authority through individualized, effective review. Her Hendricks dissent makes that a substantial alternative: difficult treatment and uncertain duration do not nullify genuine civil custody. The new personal-interview, headquarters and reason-giving requirements strengthen that path and cannot be treated as nonexistent. Here, however, a broad predicted danger or criminal category lacks the more confined current-condition predicate she considered in Hendricks. In a removal statute, hearings about danger do not by themselves answer why detention continues when removal lacks a realistic prospect.

**Barriers and conditional joins:** She refuses a universal rule that preventive custody always needs psychiatric illness, an assumption that civil confinement with an unknown endpoint is punishment, or a ruling on every inadmissible person. She can join A without a fixed-month device and can agree to case-specific timing tied to actual prospects. A new numerical presumption needs an independent justification and cannot be a mandatory release ceiling. She would require a materially narrower, actually established statutory ground before considering different safety detention; no such ground is supplied here. Separate-writing function, if needed: preserve those narrower regimes and distinguish statutory authority, punishment and conditions.

**Strongest competing path:** A invokes serious liberty concerns and an ordinary removal purpose, with Foucha supporting the need for a continuing basis. **Barriers and conditional joins:** He can join rejection of permanent-impossibility and treaty proxies as universal factual measures without accepting A's legal trigger, and can join a remand confined to a proved defect in review or exercise of discretion.
