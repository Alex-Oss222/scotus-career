## Actual intervening law

Sterling Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, protects an adult against a credible official ultimatum to disclose suspected sexual orientation to family or face disclosure by the officer, when no legitimate governmental purpose supports either course. The infringement is operative official coercion of an objectively intimate confidence. Completed officer publication is unnecessary; an idle insult or nonoperative threat is insufficient. Actual demand, responsible actor, coercive effect, causation and injury remain for proof.

The Court extends Sheets's official-dissemination and Denius's compulsory-disclosure principles to that confined coercive setting. It does not establish a general secrecy entitlement, a universal privacy test, automatic threat liability, strict scrutiny or a least-restrictive-means requirement. It neither adjudicates emergency protective disclosure nor creates a general right to conceal criminal evidence. Those limits matter to the present comparison: the legal extension rests on official arrest authority, objectively intimate subject matter and compelled surrender without a legitimate official function.

Sterling Part II retains ordinary objective, particularized conduct-date immunity and finds sufficient April 1997 notice for that supported no-purpose ultimatum, by five direct joins. O'Connor and Kennedy join the present merits rule but dissent from the notice conclusion. Part III unanimously excludes Hoban's factual participation challenge from immediate immunity review while preserving properly separable legal questions on supported assumptions. Neither part supplies a private-speaker publication rule or changes Bartnicki's distinct certified legal question.

## Materiality to Bartnicki's law, facts and scope

**Conclusion: no material changed premise.** Sterling adds a genuine official-coercion holding, which must be acknowledged as current law, but it neither decides nor changes the premises of the private-recipient disclosure question in Bartnicki.

The Bartnicki defendants did not exercise official arrest authority or compel a person to surrender a confidence. The assumed unlawful interception was an unknown third party's act; the respondents lawfully received an accurate recording and did not participate in interception. The public-school bargaining discussion and apparent physical pressure establish the particular public concern. Sterling supplies no finding that those negotiations are objectively intimate, and it does not transform confidentiality by itself into constitutional intimacy.

The draft already recognizes the independent injury caused by dissemination and the value of candid private speech. Conversely, Sterling's refusal to make completed official publication essential to its operative-coercion claim cannot be imported as a rule governing every later disclosure, every threat, or private publication damages.

No immunity issue or officer-participation evidentiary appeal is presented in Bartnicki. Its legal defense is decided on the certified assumptions rather than by choosing disputed interception facts. Sterling's immunity notice division and interlocutory factual-review boundary therefore change neither the proper procedural channel nor the proposed summary-judgment remedy.
