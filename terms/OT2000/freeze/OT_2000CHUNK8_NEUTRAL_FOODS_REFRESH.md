* **Question Presented:** Whether the First Amendment permits compulsory assessments principally financing generic mushroom advertising where the producers remain otherwise independent market competitors.

* **Material Record:** The Mushroom Promotion, Research, and Consumer Information Act authorizes an industry council and assessments on domestic fresh-market producers and importers. Most money supports generic promotion. The Secretary appoints Council members through the statutory producer/importer nomination scheme and must approve plans, projects and budgets before implementation; contracts likewise require approved plans and accounting. Those enacted controls are not findings that an official wrote, selected or controlled every message in practice. United Foods wishes to advertise its own mushrooms as superior and objects to funding a message promoting the generic product. There are no comparable marketing orders restricting output and sales, no antitrust exemption, and no comprehensive cooperative marketing structure requiring producers to surrender their independent market decisions. United Foods challenged the assessment administratively; the government brought an enforcement action. After denial of administrative relief, review and enforcement proceedings were consolidated. The district court considered Glickman v. Wileman Brothers & Elliott controlling.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Below:** The district court treated generic agricultural promotion as governed by Glickman v. Wileman Brothers & Elliott. The Sixth Circuit distinguished the absence of comprehensive cooperative restrictions and held the compelled payments unconstitutional.

**Government — primary and alternative positions:** Generic advertising promotes a common economic interest, can avoid free riding, and does not attribute the message to a particular producer or prevent brand-specific counteradvertising. The government first seeks the deference it understands Glickman to permit. It expressly argues in the alternative that the assessment is valid even under less deferential First Amendment scrutiny and denies waiver of that argument. Its three components are distinct:

- **Interest:** Maintaining and expanding existing mushroom markets and developing new uses serve producers and the national agricultural economy. The government cites Congress's concern about instability, canned-mushroom import competition, relatively low domestic consumption and smaller growers' limited capacity to fund promotion.
- **Advancement:** The government relies on experience with other commodity programs and the 1998 continuation vote. It additionally reports an independent Food Marketing and Economics Group review dated February 2000, quotes a positive overall fresh-mushroom-demand conclusion from page 7, and states that copies were lodged with the Court. It disputes United Foods' inference that increased competing supply defeats any relevant benefit and argues that profitable industry-wide expansion can serve the stated interest even without proof of gain to this producer.
- **Fit:** The government reports testimony that voluntary industry campaigns failed when some producers did not contribute. It defends the exemption for producers and importers at or below the 500,000-pound annual threshold quoted from §6102(6) and (11) as avoiding disproportionate collection and enforcement costs; its cost illustration computes $1,250 for 500,000 pounds at the reply's stated quarter-cent-per-pound rate, not a proved administrative-expense amount. It distinguishes generic market expansion from a brand's effort to increase its market share and therefore rejects automatic credit for individual advertising. It invokes the referendum's number-and-volume requirements and defends national rather than regional promotion. It disputes the claimed record support for purposeful targeting away from regions where branded advertising had already increased consumption.

These are attributed advocacy and reported evidence, not findings that the campaign was effective, that every producer benefited, that every alleged free-riding mechanism existed, or that the statutory lines were justified. The government's separate government-speech argument retains its own preservation, attribution and record questions.

**United Foods — primary and alternative positions:** The government cannot force competitors to fund another seller's preferred promotional message merely because officials or an industry majority regard it as beneficial. Its claim of superior quality conflicts with generic promotion, and the comprehensive collective-market justification in the actual Glickman holding is absent. Advertising cannot supply its own independent justification for compelled association.

United Foods also asserts waiver of a separate Central Hudson defense and argues that the assessment fails if that framework or another meaningful First Amendment inquiry applies. It challenges the asserted free-rider rationale because its substantial branded advertising receives no credit, small growers receive asserted benefits without paying assessments, and processed-market participants can shift into the fresh market. It argues that its own distribution is confined to three markets while substantial program spending benefits other regions; highly elastic supply and entry by competitors, movement from processed to fresh mushrooms and intermediary capture allegedly prevent an individual producer from receiving the claimed gain. It challenges the program's budget, local targeting and evidence of demand advancement, cites the industry's preprogram growth, and questions distinctions among agricultural commodities.

Its brief quotes a June 1996 ruling in the separate Donald B. Mills administrative matter and cites joint-appendix material and economics literature. The government responds that the cited administrative reasoning concerned the lack of sufficient years of data at that earlier stage and points to the later reported review. The separate-case ruling, research writings, forecasted market responses and both sides' assertions remain in their respective source categories. They are not a controlling holding here, an established finding of no effect, a concession by the government, or a new factual determination that United Foods cannot benefit.

**Limits and Alternatives:** The actual Southworth holding supports an independently justified, broad and viewpoint-neutral educational private-expression program; it does not establish a generic exemption for compelled financing or make every institutional message governmental. Lebron's actor classification similarly answers who bears constitutional duties, not whether every message and earmarked assessment is the government's own speech. Keller, 496 U.S. 1, 10–14, rejects treating the integrated Bar's state-law agency label and public purpose as sufficient to exempt its compelled-dues expression, while distinguishing ordinary representative-government speech and taxation. Its advisory professional structure and mandatory membership matter; it does not establish that all statutory industry councils are private speakers or that all assessments are taxes. The governmental/private-speech distinction here must be decided on the Council's actual statutory structure and established record if the alternative is reached. This is not a misleading-advertisement disclosure requirement imposed on the producer's own speech. Nor does objecting to the advertising establish a constitutional objection to every neutral economic assessment or government-funded message. Any independent government-speech argument must be both properly presented and factually supported rather than assumed from the statute's existence.
