Event: May 29, 2001; No. 00-24. Argued January 17, 2001. Research cutoff: May 29, 2001. All nine members participate at submission and decision. This context received only authorized reconciliation inputs and public law. No controlled Chief Justice material was received. No author or final Court tally is assigned.

## Reconciliation result

The independent commitments stand. Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer affirm on both Title III coverage and absence of fundamental alteration. Scalia and Thomas reverse on coverage and, independently assuming coverage, on the contest-definition ground. ## Full historical opinion comparison

The official report, 532 U.S. 661–705, contains Stevens's opinion joined by Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, and Scalia's dissent joined by Thomas. The historical judgment affirmed, seven to two. Every writing and footnote was read, including the dissent's separate service-recipient, content-of-service and rule-defined-competition arguments. Historical authorship is comparator only.

Each Associate retains the historical component alignment. The six affirmers share direct support for coverage of this qualifying and tournament opportunity and the individual no-alteration application. The historical majority did not need to resolve a universal clients-or-customers restriction because Martin qualified even on the Tour's construction; the present six likewise need no holding governing every worker or contractual relationship. Scalia and Thomas's historical dissent recognized the limited location of the customer clause and relied on structure and the professional relationship; their present opposition does not rest on misquoting its reach. Their alternative objection distinguishes access to the offered contest from compelling a changed contest and rejects courts' essential-rule and equalized-fatigue inquiries. No unsupported departure or additional join is needed.

The actual entering Sutton rule differs from historical Sutton: beneficial mitigation does not erase underlying impairment, while individual substantial limitation remains required. That difference does not change this case's outcome because Martin's actual disability and the cart's necessity are established rather than disputed under either method. Kennedy's and O'Connor's Sutton disagreement therefore supplies no coverage barrier here. Kirkingburg permits use of a particular substantive federal safety criterion despite an experimental waiver; it supplies no exemption for private tournament rules. Bragdon's direct-threat method and Olmstead's Title II resource defense are useful bounded comparisons, not substitutes for Title III's enacted alteration condition. Garrett concerns private Title I enforcement against state employers and does not decide these private Title III issues.

## Final common grounds and remedy

The six affirmers join the rule that the operator's covered golf courses offer qualifying and playing privileges to eligible entrants as well as spectators; a competitor's contribution to entertainment does not defeat this recipient relationship. Subsection (b)(1)(A)(iv) expressly limits its definition to the specified clauses. Martin is in any event a recipient of the paid qualifying opportunity, so a universal employment or contractor rule is unnecessary.

They separately join necessary reasonable modification subject to the entity's demonstrated fundamental-alteration defense. Alteration can concern an essential competitive feature even if changed equally for everyone, or a peripheral change that grants the particular competitor a material competitive advantage. Existing uniform enforcement alone does not decide either inquiry. The supported trial findings identify shot-making as essential, walking as nonessential in this setting, and Martin's fatigue even with a cart as exceeding ordinary walking fatigue. The findings, subject to ordinary factual review, establish no advantage; they are not statutory declarations about every golfer. The injunction stands subject to ordinary qualification and other valid competition rules.

Scalia and Thomas join neither coverage nor these alteration propositions. Their alternative assumes coverage for analysis and does not become a vote for it. They would remove this Title III injunction's basis without deciding an employment cause. No modification of the frozen grounds, new trial, damages award or universal cart requirement follows. No unresolved reconciliation blocker remains.
