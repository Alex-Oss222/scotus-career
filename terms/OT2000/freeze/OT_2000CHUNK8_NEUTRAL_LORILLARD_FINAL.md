## 2. Event, judgment below, and requested relief

Lorillard Tobacco Co. v. Reilly and Altadis U.S.A. Inc. v. Reilly present challenges to Massachusetts tobacco advertising and sales rules. The route is certiorari review of the First Circuit, 218 F.3d 30, decided July 17, 2000. The present argument date is April 25, 2001. The questions concern federal cigarette-labeling preemption and the First Amendment validity of the challenged advertising and product-access provisions. The supplied neutral event identification reconstructs the questions from the judicial record; the exact petition and grant order are not independent sources in this screened set. Do not manufacture narrower grant language or a ruling on preservation from that absence.

The district court's December 2, 1999 preemption decision, 76 F. Supp. 2d 124, severed §21.04(6) and otherwise rejected the asserted preemption. Its January 24, 2000 First Amendment decision, 84 F. Supp. 2d 180, sustained the principal restrictions but invalidated the geographical boundary as applied to indoor point-of-sale advertising. Judgment followed January 25. On the manufacturers' appeals and the Attorney General's cross-appeal, the First Circuit sustained the challenged outdoor and retail restrictions and restored the indoor restriction. It separately invalidated cigar-warning requirements on Commerce Clause grounds. That warning relief and the earlier small-sign relief must be kept distinct from the issues presented for present review.

Manufacturers and retailers seek reversal and declaratory and injunctive relief against the challenged provisions. The Attorney General seeks affirmance of their enforceability. No damages claim is presented here. No case-specific participation limitation is identified in the screened input.

### Small text signs: printed permission versus the appealed scheme

The reproduced regulations contain two separate permissions: §21.04(6) for cigarettes and smokeless tobacco, and §22.06(6) for cigars. Each permits, rather than requires, one sign no larger than 576 square inches, outside or visible from outside each sales location, with black text on a white background limited to a statement that tobacco products are sold there. It is not a permission for brand advertising.

**Section 21.04(6) is not an operative alternative channel in the appealed cigarette or smokeless scheme.** The district court expressly struck and severed §21.04(6), Pet. App. 57a and 59a–60a. Footnote 11 expressly extended that result to smokeless tobacco because the provision applied indistinguishably to cigarettes and smokeless tobacco. The First Circuit states that this relief was not appealed, Pet. App. 6a n.2 and 34a. Its fit discussion treats the sign as part of the Attorney General's original calculation, acknowledges that its removal increases the burden, and nevertheless upholds the remaining scheme. It does not restore the sign.

**Section 22.06(6) remains a separate printed cigar permission; the supplied operative ruling does not strike it.** The First Circuit's introductory description cites both sections and then refers generically to the district ruling. The district's actual operative text identifies only §21.04(6); its extension in n.11 identifies smokeless tobacco, not cigars. Those generic introductory descriptions cannot establish broader relief against §22.06(6). Evaluate the cigar scheme with its separate permission and do not import the cigarette severance into it without an additional controlling order. No such order is supplied or needed to describe the supported scope.

### Severability is an argument and a remedial question

Sections 21.06 and 22.08 separately preserve the remainder of their chapter and its applications to other persons and circumstances if a provision or application is invalid. These clauses do not themselves decide every possible application of severability. The district used §21.06 to sever the text-sign provision while extending that provision's invalidity across cigarettes and smokeless tobacco. It did not decide the result if the other cigarette rules were preempted. USSTC's broader inseverability argument remains distinct; the State invokes the regulatory structure and severability clauses in defense of the remaining provisions. Possible narrower geographical, party-specific, or product-specific relief likewise requires its own legal basis, rather than an assumption that every provision falls together or that every provision necessarily survives.

## 4. Complete material regulatory qualifications

The full original text and amendment instructions are preserved in the linked source-text unit. Read the two together. The rules were promulgated January 22, 1999 and amended January 19, 2000. The amendments change both chapters' printed effective dates from August 1, 1999 to **February 1, 2000**, add adult-only facilities and permissions, narrow the premium prohibition, and revise the height provision. The original wording is not a competing operative rule.

**Separate products and definitions.** Chapter 21 governs cigarettes and smokeless tobacco and expressly excludes cigars and little cigars, which chapter 22 governs. The advertising definition covers oral, written, graphic, or pictorial representations made by or for covered sellers with the purpose or effect of promoting use or sale. It includes product-identifying logos, symbols, colors, patterns, and specified utilitarian items and fixtures, but excludes utilitarian items of 200 cubic inches or less. The terms are to be interpreted consistently with the Attorney General's retail advertising rules. This definition matters to claimed burdens on signs, fixtures, packages, and displays; it is not limited to billboards or explicit purchase commands.

**Outdoor rule.** Sections 21.04(5)(a) and 22.06(5)(a) prohibit outdoor advertising within a 1,000-foot radius of a public playground, playground area in a public park, elementary school, or secondary school. Outdoor advertising includes advertising in enclosed stadiums and advertising from inside a retail establishment that is directed toward or visible from outside. The trigger is not every part of every park or every educational institution. The definition of playground describes outdoor premises operated by the State, public bodies, schools, child day-care centers, or youth centers containing fixed or portable recreational or athletic devices for persons under 18; the operative subsection still specifies the listed geographical triggers. There is no general adult-only-facility exception to the outdoor restriction.

**Indoor height rule.** Amended §§21.04(5)(b) and 22.06(5)(b) cover point-of-sale advertising any portion of which is below five feet from the floor in a retail establishment within the same 1,000-foot radius, unless it is an adult-only retail establishment. The retailer must ensure that no person younger than 18 is present or permitted to enter the adult-only facility at any time. There is no exception for children accompanying an adult. A retailer's argument that policing that condition would be costly is a claimed compliance burden, not text requiring a particular guard. Advertising above five feet can still be prohibited if visible or directed outside under subsection (5)(a). The adult-only height exception does not exempt outside advertising.

**Physical access.** Sections 21.04(2)(c)–(d) and 22.06(2)(c)–(d) generally prohibit self-service displays and require tobacco to be out of consumers' reach in a location accessible only to outlet personnel. They are subject to the separate subsection (4) exceptions. The January amendments expressly permit self-service displays in adult-only retail facilities. These rules therefore do not categorically prohibit adult handling in every outlet, prohibit all visible display, or prohibit every clerk-assisted inspection. Whether the remaining modes adequately preserve claimed communication and inspection interests is disputed.

**Sampling.** Subsection (1)(a)'s general prohibition on sampling, promotional giveaways, and other free distribution is subject to subsection (4). In an adult-only facility, the amended exception permits at most one free sample per day to an individual adult. The permitted sample is one pack of cigarettes in the manufacturer's smallest individual-consumer size; one smallest-size can of smokeless tobacco; one smallest-size pack of little cigars; or one smallest-size pack of cigars, with at most five individual cigars if the cigars are not distributed in packs. An exception for these limited samples is not an unrestricted permission to give products away.

**Mail and vending exceptions.** Mail-order sales, excluding mail-order coupon redemption and free samples, require verification that the recipient is at least 18, including at minimum a legible photocopy of valid government identification from that recipient. The vending exception requires a facility licensed for on-premises alcohol consumption; a staff-operated lockout that does not permit continuous operation and whose release mechanism is inaccessible to consumers; immediate staff proximity and exclusive control allowing every purchase to be observed; and posted notice barring minors, explaining the lockout, and identifying the staff to contact. The saved cigar licensing-subsection reference has an OCR difference from chapter 21. Preserve that text rather than silently changing the statutory subsection or resolving a licensing question not presented here.

**Retail safeguards and other prohibitions.** Retail sales must be face to face unless a subsection (4) exception applies. Age verification uses valid government photographic identification, except for a purchaser who appears at least 27. The January amendment narrows the purchase-premium prohibition to apparel or other non-tobacco merchandise bearing a brand name; the original broader gift language is superseded. Brand name is separately defined to include identifying matter beyond a literal name. Chapter 21 prohibits breaking packages to distribute less than the manufacturer's smallest individual-consumer package; the corresponding chapter 22 unpackaging rule concerns little cigars, and must not be expanded to every individual cigar.

Subsection (3) requires all reasonably necessary measures to prevent sales to persons under 18. It provides compliance protections using employee training and, for retailers with six or more employees, a secret-shopper program at all their outlets; retailers with five or fewer employees have the training alternative. The defined program requires at least six visits per year at different days and times and, where practical, involving different employees. Subsection (3)(a) also provides presumptive compliance with the secret-shopper measure for the next calendar year at each identified outlet after official written notification from a state or local governmental authority that the outlet passed a tobacco compliance check. It does not impose an identical private-testing program on every retailer or displace the underlying prevention duty.

Chapter 22 also contains a sign requirement of at least 50 square inches for hand-rolled cigars or manufactured cigars displayed outside their original package, clearly visible to purchasers, §22.06(2)(e). It is background to the regulatory structure, not an additional litigated component supplied merely by reproducing the full regulations. Keep it distinct from the label-and-advertising warning sections adjudicated below.

**Other law and signs.** The rules preserve other common-law and statutory duties and local permit, licensing, and tobacco controls, with the more restrictive regulation controlling an inconsistency. The two small-sign provisions have the different present statuses explained in section 3. The two severability clauses also remain distinct. These qualifications limit any characterization of the rules as an absolute ban, an unrestricted adult-only exception, or a single indivisible tobacco program.

## 5. Judicial determinations, assumptions, and party assertions

### Summary judgment and the first commercial-speech step

The First Circuit explains that the Attorney General was unwilling to concede every advertisement was truthful and nonmisleading, but assumed lawful, truthful, nonmisleading speech for purposes of summary judgment, Pet. App. 17a. The State's brief p.35 n.17 denies a general stipulation or finding and requests an opportunity to try factual issues if its judgment cannot stand on that assumption. The cigar reply p.2 n.1 disputes that procedural position, arguing that the State's response to the cigar parties' cross-motion did not reserve a factual challenge. The smokeless reply likewise characterizes the State's position as a concession.

The supported neutral premise is the assumption on which the lower judgment was reviewed, accompanied by the parties' dispute over its procedural consequences. Do not convert it into a finding that every advertisement is truthful, a finding that the challenged speech is deceptive, an unconditional concession, or an adjudication of entitlement to a later trial. The statutory designation of regulated practices as unfair or deceptive is not a factual finding that the particular advertisements are false. The First Circuit itself was unconvinced that these restrictions furthered prevention of false or misleading advertising, Pet. App. 28a n.11, while relying on the other asserted interests.

### What the First Circuit actually reasoned

On preemption, the First Circuit construed the federal clause to preserve the location restrictions, emphasizing traditional state regulation and nationally uniform cigarette messages. It did not adopt the district court's conclusion that these restrictions were not based on smoking and health; see Pet. App. 13a n.6. The two courts' rationales must not be collapsed into one holding. Neither lower ruling supplies the governing Supreme Court answer.

On speech, the First Circuit used the commercial-speech framework, rejected the demand for strict scrutiny despite the tobacco-specific subject matter, and identified youth health and prevention of unlawful sales as substantial interests. It relied on reports, studies, and common-sense inferences to find material advancement. It discussed smokeless and cigar differences rather than declaring that any evidence about cigarettes automatically proves every tobacco restriction. It acknowledged the force of the cigar industry's small advertising footprint and of burdens on small retailers.

The court accepted that the geographical rule had very extensive urban effects, but connected schools and playgrounds with children's exposure, credited the FDA's prior investigation and the industry's voluntary 500-foot billboard policy, and regarded the 1,000-foot line as reasonable. It reversed the district court's indoor-boundary ruling. It expressed misgivings about assuming that children below five feet would not look upward, but accepted the height rule and the remaining above-five-foot indoor channel, subject to the outside-visibility rule. Those are the court's judgments about this record, not a command in the regulation or a neutral concession by petitioners.

The court rejected an exhaustion requirement for nonspeech alternatives, emphasized Massachusetts's existing tobacco-control efforts, and distinguished restricting supply from reducing youth demand. It recognized that newspapers and magazines could be too costly for small retailers. Its reference to the former §21.04(6) sign concerns the regulator's original calculation, expressly acknowledging the additional burden after severance, Pet. App. 33a–34a. It did not find that the sign remained available.

On retail access, the First Circuit principally treated physical placement of ordinary sealed tobacco packages as conduct, noting possible empty-package displays. For premium cigars it assumed that customers' physical inspection might involve protected communication and upheld the rule in the alternative under the commercial-speech analysis. Do not present that alternative holding as a categorical determination that no cigar inspection can be speech. The independent Commerce Clause treatment of cigar warnings is outside the present requested review.

## 6. Evidence, provenance, and disputes material to the questions

**Geographical coverage.** Petitioners rely on figures placing roughly 87–91 percent of Boston, Worcester, and Springfield beyond permissible outdoor advertising and emphasize lawful adult listeners, neighborhood shops, prices, brands, and entry by new sellers. The State disputes attributing the entire figure to the challenged 1,000-foot rule: its brief p.10 describes maps combining those zones with residential areas, colleges, cemeteries, parks, schools, and other exclusions. No agreed incremental percentage uniquely caused by this rule is supplied. The First Circuit nevertheless accepted the substantial urban reach as a serious point, including effects approaching 90 percent. Preserve both the acknowledged breadth and the contested attribution. A calculation that halves a radius does not independently establish actual usable urban advertising space where zones overlap and other restrictions apply.

**Which evidence belongs to which record.** The lower record, as described in the opinions and briefs, includes Surgeon General and Institute of Medicine reports, the FDA rulemaking materials, studies, affidavits, and the parties' expert submissions. The fact that the FDA lacked the asserted regulatory authority does not itself erase the underlying observations; conversely, the FDA's legal conclusions do not become governing law or prove that its cigarette-focused evidence establishes each cigar or smokeless proposition. The State also submitted a later Lodging containing the 2000 Surgeon General report and newer studies, some postdating the appellate decision. USSTC objects to their use and challenges their substance. Identify those as later submissions, not evidence necessarily considered by the district court or a new judicial finding. The complete briefs expose the dispute; they are not substitutes for the full raw studies if an eventual decision turns on an unquoted methodological detail.

**Cigarettes and advertising.** The State argues that youth advertising exposure, recognition, longitudinal studies, marketing expenditures, and international experience support reduced initiation and consumption. Petitioners dispute causation and the marginal effect of these location rules, emphasize family and peer influences, and invoke experts' critiques of controls and the distinction between brand switching and additional demand. The parties dispute the weight and product-specific implications of the studies. Neither a universal causal concession nor a judicial finding that there is no supporting evidence follows from their disagreement.

**Settlement constraints.** The 1998 cigarette settlement binds its signatories and already limits specified billboards, transit, stadium, mall, and other advertising, with a retail exterior-sign exception of no more than 14 square feet. It also addresses youth targeting and cartoons. Petitioners invoke those constraints and existing federal broadcast restrictions when describing cumulative burdens. The State notes remaining indoor advertising and increased expenditures and contends that nonsigners and potential entrants are not necessarily covered. USSTC's separate smokeless settlement did not include Massachusetts, and the cigar companies were not parties to the cigarette settlement. Do not treat one settlement as a legal prohibition applicable to every product or as immunity from otherwise lawful regulation.

**Smokeless tobacco.** USSTC emphasizes falling use, limits of the evidence, product differences, adult communication, and the State's asserted inability to isolate effects of its proposed restrictions. Its briefs cite a decline among high-school students from 9.4 percent in 1993 to 6 percent in 1997 and 4.9 percent in 1999. These figures concern use during the preceding 30 days, not necessarily regular use or addiction. The First Circuit also discusses an earlier study's 8.0-to-4.5-percent decline between 1993 and 1996; those are not interchangeable series. The State argues that continuing youth use and historical promotion support prevention despite a decline. The reply attacks causal inferences, including the Choi evidence and later report material, and argues that tax changes provide alternative explanations. USSTC also invokes studies of purchase refusal and products accessible for theft in support of its retail argument. Those numerical studies remain attributed party evidence, not an independent judicial finding that access controls are useless or sufficient.

**Cigars.** The lower opinion recognizes very small outdoor spending, including approximately $50,500 nationally in 1997, and the absence of cigar billboards in Massachusetts. Petitioners argue that the storefront research contains only a small number of cigar advertisements among thousands of exposures and that cigarette data cannot establish cigar effects. They rely on descriptions of premium-cigar marketing and argue that reported growth in cigar use coincided with editorial and celebrity publicity, not the regulated commercial speech. The State relies on the broader increase in cigar use, risks to young people, promotion, and the possibility that even a small restriction helps particular children. The First Circuit credited a small benefit with a correspondingly small advertising burden. The parties also disagree over later youth-use statistics, including whether occasional past-month use is being confused with frequent use. None of these claims establishes that every cigar is a premium cigar, that all children use cigars regularly, or that a conceded health interest establishes advertising causation.

**Height and product access.** The State invokes children's encounters with low advertisements and evidence about signs below roughly three and one-half feet, largely concerning cigarette storefront marketing. Petitioners answer that older minors exceed five feet, younger children can look upward, and the rule burdens adult communication without a demonstrated product-specific benefit. On access, petitioners claim that packages and displays convey brands, prices, and freshness; premium-cigar purchasers also seek texture, weight, aroma, and comparison through handling. The State emphasizes theft prevention, clerk-mediated age checks, visible or empty displays, and adult-only facilities. Petitioners answer that excluding children, including those accompanying adults, burdens retailers and customers. Distinguish actual regulatory commands from predicted compliance costs and the disputed adequacy of alternatives.

**Other means and State efforts.** Petitioners propose enforcement of minimum-age sales rules, licensing, retailer education, penalties, checks, taxes, and counter-advertising. The State describes local licensing, 31,603 compliance operations during 1994–1998, substantial counter-advertising expenditures, and a high tax, and argues that many minors obtain cigarettes from sources other than direct store purchases. The cigar reply disputes the relevance of cigarette enforcement to cigars; petitioners dispute the adequacy of statewide enforcement and licensing. A claim that a statewide licensing system is absent is not proof that local licensing is absent. Nor does the State's position concede that it considered no alternatives. The relevance of these means to supply, demand, advancement, and fit remains a legal and evidentiary question under the governing standard.

## 7. Effective law and reference limits

The actual entering-law sources, rather than historical descriptions embedded in the parties' briefs, control. The June 25 dependency has been discharged. Nothing in this final factual packet reopens it or supplies a new legal gate.

**Cigarette preemption.** The exact federal clause is preserved at the beginning of C_SOURCE_TEXT. Its subsection (b) concerns state-law requirements or prohibitions based on smoking and health with respect to advertising or promotion of cigarettes whose packages comply with the federal labeling provisions; subsection (a)'s package-statement rule is distinct. Actual Cipollone must be read by statutory period and predicate duty, using the Holdings and Standards entries. It does not create general cigarette-industry immunity, make every duty to avoid deception preempted, or itself resolve the precise geographical restrictions here. Its controlling relationships among covered warning and advertising duties, surviving independent duties, and the narrower reasoning supporting other judgments must be preserved rather than describing one entire rationale as a uniform majority holding.

The complete authorized supplement supplies Medtronic, Geier, and Reno v. ACLU. Medtronic addresses the actual federal requirements and asserted state duties under its own statute. Geier requires an actual conflict with the operative federal objective, rather than mere subject-matter overlap. Neither supplies a cigarette-specific outcome. Current Buckman and Egelhoff likewise retain their particular statutory predicates; neither substitutes for construing this federal clause. Mead distinguishes delegated authority to speak with controlling force from potentially persuasive agency material; an evidentiary report's persuasive value is not identical to its legal authority.

**Commercial speech.** Central Hudson and Fox remain the governing ordinary framework for the lawful, truthful, nonmisleading commercial speech assumed here: substantial interest, actual material advancement, and reasonable fit between the restriction and its objective. The State bears its justificatory burden. This is not a universal least-restrictive-means test, a categorical empirical-study requirement, or automatic strict scrutiny whenever a restriction identifies a product. The Standards entries on commercial-speech advancement and fit, alcohol regulation and freedom of speech, and cigarette-labeling preemption should be used in their actual scopes and effective dates.

Discovery Network, Edenfield, Rubin, 44 Liquormart, and Greater New Orleans supply their actual advancement and fit rules and limits. They require attention to the asserted interest, evidence or adequately supported inference, exclusions and inconsistencies, the speech burden, and relevant alternatives. Their results do not establish that every incremental measure fails, that every common-sense inference suffices, or that every product must be governed identically. In actual 44 Liquormart the advancement and fit portions have different direct support; stronger published proposals do not become controlling law merely because the judgment invalidated the restriction. Actual Greater New Orleans preserves ordinary analysis with its as-applied scope and separate reasoning. Do not import the historical outcome of Florida Bar or other divergent precedent from a brief's quotation. The saved actual-law entries resolve that authority question.

Reno concerns content-based criminal restrictions on Internet expression and the adult-speech burden, including limits on judicial rewriting and severability. Its relevance to adult access does not itself transfer strict scrutiny or protected noncommercial overbreadth doctrine to every commercial claim here. The parties' stronger-scrutiny and adult-listener arguments should be evaluated as arguments, with attention to the distinct speech and regulatory setting.

**June 25 law.** The exact public United Foods handoff in the law-refresh file controls its independent-function rule for compulsory private promotional financing. That decision retains Central Hudson in its proper field, makes no empirical application to tobacco advertising, and does not find the government's commercial-speech argument waived. Its treatment of a separate operational government-speech theory is discretionary review treatment, not a universal forfeiture rule. The exact handoff preserves its distinct published noncontrolling positions. No compulsory financing obligation or tobacco evidentiary finding may be inferred from it.

The exact Colorado II handoff concerns demonstrated circumvention of candidate-contribution restrictions through coordinated party spending. It does not replace the commercial-speech framework. The other completed June 25 cases do not change the cigarette clause or supply tobacco facts. No Justice's position on those different statutory and factual predicates decides a commitment here.
