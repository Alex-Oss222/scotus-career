# K.D.M. v. Reedsport School District

# SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** K.D.M. v. Reedsport School District
* **Citation or Docket:** 196 F.3d 1046; Ninth Circuit judgment of November 15, 1999.
* **October Term:** 2000.
* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS; review of the lower-court judgment; merits disposition.
* **Simulated Event Date:** June 25, 2001, organizationally assigned.
* **Question Presented:** Whether the Free Exercise and Equal Protection Clauses permit a State to withhold onsite special-education services solely because a child attends a religious school, when adequate services and transportation are provided at a nearby secular location?
* **Question Granted:** Review encompasses the question presented.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** Ninth Circuit reversal of the district court's constitutional judgment and injunction; both courts rejected an independent statutory entitlement to onsite delivery under IDEA.
* **Relief Requested:** K.D.M. seeks restoration of constitutional relief against the religious-location restriction and provision of the vision specialist at his school. The school district and state superintendent seek preservation of their delivery arrangement.
* **Material Record:** K.D.M., a blind child with cerebral palsy, formerly received special-education services in public school. His parents transferred him to Harbor Baptist Church School for religious reasons. The district continued supplying equipment there, including braillers and computers, but provided the vision specialist at a fire hall nearby because Oregon required a religiously neutral setting. Services occurred about ninety minutes twice weekly, with district transportation. Their adequacy and the safety of travel were stipulated. Even at the religious school, instruction would occur in a separate room rather than in the ordinary classroom. The case was tried on stipulated facts. Ninth Circuit footnote 2 states that the record does not disclose whether this or another district actually provided onsite services at a nonsectarian private school, although the rule permits doing so. The comparison is therefore the rule's legal religious distinction, not a proved actual secular onsite recipient. An asserted loss of necessary classroom integration cannot be invented to support relief.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The constitutional claim concerns the religion-specific legal delivery restriction on services the district has chosen to furnish. There is no established actual nonsectarian onsite recipient; that absence does not decide the legal significance of an express classification. The statutory cross-appeal was resolved against an individual right to onsite services. Constitutional permission, statutory entitlement, and nondiscriminatory administration remain separate questions. The actual educational need and the continuing location restriction support the prospective claim; the record establishes no mootness.
* **Relevant Dates:** Appellate judgment November 15, 1999, before the assigned event.

**Companion or Related Matters:** No separately briefed companion. Any earlier controlling decision concerning religious funding must be applied within its scope.

**Participation Matter:** None identified.

### Issue 1: Religious classification in delivery of an available service

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah holds that objective religious targeting defeats neutrality and requires compelling justification with narrow tailoring. Employment Division v. Smith remains applicable to genuinely neutral, generally applicable regulation. Agostini v. Felton permits publicly controlled, operationally separate secular instruction on religious premises; that permission creates no unconditional onsite entitlement.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** A religious disability must be assessed through its text, operation, and actual comparators. Religious impact alone is insufficient; formal religion-based differential treatment cannot be absorbed into a generic administrative balance. Effective secular controls address the funded function.
* **Other Governing Law:** The Free Exercise and Equal Protection Clauses; IDEA's private-school services framework and Oregon's location requirement as reproduced in the opinion.
* **Material Other Authority:** Mitchell v. Helms rejects affiliation or possible diversion as a substitute for actual prohibited religious financing. Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills School District requires an antecedent-law remand and does not decide the Establishment Clause merits.
* **Existing Rule's Reach:** Onsite public service is not presumptively devotional. Nor does constitutional permission dictate every administrative site choice. Comparable secular logistical restrictions remain possible. Freiler applies Agostini's single inquiry into governmental religious authority and actual function: purpose, effects and institutional relationships are evidence, not separate invalidation prongs. Official attribution identifies the public act; it does not by itself establish that act's religious function. Here, the public service's actual content, control and operational separation must be assessed without treating its religious-school location as proof of religious teaching. Conversely, Freiler's recognition that secular objectives cannot justify an established prohibited religious function cannot be turned into permission for every delivery arrangement. It does not decide K.D.M.'s distinct challenge to an express religious classification, require a secular building, or create an onsite benefit entitlement.
* **Open Point:** Whether a sufficient and safely accessible alternative eliminates a constitutional objection to the express religious classification, and what remedy follows if that classification is invalid.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

The Ninth Circuit majority emphasized the absence of demonstrated educational or travel harm and concluded that the regulation, as applied, did not impermissibly burden religious exercise. It also rejected the district court's premise that onsite delivery necessarily meant classroom integration and upheld the State's interest in separating the public service from a religious setting. The dissent considered the express difference between religious and secular private-school children sufficient to trigger rigorous review despite the alternative service.

K.D.M.'s strongest rule is that government cannot impose a special condition on receipt of an available secular benefit solely because of religious enrollment, even when compliance is manageable. He must accept the stipulated adequacy of the alternative and cannot derive an absolute right to onsite delivery from IDEA or Agostini v. Felton.

The government argues that it fully supplies the relevant benefit, that safe transport and a nearby site impose no meaningful interference with religious education, and that it may organize publicly financed instruction to maintain institutional separation. It asks the Court to distinguish service availability from preferred location. Its account must explain why religion alone changes the delivery rule when the same secular instruction can remain independently controlled onsite. Actual devotional use cannot be assumed from the setting.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

Affirmance could hold that this delivery arrangement imposes no actionable impairment of religious exercise or unequal access given the stipulated equivalent service. That rule would have to preserve claims involving actual loss of access or more onerous religion-specific conditions.

Reversal could hold the express religious criterion unjustified and require a new location decision under neutral educational and administrative criteria. Such a ruling would not itself mandate the child's preferred room or foreclose offsite delivery for comparable secular reasons. The district decree enjoined enforcement of OAR 581-15-166 and religiously different terms but expressly declined an unconditional right to a vision specialist. It allowed refusal of private-school services on equal nonreligious terms consistent with IDEA. Its text is therefore not an unconditional onsite-delivery order. Any reinstatement, modification or remand must address that actual decree, its formal reach against the regulation, and the continuing statutory equitable-participation limits, without relitigating the stipulated adequacy and safety facts.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** The question supports deciding whether an express religious condition on service location escapes scrutiny because its practical cost is modest. It does not require a universal constitutional right to private-school services, compulsory subsidy of religious instruction, or reconsideration of IDEA funding levels. Any remedy must separate equal eligibility for onsite delivery from guaranteed delivery there.
