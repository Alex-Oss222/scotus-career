## Governing issue and competing constructions

The question concerns only claims for mental or emotional injury suffered in custody, barred solely by section 1997e(e), brought by six plaintiffs confined on March 10, 1997 and released before the June 24, 1998 district judgment. The absence of the required physical-injury showing is the accepted premise of this temporal review, not an adjudication of the alleged shakedown wrongs. The other five plaintiffs receive no new relief because of those six releases.

**A. Filing-time restriction; affirm.** The statutory event is bringing the action. Post-filing release does not change that historical condition, and Rule 15(d) may record the later event without eliminating the statute's effect on the existing action. Affirmance preserves the en banc judgment's without-prejudice modification for these covered claims. It does not create permanent disability, prescribe a later action's limitations or classify the restriction as jurisdictional.

**B. Ordinary discretionary supplementation; reverse in relevant part and remand.** The statute bars the covered filing during confinement but does not foreclose the ordinary ability to cure a personal disability after release. Under Rule 15(d), a court may permit a supplemental pleading on proper motion, reasonable notice and just terms even when the original pleading was defective. The lower courts must consider the real procedural request under that rule, rather than deem release categorically incapable of supporting it. Release alone neither compels permission nor establishes compliance or damages entitlement.

**C. Exceptional discretion with affirmance here.** Anderson would ordinarily dismiss but recognize rare discretion in unusual circumstances; his example involves a colorable physical-injury claim already tried to a jury where dismissal would manifestly waste judicial resources. The example is not an exhaustive trigger or a command to grant leave. He would affirm because he finds remand futile in this case. This combines A's proposed judgment with a legal exception that A rejects, and overlaps B's recognition of discretion without adopting B's ordinary scope or remand.

Actual McNeil unanimously preserves an express prelitigation administrative sequence and rejects later exhaustion as a cure even where little litigation occurred. Actual Semtek preserves the Rules Enabling Act boundary. For A, these reinforce the enacted commencement trigger and defeat cure through procedural preference. For B, Mathews's later-application cure concerns a personal qualification that can be satisfied during the action, distinct from its separately waived exhaustion issue; McNeil protects an agency opportunity that cannot be restored after premature commencement. Under B, allowing an eligible former prisoner to supplement creates no substantive damages right. Neither Artuz's provision-specific classification nor Randell's bounded custody-review exception already decides this statutory question. Same-day Booth is not intervening law here.

## Exact coalitions, objection resolution and limits

O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support A's complete filing-time rule and affirmance. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support B's complete permissible-supplementation rule and limited reversal/remand. No Associate adopts C as the sufficient ground. Four votes for A's judgment plus B's abstract recognition that discretion exists cannot be combined into an Anderson compromise; the operative rationales and remedies differ. No final majority, equal-division result or Marks synthesis is asserted.

The strongest objection to A is that a former prisoner may file the same covered claim after release and Rule 15(d) expressly allows supplementation of a defective pleading. A answers that cure depends on what the statute makes legally relevant, and an unchanged historical commencement condition cannot be overcome by duplication concerns. The strongest objection to B is that the statute expressly selects commencement and procedural rules cannot amend that condition. B accepts the original defect but distinguishes removal of a personal disability from loss of a mandatory prior administrative opportunity; it finds no statutory foreclosure of ordinary later-event cure. The third path's attraction is accommodating both concerns, but A finds no source for its exception and B finds no source for its rarity restriction. The fresh review resolves these legal comparisons without inventing facts or changing votes.

Affirmance preserves dismissal without prejudice only for the six plaintiffs' covered claims barred solely by the provision. Reversal removes categorical legal foreclosure and requires consideration of actual motion, notice, just terms and permission, together with ordinary remaining claims and defenses. It does not deem the original filing lawful, automatically validate the supplement, require permission, find physical injury or order damages. The fifteen-and-a-half months before judgment is not itself a finding that supplementation must be allowed or refused.

Neither path decides physical-injury thresholds in other cases, nominal or punitive damages as general categories, prospective or independent economic relief outside the accepted question, the statute's constitutional validity, or the limitations, tolling and preclusive effect of a new action. Dismissal without prejudice promises no later success. No underlying constitutional violation is adjudicated.
