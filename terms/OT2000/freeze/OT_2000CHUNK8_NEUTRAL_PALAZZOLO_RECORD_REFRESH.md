## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Palazzolo v. Rhode Island

* **Citation or Docket:** No. 99-2047; 533 U.S. 606.

* **October Term:** OT2000.

* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS, decision.

* **Simulated Event Date:** 2001-06-28 (historical calendar date).

* **Question Presented:** Whether the agency's denials and the remaining-use record make the wetlands regulatory-takings claim ripe; whether acquisition after enactment of the restrictions categorically defeats the claim; and whether the retained upland use forecloses a complete-deprivation theory while leaving contextual takings review available.

* **Question Granted:** These questions as reconstructed from the judicial record, not a new claim confined to wetlands as a separate parcel.

* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Rhode Island Supreme Court, 746 A.2d 707, affirmed rejection of the inverse-condemnation action.

* **Relief Requested:** Palazzolo seeks compensation, valuing an asserted residential subdivision at $3,150,000. The State seeks to preserve the judgment denying compensation.

* **Material Record:** Shore Gardens acquired the waterfront property in 1959. Palazzolo became its sole shareholder. Most of the property was salt marsh requiring substantial fill for building. Earlier development applications were unsuccessful. The State established its coastal-management council in 1971; the regulatory program designated salt marsh as protected coastal wetlands. When the corporation's charter was revoked in 1978, title passed to Palazzolo. His unsuccessful applications included a 1983 proposal to fill the marsh and a 1985 proposal to fill 11 of 18 wetland acres for a private beach club. Application dates are not automatically denial dates. The supplied objective supplement reports that petitioner identified the final denial in 1986 as the alleged taking event; that account of his contention is not a judicial finding that a taking then occurred. The private beach-club proposal was denied under the special-exception requirement. Section 130A permits, but does not require, an exception to otherwise prohibited activity only if and when the applicant demonstrates all three conditions: (1) a compelling public purpose benefiting the public as a whole rather than individual or private interests, consisting of one or more of public-infrastructure activity such as utility, energy, communications or transportation facilities; a water-dependent activity generating substantial economic gain to the State; or an activity providing shore access to broad segments of the public; (2) all reasonable steps to minimize environmental impacts and/or use conflict; and (3) no reasonable alternative means or location for serving the cited compelling public purpose. The supported account attributes the denial to failure of the public-purpose requirement; it does not establish independent findings about compliance with conditions (2) and (3). The regulation's terms, the Council's reason for this denial, and the constitutional finality or compensation consequences are separate questions. C_SOURCE_TEXT contains the exact judicial quotation from the joint appendix, pages 37–38. The state-court finding quoted in objective account B identifies undisputed evidence that at least one single-family home could be built on existing upland without additional fill. Approximately $200,000 of retained value is supported; neither that finding nor this packet establishes that one home or $200,000 is the maximum permitted use or value. Account A describes the figure as uncontested and cited by the State; account B quotes willingness to approve a single home and disputes treating that statement as a ceiling. The State's reported trial memorandum likewise says it would have been happy to allow a home realizing $200,000. The source-attributed statements are not silently converted into a waiver of every other development possibility.

Account A reports possible additional upland on an island requiring a road across wetlands. Account B quotes Clarke's testimony that applying for development at more than one location was realistic, his report of supporting information from Chateauneuf, and Fugate's testimony that building might be possible on two, perhaps three, perhaps four lots. These are testimony and reported descriptions, not issued permits, settled development entitlements or findings that section 130A's conditions were met. The road/access problem and the possibility of additional sites therefore remain part of the contested finality record. The compensation demand's 74-lot subdivision was not itself a submitted permit application.

* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The State disputes finality because further applications might clarify permissible development, including smaller projects or additional upland sites. Palazzolo responds that the actual standards and denials sufficiently establish the restrictions on the proposed private filling; he relies on the reported upland opportunity without proving that every additional application would be futile. The record must be evaluated without treating the $200,000 figure as an agreed ceiling or the possible additional sites as approved uses. One published recitation describes the Attorney General's responses at oral-argument transcript pages 26 and 31 as limiting ordinary-use wetlands filling, but the supplied supplement does not quote those responses or establish the scope of a binding concession. A path depending on such a concession requires the bounded transcript passage from control.

The plaintiff's quoted complaint alleges a taking from refusal to allow any development, and his trial memorandum urges that Lucas suffices. The trial decision, state appellate decision and the State's supplemental post-trial memorandum nevertheless discuss Penn Central. These source facts do not by themselves prove a fully developed partial-taking claim throughout the litigation, nor do they establish that contextual takings analysis was absent from the decisions. Preservation, question scope, any independently sufficient lower-court ground and a proposed remand's permissible work require separate assessment.

The 1978 acquisition is distinct from the asserted 1986 injury. No supplied finding locates a taking at enactment or at denial, and no finding establishes transfer of an accrued compensation claim with title. The categorical effect of notice must not be conflated with ownership of a claim for an injury that may have occurred at another time. Whether the wetlands alone form a separate denominator was not pressed below or presented in the certiorari petition; the case therefore proceeds on the entire parcel. A compensation action is not an entitlement to a building permit.

### Issue 2: Acquisition, complete deprivation, and contextual review

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council requires complete deprivation of economically beneficial use of the relevant property for its categorical rule. A genuine background state property or nuisance limitation may establish that the asserted use was never within title; a newly enacted declaration of harm cannot do that alone. Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City remains the framework for partial deprivation.

* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Economic impact, reasonable investment-backed expectations, and the character of governmental action require contextual application. Dodd v. Hood River County (November 19, 1998), included in C_HOLDINGS, already holds that retained value, acquisition under an existing restriction, or a legitimate regulatory objective alone is not dispositive of a partial-taking claim. Its rejection of compensation rested on the combined record, including profitable timber use and the actual dwelling condition. No loss percentage automatically establishes or defeats compensation. That controlling rule bears directly on a categorical acquisition-notice defense here without supplying a factual outcome for this coastal property. It does not decide when this owner's alleged compensation claim accrued or who owns a claim for any earlier injury.

* **Other Governing Law:** The Takings Clause as applied to the State and the operative coastal regulations; the actual state-law content of title remains relevant to a properly supported background-law defense.

* **Material Other Authority:** None beyond the authorities identified in this issue.

* **Existing Rule's Reach:** The retained meaningful development use matters to the total-deprivation claim; notice of regulation may matter to expectations.

* **Open Point:** Whether notice categorically defeats the presented successor owner's claim rather than informing expectations; separately, whether the asserted injury occurred during his ownership and what consequence follows from any actual accrual or claim-ownership premise. No timing or transfer fact is supplied by the legal label successor.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Below:** The state court found the claim unripe, treated post-regulation title acquisition as fatal, and rejected total deprivation because of retained upland use. The supplement quotes its at-least-one-home finding. Both state decisions and the State's supplemental post-trial submission discussed Penn Central, while the plaintiff's quoted presentation emphasized complete deprivation. Whether the lower judgment contains an independently sufficient contextual ground, and whether the proposed remand is within the litigated claim, remain questions for legal assessment rather than facts predetermined here.

**Palazzolo's Position:** More applications cannot change the Council's refusal to permit filling for a private purpose. A regulation cannot become an unchallengeable background title limitation merely through transfer of ownership. The rule would impair both successors and original owners trying to sell. Meaningful partial-loss review remains necessary even if the upland prevents categorical recovery. His strongest limited position does not require treating every post-regulation expectation as reasonable or accepting his proposed subdivision value as proved compensation.

**State's Position:** The large proposals do not establish what a smaller project could obtain, and the owner cannot demand compensation before the agency defines the actual burden. The existing restrictions shaped the interest acquired and the reasonableness of his expectations. The upland's value defeats total loss. Environmental protection and the natural condition of the marsh also bear on the asserted right to fill, although a general public-interest claim cannot substitute for an identified background property rule.

**Material Unresolved Matters:** Maximum permissible development, road/access requirements, valuation beyond the supported retained-use figure, contextual-claim presentation, claim accrual and ownership, applicable background limitations and actual duration remain distinct. The unpresented wetlands-only denominator theory is not an alternative route to a total-loss judgment here. Neither the reported multi-site testimony nor the single-home quotation decides finality or an ultimate taking.
