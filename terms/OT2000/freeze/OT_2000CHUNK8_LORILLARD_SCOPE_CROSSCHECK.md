# Lorillard / Altadis: neutral scope crosscheck

Cutoff: June 28, 2001. This note reports the positions and procedural descriptions in three predecision party filings. It neither determines preservation nor resolves any factual or legal dispute. All three saved bodies, including footnotes and end matter, were read in full.

Source key (saved-file line references; printed pages supplied where useful):

- CIG: `OT_2000CHUNK8_LORILLARD_SOURCE_CIGARETTE.md`, 2,600 lines.
- CGR: `OT_2000CHUNK8_LORILLARD_SOURCE_CIGAR.md`, 1,214 lines.
- REP: `OT_2000CHUNK8_LORILLARD_SOURCE_CIGAR_REPLY.md`, 689 lines.

| Component | What these filings expressly press or report | Scope limit |
|---|---|---|
| Cigarette outdoor and indoor advertising, section 21.04(5)(a), (b) | CIG printed p. 5 n. 2, lines 545–549, expressly states that all four cigarette manufacturers challenge these provisions on both preemption and First Amendment grounds. Its developed preemption argument addresses advertising restrictions; its constitutional argument seeks strict scrutiny for content and viewpoint discrimination and alternatively disputes the third and fourth parts of Central Hudson. | The filing does not extend its cigarette preemption claim to retail-display restrictions or to cigars and smokeless tobacco. |
| Retail product displays, section 21.04(2) | CIG p. 5 n. 2 expressly identifies only Brown & Williamson and Lorillard as pressing this challenge in this Court, and only under the First Amendment, as below. The accompanying text identifies subsections (2)(c), (d). CIG p. 46 n. 7 invokes Carey and applies its advertising arguments to these display provisions. | This is an express limitation, not an inference from silence. Do not turn it into a preemption issue or a challenge by every petitioner to every paragraph of subsection (2). See CIG lines 525–549, 2384–2390. |
| Cigar advertising, section 22.06(5)(a), (b) | CGR p. 2 identifies the outdoor/visible-from-outdoors and indoor-height provisions. CGR pp. 5, 8 joins the cigarette petitioners' strict-scrutiny argument; its own principal argument disputes cigar-specific evidentiary support and tailoring under Central Hudson. | The cigar petitioners expressly do not contest the substantial interest in reducing youth cigar smoking. Their asserted lack of evidence connecting these advertisements to youth use remains their argument, not a finding by this crosscheck. |
| Cigar self-service and customer access, section 22.06(2)(c), (d) | CGR p. 2 expressly identifies both. CGR pp. 23–24 n. 9 develops the claim that handling and comparing size, weight, texture, density, and aroma conveys purchasing information. REP p. 10 n. 7 renews that claim, distinguishes Hoffman Estates, and disputes the theft justification. | These are developed arguments, despite appearing in footnotes. CGR pp. 20–21 and n. 7 acknowledge an adults-only exception and argue that excluding even accompanying children burdens retailers. See CGR lines 205–215, 1077–1084, 1163–1170, 1201–1212; REP lines 517–536. |
| Free cigar sampling | CGR p. 2 expressly identifies sampling in retail outlets under section 22.06(1)(a) as at issue. | After full reading, no sampling-specific developed argument appears in these three bodies. The express identification prevents treating sampling as absent merely because the principal argument concerns advertising. Conversely, that identification alone does not establish the scope of a challenge to every paragraph of section 22.06(1), nor establish preservation, abandonment, or the merits. |
| Premiums or branded merchandise | No developed challenge to such restrictions was located in these three complete bodies. | No explicit statement establishing an earlier unappealed ruling on these restrictions was located either. Absence of argument is distinct from proof of nonappeal. References to brands, logos, ordinary product advertising, or an appendix containing regulations do not establish a challenge to premiums or branded merchandise. |
| Cigar package and advertising warnings, sections 22.04, 22.05 | CGR pp. 2–3 n. 1 describes the earlier challenge and reports that the court of appeals accepted the Commerce Clause objection and that the Attorney General did not seek this Court's review of that determination. See CGR lines 228–230, 269–277. | This is an express statement about the absence of a request for further review of the warning ruling. It must not be described as a warning challenge never appealed below, or converted into a live warning-label merits claim here. |

Additional procedural limits: REP p. 2 n. 1, lines 174–181, agrees that the Attorney General's failure to challenge truthfulness on his own summary-judgment motion would not itself preclude a later challenge if that judgment were reversed. The reply nevertheless asserts preclusion from his failure to challenge truthfulness when opposing the petitioners' motion. This is the reply's procedural argument; the opposing filing and underlying motions were not independently inspected. CGR pp. 7, 23 expressly calls the secret-shopper provisions unchallenged and invokes their enforcement as an alternative to advertising restrictions. A general request to reverse does not independently enlarge the identified issues.

These three filings support the distinctions above. A firmer conclusion about premiums, the entire sampling subsection, or contested preservation would require the relevant petition, response, appellate filings, or orders; this limited source review supplies no such conclusion.
