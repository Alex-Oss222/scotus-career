## Historical evidence and the actual comparison

Historically the Court unanimously reversed the Sixth Circuit's refund judgment. Ginsburg's opinion was joined by Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. Scalia concurred only in the judgment. The main opinion rejected the contention that payment language alone dispositively resolved the temporal treatment of backpay, because Nierotko had read corresponding paid-period language to permit earlier benefit allocation. It distinguished the benefit and tax functions, considered the rival fairness and administration arguments, and sustained the reasonable, longstanding interpretation of Treasury's regulations. It did not decide that revenue rulings independently receive controlling statutory weight.

Scalia's separate objection was substantive. If the statutory text itself directly resolved these damages, the same-language argument from Nierotko might favor the taxpayer. He rejected the main opinion's judgment that differences in tax and benefit inequities sufficiently justified different meanings. He instead regarded the temporal treatment of a damages substitute as a statutory gap. The administering agency could reasonably choose payment-year treatment, just as the benefit rule could reasonably use the earlier period. Neither result followed by direct application of the ordinary wage-payment words. He therefore supported the judgment deferring to the regulations without joining the main explanation.

The final independent handoff now gives the seven Associates an actual agency interpretation, separate rate and base provisions, genuine interpretive room, and a bounded own-regulation rationale. It no longer offers eight votes for an unexplained payment-year formula. This resolves the earlier source and seven-Justice rationale problems. Its Scalia row, however, selects a direct statutory ground despite the neutral return's express alternative of permissible agency gap resolution. That selection must still be compared with his own precisely contrary historical reasoning and with the actual law.

## Why Scalia's direct statutory prediction is corrected

The strongest case for the final independent prediction is provision-specific: the rate statutes identify payment years, the separate base statutes aggregate annual remuneration, the parties stipulated wage status, and Nierotko concerned benefits under a different account. The neutral return also identifies older tax wording in Nierotko's notes. Those are real arguments, not a bare appeal to textualism. But they do not identify a changed premise relative to the historical concurrence. The same stipulated salary damages, absence of current employment, later rate and base texts, and distinction between tax and benefit accounts were present historically. Nierotko's operative benefit allocation confronted paid-quarter wording; the older tax wording in its notes does not remove that central analogy. The new source inventory authenticates the existing agency treatment. It does not enact a new statutory allocation command or change the awards' legal character.

Actual Gitlitz does not supply the missing change. It held that the specific income and basis provisions both classified the excluded discharge item and prescribed the later reduction sequence. Its rejection of a policy override applies where the Code has supplied that classification and sequence. It expressly preserves authorized interpretation within genuine room. Here the contested antecedent question is how a compensatory wage substitute enters the annual payment account. The wage stipulation establishes taxability, not temporal attribution. Treating Gitlitz as deciding that antecedent question solely from the broad stipulation would extend its holding beyond its actual premise.

Actual Auer likewise supplies no reason to abandon the gap approach. Scalia joined the responsible agency's fair and considered own-regulation standard, including its textual and actual-ground limits. The now established prelitigation treatment supplies an interpretation to assess under those limits; it is not a new litigation invention. His actual Christensen concurrence rejects disqualification by advisory form alone while requiring a genuine implementing delegation and reasonable textual fit. The controlling regulatory holding there barred an amendment of clear permission into a prohibition. It neither requires finding statutory clarity here nor disqualifies this existing construction. Actual Thomas Jefferson University similarly preserves a supported agency ground without judicial invention. These authorities permit the historical gap rationale and do not compel the frozen direct-text prediction.

There is therefore no supported material change in statute, facts, Nierotko's force, responsible agency treatment or actual prior law that explains why Scalia would now take the direct statutory position he expressly rejected on this same issue. His specific historical analysis outweighs the independent analogy after that analogy has been tested on its own terms. The reconciled choice is reversal on reasonable agency resolution of the statutory gap, with a judgment-only relationship to the seven's common explanation. This is a reasoned Stage C correction, not an alteration of the frozen independent file and not a declaration that history always controls. The alternative, its source predicates and its objections were already presented through the neutral/model stages; no new material fact or legal ground is being introduced without those stages.

## Operative grounds and assembly boundaries

Eight Associates support reversal and application of the payment-year employer-tax rules to the stipulated non-interest salary awards. Seven—Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer—directly adopt the final independent handoff's ground I. Its sufficient rule rests on the actual fair and considered interpretation of separate Treasury rate and annual-base regulations within the permissible statutory framework. Nierotko remains controlling for its wage-classification and benefit-allocation holdings; it defeats a semantic shortcut but does not compel the same employer-tax allocation. The regulations do not expressly discuss backpay, and the agency's established interpretation does the necessary additional work. The ruling neither amends contrary text nor gives independent controlling force to every revenue ruling.

Scalia agrees with the same operative payment-year applications through the distinct statutory-gap ground stated above. He accepts lawful reasonable administrative resolution and the judgment; do not count him as an eighth full join to ground I, as a direct statutory-command vote, or as accepting the common explanation of benefit-tax asymmetry. No Marks synthesis is necessary because seven Associates directly support the common rationale. No historical author or separate filing is automatically assigned.

The component applications remain those in the final independent handoff: 1994 Social Security rate of 6.2 percent and annual base of $60,600; Medicare rate of 1.45 percent without the former wage ceiling; FUTA rate of 6.2 percent and statutory annual base of $7,000. Rate and base are separate propositions. The unchanged Medicare percentage does not erase the base change; the older printed $3,000 FUTA regulatory figure does not supersede the statute. The payment-year principle preserves actual-or-constructive payment, applicable statutory exclusions and the distinct nonqualified-deferred-compensation provisions. No earlier constructive payment or qualifying deferred-compensation plan is established here.

The all-but-one earlier-maximum qualification must remain. The Company paid the recipients no other wages in 1994, but the Court does not decide an employee's separate multiple-employer credit, a benefit account or an unproved election. The reported employer payments and refund judgment are different amounts; no final computation for the exceptional player is invented. Reversal and remand require application of the component rules to the employer refund claim, without an automatic new hearing, fixed-dollar tax award, recalculation of individual benefits or adjudication of unpresented interest payments.
