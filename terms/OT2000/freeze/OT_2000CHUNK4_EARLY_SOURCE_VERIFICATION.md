## Buford v. United States

- Historical event: No. 99-9073; argued January 8, 2001; decided March 20, 2001; 532 U.S. 59. The judgment at 201 F.3d 937 was affirmed unanimously. Breyer wrote for all nine Justices; no separate writing.
- Cornell: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-9073.ZS.html and https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-9073.ZO.html . Official: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-532/pdf/USREPORTS-532-59.pdf . Saved writing: `Buford-ZO.txt`.
- The narrow issue was appellate review of a district judge's fact-bound determination that prior convictions were not functionally consolidated for sentencing. The Court required deferential review, relying on the district court's comparative institutional competence, familiarity with local sentencing practices, and capacity to assess the particular proceedings. Section 3742(e)'s due-deference instruction and Koon support this allocation. The decision does not make every interpretation of the Guidelines deferential.
- The Government conceded that the four robbery convictions were related; the disputed question concerned the separate cocaine conviction. The same judge, same sentencing date, and concurrent sentences did not alone compel functional consolidation. The governing Seventh Circuit functional-consolidation doctrine supplied the question presented; its substantive validity was not independently resolved.
- Limited appellate precedent value in such fact-specific determinations and the Sentencing Commission's capacity to promote uniformity answered the argument for universal de novo review. Do not convert that explanation into a general abandonment of appellate uniformity.

## TrafFix Devices, Inc. v. Marketing Displays, Inc.

- Historical event: No. 99-1571; argued November 29, 2000; decided March 20, 2001; 532 U.S. 23. The judgment at 200 F.3d 929 was reversed and remanded unanimously. Kennedy wrote for all nine Justices; no separate writing.
- Cornell: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-1571.ZS.html and https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-1571.ZO.html . Official: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-532/pdf/USREPORTS-532-23.pdf . Saved writing: `TrafFix-ZO.txt`.
- An expired utility patent is strong evidence that the features it claims are functional. A party seeking trade-dress protection for those features bears a heavy burden to show nonfunctionality; an ornamental, incidental, or arbitrary feature of a patented article can present a different case. The statutory burden for unregistered trade dress rests on the claimant under section 1125(a)(3).
- Inwood's traditional test controls when a feature is essential to an article's use or purpose or affects its cost or quality. Once that test is met, the claimant cannot require additional proof of competitive necessity, and courts need not explore alternative designs. Qualitex's significant non-reputation-related disadvantage formulation has particular significance for aesthetic functionality; it does not displace traditional utilitarian functionality.
- The dual-spring feature was the central functional advance of the expired patents. Secondary meaning could not rescue protection for that functional feature. The holder was not required to obscure a useful design merely to preserve a competing producer's preferred visual distinction.
- The separate WindMaster/WindBuster trademark dispute and the antitrust counterclaim were outside the issue reviewed. A categorical constitutional bar to post-patent trade-dress protection was expressly unnecessary to decide. Cornell's reference to the Trademark Act of 1964 is a transcription error; the official report says 1946.

## Circuit City Stores, Inc. v. Adams

- Historical event: No. 99-1379; argued November 6, 2000; decided March 21, 2001; 532 U.S. 105. The judgment at 194 F.3d 1070 was reversed and remanded, 5–4.
- Kennedy wrote for Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia, and Thomas. Stevens dissented, joined throughout by Ginsburg and Breyer, and joined by Souter only in Parts II and III. Souter separately dissented, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Souter's partial join must not be described as a complete join of Stevens's dissent.
- Cornell: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-1379.ZS.html ; writings https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-1379.ZO.html , https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-1379.ZD.html , and https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-1379.ZD1.html . Official: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-532/pdf/USREPORTS-532-105.pdf . Saved prefix: `Circuit-City`.
- The majority construed FAA section 1's residual category in light of seamen and railroad employees, excluding employment contracts of transportation workers rather than employment contracts generally. Its reading distinguishes section 1's language about workers engaged in commerce from section 2's broader commerce formulation. The enumerated classes, associated-words principle, and available alternative dispute-resolution schemes supported the construction. The decision did not reopen Southland; the proceeding before the Court originated in federal court.
- Stevens's dissent distinguished the original commercial-arbitration purpose from employment relationships, invoked the legislative record and Lincoln Mills, and rejected the idea that a clarifying labor exclusion must become surplusage under the broader reading. His Part I separately questioned section 2 coverage; Souter withheld assent from that part. Souter's own dissent read the affirmative coverage and employment exclusion coherently as commerce power expanded, treating the enumerated occupations as cautionary examples rather than an instruction to contract the residual language. Legislative purpose and history defeated the majority's associated-words inference for the dissenters.
- Adams was a sales counselor at a Santa Rosa retail store. He pursued state employment-discrimination claims; Circuit City brought a federal proceeding to enjoin those claims and compel arbitration. The Supreme Court's factual recitation does not identify the independent basis of federal subject-matter jurisdiction. The lower opinion, available at https://openjurist.org/194/f3d/1070/circuit-city-stores-inc-v-adams , paragraphs 11–12, records an assertion under FAA section 4 and 28 U.S.C. 1332, recognizes that the FAA itself does not supply jurisdiction, and declines to decide the alternative underlying-federal-question dispute. That lower opinion was read through its available primary-text web presentation but was not saved by the download script because direct access was refused. Do not infer that the FAA independently confers jurisdiction or that the Supreme Court decided a contested diversity defect.

## Egelhoff v. Egelhoff

- Historical event: No. 99-1529; argued November 8, 2000; decided March 21, 2001; 532 U.S. 141. The judgment at 139 Wash. 2d 557, 989 P.2d 80 was reversed and remanded, 7–2.
- Thomas wrote for Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg. Scalia concurred, joined by Ginsburg, while joining the Court's opinion. Breyer dissented, joined by Stevens.
- Cornell: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-1529.ZS.html ; writings https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-1529.ZO.html , https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-1529.ZC.html , and https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-1529.ZD.html . Official: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-532/pdf/USREPORTS-532-141.pdf . Saved prefix: `Egelhoff`.
- The majority held RCW 11.07.010 preempted by ERISA insofar as applied to ERISA plans. Designating the person entitled to benefits bears directly on central plan administration; the state rule requires administrators to depart from plan documents and know potentially differing state divorce law. An opt-out does not cure that burden because it itself requires knowledge and amendment. The traditional state role in family and probate law does not overcome this sufficiently direct connection. The Court did not resolve the treatment of state slayer statutes or every state rule affecting benefits.
- Scalia, joined by Ginsburg, reiterated a preference for ordinary conflict and field preemption as the intelligible content of ERISA's language and joined because this statute directly conflicted with plan-document administration. Breyer and Stevens treated the rule as a default interpretation of a beneficiary designation after divorce, filling rather than contradicting the plan's silence; they found no sufficient conflict, field occupation, or practical administrative burden, and stressed participant intent, easy opt-out, and the family-law presumption.
- The official historical statutory source is section 2 of Washington Laws 1994, chapter 221, at https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/biennium/1993-94/Htm/Bills/Session%20Laws/House/2270-S.SL.htm . Full saved bill text: `Egelhoff-1994-SH2270-session.txt`. The entire challenged section, copied by script, is in `runtime/OT_2000CHUNK4_EGELHOFF_STATUTE.md`. It includes all exceptions, payor protections and options, recipient liability, actual-knowledge definitions and timing presumptions, nonprobate-asset definition, and temporal provisions.
- In particular, subsection (2)(b) excepts an instrument expressly providing otherwise; a decree requiring the decedent to maintain an asset for the former spouse or children where no other assets fulfill that decree at death; and a designation the decedent could not unilaterally revoke immediately after dissolution. These are distinct exceptions, not a single discretionary waiver. Subsection (3)'s optional payor notice, withholding, and security provisions must remain distinct from subsection (4)'s recipient-liability rules.
- The beneficiary appendix identifies Donna as wife; it does not establish that a relationship description automatically overrides the named beneficiary. The opinions use different shorthand descriptions of the divorce-to-death interval. No new date finding should be manufactured from that discrepancy.

## Ferguson v. City of Charleston

- Historical event: No. 99-936; argued October 4, 2000; decided March 21, 2001; 532 U.S. 67. The judgment at 186 F.3d 469 was reversed and remanded, 6–3 in judgment.
- Stevens wrote for O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Kennedy concurred in the judgment only. Scalia dissented; Rehnquist and Thomas joined only Part II of his dissent. Scalia's Part I consent and third-party-disclosure rationale is his alone.
- Cornell: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-936.ZS.html ; writings https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-936.ZO.html , https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-936.ZC.html , and https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-936.ZD.html . Official: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-532/pdf/USREPORTS-532-67.pdf . Saved prefix: `Ferguson`.
- The five-Justice opinion treated the public hospital personnel as state actors and the drug screens as searches. Assuming absence of informed consent for purposes of deciding the special-needs issue, the policy's immediate objective of obtaining evidence for law enforcement, its planned coercion through arrest and prosecution, and extensive police and prosecutor involvement placed it outside the special-needs exception. A beneficial ultimate medical purpose did not remove these features. Ordinary medical treatment that incidentally reveals reportable information was not the question decided.
- The jury had found consent under instructions requiring consent to sample collection, cocaine testing, and possible police disclosure. The Court of Appeals had affirmed on special needs without reaching consent. The Supreme Court did not finally resolve the legal and factual consent questions; it remanded them for initial appellate resolution. The holding therefore is not an unconditional liability or damages determination.
- Kennedy rejected a rigid immediate-versus-ultimate-purpose formulation, since many accepted searches collect evidence and public health is a legitimate state purpose. He nonetheless found the unusually direct planned use of penal enforcement outside prior special-needs decisions. Consent could change the analysis; ordinary reporting duties were untouched. He did not resolve the separate questions Scalia raised.
- Scalia Part I separated collection, testing, and disclosure and relied on consent to provide the sample, abandonment and third-party trust principles, and Hoffa, White, and Miller. He disputed a requirement for additional consent to testing or police disclosure. Part II, joined by Rehnquist and Thomas, relied on the trial court's medical-purpose finding and the absence of an exclusivity requirement in special-needs law; Griffin showed that a health or supervisory purpose can coexist with law-enforcement consequences. These different theories must not be merged into a three-Justice adoption of Part I.

## Gibbs v. Babbitt

- Lower historical event: No. 99-1218; argued October 28, 1999; decided June 6, 2000; 214 F.3d 483. Wilkinson, joined by Michael, affirmed the district court; Luttig dissented. The official PDF includes a June 20, 2000 correction of an internal reference. Source: https://www.ca4.uscourts.gov/Opinions/Published/991218.P.pdf .
- The actual challenge concerned restrictions on taking experimentally reintroduced red wolves on private land in eastern North Carolina, not a plenary challenge to every endangered-species protection. The majority rejected the channels and instrumentalities arguments presented and sustained the rule under the substantial-effects branch and its role in a broader economic regulatory scheme.
- The majority connected the regulated taking to protection of livestock and crops, tourism and wildlife observation, scientific activity, potential markets, and preservation of the species within the national regulatory program. Its combined assessment did not hold each economic connection independently sufficient. Hodel's treatment of integrated regulation and Lopez's reservation concerning a broader economic regulatory scheme were central. The majority declined to decide the treaty-power alternative because commerce authority sufficed.
- Luttig regarded the relevant local taking as noneconomic and the proposed interstate effects as speculative and attenuated; he rejected aggregation on these facts and disputed that taking all privately located wolves would substantially affect commerce. His position did not require disabling federal spending or federal-land management, or deciding every other provision of the Endangered Species Act.
- The regulation's private-land qualifications include unintended, non-willful taking; defense of human life; specified defense of livestock or pets with the required evidence; nonlethal, noninjurious harassment; and written authorization after the Service abandons capture efforts. The opinion supplies the terms and reporting requirements; no general license to kill nuisance wolves should be inferred.
- Historical Supreme Court treatment: certiorari was denied February 20, 2001, as Gibbs v. Norton, No. 00-844, 531 U.S. 1145. Official bound-order source: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep531/usrep531orders/usrep531orders.pdf . This disposition supplies no Supreme Court merits opinion or disclosed merits coalition. It must not replace the simulated grant or be treated as a merits endorsement.
- The appellate text contains incidental source errors and imprecise recitations; none is needed for the commerce analysis. In particular, do not infer that all experimental populations must be nonessential from a shorthand statement in this opinion, or import its geographical slip about the location of Pocosin Lakes.
