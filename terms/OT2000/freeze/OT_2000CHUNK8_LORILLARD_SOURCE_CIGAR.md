QUESTION PRESENTED 


Whether Massachusetts’ viewpoint-based 
prohibition on virtually any public display of cigar 
advertisements violates the First Amendment, either 
under the strict scrutiny standard that Petitioners 
contend is applicable or under the test set forth in 
Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corporation v. Public 
Service Commission, 447 U.S. 557 (1980).

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


Petitioners join in the argument presented by the 
petitioners in Lorillard Tobacco Co. et al. v. Reilly, No. 
00-596 (“the Cigarette Manufacturers”), that the 
Commonwealths' consent and _ viewpoint-based 
discrimination against truthful speech about tobacco 
products should be subject to and cannot survive strict 
scrutiny. This brief, however, focuses on the unique 
aspects of cigar advertisements that render the ban on 
cigar advertising unconstitutional even under the less 
stringent, intermediate scrutiny test outlined in 
Central Hudson, because the Attorney General failed 
to show either that the ban on cigar advertisements 
will directly and materially advance the 
Commonwealth's interest in reducing youth cigar 
smoking, or that the regulations are narrowly tailored 
to serve that interest. 


I. Evidence introduced by the Attorney General 
establishes that cigars increased in cultural acceptance 
during the mid-1990s, and that certain health 
organizations and the Attorney General disapprove of 
this development, but there is simply no evidence in 


6 


the record demonstrating that outdoor cigar 
advertisements contribute to youth cigar smoking or 
that banning such advertisements would decrease 
youth cigar smoking. The lack of any evidence linking 
cigar advertisements to youth cigar smoking should 
have been fatal under any application of Central 
Hudson. 


The court of appeals, however, erroneously upheld 
the ban on cigar advertisements based on “anecdotal” 
evidence of a correlation between cigarette advertising 
and youth cigarette smoking. The court then 
compounded that error by applying an unprecedented 
balancing test, concluding that the Attorney General 
did not need to show that banning cigar advertising 
would materially reduce youth cigar smoking because, 
in the court's view, the cigar industry (which does not 
advertise much) will not suffer much from the ban. 
Neither the court's reliance on anecdotal evidence 
about cigarettes, nor its constitutionally inappropriate 
balancing analysis, can be squared with this Court's 
jurisprudence. The lack of proof that the advertising 
ban will directly and materially reduce youth smoking 
renders the ban plainly violative of the First 
Amendment under the third prong of Central Hudson. - 


II. The cigar advertising ban is also insufficiently 
tailored to pass muster under the fourth prong of 
Central Hudson. The evidence in this case shows that 
the advertising ban's 1000-foot radius would prohibit 
all window and other outdoor advertising, as well as all 
indoor advertising below five feet, in more than 90% of 
the Commonwealth's three largest cities, with 
comparable effects in its other populated regions. 
These prohibitions would eliminate the ability of many 
small merchants to advertise cigar products, and 
impermissibly relegate the vast majority of adults in 


7 


the Commonwealth to that speech which the Attorney 
General deems suitable for children. 


The court of appeals upheld the Attorney General's 
assertion that these severe burdens on commercial 
speech are necessary to curb youth cigar smoking 
based primarily on the FDA's proposal of a 1000-foot 
advertising ban. That was plain error. To the extent 
the FDA's proposal provides any support for the 
Commonwealth's regulation of other tobacco products, 
it manifestly does not support the necessity of a 1000- 
foot ban on cigar advertising, which the FDA 
expressly exempted from regulation. 


The Attorney General's failure to demonstrate a 
need for (or, indeed, any purpose to) the 
Commonwealth's near-total ban on outdoor and in- 
store cigar advertising is all the more striking for the 
Attorney General's correlative failure to refute the 
availability of feasible, less speech-restrictive 
alternatives. For example, the record demonstrates 
little in the way of any serious governmental attempt 
to curb youth cigar smoking through its own anti-cigar 
message, and the Commonwealth has chosen not to 
follow the lead of many other states that have enacted 
laws making underage purchases of tobacco products 
illegal or establishing licensing requirements, even 
though the Attorney General has conceded that such 
licensing requirements could drastically reduce sales of 
tobacco products to minors. Moreover, unchallenged 
portions of the Cigar Regulations include “secret 
shopper” requirements that require retailers to 
increase surveillance to ensure that their employees do 
not sell cigars to minors. The Attorney General's 
failure first to gauge the effectiveness of counter- 
speech campaigns and laws that operate directly to 
restrict minors’ access to cigars fatally undermines his 


8 


contention that the Commonwealth's reflexive ban on 
truthful speech is narrowly tailored to achieve an end 
- not attainable by less speech-restrictive alternatives. 


ARGUMENT 


Petitioners join fully in the argument of the 
Cigarette Manufacturers that strict scrutiny should 
apply here. The Attorney General has never 
contended that the cigar advertising ban could survive 
that review — for good reason. As discussed below, the 
challenged portions of the Cigar Regulations are 
patently invalid even under the relaxed, intermediate 
scrutiny standard applied in Central Hudson. 


Central Hudson outlines a four-part test: 


At the outset, we must determine whether the 
expression is protected by the First 
Amendment. For commercial speech to come 
within that provision, it at least must concern 
lawful activity and not be misleading. Next, we 
ask whether the asserted governmental interest 
is substantial. If both inquiries yield positive 
answers, we must determine whether the 
regulation directly advances the governmental 
interest asserted, and whether it is not more 
extensive than is necessary to serve that 
interest. 


Central Hudson, 447 U.S. at 566. 


As the court of appeals noted, the Attorney General 
did not challenge on summary judgment that the cigar 
advertisements at issue here concern lawful activity 
and are not misleading. Pet. App. 19a. Likewise, 
Petitioners have not challenged that the 
Commonwealth has a substantial interest in reducing 
youth cigar smoking. The cigar advertising ban 
founders, however, on the third and fourth prongs of 


9 


the Central Hudson test, because the Attorney 
General failed to adduce evidence establishing that the 
ban on cigar advertising will directly and materially 
advance the Commonwealth's interest in reducing 
youth cigar smoking, or that this near-total ban on 
cigar advertising is narrowly tailored to serve that 
interest.
