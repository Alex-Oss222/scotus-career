## Interest timing and rates

The Third Report selected 1969 because the Master concluded that Colorado then knew or should have known its pumping violated the Compact. The Master also found no awareness of a Compact violation in the early years after adoption and considered the long interval and the cumulative effect of compounding. Kansas requested interest from 1950, arguing that compensation does not turn on a wrongdoer's awareness and that rewarding ignorance weakens incentives to monitor compliance. Colorado requested no interest; alternatively it proposed 1985, associated in the later discussion with commencing the action, rather than 1969. The report's procedural introduction dates the complaint to 1986 while its interest discussion repeatedly uses 1985. These statements must not be silently harmonized into a verified filing date. If the exact filing date determines a chosen remedy, verify the complaint or docket; otherwise identify the requested 1985 commencement year as the disputed remedy proposal and retain the inventory event date.

The Master credited three Kansas experts' year-specific rates for compensating loss. They used rates applicable to individuals rather than lower borrowing rates available to States. Rates varied with inflation and market conditions. Colorado objected that the State prosecutes and receives the award, so state rates should apply. The specific numerical schedule is not reproduced in the opinion. A holding adopting the Master's identified rate methodology need not fabricate a numeric rate. No exact compounding interval has been verified from this source.

The historical background recites that the magnitude and character of the violations remained difficult to ascertain after excessive pumping became apparent, and that Kansas later amended its original water-delivery prayer to request money following Texas v. New Mexico. These are contextual record descriptions, not a new conclusion that Kansas waived or lost its claim. Earlier effective decisions in this timeline govern liability, laches and the scope of this remedial proceeding.

## Crop-loss evidence and objections

The Master's calculation estimated affected acreage, crops planted, crop prices, and the difference between production with the missing water and production with water actually received. The parties generally agreed about annual water shortage, the portion attributable to Colorado, planted crops and prices. The disputed item was the marginal crop yield from additional water. Both accepted the general relationship between more usable water and greater yield up to saturation; Colorado disputed the reliability of Kansas's specific coefficients.

Kansas's expert Norman Whittlesey used published studies to estimate crop-specific additional yields per unit of water. Kansas reduced predicted yields by 25 percent for real farming conditions, including heat, wind, insects and other stresses. Colorado's expert abandoned his initial alternative model after deficiencies in its data were identified, as recited at transcript 197, pages 44-46. Colorado argued that Kansas's estimates implied farmers would profitably have drilled supplementary wells, so their failure to do so discredited those estimates. The Master accepted that wells might have been profitable but identified imperfect contemporary cost information, farmers' debt avoidance, capital constraints, and limited intermediate-term credit as reasons noninvestment did not necessarily refute the crop estimates. These are the Master's findings and treatment of the evidence; the Court must independently evaluate the exceptions.

Colorado also compared model values against literature from other settings. The described Kansas losses involved additional water within a growing season without added capital costs; comparison values from settings involving different investments could therefore answer a different question. The absence of a successful alternative model alone does not establish Kansas's burden has been met.
