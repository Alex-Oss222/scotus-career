## Reconciled legal and remedial scope

**I1, all eight:** Alaska requires separate clear submerged-land inclusion and clear intent to defeat future state title for an appropriate public purpose. Inclusion and congressional notice are conceded; neither independently supplies the second showing. Shively supplies territorial power; Montana and Utah Division supply the strong state-title presumption and separate inquiry. The earlier Coeur d'Alene immunity disposition does not adjudicate title or bar this United States suit. Atkinson's regulation rule does not establish ownership.

**I2, five Associates:** The enacted 1886, 1888, and 1889 measures, with notice of this particular submerged estate, show clear protection of the remaining reservation pending consensual, compensated cession. The 1886 cession negotiations concern land outside the existing reservation. The 1888 right-of-way requires actual approval, consent and compensation and preserves tribal tenure. The 1889 purchase authorization concerns only portions the Tribe consents to sell, on its prescribed terms and subject to ratification. Their combined timely legal treatment protects the identified estate; the unratified agreements themselves are not prematurely binding. Statehood did not transfer the remaining beds during that process. The admission disclaimer corroborates the specific retention; it is not an automatic rule for all Indian reservations. Later ratification may confirm but cannot newly confiscate vested state title.

The strongest I3 objection is that actual access, negotiation, compensation, and tenure obligations can coexist with the future State's bed ownership. The five answer with Congress's enacted choice to obtain identified reservation portions only by consent while preserving the unceded estate, rather than with geography, fishing dependence, legislative sympathy, or an unenacted Senate vote alone. The existing purpose and unchallenged findings explain that specific enacted scheme. Simulated Alaska's legally operative temporary segregation supports examining real legal effect; it does not make all negotiations equivalent to Alaska's statutory wildlife exclusion.

**I3, three Associates:** These statutes protect present use and authorize a contingent transaction but do not clearly accomplish the separate future-state-title consequence. Alaska's operative statehood provisions did that work for its identified estate; general congressional knowledge, temporary tribal use, and a purchase process do not themselves supply it here. No special verbal formula is demanded. Scalia and Thomas's post-1953 Submerged Lands Act objection remains confined to that statute and is not imported into Idaho's 1890 admission. Kennedy may join I3 without adopting that separate statutory position.

**Actual decree:** Affirmance preserves trustee title in the United States, the Tribe's beneficial interest and exclusive use, occupancy and quiet enjoyment, the fitted injunction against Idaho's adverse claim, and denial of its counterclaim within the reservation described by the 1891 and 1894 Acts. The waters and submerged lands Idaho claims within Heyburn State Park remain expressly excluded. No enlarged coordinate description, regulation holding, fishing entitlement, automatic interim injunction, or new trial finding follows.

**I4 and the rejected-reservation remedy:** The preserved aboriginal-title theory was not reached below; the Tribe expressly did not develop its merits in this Court and identified aspects suitable for first-instance adjudication. The historical majority states that limit in footnote 5. The neutral packet and original brief note 12 establish the same facts. They are not newly changed historical premises. If the reservation ground fails, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas would return the case for lawful lower-court treatment of the preserved independent ground, without deciding its success or directing unconditional quiet title in Idaho. The historical dissent rejects reservation retention but supplies no separate analysis resolving the aboriginal alternative and no express unconditional contrary-decree direction. The scoped remand therefore should not be described as a demonstrated departure from an adjudicated historical aboriginal-title holding.

The separately suggested ratification/relation-back theory is not adopted. A proposal affirmatively to decide that doctrine against intervening equal-footing title requires the source review identified in the neutral packet; neither affirmance on I2 nor the bounded alternative remand requires that new holding. No later enactment is treated as a power to take back vested state title.
