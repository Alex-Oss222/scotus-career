## Posture and five distinct contest claims

The state court reviewed a final December 4 trial judgment rejecting the November 27 election contest, following a December 2–3 evidentiary hearing. The historical state narrative reports a November 26 certification with Bush leading by 537 votes, 2,912,790 to 2,912,253. Those are the narrative's starting totals, not a finding of the result after the ordered proceedings. Their continued applicability must be checked against the actual predecessor and intervening events.

The majority's treatment is component-specific despite the conclusion's general reversal language:

| Claim | Original opinion's treatment and qualification |
|---|---|
| Palm Beach net addition | The majority requires inclusion of additional legal votes identified by the county board. The stated 215 net Gore votes are disputed: footnotes 6, 19, and 23 identify Bush's asserted audited figure of 176 and expressly leave the correct number for trial-court determination. The opinion does not finally adjudicate 215 as the undisputed addition. |
| Miami-Dade partial recount | The opinion directs inclusion of the net 168 Gore votes from the partial manual count: 302 additional Gore votes and 134 additional Bush votes, 436 in total. It describes the count as covering twenty percent of precincts and fifteen percent of votes cast. These are partial-count figures, not a final county or statewide recount result. |
| Nassau County substitution | The complaint challenges use of election-night returns instead of the machine-recount tabulation, allegedly adding 51 net Bush votes. The state majority finds no demonstrated improper board action and sustains denial of relief on this component. The complaint's allegation is not a finding of 51 illegal votes. |
| Approximately 3,300 Palm Beach ballots | The board had examined these ballots and found no discernible voter intent. The majority sustains denial because the challengers supplied no evidence rebutting the conclusion that legal votes had not been rejected. A circuit court need not repeat an otherwise proper manual examination simply because a contest was filed. This differs from ballots never manually reviewed. |
| Approximately 9,000 Miami-Dade undervotes | These remained unexamined by hand after the board stopped its recount. The majority orders their immediate manual examination. The existence of the undervote pool and a threshold showing that legal votes within it could place the result in doubt are not findings that all ballots are valid or favor one candidate. |

Source locations: pages 2–5; 28–35; 39 and notes 6, 16–19, 23. The opinion recounts a pool of 10,750 Miami-Dade undervotes before partial examination; approximately 9,000 remained. Do not interchange the original pool, the remaining pool, the 436 recovered votes, and the net 168 addition.

## Majority's actual state-law reasoning and decree

The majority relies on the legislature's contest provisions, particularly §102.168(3)(c)'s ground involving enough rejected legal votes or received illegal votes to change or place the election result in doubt and §102.168(8)'s remedial authority. It treats protest and contest proceedings as distinct: a prior protest is not a condition of a contest, and the trial court does not sit merely as an appellate reviewer of a canvassing board's discretionary decisions. Board decisions may be evidence without receiving dispositive abuse-of-discretion deference. Pages 13–14, 32–33.

The majority rejects a requirement that the contestant first establish a reasonable probability that he would have won. It finds a threshold basis for examining the unreviewed ballots from the large undervote pool, the small certified margin, statistical evidence, and actual recount experience. It still requires legal votes sufficient to change or place the result in doubt, and does not equate mere existence of an undervote with a legal vote. The discussion of historical recovery rates is the majority's assessment of the showing for examination; it is not an adjudicated allocation of the remaining ballots. Pages 20–28, 35–36.

The adopted ballot criterion is a clear indication of voter intent. The majority derives it from §§101.5614(5)–(6), 102.166(7)(b), and state election decisions. It identifies no detailed physical-mark rule for every kind of chad. The requirement is not permission to assign a vote where intent is indiscernible. The majority also treats failure to count an ascertainable legal vote as rejection within the contest statute. Pages 23–28, 40.

The trial court must begin the Miami-Dade hand tabulation immediately and may use the Leon County Supervisor of Elections or sworn designees. It must enter orders needed to add legal votes to statewide certifications and include the Miami-Dade addition and the properly determined Palm Beach addition. Further appropriate relief remains open. The opinion requires statewide consideration of the legal votes within undervotes not previously manually tabulated, with local examination where those ballots are held, and directs further statewide relief to proceed promptly and simultaneously with the Miami-Dade examination. It does not require recounting every already-counted vote. Pages 15–20, 38–40.

Read the statewide directions as a whole: the reasoning calls statewide treatment essential, page 39 describes county officials' role in counties lacking manual review, and the concluding directions authorize immediate implementing orders. The original language does not itself identify every county, appoint every counter, prescribe all objection procedures, or report that any resulting order was obeyed. No motion for rehearing is allowed. Pages 38–40.

The majority quotes §5, recognizes federal calendar constraints, and says Florida's election scheme must yield in an actual conflict. It speaks of an outside federal deadline and expresses confidence that officials can count in the required period, while acknowledging that practical difficulty may ultimately control. It does not name December 12 in its operative decree or make an adjudicated finding of how many hours or ballots remain on December 12. The confidence expressed in note 22 is not proof that completion actually occurred. Pages 5–6, 18 n.11, 36–39 nn.21–22.

## Separate state grounds and limits on factual attribution

**Wells's dissent:** Wells would affirm. He treats statutory authorization as restrictive, favors deference to election officials, and reads the protest and contest provisions together. He disputes judicial authority for partial undervote recounts and the majority's threshold showing. He distinguishes allegations supporting a contest from proof warranting relief, relies on the trial court's rejection of fraud and other misconduct, and regards the majority's new count as beyond the legislative rules. Pages 40–54.

His separate concerns include the limited context of §101.5614(5), the lack of detailed physical-mark standards, county disagreement about dimpled ballots, and the exclusion of overvotes from the required review. He identifies unresolved issues concerning counters, observers, objections, personnel, and consistent standards. He argues that timely and reliable completion, including objections and judicial review, is impracticable and that finality should prevail. Those are his legal arguments and assessments; they do not become majority factual findings. His references to an earlier federal action must be assessed only against the actual effective Palm Beach decision. Pages 46 n.26, 51–60.

**Harding's dissent, joined by Shaw:** Harding agrees that the trial judge used erroneous abuse-of-discretion and reasonable-probability standards but regards those errors as nondispositive. He finds the statewide showing insufficient, supports enforcement of the earlier late-return deadline, and insists that a selective county count cannot establish a statewide result. He raises equality and fairness objections, practical impossibility, and lack of opportunity to be heard for officials not joined or served. His large no-vote totals include a broader class than the approximately 9,000 Miami-Dade undervotes addressed by the immediate order. His asserted December 12 cutoff and claim of party agreement require their own support and compatibility analysis; they cannot silently become a majority holding, an independently verified concession, or a federal prohibition. Pages 61–69, especially note 33.

Wells joins Harding except for Harding's conclusions concerning trial-court error and the separateness of protest and contest proceedings. The disagreements among the state writings are therefore not a single undifferentiated objection. Page 40.

## Required qualifications to the existing neutral packet

1. Preserve all five claim dispositions, the disputed Palm Beach number, and the distinction between recovered legal votes and remaining undervotes. A description merely saying that the state court reversed denial of the contest hides the components on which it sustained denial.
2. Identify the precise source status of alleged unequal treatment. This original opinion supports Wells's report of county disagreement and his objection to the omission of overvotes, along with the majority's general voter-intent standard. It does not independently establish every later claim of changing standards within a county, different counting teams' treatment of identical ballots, or differing actual inclusion of overvotes during proceedings after December 8. A material equality theory relying on those particular facts needs a compatible, date-eligible source.
3. The record of Miami-Dade's incomplete pre-decree count does not establish the progress of the new count. Neither a final tally nor a new certification nor a winner can be inferred from it.
4. There is no entered December 9 stay in the supplied current chronology. Do not attribute stopped work, lost time, an inability to finish, or a restarted count to such an order. Any December 12 feasibility assessment must use the actual procedural and factual state.
5. A completed state certification judgment and this remand for further examination present different finality questions. Apply the existing §1257/Cox analysis to the actual operative order and remaining proceedings; urgency and the prohibition on rehearing do not by themselves establish an exception to finality.
