INDOPCO, Inc. v. Commissioner; No. 90-1278; OT1991. Historical reporter anchor: 503 U.S. 79 (1992). Merits decision, 1992-02-26. Argued November 12, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, 918 F.2d 426. The taxpayer, formerly National Starch and Chemical Corporation, seeks current deduction of professional acquisition expenses disallowed by the Tax Court and Third Circuit.

Questions: Whether the target’s professional expenses in this friendly acquisition are deductible under §162(a), and whether creation or enhancement of a separate and distinct asset is necessary for capitalization under §263.

## Law of the decision

### A separate asset is sufficient, but not necessary, for capitalization

**Holding and operative rule:** Creation or enhancement of a separate and distinct asset is a sufficient ground for capitalization under §263, not an indispensable condition; the character, duration and extent of a transaction’s benefits also matter. The professional expenses facilitating this acquisition’s established enduring corporate restructuring benefits are capital expenditures, not immediately deductible §162(a) operating expenses; mere incidental future benefit alone does not compel capitalization.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s opinion, joined by the other eight Justices; unanimous statutory holding and record application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a sensible business expenditure need not be a current operating expense. Lincoln Savings classified the particular asset-producing payment before it as capital; it did not make creation of a separate asset the only route to capitalization. Welch recognizes that the distinction depends on the expenditure’s character and circumstances, while Woodward and Hilton Hotels connect professional costs to the capital transactions they facilitate. Here the record supports substantial corporate benefits extending beyond 1978: access to Unilever’s resources, simplified ownership and lasting changes in corporate structure. Those benefits belong to the corporation and do not depend solely on a shareholder’s estate-planning preference or an invented profit forecast. The taxpayer’s proposed asset prerequisite would turn a sufficient explanation into an exclusive statutory definition. Its concern about routine expenses is answered by preserving the difference between substantial enduring benefits and incidental future usefulness. The Court affirms only these expenses’ capital classification and determines neither every acquisition’s treatment nor a later recovery period.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Commissioner v. Lincoln Savings & Loan Association*, 403 U.S. 345 — clarified: a separate asset supplies a sufficient capitalization ground, not an exclusive prerequisite.
- *Welch v. Helvering*, 290 U.S. 111 — applied for the contextual distinction between necessary business outlays and current expenses.
- *Woodward v. Commissioner*, 397 U.S. 572 — applied for the connection between professional expenses and the underlying capital acquisition.
- *United States v. Hilton Hotels Corp.*, 397 U.S. 580 — applied for acquisition-related professional costs’ capital character.
- *Commissioner v. Idaho Power Co.*, 418 U.S. 1 — preserved: ordinary expense categories do not override required capitalization.
- *Interstate Transit Lines v. Commissioner*, 319 U.S. 590 — the taxpayer’s burden to establish an authorized deduction remains unchanged.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Character of this acquisition expenditure

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reach the statutory classification and the taxpayer's proposed necessary condition.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply §§162 and 263 and read Lincoln Savings according to the proposition it decided. No statutory precedent is overruled.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Creation of a distinct asset is sufficient but not necessary for capitalization; these expenses facilitating a transaction with established enduring corporate benefits are not deductible as current ordinary operating expenses.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The Code distinguishes the time and character of an outlay, not simply whether management acted sensibly. Lincoln Savings explains why its particular expenditure was capital; it does not convert that explanation into the exclusive definition for all transactions. Here the company changed from independent public ownership to subsidiary status and obtained access to a larger enterprise's resources. Those established benefits and the expenses' direct relation to the restructuring support the courts' classification without needing speculative profit estimates. The shareholder estate-planning preference alone would not establish a benefit to the corporation; the record supplies separate corporate grounds. The answer follows ordinary tax law and carries no additional constitutional or anti-corporate premise.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Incidental future benefit does not automatically capitalize a routine expense. No categorical rule for every acquisition or professional fee is adopted, and the immediate-deduction issue does not itself determine later cost recovery.
