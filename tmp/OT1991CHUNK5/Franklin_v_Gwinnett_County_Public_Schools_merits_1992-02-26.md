# Canonical Decision Record — Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools

## Event and chronology

Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools; No. 90-918; OT1991. Historical reporter anchor: 503 U.S. 60 (1992). Merits decision, 1992-02-26. Argued December 11, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, 911 F.2d 617. The district court dismissed the complaint on the categorical ground that Title IX did not permit damages; the Eleventh Circuit affirmed.

Questions: Whether compensatory damages are available in the recognized Title IX private action for intentional discrimination, and whether the Spending Clause notice principle bars that relief.

Chronology: The four February 26 events are uncoordinated and share a start-of-day baseline including the committed February 25 decisions. None uses another February 26 decision as earlier law.

Relief sought and material record: Franklin seeks reversal and ordinary litigation of her damages claim. She alleges repeated sexual harassment and abuse by teacher/coach Andrew Hill while she attended a federally funded high school, official awareness and failure to act, discouragement of her complaint and closure of the investigation following Hill’s conditional resignation. These are allegations, not judicial findings. She no longer attends the school. The lower court relied on its Title VI precedent Drayden and a Spending Clause limitation; Judge Johnson regarded Drayden as controlling without reaching the additional constitutional characterization. Respondents and the United States defend the damages bar. Backpay and prospective student relief would not redress the pleaded completed injury.

Stage before: plenary merits review. Stage after: Supreme Court review ends with reversal and remand. The damages complaint returns for ordinary proceedings on liability, attribution, causation and compensable loss. No amount is awarded and no prospective school-supervision order is entered.

## Law of the decision

### Compensatory relief in the established Title IX action

**Holding and operative rule:** When a cognizable federal statutory cause of action exists, courts may award appropriate relief absent clear congressional direction limiting it; recognizing that remedy does not create a new cause of action. In Title IX’s established private action, compensatory damages are available for proved intentional discrimination, subject to proof of recipient responsibility, causation and compensable loss.

**Authority:** White’s opinion, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices control the remedial rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Title IX does not confine an established private action to remedies incapable of redressing a completed personal injury. Cannon supplies the cause of action; Bell v. Hood and Davis v. Passman distinguish that authority to sue from the appropriate relief a court may grant. Borak confirms that statutory silence about remedy does not itself confine a recognized action to a declaration. Guardians and Darrone support compensatory relief for intentional violations of closely related discrimination statutes. Congress’s 1986 reference to remedies at law and in equity, followed by legislation preserving private enforcement, supplies no direction to bar damages. Recognizing compensation therefore implements the existing action rather than expanding judicial power to hear a new one. Franklin’s departure from school shows why backpay and prospective relief cannot compensate the pleaded past injury. The allegations still require adjudication; the Court awards nothing and decides neither a final recipient-attribution rule nor punitive or unintended-effects liability.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Cannon v. University of Chicago*, 441 U.S. 677 — applied as the established Title IX cause of action; not reopened.
- *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678 — applied for appropriate remedies in an existing cognizable action, absent a congressional limit.
- *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 — applied to separate existence of an action from available relief.
- *J. I. Case Co. v. Borak*, 377 U.S. 426 — applied against limiting relief merely because the statute does not enumerate the remedy.
- *Guardians Association v. Civil Service Commission*, 463 U.S. 582 — its intentional-discrimination remedial support is applied without inventing a single majority rationale.
- *Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Darrone*, 465 U.S. 624 — analogous monetary relief supports the availability of compensation.

### Notice does not bar damages for the pleaded intentional violation

**Holding and operative rule:** The Spending Clause notice principle does not categorically bar compensatory relief for an intentional violation of Title IX’s express prohibition of sex discrimination within its established private action. Ambiguous funding conditions, unintentional effects and the separate requirements for attributing misconduct to a recipient are not resolved.

**Authority:** White’s opinion, joined by the same seven Justices, rejects the distinct funding-notice ground offered to sustain dismissal.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the asserted lack of notice cannot sustain this categorical damages bar. Pennhurst requires fair notice of conditions imposed on recipients of federal funds; it does not authorize intentional breach of a clear nondiscrimination condition while limiting the injured person to ineffective prospective relief. Title IX expressly forbids sex discrimination, and Cannon supplies the established enforcement action. The pleaded sexual harassment and abuse describe intentional discrimination of the kind addressed by Meritor Savings Bank’s explanation of sex-based harassment, without deciding here when a school is responsible for a teacher’s conduct. Darrone also defeats the premise that a spending-based statute necessarily excludes a monetary award for intentional violations. The objection cannot succeed merely by describing compensation as payment from a recipient’s treasury. This conclusion rests on the enacted condition and existing enforcement law, not a general power to redress every injury. Genuinely ambiguous conditions, proof of institutional responsibility and unintended discriminatory effects remain distinct questions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman*, 451 U.S. 1 — its funding-condition notice rule is preserved; it does not bar relief for this alleged intentional violation.
- *Meritor Savings Bank, FSB v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57 — its identification of sex-based harassment informs the pleaded discrimination; no Title VII attribution formula is imported.
- *Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Darrone*, 465 U.S. 624 — applied against a categorical objection to monetary remedies under spending legislation.
- *Cannon v. University of Chicago*, 441 U.S. 677 — the established action supplies the enforcement premise for the notice analysis.

## Law after the decision

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Effective relief under a recognized federal action

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Accept Cannon's established cause and reach the categorical remedy bar actually decided below.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply ordinary remedial principles and Congress's statutory treatment of the action; no overruling or new cause of action is required.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A private plaintiff may seek compensatory damages for intentional violation of Title IX; the statute does not confine this recognized action to relief incapable of addressing a completed personal injury.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Cannon supplies enforcement authority. Bell and the analogous discrimination cases support appropriate relief unless Congress limits it. The 1986 law's reference to remedies at law and in equity, and subsequent legislation retaining the private-action structure, oppose a categorical implication of no damages. Franklin's departure from school illustrates the concrete remedial gap without itself creating judicial power: statutory enforcement and traditional remedies supply the source. Compensation must remain tied to a proved violation and resulting loss, rather than an abstract interest in punishing institutions. The pleaded official knowledge and conduct must still be tested; they are not assumed to establish a final recipient-liability standard.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** No decision on punitive relief, liability for unintended effects, or every possible harassment-attribution rule. Reopening Cannon would exceed the necessary remedial question.

### Issue 2: Notice of intentional discrimination liability

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Address the Spending Clause objection because it is advanced as a bar to the requested remedy.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply Pennhurst's notice principle without converting it into a universal requirement that every compensatory consequence be individually enumerated.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The notice principle does not categorically bar relief for the pleaded intentional violation of the express nondiscrimination condition.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** An institution accepting education funds has statutory notice that intentional sex discrimination is forbidden, and the established private action gives the remedial inquiry a concrete legal foundation. A theory imposing unexpected liability for otherwise permissible conduct is a different case. This resolution rests on the statutory condition and entering enforcement law, not a generalized judicial duty to remedy every wrong.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Preserve the separate proof of recipient responsibility and questions about genuinely ambiguous funding conditions.
