## Law of the decision

### Limited injury does not defeat this established nontrivial malicious beating

**Holding and operative rule:** A nontrivial beating inflicted maliciously and without disciplinary justification on a restrained convicted prisoner violates the Eighth Amendment even when the resulting physical injuries are limited, where the established conduct satisfies even Whitley’s demanding malicious-and-sadistic inquiry. Injury remains relevant evidence and bears on compensation; accidental harm or a merely technical battery is not enough.

**Authority:** Part A of O’Connor’s opinion, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter: six Justices adopt this rule and its application. The general extension proposed in Part B has only four votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Constitution does not make protection from this beating depend on how much lasting injury the prisoner suffered. Whitley v. Albers distinguishes a good-faith effort to preserve discipline from force maliciously and sadistically applied to cause harm; the findings here satisfy even that demanding inquiry. Hudson was restrained, no force was needed, the guards repeatedly struck him, and their supervisor condoned the assault. His dental and facial injuries also establish that the force was not trivial. Wilson v. Seiter requires an objective constitutional deprivation, but its treatment of confinement conditions does not impose a separate significant-medical-injury requirement on these established acts of violence. Estelle v. Gamble supplies the prohibition on unnecessary and wanton pain while retaining its distinct medical-care requirements. The contrary injury floor would excuse serious custodial violence because the victim happened to withstand it. The Court leaves a comprehensive mental-state rule for other force settings, psychological injury alone, individual defenses and lawful measurement of damages undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Whitley v. Albers*, 475 U.S. 312 — applied as a sufficient ground on these findings; its disturbance rule remains in force, but a universal extension receives no majority here.
- *Wilson v. Seiter*, 501 U.S. 294 — distinguished by the character of the constitutional wrong; the objective-deprivation requirement survives without becoming a significant-injury floor for this beating.
- *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97 — applied for unnecessary and wanton pain; serious medical need and deliberate indifference remain the medical-care rules.

## Law after the decision

The existing rules for medical care, conditions, legitimate discipline, personal defenses and compensatory proof remain distinct. No new mandatory test for every prison-force setting is created.

## C. ISSUE POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Gratuitous custodial violence

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Reach the injury element that defeated the established claim.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether the absence of serious injury defeats this deliberately unnecessary, nontrivial beating; a comprehensive mental-state rule for every force setting is unnecessary.
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply the Eighth Amendment's force doctrine in context and reject the circuit's additional injury prerequisite. Wilson's conditions analysis is distinguished, not overruled.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A nontrivial beating inflicted maliciously and without a disciplinary justification on a restrained convicted prisoner can violate the Eighth Amendment despite limited resulting physical injury.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Whitley treats injury as evidence within the force inquiry, not a license to administer cruelty so long as the body withstands it. Wilson's objective dimension is satisfied by the character and circumstances of serious custodial violence; the seriousness appropriate to a food or medical-deprivation claim cannot be transplanted as an identical injury element. Hudson's restraint, the absence of need, repeated blows and supervisory condonation satisfy the demanding wanton-force inquiry on the findings below. His dental and facial injuries reinforce that this was no trivial touch. The rule preserves room for disciplinary judgment because it requires constitutionally culpable force, while refusing to make constitutional protection depend on the victim's physical resilience.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** No rule that any technical battery is cruel punishment, no strict liability for accidental harm and no general holding on isolated unauthorized conduct. That alternative cannot defeat this record, which includes supervisory approval. Compensatory damages still require their own lawful measurement; recognition of the right does not determine every remedy or defense.
