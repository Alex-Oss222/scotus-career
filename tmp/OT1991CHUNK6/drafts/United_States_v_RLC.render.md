# United States v. R. L. C.

## Event

United States v. R. L. C.; No. 90-1577; October Term 1991. Merits decision, 1992-03-24; argued December 10, 1991. On writ of certiorari to Eighth Circuit, 915 F.2d 320.

Question: Whether the juvenile-detention ceiling in 18 U.S.C. §5037(c)(1)(B) incorporates the maximum Guidelines sentence authorized for the comparable adult offender and offense.

Record and relief sought: At age 16, R.L.C. caused a child’s death while driving recklessly and intoxicated in Indian country. After a bench adjudication the district court found juvenile delinquency corresponding to involuntary manslaughter and imposed three years, the offense statute’s adult maximum. The Eighth Circuit held that §5037’s adult comparison incorporates the Guidelines; the agreed comparable adult range was 15–21 months. It vacated and remanded. Without a Government stay, the district court subsequently imposed 18 months, which R.L.C. has served. The Government seeks restoration of the original statutory-maximum approach, so possible renewed detention prevents mootness. No specific additional departure basis is established by this record for present decision.

Render form: full; the partially joined opinion and changed noncontrolling history coalition require an explicit topology to prevent attributing a majority lenity method; full form is selected for the fracture in reasoning.

## Holdings

### The juvenile ceiling incorporates the lawful adult Guidelines maximum

**Holding and operative rule:** For juvenile detention governed by §5037(c)(1)(B), the maximum authorized adult sentence includes the applicable Guidelines ceiling for the comparable offender and offense, subject to a properly supported lawful departure. The Guidelines determine that upper limit only; they do not impose a Guidelines minimum or otherwise require plenary adult Guidelines sentencing for the juvenile.

**Authority:** Parts I, II-A and III of Souter’s opinion, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; seven Justices. The legislative-history and competing lenity explanations do not command five votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a sentence is authorized only if it complies with statutes that limit the sentencing judge as well as those that define the offense. Section 3553(b)’s command to apply the Guidelines is itself statutory. The Government’s focus on the three-year offense maximum therefore does not establish that an adult court could impose that term despite the applicable Guidelines ceiling. Section 5037 compares this juvenile with an adult sentenced in comparable circumstances, which requires identifying the proper adult range. Here that range is 15–21 months, absent a supported lawful departure. This conclusion does not make the adult Guidelines govern juvenile disposition in full. The juvenile court retains its discretion below the applicable cap and remains subject to the other statutory limitations. The dissent’s concern for rehabilitation does not remove the enacted upper limit, while calculating the limit does not require adoption of every adult sentencing rule. No particular additional departure ground is approved and no common methodological rule for lenity is announced.

## Law After Decision

Effective March 24, §5037(c)(1)(B) has a controlling adult-Guidelines upper-limit construction. A lawful departure can affect that ceiling, but none is established here; the adult Guidelines minimum does not become a juvenile floor. Seven join the Court’s text/result sections; Souter’s history/lenity sections have three votes. Scalia’s legislative-history restriction and Thomas’s qualification remain noncontrolling; Marks is unnecessary for the seven-Justice ceiling and supplies no synthetic majority methodology.

## Separate Writings

Souter, joined by White and Stevens, additionally relies on statutory development and legislative history and regards lenity as a last resort after that inquiry. Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, would not use legislative history to resolve textual punishment ambiguity against the accused. Thomas separately stresses that established legal rules of construction precede lenity. Stone relies on text and statutory context, uses residual lenity only to confirm narrower punishment, and preserves juvenile discretion below the ceiling. O’Connor, joined by Blackmun, dissents: in her view, the offense-statute maximum preserves the separate rehabilitative scheme, and calculating an adult range imports procedures Congress did not require. The Court adopts the cap without resolving those methodological disagreements.

## Procedure After Action

Supreme Court review ends in affirmance of the Eighth Circuit. The district court’s already-entered 18-month disposition is not replaced by a new remand order. No additional detention or new departure finding is made. The Government’s effort to restore the three-year approach fails.
