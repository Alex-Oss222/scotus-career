# Dawson v. Delaware

## Event

Dawson v. Delaware; No. 90-6704; October Term 1991. Merits decision, 1992-03-09; argued November 12, 1991. On writ of certiorari to Delaware Supreme Court, 581 A.2d 1078.

Question: Whether Delaware violated the First and Fourteenth Amendments by introducing the stipulated Aryan Brotherhood membership evidence at capital sentencing; no challenge to the convictions is decided.

Record and relief sought: Dawson and the murder victim were white. The parties stipulated that the Aryan Brotherhood began as a racist California prison gang and that a separate gang with that name existed in Delaware. The stipulation and tattoo evidence did not establish the Delaware organization’s violent conduct or aims, an organizational connection with the crime, or racial motivation for this murder. Dawson preserved his constitutional objection to the agreed evidentiary presentation. His mitigation included family kindness and participation in drug and alcohol programs; the State established three statutory aggravators. Delaware upheld the death sentence, treating the membership evidence as relevant to character and mitigation. Dawson seeks relief from that sentence; Delaware defends relevance and alternatively raises harmless error. The stipulation is not evidence of every fact about other prison gangs.

Render form: full; the remand discussion has a changed controlling coalition and Stone publishes a distinct remedy position; component-specific authority must be shown.

## Participation

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision. Every argument occurred after Thomas’s October 23, 1991 oath. No established recusal or nonparticipation. The judgment sustaining the death sentence is vacated and the case remanded, 8–1. The convictions remain intact.

## Holdings

### Protected association must bear on a legitimate sentencing issue

**Holding and operative rule:** The State may not use protected association to aggravate a capital sentence when the evidence establishes only abstract beliefs and supplies no supported connection to the crime, future danger, another legitimate sentencing issue, or the mitigation actually offered. The stipulated association evidence here lacks that connection and its admission violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments.

**Authority:** Part A of White’s opinion; Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter join White at this level of generality.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an unpopular association does not become sentencing evidence merely because its beliefs are objectionable. NAACP and Bates protect association; Abel shows that those protections do not exclude evidence that actually establishes a witness’s bias. Barclay likewise involved demonstrated racial motivation for the crime. This stipulation supplies neither connection. Even assuming that the Delaware group shared racist beliefs, the State established no link between those beliefs and this murder, and no violent organizational objective attributable to Dawson. The evidence also does not contradict his actual mitigation concerning family kindness and participation in rehabilitation programs. Payne permits relevant rebuttal, not punishment for a belief labeled bad character. Zant’s distinction between legitimate aggravation and protected beliefs therefore controls the application. The Court announces no universal bar against evidence of association and no rule that a white victim always excludes racial motive. A materially different evidentiary record may establish relevance that this stipulation does not.

**Precedent treatment:**

- NAACP v. Alabama, 357 U.S. 449 — applied: freedom of association protects unpopular affiliations.
- Bates v. Little Rock, 361 U.S. 516 — applied: protected association cannot itself justify adverse governmental treatment.
- United States v. Abel, 469 U.S. 45 — distinguished on the facts: association established witness bias there; relevant association remains admissible.
- Barclay v. Florida, 463 U.S. 939 — distinguished: demonstrated racial motivation connected the association to the crime.
- Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862 — applied: protected beliefs cannot themselves supply an aggravating circumstance.
- Payne v. Tennessee, 501 U.S. 808 — applied with its relevance limit: rebuttal of mitigation does not make unrelated belief probative.
- Schware v. Board of Bar Examiners, 353 U.S. 232 — applied by analogy: an affiliation without a rational connection to the relevant character inquiry is insufficient.

### Scope of the remand

**Holding and operative rule:** The state judgment sustaining this sentence cannot stand on the constitutional reasoning it adopted. On remand the state court may consider Delaware’s harmless-error contention; this Court decides neither whether harmless-error analysis is available for this violation nor whether the error was harmless.

**Authority:** Part B of White’s opinion, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices control the remand’s reserved scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that correction of the constitutional error requires vacating the judgment that sustained the sentence, while leaving the convictions intact. The relevance ruling answers the question reviewed here. It does not establish how the challenged evidence affected this jury’s sentencing decision, and it does not decide every condition under which constitutional evidentiary error can be harmless. Delaware advanced harmlessness as an alternative ground, but the state court did not decide that contention under the constitutional rule now stated. Chapman supplies a demanding burden when its form of harmless-error review applies; citing that framework does not decide its availability for this particular infringement. The proper course is to permit consideration of the issue on remand without prejudging either branch. Neither the existence of other aggravators nor the Court’s finding of irrelevance supplies an automatic harmlessness finding. Conversely, the Court does not now command a new sentencing hearing regardless of further lawful review.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 — preserved: its constitutional harmless-error burden is not relaxed; applicability and satisfaction in this case remain undecided.

## Law After Decision

Effective March 9, the constitutional relevance rule is applied to this stipulation; no categorical association-evidence ban is added. The remand’s seven-Justice reservation leaves both availability and application of harmless-error treatment open. Stone’s mandatory Chapman/new-sentencing sequence is not the Court’s instruction. No earlier simulated rule is displaced.

## Separate Writings

Stone concurs in the relevance ruling and vacatur but would require Delaware to prove harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt on the trial record, with new sentencing otherwise. Blackmun joins the Court and emphasizes that the permissibility of harmless-error treatment itself remains unresolved; this safeguard may protect against chilling association as well as an unreliable sentence. Thomas dissents: voluntary prison-gang membership can support a character and future-danger inference and rebut favorable character evidence. He would treat ordinary evidentiary fairness, rather than a categorical First Amendment insulation, as the inquiry. His inference is not a Court finding about this Delaware group.

## Procedure After Action

Supreme Court review of the sentence ends; the case returns to Delaware for further proceedings. No resentencing date, harmlessness finding, stay, or retained Supreme Court supervision is created. The convictions remain intact.
