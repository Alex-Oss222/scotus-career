# Connecticut National Bank v. Germain

## Event

Connecticut National Bank v. Germain; No. 90-1791; October Term 1991. Merits decision, 1992-03-09; argued January 21, 1992. On writ of certiorari to Second Circuit, 926 F.2d 191.

Question: Whether 28 U.S.C. §158(d) excludes otherwise available §1292 review of a district court order entered in its bankruptcy appellate capacity.

Record and relief sought: A bankruptcy trustee sued Connecticut National Bank and demanded a jury trial. The bankruptcy court refused to strike that demand; the district court permitted an appeal, affirmed, and certified an interlocutory question. The bank sought circuit permission under §1292(b). The Second Circuit dismissed for lack of jurisdiction, reasoning that §158(d)’s final-order provision implicitly foreclosed interlocutory review. The bank seeks reversal of that jurisdictional dismissal. No jury entitlement or final satisfaction of the discretionary appeal requirements is before this Court for decision.

Render form: compact; 9–0 judgment with no material change from the comparator; the five-Justice rationale and four judgment-only positions are supplied explicitly.

## Holdings

### Section 158(d) does not exclude eligible interlocutory review

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 158(d) does not preclude court-of-appeals review under §1292 of a district court’s order entered in its bankruptcy appellate capacity. Review remains subject to the particular §1292 route’s requirements, including certification and circuit permission where §1292(b) applies; the provision does not grant an automatic appeal.

**Authority:** Thomas’s opinion, joined by Stone, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; five Justices adopt the text/coexistence rationale. White, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor agree in judgment on their separate stated reasoning.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an affirmative grant of jurisdiction over final bankruptcy decisions does not silently remove another statute’s grant over specified interlocutory district-court orders. Section 1292 speaks of district courts without distinguishing their original and appellate capacities. Section 158(d) contains no contrary command. Wood requires that both statutes operate where they do not positively conflict, and Ron Pair and Rubin direct attention to the enacted terms. Some overlap with final-order jurisdiction under §1291 does not justify creating an exclusion Congress did not write; the statutes also have different reach, including the bankruptcy appellate panels covered by §158. The bank’s proposed review must therefore be considered under the express interlocutory statute. This does not expand jurisdiction for convenience or dispense with certification, timely application or discretionary acceptance. It also does not extend district-court language to bankruptcy appellate panels without statutory authority. The Court decides the categorical jurisdictional objection only, leaving the jury question and remaining prerequisites for further proceedings.

## Law After Decision

Effective March 9, the Court rejects an implied categorical §158(d) exclusion of §1292 review for district-court bankruptcy appellate orders. The text/coexistence explanation has five votes, while the reversing judgment has nine. Certification, timeliness, circuit discretion and subject-matter limits remain; no jury-trial or bankruptcy-appellate-panel holding is added.

## Separate Writings

Stevens concurs in the judgment because both statutory language and legislative history support the available route. O’Connor, joined by White and Blackmun, accepts that redundancy is a real issue but finds the historical statutory structure insufficient to show withdrawal of interlocutory review. These four Justices agree with reversal without joining Thomas’s complete textual methodology.

## Procedure After Action

Supreme Court review of the jurisdictional dismissal ends. The case returns to the Second Circuit to consider §1292’s applicable requirements and whether to permit the appeal. The underlying jury dispute remains undecided here.
