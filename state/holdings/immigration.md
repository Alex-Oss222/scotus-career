## Immigration

### Persecution and review of agency findings

#### INS v. Elias-Zacarias, 502 U.S. 478 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-1342.
**Decided:** January 22, 1992.

##### Whether politically motivated coercive recruitment necessarily establishes persecution on account of the recruit's political opinion

**Holding:** Coercive recruitment by a politically motivated organization does not necessarily constitute persecution on account of the recruit's political opinion; the protected reason for targeting the applicant must be supported by evidence concerning the particular case. Direct evidence of motive is unnecessary, but the group's general agenda and the mere fact of refusal do not establish that connection by a categorical presumption.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's opinion of the Court, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; five Justices support this exact proposition. Stone's agreement in rejecting the categorical premise is not a join in the Court's opinion.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Circumstantial evidence may establish motive. Political neutrality is not categorically excluded. The Court assumes for purposes of its reasoning that an attributed opinion could qualify, without resolving every imputation issue; persecution because of the attribution still requires evidentiary support. Asylum's and withholding's distinct probability thresholds remain unchanged.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- *INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421 — asylum's well-founded-fear threshold remains distinct from withholding's higher probability requirement; neither removes the protected-ground nexus.
- *INS v. Stevic*, 467 U.S. 407 — withholding's distinct mandatory standard is preserved.
- *INS v. Abudu*, 485 U.S. 94 — distinct reopening grounds remain; the statutory nexus question is not replaced by discretionary denial of mandatory protection.

##### Whether the existing evidence permits displacement of the Board's nexus determination under §1105a(a)(4)

**Holding:** Under §1105a(a)(4), a court may overturn the Board's supported factual determination only when the record compels the contrary finding, not merely because that finding could be supported. Here the evidence does not compel a finding of feared persecution because of the applicant's political opinion, so the Ninth Circuit cannot displace the Board's denial on its stated nexus rationale.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's opinion joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; five Justices support both the evidentiary conclusion and the operative restoration of the Board's denial. Stone does not join this holding or its remedy.

**Material application:** The applicant explained fear of government retaliation for joining, and renewed visits established continuing recruitment pressure without compelling the inference that pressure arose because of his political opinion. The Court makes no credibility finding and requires no direct proof of motive.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The nexus-based review here does not establish one review standard for every prima-facie determination or every reopening denial.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Ninth Circuit must implement reversal and leave the Board's denial effective without the additional nexus or reopening review proposed by Stone. No asylum or withholding is awarded.

**Material judgment:** Six Justices—Stone, White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—support reversal of the categorical disposition; Blackmun, Stevens and O'Connor would affirm. Only White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support the existing-record resolution that leaves the Board's denial effective; Stone would require further review. The five-member coalition supplies the controlling remedy.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292 — applied for the distinction between evidence permitting a finding and evidence requiring displacement of the agency's finding.
- *INS v. Abudu*, 485 U.S. 94 — preserved; the Court does not expand this case's nexus review into a universal rule for all reopening determinations.
- *INS v. Doherty*, simulated January 15, 1992 — its dispositions created no new controlling reopening rationale and supply no contrary rule or merits finding here.

### Release conditions

#### INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights, Inc., 502 U.S. 183 (1991)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-1090.
**Decided:** December 16, 1991.

##### Whether INA §242(a) authorizes the narrowed release condition against unauthorized employment

**Holding:** INA §242(a) permits a generally applicable release condition forbidding unauthorized employment when the regulation is construed not to reach authorized workers and retains individual status determinations and discretionary relief. Officials must determine existing work authorization, address colorable citizenship or status claims, and consider the regulation's avenue for discretionary relief.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens's unanimous opinion; all nine Justices support the proposition.

**Material application:** Section 242(a) does not restrict every release condition to securing physical appearance. The regulation's construction limits its operation to unauthorized employment and preserves the individual determinations on which the facial statutory ruling rests.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not approve interference with lawful employment, presume disputed deportability, resolve constitutional objections, or foreclose an as-applied challenge where the represented procedures are unavailable or misused.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The judgment is reversed and the case remanded; constitutional and as-applied claims remain reserved.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Witkovich requires delegated immigration authority to remain connected to the statutory scheme; Congress's labor-market protections provide that connection here. American Hospital Association permits general rules to structure discretion while preserving legally required individual determinations. Carlson and Seminole Rock are applied and preserved. Ardestani remains confined to the Equal Access to Justice Act's §554 cross-reference.

### Reopening and relief from deportation

#### INS v. Doherty, 502 U.S. 314 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-925.
**Decided:** January 15, 1992.

##### Whether the Second Circuit could require reopening of the asylum application

**Authority of the judgment:** The asylum reopening requirement is reversed, 8-0, by Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter. The per curiam judgment supplies no substantive reopening rationale. White, joined by Blackmun and O'Connor, relies on the new-evidence requirement; Kennedy relies on tactical withdrawal; Stone, joined by Stevens, Scalia and Souter, relies on lawful discretion to deny asylum as distinguished from mandatory withholding. These independent grounds are not logical subsets of one another, and no controlling rationale exists under *Marks v. United States*, 430 U.S. 188. Thomas did not participate.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No substantive reopening proposition commands five of the eight participants. The separate withholding component was affirmed by an equally divided Court and supplies no precedent or adoption of the Second Circuit's reasoning; four Justices sought reversal and four sought vacatur and remand for review of exclusions. Neither disposition decides the statutory exclusions, awards protection, or adjudicates destination selection or detention. The Court did not order the proposed intervening review of exclusions.

### Juvenile custody and facial review

#### Reno v. Flores, 507 U.S. 292 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-905.

**Decided:** March 23, 1993.

##### Statutory authority for the juvenile release regulation

**Holding:** Section 1252(a)(1) permits the Attorney General to use the family-custodian and discretionary-release arrangements in 8 C.F.R. §242.24 for temporary juvenile custody pending deportation proceedings; the regulation is not facially beyond that authority because it does not mandate release to every willing responsible adult. Custody remains subject to the statute's reasonable-dispatch limitation, its habeas remedy upon the requisite conclusive showing of unnecessary delay, the regulation's release avenues and the existing conditions agreement.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's opinion for the Court, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six votes for this rule. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on a narrower statutory and facial-review ground.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524 (1952): applies discretion bounded by the immigration statute; it does not authorize indefinite custody.
- National Center for Immigrants' Rights, December 16, 1991, decision: applies general immigration-release authority with preserved individual safeguards; does not enlarge that decision into universal detention authority.
- United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739 (1987): applies facial-review limits to the whole-regulation remedy.

##### The facial substantive-due-process challenge fails

**Holding:** The Constitution does not give a juvenile without an available parent, guardian or close adult relative a fundamental right to placement with a willing unrelated adult instead of temporary, nonpunitive government care under this regulation and the binding conditions agreement. On this facial record, the family preferences and other release avenues reasonably serve care and attendance interests; individual unconstitutional confinement and enforcement of the agreement remain open.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. O'Connor and Souter also explain the substantial liberty interest in avoiding institutional confinement in a separate concurrence; Stone-Zsela does not join this formulation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Schall v. Martin, 467 U.S. 253 (1984): applies the distinction between protective juvenile custody and punishment.
- Parham v. J. R., 442 U.S. 584 (1979): applies the special circumstances of juvenile care; does not extinguish a child's liberty interest.
- Youngberg v. Romeo, 457 U.S. 307 (1982): preserves constitutional limits on institutional care.
- Foucha v. Louisiana, May 18, 1992, decision: distinguishes psychiatric confinement after its predicate ends; no universal custody test is inferred.

##### Request-based review is not facially inadequate

**Holding:** The described examination, notice and opportunity to obtain immigration-judge review on request do not facially require replacement with an automatic hearing for every detained juvenile. That result depends on the available procedures and the absence of a record establishing general inability to invoke them; it does not approve ineffective notice, denial of requested review, improper delay or unlawful custody in a particular case.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. Stone-Zsela agrees with setting aside the universal automatic-hearing remedy but reserves a distinct Mathews-based implementation inquiry.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Fare v. Michael C., 442 U.S. 707 (1979): applies the recognition that juveniles can make a knowing waiver; no universal finding of comprehension follows.
- The Japanese Immigrant Case, 189 U.S. 86 (1903): applies aliens' entitlement to due process in deportation proceedings.

##### The challenged classifications do not establish facial unequal treatment

**Holding:** The regulation's distinction between juveniles with available family custodians and those without them survives the facial equal-protection objection because family availability bears a rational relation to safe placement and attendance. The separate federal juvenile-delinquency release scheme does not require identical release rules for immigration custody, and this ruling does not resolve unequal administration in a particular case.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on this component and adds no ground.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Schall v. Martin, 467 U.S. 253 (1984): applies the legitimate relevance of protective care in juvenile custody; no general exemption from equal protection follows.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Subsection (b)(1) requires release in order of preference to a nondetained parent, legal guardian, or adult brother, sister, aunt, uncle or grandparent unless detention is required to secure timely appearance before the Service or immigration court, or ensure the juvenile's safety or that of others. If the only listed custodian is detained, (b)(2) provides discretionary case-by-case consideration of simultaneous release. Under (b)(3), a parent or guardian detained or outside the United States may designate another capable and willing person by sworn affidavit before an immigration or consular officer; that person must agree to care for the child and ensure presence at all future proceedings. Subsection (b)(4) permits other agreeing adult custodians only in unusual and compelling circumstances, at the district director's or chief patrol agent's discretion. These are distinct release avenues, not one undifferentiated exception.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The 1987 conditions agreement remains enforceable and is not a finding that each facility complies. Custody review under §1252(a)(1) permits habeas upon a conclusive showing of failure to proceed with reasonable dispatch warranted by the particular facts. For a juvenile not seeking voluntary departure, the described 24-hour process uses a different examiner unless no qualified alternative is available; English/Spanish notice allows review on request and withdrawal of waiver. No universal ruling on voluntary-departure safeguards, 30-day custody cap or approval of defective notice, ineffective assistance, delayed review or unconstitutional individual/systemic implementation follows.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the facial juvenile-release and automatic-hearing injunctions, 7–2 in judgment, and remand consistently with the six-Justice rationale. No particular child’s release is ordered; conditions enforcement and properly supported implementation challenges remain.

### Offshore interdiction and return

#### Sale v. Haitian Centers Council, 509 U.S. 155 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-344.
**Decided:** June 21, 1993.

##### Section 243(h) does not govern this offshore return operation

**Holding:** Section 243(h)’s restriction on deporting or returning an alien to threatened persecution does not govern the challenged interdiction and repatriation beyond the territorial sea. The 1980 removal of the words within the United States does not extend this domestic immigration provision to the coordinated offshore operation.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, Section 243(h) does not govern this offshore return operation portion; White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- EEOC v. Arabian American Oil Co., 499 U.S. 244 — geographic presumption applied.
- INS v. Stevic, 467 U.S. 407 — withholding standard distinguished from territorial reach.
- INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421 — asylum/withholding distinction preserved.
- Youngstown, 343 U.S. 579 — no power to disregard an applicable statute inferred; coverage is the issue decided.

##### Article 33 does not independently limit the high-seas operation

**Holding:** Article 33 of the Refugee Convention, as undertaken through the 1967 Protocol, does not extend its return prohibition to this high-seas repatriation operation. That territorial construction resolves the asserted treaty restraint without deciding whether the Convention otherwise supplies an independently enforceable cause of action. Article 33(2) withholds its benefit where reasonable grounds identify a danger to the security of the country of presence, or a refugee convicted by final judgment of a particularly serious crime constitutes a danger to that country’s community.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, Article 33 does not independently limit the high-seas operation portion; White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Statutory and treaty coverage are separately resolved. No refugee-status determination, general executive exemption from law or independent treaty cause-of-action ruling is made; domestic custody and other sources constraining different operations remain distinct.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the appellate coverage ruling under §243(h) and Article 33; no admission order, individual screening determination or personal injunction against the President.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- 1951 Refugee Convention Article 33 and 1967 Protocol — construed geographically for this operation, without a self-execution holding.
- Stevic, 467 U.S. 407 — treaty/statutory relationship considered without importing a general offshore duty.

### Original-order and reconsideration review periods

#### Stone v. Immigration and Naturalization Service

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-1199.
**Decided:** April 19, 1995.

##### Reconsideration does not postpone the original-order filing period under this INA scheme

**Holding:** Under former 8 U.S.C. §1105a, a motion to reopen or reconsider does not postpone the finality or filing period of the original deportation order for judicial review; a petition directed to that order must satisfy its own statutory period. Separately reviewable reconsideration dispositions have their own review period, and subsection (a)(6) requires consolidation when both reviews are properly pending; this rule does not make every reconsideration refusal reviewable.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Kennedy), joined by Stevens, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg: five Justices.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- ICC v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, 482 U.S. 270: distinguishes its ordinary administrative finality rule because the amended INA supplies a specific review modification; its separate treatment of original orders and reopening refusals remains intact.
- United States v. Ibarra, October 15, 1991: preserves its timely criminal-reconsideration rule and distinguishes this particular statutory review arrangement.
- Darby v. Cisneros, June 21, 1993: preserves its limits on extra APA exhaustion requirements; it does not decide the filing finality of an INA order.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The original deportation order and a separately reviewable reconsideration disposition carry independent filing periods under former §1105a. Optional reconsideration is not a prerequisite to judicial review. Finality for filing, agency enforceability, exhaustion, and a judicial or administrative stay remain distinct. No general equitable-tolling rule, blanket bar to reconsideration review, or immigration merits rule is decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Dismissal of the original deportation-order challenge as untimely is affirmed. The separate denial of review of reconsideration remains undisturbed and outside the question decided. No merits remand, suspension of deportation, deportability ruling or new stay is ordered.

### Discretionary relief from entry fraud

#### INS v. Yueh-Shaio Yang

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-938.

**Decided:** November 13, 1996.

For the other questions in INS v. Yueh-Shaio Yang, No. 95-938, decided November 13, 1996, see Federal Courts - Immigration review transition (Review under the corrected 1996 transition provisions).

##### Eligibility does not exclude the continuing fraud pattern from discretion

**Holding:** Former INA §241(a)(1)(H), 8 U.S.C. §1251(a)(1)(H), permits the Attorney General to consider a continuing pattern of immigration fraud when choosing among eligible waiver applicants, even though particular acts are connected with entry. Eligibility does not erase that conduct or compel a waiver; the Court neither permits automatic denial to every eligible applicant because of the qualifying fraud nor decides whether the minimum qualifying fraud alone could sustain a particular individualized denial.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part II, unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not reweigh the agency's balance. A granted waiver reaches entry-inadmissibility grounds directly resulting from the fraud; eligibility and this consequence remain distinct from discretionary selection.

The statute makes eligibility and favorable discretion distinct. It reaches covered entry fraud or misrepresentation, whether willful or innocent. An applicant must be the spouse, parent, son or daughter of a citizen or lawful permanent resident, have possessed an immigrant visa or equivalent document, and otherwise have been admissible at entry. The exceptions for labor-certification and immigrant-documentation grounds under §§1182(a)(5)(A) and (7)(A) apply only when those grounds directly resulted from the fraud. The separate exclusion for assistance in Nazi persecution or participation in genocide remains. An actually granted waiver also reaches entry-inadmissibility grounds directly resulting from the fraud. These provisions define eligibility and the consequence of relief; they do not remove conduct from the discretionary decision.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Jay v. Boyd supplies the distinction between eligibility and favorable discretion. Errico's mandatory predecessor is distinguished without overruling its construction. National Center for Immigrants' Rights supplies statutory and individualized-consideration limits by analogy.

##### Permissible factors remain subject to reasoned and consistent administration

**Holding:** Statutory permission to consider a factor does not excuse arbitrary decisionmaking, pretext, disregard of a binding rule, or an unexplained departure from a genuinely settled agency policy. Here the Board rationally distinguished continuing immigration fraud from the initial entry fraud it disregarded; the record supplies no policy reversal requiring a new administrative balance.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part III, unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** A genuinely settled policy may be changed openly with adequate reasons, subject to binding law and reliance; litigation explanations cannot repair an unexplained departure. No policy reversal is established here and no new Board balancing is ordered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Good Samaritan and Smiley preserve reasoned administration, explained policy change and attention to legitimate reliance. Doherty's fractured disposition supplies no controlling reopening rationale for this waiver question.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the Ninth Circuit's categorical factor exclusion and remand. No waiver, removal, reopening, new hearing or new Board balance is ordered.

### Waiver discretion and supplementary evidence

#### Reyes-Hernandez v. INS

**Docket or dockets:** Seventh Circuit No. 95-3677; Supreme Court docket unassigned.
**Decided:** 1997-05-12.

For §242(g) and the separate AEDPA eligibility/review restrictions, see Federal Courts — Immigration review transition, Reyes-Hernandez, May 12, 1997, this docket.

##### Eligibility does not compel a favorable waiver balance

**Holding:** An eligible §212(c) applicant remains subject to a reasoned individualized exercise of lawful discretion, and the BIA may adopt an immigration judge's adequate reasons without independently repeating the entire balance. Here consideration of rehabilitation and family hardship against serious, recent and repeated drug offenses lawfully supports denial; the reviewing court may not replace that balance merely because the favorable equities are substantial.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens's Parts II–IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; six direct joins. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would dismiss under §440(a) and supply no merits votes.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The favorable rehabilitation and family-hardship evidence was considered against serious, recent and repeated drug offenses. No presumption against all drug offenders follows; arbitrary action, pretext, binding-rule violations and unexplained departures from settled policy remain reviewable.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Yang separates eligibility from reasoned individualized discretion; adopting adequate immigration-judge reasons is not a failure to explain.

##### A supplemental appellate brief did not require original factfinding or automatic reopening

**Holding:** On this record, the BIA could require the established motion to supplement, remand or reopen before considering new post-hearing evidence offered in an appellate brief, rather than treating that brief as a different motion or conducting original appellate factfinding. The decision does not permit disregard of material evidence already admitted or decide the eligibility, timeliness, merits or disposition of any later motion.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens's Parts II–IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; six direct joins. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would dismiss under §440(a) and supply no merits votes.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm denial of the petition for review, 6–3. No waiver, reweighing, new hearing, later reopening disposition, habeas ruling, removal command or stay is ordered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Abudu preserves the established reopening channel and distinction between admitted record evidence and new material. Doherty's fracture supplies no general controlling reopening rationale.

### Fractured citizenship-transmission judgment

#### Miller v. Albright

**Docket or dockets:** No. 96-1060.

**Decided:** 1998-04-22.

##### The affirmance supplies no controlling merits or remedial rule

**Authority of the judgment:** Five Justices affirm denial of Miller's requested declaration, but no majority sustains the sex-based recognition condition under heightened scrutiny, adopts rational review of every child's own claim, or holds the requested citizenship remedy categorically unavailable.

Stevens relies on the recognition condition's constitutional fit; O'Connor and Kennedy rely on their claimant distinction and rational review of the daughter's own claim; Scalia and Thomas rely on limits on judicial citizenship relief. These grounds are not logical subsets of one another. A conclusion about validity does not decide who may assert a claim or what remedy follows if it is invalid. O'Connor and Kennedy's conditional heightened-scrutiny criticism is not an operative vote deciding the father's claim. Their views cannot be combined with other writings to create a majority merits holding.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The judgment neither validates all of §1409 nor resolves separate support or physical-presence conditions, makes late recognition timely or grants citizenship merely upon accepted biological parentage. The independent third-party permission appears under Federal Courts.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The requested declaration is denied by affirmance, 5–4: Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support affirmance; Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer oppose. No controlling constitutional or citizenship-remedy rationale is inferred.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Virginia, Kelley and Klinger retain their actual-classification, genuine-objective and substantial-fit rules. The different applications proposed here establish no controlling alteration of those rules.
- Fiallo's immigration-admission holding receives no controlling extension to all citizenship-at-birth classifications.
- Pangilinan, Wong Kim Ark and Bellei receive no majority extension into the remedial rule proposed by Scalia and Thomas.
- Mathews and Westcott receive no controlling new application requiring either extension or withdrawal of this citizenship benefit. The separate constitutional-remedy proposals remain noncontrolling.
