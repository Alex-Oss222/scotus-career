## Environmental Law

### Interstate water-pollution permits

#### Arkansas v. Oklahoma, 503 U.S. 91 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** Nos. 90-1262 and 90-1266.
**Decided:** February 26, 1992.
**Companion or consolidated matters:** *EPA v. Oklahoma*, No. 90-1266, consolidated with *Arkansas*; both concern EPA’s federal discharge permit for Fayetteville.

##### Whether EPA may condition this upstream federal permit on compliance with downstream state water-quality standards

**Holding:** The Clean Water Act authorizes EPA, in issuing this federal permit under §402(a), to require compliance with applicable downstream state water-quality standards through its valid regulations. A downstream State’s limited direct role in permitting does not restrict that federal authority; whether the Act itself commands identical compliance in every permitting setting remains open.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s opinion, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; unanimous statutory-authority holding.

**Current force:** Remains controlling as stated.

**Material application:** Section 402(a)(2) authorizes permit conditions, §301(b)(1)(C) identifies attainment of applicable standards, and 40 C.F.R. §122.4(d) reasonably implements that scheme through assurance of affected-state compliance. Limits on Oklahoma’s independent authority do not negate EPA’s delegated power.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court decides neither a universal statutory command governing every federal or state permit nor an independent source-state tort remedy. Technical expertise alone creates no authority.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse in both dockets and sustain EPA’s permit under its existing compliance, study and modification conditions. No new numerical limit or order concerning the separate White River discharge is entered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Milwaukee v. Illinois*, 451 U.S. 304, retains the Act’s displacement of federal nuisance common law. *International Paper Co. v. Ouellette*, 479 U.S. 481, is distinguished: limits on affected-state common-law control and direct permitting participation do not restrict this authorized federal condition. *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, applies within Congress’s delegation. *Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB*, decided January 27, 1992, is preserved: deference operates within controlling statutory precedent, and no contrary construction is displaced.

##### Whether existing downstream degradation categorically prohibits a new discharge

**Holding:** The Clean Water Act does not categorically forbid a new discharge merely because its effluent will reach waters already failing a water-quality standard, irrespective of whether it will cause a detectable violation under the applicable permit standards. Courts may not substitute that categorical prohibition for the Act’s actual permitting and pollution-reduction scheme.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s opinion, joined by Stone, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; unanimous rejection of the court of appeals’ independent statutory ground.

**Current force:** Remains controlling as stated.

**Material application:** Section 303(d) provides a framework for pollution reduction, and §402(h) addresses additional pollutants accepted by a treatment plant violating its own permit; neither supplies the broader downstream prohibition the Tenth Circuit imposed. EPA applied the no-detectable-violation approach to this discharge.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Existing degradation remains relevant under the actual governing standards. No detectable violation is excused and no later permit condition is decided on facts outside the record.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Remove the categorical already-degraded-waters bar and sustain the permit under the existing conditions in both dockets.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Milwaukee v. Illinois*, 451 U.S. 304, describes a comprehensive statutory program; it does not authorize courts to add a categorical prohibition.

For *Arkansas v. Oklahoma*, Nos. 90-1262 and 90-1266, decided February 26, 1992, on construction of the incorporated downstream standards and judicial review of EPA’s findings, see Administrative Law, “Federal permit interpretation and review.”
