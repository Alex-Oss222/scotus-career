## Antitrust

### State-action immunity for private rate fixing

#### FTC v. Ticor Title Insurance Co., 504 U.S. 621 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-72.
**Decided:** June 12, 1992.

For the Connecticut/Arizona reconsideration and statutory fact-review holding in *FTC v. Ticor Title Insurance Co.*, June 12, 1992, No. 91-72, see Administrative Law — Commission findings and appellate reconsideration.

##### Whether private collectively fixed rates taking effect without a veto receive immunity from regulatory capacity alone

**Holding:** Private parties claiming state-action antitrust immunity for collectively fixed rates must establish both a clearly articulated state policy and actual state supervision of the particular restraint. When private rates take effect unless vetoed, the claimant must show that state officials exercised sufficient independent judgment and control to make the rate scheme a product of deliberate state intervention; an available veto, operating regulatory machinery or nominal approval alone is insufficient.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia and Souter; six Justices adopt this proposition. Stone concurs in the judgment without a formal opinion join; O'Connor and Thomas dissent from the 7–2 reversal.

**Material application:** State officials must exercise substantive control over the particular private restraint. Staffed agencies, veto power, enforceable duties and the legal effect of silence establish capacity or nominal approval, not necessarily its exercise.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Clear articulation remains independently necessary and is conceded for the four States before the Court. No hearing format, paperwork requirement or rejection quota is prescribed; the inquiry asks whether the State actually decided, not whether regulation is wise or efficient. Distinct sovereign action, materially different comprehensive supervision, infrequent lapses, other insurance exemptions and any difference between FTC Act and Sherman Act immunity remain undecided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Third Circuit capacity-based immunity ruling is reversed and remanded. Wisconsin/Montana immunity is rejected; Connecticut/Arizona require the separate reconsideration stated under Administrative Law.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Parker v. Brown, 317 U.S. 341 (1943): applied for the sovereign-policy basis of state-action immunity; mere state permission for private restraints is not enough.
- California Retail Liquor Dealers Assn. v. Midcal Aluminum, Inc., 445 U.S. 97 (1980): applied and specified; its clear-articulation and active-supervision requirements remain independently necessary for this private rate fixing.
- Patrick v. Burget, 486 U.S. 94 (1988): applied; officials must possess and exercise substantive control, and limited judicial review does not substitute for it.
- Southern Motor Carriers Rate Conference, Inc. v. United States, 471 U.S. 48 (1985): distinguished; its rejection of a compulsion requirement concerns clear articulation, and its conceded supervision establishes no automatic rule for negative-option systems.
- Lafayette v. Louisiana Power & Light Co., 435 U.S. 389 (1978), and Cantor v. Detroit Edison Co., 428 U.S. 579 (1976): applied for refusing to infer immunity merely from regulation; limited state ends do not silently authorize all associated restraints.
- Columbia v. Omni Outdoor Advertising, Inc., 499 U.S. 365 (1991), Fisher v. Berkeley, 475 U.S. 260 (1986), and 324 Liquor Corp. v. Duffy, 479 U.S. 335 (1987): left intact; local zoning, unilateral governmental decisions and different methods of comprehensive rate supervision are not resolved by this record.

##### Whether Wisconsin and Montana substantively supervised these private rates

**Holding:** The supported findings that Wisconsin and Montana performed at most arithmetic checks, left some filings unchecked and allowed rates to remain effective despite missing requested information establish no actual substantive state supervision of these challenged private rate agreements. The agreements therefore receive no state-action antitrust immunity in those two States, notwithstanding the legal effect that state law assigns to official inaction.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia and Souter; six Justices adopt this proposition. Stone concurs in the judgment without a formal opinion join; O'Connor and Thomas dissent from the 7–2 reversal.

**Material application:** Commission findings show arithmetic-only or absent review, rates effective despite missing requested information in Montana, and an information request seven years after a Wisconsin filing became effective. The findings establish absent substantive control, not economically mistaken state judgment; limited judicial review cannot cure that absence.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court decides the claimed state-action immunity, not the validity of every title-insurance charge. McCarran-Ferguson and Noerr-Pennington defenses remain for further proceedings; the underdeveloped separation-of-powers claim is not decided. New Jersey and Pennsylvania lie outside this grant.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Wisconsin and Montana receive no state-action immunity on these findings. Further statutory proceedings remain; the Court sets no rate, awards no damages and does not automatically reinstate the entire Commission order.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Patrick v. Burget, 486 U.S. 94 (1988): applied to the supported record; limited judicial review cannot cure the absence of substantive agency supervision.
- California Retail Liquor Dealers Assn. v. Midcal Aluminum, Inc., 445 U.S. 97 (1980): applied to defeat immunity where particular private prices lack actual state control.
- FTC v. Superior Court Trial Lawyers Assn., 493 U.S. 411 (1990): applied for the gravity of horizontal price fixing; this case does not expand the category of challenged conduct beyond the private rate agreements before it.

### Tying and aftermarket monopolization

#### Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, Inc., 504 U.S. 451 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-1029.
**Decided:** June 8, 1992.

##### Whether demand for separate parts and service and conditional parts sales create a triable tying claim

**Holding:** For a Sherman Act §1 tying claim, functionally related products are distinct when sufficient separate consumer demand makes separate provision efficient. Evidence that Kodak parts and service meet that test, and that Kodak conditions parts sales on buying its service or forgoing independent service, creates genuine disputes about distinct products and conditioning; respondents must still prove all remaining tying elements.

**Proposition-level authority:** Blackmun's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices.

**Material application:** Independent service providers, customer preference for their services and self-servicing customers permit a finding of efficient separate provision. Restrictions on parts sales and use of independent service permit a finding of conditioning; installation of parts during service does not conclusively merge the products. The tied-commerce volume is undisputed here.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No final tie, market power or liability finding is made. An independent original-equipment-manufacturer conspiracy claim is not adjudicated; the rule-of-reason theory unpreserved in opposition to summary judgment is outside review.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm reversal of summary judgment on the preserved §1 tying claim; respondents retain proof of its elements and the requirements for recovery.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Jefferson Parish Hospital District No. 2 v. Hyde*, 466 U.S. 2, supplies separate demand, conditioning, tying power and tied commerce. *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, preserves the obligation to produce evidence on challenged elements at summary judgment.

##### Whether equipment-market competition categorically precludes aftermarket power

**Holding:** Competition in an equipment market does not as a matter of law preclude power in its parts or service markets. Rule 56 does not permit summary judgment solely through an economic prediction contradicted by evidence allowing a rational inference of power, including material information and switching costs and actual price or exclusion effects; unsupported speculation remains insufficient.

**Proposition-level authority:** Blackmun's opinion, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices reject the categorical defense.

**Current force:** Remains controlling as stated; equipment competition remains relevant evidence.

**Material application:** Costly equipment replacement and difficulty predicting lifetime service prices permit a finding that installed customers cannot readily escape adverse terms. Evidence of price and exclusion effects supports further proceedings. Kodak may establish that informed new customers and equipment competition adequately constrain its policies, but the conclusion is not inevitable as a matter of law.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court presumes neither market power from imperfect information nor monopoly status for every durable-product seller. Prices, effects and asserted policy changes remain disputed; no universal grandfathering, deception or retroactive-policy-change finding is made.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The preserved claims survive summary judgment, with actual markets, power and liability remaining to be proved.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 475 U.S. 574, preserves rational inference from evidence but is distinguished because immediate aftermarket gains differ from its prolonged loss-producing conspiracy. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, preserves the factfinder's role on genuine material disputes. *Jefferson Parish*, 466 U.S. 2, continues to require proof of actual tying power.

##### Whether one brand's parts or service can constitute a relevant market on this record

**Holding:** A relevant product market may consist of one brand's parts or service when actual commercial alternatives and competitive constraints support that definition; brand identity alone neither establishes nor defeats it. Kodak-specific compatibility, very high parts and service shares, and entry and switching barriers create a genuine dispute about monopoly power sufficient to resist summary judgment under §2.

**Proposition-level authority:** Blackmun's opinion, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices permit a market and power finding without conclusively establishing either.

**Material application:** Owners cannot necessarily substitute another brand's parts, and replacing the equipment may cost much more than the service at issue. Evidence supports nearly complete control of compatible parts and an eighty-to-ninety-five-percent service share, together with impediments to independent supply. Equipment competitors remain relevant to whether lifetime prices are constrained.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No universal single-brand-market rule or monopoly presumption follows from patents, proprietary design or numerical share apart from actual competitive constraints. Actual market definition and power remain unresolved.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm reversal of summary judgment on the preserved §2 claims, without a final market or power finding.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *United States v. Grinnell Corp.*, 384 U.S. 563, requires monopoly power in a relevant market, distinct from exclusion. *United States v. E. I. du Pont de Nemours & Co.*, 351 U.S. 377, supplies practical substitution and competitive-constraint analysis without a categorical bar to one-brand markets.

##### Whether evidence of exclusion and Kodak's business explanations conclusively resolve monopoly maintenance

**Holding:** Monopolization under §2 requires monopoly power plus willful acquisition or maintenance, distinguished from success through a superior product, business acumen or accident. Parts-access restrictions that may exclude service rivals create a genuine issue of unlawful maintenance, and Kodak's quality, inventory and free-riding explanations do not conclusively eliminate that issue here; no general duty to supply rivals or final pretext finding is imposed.

**Proposition-level authority:** Blackmun's opinion, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices.

**Current force:** Remains controlling as stated for evidentiary sufficiency and the distinct power and conduct requirements.

**Material application:** The quality explanation must be tested against self-service exceptions and independent-service evidence; the inventory explanation against evidence that parts needs do not depend on the service provider; and free-riding claims against the restrictions' operation and alternative-supply obstacles. Kodak may prove legitimate reasons and defeat liability.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Attempted monopolization remains distinct, with its own intent and dangerous-probability requirements. Ultimate markets, power, unlawful conduct, pretext, antitrust injury, causation, damages and remedial design remain unresolved. Injury to a competitor alone establishes neither antitrust injury nor damages entitlement.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm reversal of summary judgment on the preserved §2 monopolization and attempt claims. No damages, final injunction or compulsory parts-supply order issues; respondents must prove each claim and recovery requirement.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Grinnell*, 384 U.S. 563, distinguishes unlawful maintenance from lawful success. *Aspen Skiing Co. v. Aspen Highlands Skiing Corp.*, 472 U.S. 585, makes exclusion and business justification material without creating a universal supply duty. *Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc.*, 429 U.S. 477, retains the distinct antitrust-injury requirement. *Holmes v. Securities Investor Protection Corp.*, March 24, 1992, retains its RICO directness and SIPA holdings without deciding these Sherman Act elements or injury.

### Attempted monopolization and legally defective verdict grounds

#### Spectrum Sports v. McQuillan, 506 U.S. 447 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-10.

**Decided:** January 25, 1993.

##### Conduct and intent cannot replace dangerous probability

**Holding:** Attempted monopolization under Sherman Act §2 requires anticompetitive or predatory conduct, specific intent to monopolize, and a dangerous probability of achieving monopoly power, evaluated through the relevant product and geographic market and the defendant’s economic power there. Conduct may support intent and other permissible evidentiary inferences, but neither conduct nor intent eliminates independent proof of dangerous probability; no categorical small-firm immunity or prohibition of circumstantial proof follows.

**Proposition-level authority:** White’s opinion of the Court for Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; nine Justices adopt the rule and its explanation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Swift & Co. v. United States, 196 U.S. 375 — applied: dangerous probability requires proximity and degree beyond intent alone.
- Copperweld Corp. v. Independence Tube Corp., 467 U.S. 752 — applied: unilateral conduct must actually monopolize or dangerously threaten monopolization for these §2 offenses.
- Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, June 8, 1992 — preserved at its stated scope: evidence may establish a single-brand market and power; no presumption replaces separate attempt elements.
- Lessig v. Tidewater Oil Co., 327 F.2d 459 — rejected insofar as its conduct-based inference dispenses with market and dangerous-probability proof.

##### The undifferentiated verdict requires further proceedings

**Holding:** The judgment against these petitioners cannot stand on a §2 verdict that may rest solely on an attempted-monopolization instruction dispensing with dangerous-probability proof, when the appellate affirmance rests on that legally erroneous route. Reverse and remand for application of the correct elements and consideration of any properly preserved independent grounds; this decision establishes neither defeat nor success on those grounds and orders no automatic new trial or judgment for petitioners.

**Proposition-level authority:** White’s unanimous opinion; all nine Justices adopt the remedial holding and explanation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Sunkist Growers, Inc. v. Winckler & Smith Citrus Products Co., 370 U.S. 19 — applied: the possibility of reliance on a legally erroneous submitted ground requires reversal here.
- Griffin v. United States, December 3, 1991 — distinguished at its stated scope: its factual-insufficiency rule does not validate an element-removing instruction or decide the unreviewed civil claims.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No market-share floor, small-firm immunity, general supply duty or automatic inference of market power from a proprietary product is adopted. Antitrust injury and other recovery elements remain necessary but are not separately decided. RICO, state claims and other parties’ judgments are not resolved.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse as to these petitioners and remand under the correct attempt elements and any preserved independent grounds. The undifferentiated verdict may rest on legal error; neither a universal new trial nor judgment for defendants is commanded.

### Antitrust petitioning immunity

#### Professional Real Estate Investors, Inc. v. Columbia Pictures Industries, Inc., 508 U.S. 49 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-1043.
**Decided:** May 3, 1993.

##### A single suit must be objectively baseless before motive defeats petitioning protection

**Holding:** Under the sham theory presented, a lawsuit loses Noerr protection only if it is objectively baseless, so that no reasonable litigant could realistically expect success on the merits, and then is shown to use the governmental process itself, rather than its outcome, as an anticompetitive weapon. The copyright suit had an objectively reasonable basis, ending this inquiry without motive discovery; even a sham finding would leave the ordinary antitrust elements to be proved.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Fraud in obtaining governmental action, serial proceedings and independently unlawful conduct remain unresolved. Loss of petitioning protection would not establish the ordinary antitrust elements.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm rejection of the antitrust counterclaim and motive discovery; the studios’ copyright loss remains undisturbed.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Eastern Railroad Presidents Conference v. Noerr Motor Freight, Inc., 365 U.S. 127, and United Mine Workers v. Pennington, 381 U.S. 657: applies protection for government-directed competitive advocacy.
- City of Columbia v. Omni Outdoor Advertising, Inc., 499 U.S. 365: applies process/outcome distinction.
- California Motor Transport Co. v. Trucking Unlimited, 404 U.S. 508: clarifies sham litigation without deciding every pattern or fraud case.
- Bill Johnson’s Restaurants, Inc. v. NLRB, 461 U.S. 731: applies distinction between baseless and reasonably grounded suits.
- Spectrum Sports, January 25, 1993: ordinary antitrust elements remain necessary; no new exemption from their proof.

### Primary-line predatory pricing

#### Brooke Group v. Brown & Williamson, 509 U.S. 209 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-466.
**Decided:** June 21, 1993.

##### Below-cost pricing must carry a prospect of recoupment

**Holding:** A primary-line predatory-pricing claim requires pricing below an appropriate measure of the defendant’s costs and a reasonable prospect of recovering the predatory investment through anticompetitive returns. Tacit oligopolistic coordination is a legally possible mechanism; actual success, an express conspiracy or a single-firm monopoly is not indispensable, but intent, concentration and injury to a rival alone are insufficient.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, Below-cost pricing must carry a prospect of recoupment portion; O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (5 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Brown Shoe, 370 U.S. 294 — competition/competitor distinction applied.
- Matsushita, 475 U.S. 574 — recoupment logic applied without requiring an express conspiracy here.
- Cargill, 479 U.S. 104 — low prices distinguished from actionable predation.
- Utah Pie, 386 U.S. 685 — read consistently with competitive injury; not treated as rival-protection liability.
- Spectrum Sports, January 25, 1993 — intent cannot replace competitive danger; Sherman Act elements not transplanted wholesale.

##### This record lacks the necessary recoupment showing

**Holding:** Even crediting below-cost pricing and predatory purpose and drawing reasonable inferences for Liggett, the evidence does not permit a reasonable jury to find the required prospect of Brown & Williamson recouping its losses through the alleged coordinated pricing mechanism. The failure of that necessary element sustains judgment as a matter of law without a categorical oligopoly exemption.

**Proposition-level authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, This record lacks the necessary recoupment showing portion; O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (5 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No exclusive cost measure is selected. Actual recoupment, express conspiracy or single-firm monopoly is not required, but the defendant’s own recovery must be supported. Sherman Act dangerous probability is not automatically substituted for Robinson-Patman’s reasonable prospect. Alternative antitrust-injury and causation grounds remain undecided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm judgment as a matter of law for Brown & Williamson because recoupment proof fails; no reinstatement of Liggett’s verdict or categorical oligopoly exemption.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Matsushita, 475 U.S. 574 — economically plausible mechanism must have evidentiary support.
- Cargill, 479 U.S. 104 — rival losses do not alone establish competitive harm.
- Utah Pie, 386 U.S. 685 — circumstantial proof permitted, but necessary recovery inference absent here.

### Insurance antitrust exemptions and foreign conduct

#### Hartford Fire Insurance v. California, 509 U.S. 764 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** Nos. 91-1111 and 91-1128.
**Decided:** June 28, 1993.

##### Insurance exemption follows activity rather than association

**Holding:** Otherwise qualifying business of insurance regulated by state law does not lose McCarran-Ferguson § 2(b) antitrust protection merely because domestic insurers agree with foreign reinsurers. Whether the activity itself qualifies and is state-regulated remains essential; neither domestic nor foreign nationality establishes that condition categorically.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Group Life & Health Insurance v. Royal Drug, 440 U.S. 205: activity/entity distinction applied; loose outsider analogy confined to its context.
- Union Labor Life v. Pireno, 458 U.S. 119: practice-specific criteria retained.
- SEC v. National Securities, 393 U.S. 453: regulation of insurance business distinguished from regulation of companies.
- Case-Swayne v. Sunkist, 389 U.S. 384, and United States v. Borden, 308 U.S. 188: producer/entity-based exemptions distinguished.
- Fabe, June 11, 1993: separate first-clause holding not expanded into the antitrust proviso.

##### Collateral refusals distinguish boycott from agreement on terms

**Holding:** A concerted refusal of collateral or unrelated transactions to compel desired terms in a targeted transaction can constitute a McCarran-Ferguson § 3(b) boycott; a mere joint insistence on terms of the refused transaction does not, without more, do so. The pleaded broader withdrawals permit California I–IV/Connecticut I–II to proceed, subject to proof and whether other business genuinely affects the reinsured risk; California VII/Connecticut VI allege no refusal mechanism and cannot defeat domestic exemption on the reviewed grounds.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- St. Paul Fire & Marine v. Barry, 438 U.S. 531: collateral-pressure application retained; not limited to boycotts of competitors.
- Eastern States Retail Lumber v. United States, 234 U.S. 600: unrelated trade used as leverage illustrates the boundary.
- United States v. South-Eastern Underwriters, 322 U.S. 533: coercive exclusion distinguished from mere agreement on terms; not abrogated.
- McLain v. Real Estate Board, 444 U.S. 232, and Conley v. Gibson, 355 U.S. 41: then-operative pleading standard applied; no actual liability finding.

##### The asserted British permission does not bar these American-market claims

**Holding:** The pleaded London conduct intended to produce, and producing, substantial effects on American insurance coverage may be adjudicated under the Sherman Act; British permission without an incompatible duty or another established ground requiring nonapplication here does not warrant the asserted comity dismissal. The Court reserves a universal comity rule and §6a’s precise applicability, while preserving its qualifying-effects and claim-connection requirements wherever applicable.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Qualifying activity and actual state regulation remain required. Section 6a’s precise applicability is reserved; where applicable its direct, substantial and reasonably foreseeable domestic effect and claim-connection requirements remain, and the pleaded effect here gives rise to these claims. No universal comity-impossibility rule, general independent coercion definition, proven cartel or actual liability is adopted.

**Operative remedy or transition:** No. 91-1111: reject association-based loss of exemption; affirm reinstatement of California I–IV/Connecticut I–II; reverse reinstatement of California VII/Connecticut VI against domestic defendants for lack of an alleged refusal mechanism, preserving unresolved state-regulation questions. No. 91-1128: affirm continued adjudication of California V, VI, VIII/Connecticut III–V against London defendants and remand for proof and other defenses. California IX–XI and Connecticut VII remain untouched; no damages or injunction.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- United States v. Aluminum Co. of America, 148 F.2d 416, and Matsushita, 475 U.S. 574: intended substantial domestic effects standard applied.
- Continental Ore v. Union Carbide, 370 U.S. 690: foreign permission does not itself immunize conduct.
- Société Nationale Industrielle Aérospatiale, 482 U.S. 522: actual foreign/domestic conflict considered; Blackmun’s separate analysis identified as not the whole Court’s holding.
- Charming Betsy, 2 Cranch 64: interpretive canon retained; does not require the asserted dismissal here.
- Aramco, 499 U.S. 244, and Sale, June 21, 1993: source-specific geographic scope preserved; different statutes do not erase established Sherman Act reach.

### Nonstatutory labor exemption

#### Brown v. Pro Football, Inc., 518 U.S. 231 (1996)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-388.
**Decided:** June 20, 1996.

##### Whether joint implementation of the last wage proposal after bargaining impasse remains exempt

**Holding:** The nonstatutory labor exemption protects members of an existing consensual multiemployer bargaining group when they jointly implement their last lawful wage proposal during or immediately after bona fide bargaining reaches impasse, the conduct directly arises from that bargaining, and it concerns mandatory employment terms and the bargaining participants. Contract expiration, impasse and the union's rejection of the particular proposed wage do not by themselves end that protection; it does not cover product-market collusion, agreements with nonlabor parties, sham bargaining, or restraints remote in time or purpose from the labor relationship.

**Proposition-level authority:** Breyer’s opinion of the Court; eight Justices, all except Stevens.

**Material application:** The clubs implemented the last lawful developmental-player wage proposal within a genuine continuing consensual multiemployer relationship. The exemption concerns this bargaining process, not the wage’s desirability or the industry’s identity.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The precise later point at which conduct becomes detached from bargaining is left open. No single-actor antitrust status, labor-law violation, sports exemption or comprehensive labor-market immunity is established.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirmed, 8–1. The exemption defeats this antitrust recovery; no reinstatement of the trial damages, injunction or fee award.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Jewel Tea, 381 U.S. 676:** Applied for the exemption's collective-bargaining foundation, preserving the distinction between White's and Goldberg's different explanations rather than treating either as a six-Justice rationale.
- **Connell, 421 U.S. 616:** Applied for the accommodation of labor and antitrust law; its refusal to exempt exclusion of unrelated subcontractors from the product market remains in force and marks the boundary against restraints on outsiders.
- **Bonanno, 454 U.S. 404:** Applied for the proposition that impasse alone does not dissolve consensual multiemployer bargaining; the precise later endpoint of this exemption remains undecided.
- **Buffalo Linen, 353 U.S. 87:** Applied for the recognized legitimacy of coordinated employer bargaining responses within the statutory labor relationship; it does not create immunity for every employer agreement.

### Vertical resale-price agreements

#### State Oil Co. v. Khan

**Docket or dockets:** No. 96-871.
**Decided:** November 4, 1997.

##### Whether vertical maximum resale-price agreements remain unlawful per se

**Holding:** A vertical maximum resale-price agreement is subject to the rule of reason under Sherman Act §1, rather than automatic condemnation because it sets a ceiling. Albrecht's contrary per se rule is overruled only to that extent; harmful ceilings remain actionable under the rules applicable to their actual competitive effects and character.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Scalia), unanimous.

**Material application:** The rebate obligation makes State Oil's arrangement a price agreement, not a unilateral suggestion. A ceiling may suppress dealer services or facilitate unlawful restraints, but may also reduce markups and promote competition between brands. That variation defeats Albrecht's categorical premise. Respect for statutory precedent does not cure that defect, and reliance on categorical condemnation has limited weight where antitrust remedies remain available. Lower courts properly obeyed Albrecht until this Court overruled it.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Minimum resale-price restraints and horizontal price fixing retain their distinct rules. Service suppression, exclusion, exploitation of dealer dependence, disguised minimum prices and proved horizontal arrangements remain examinable. The decision finds no market power, competitive injury, damages or ultimate evidentiary significance; lost dealer margin alone is not antitrust injury. It adopts no general stare decisis checklist.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The antitrust disposition is vacated for rule-of-reason proceedings and properly available remaining issues and defenses. The district court's antitrust summary judgment is not automatically reinstated, nor is every evidentiary or injury issue automatically reopened. The separate affirmance of the state-contract dismissal remains intact.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Albrecht v. Herald Co., 390 U.S. 145, loses its per se rule for vertical maximum resale prices. Sylvania's vertical-distribution analysis and Business Electronics' distinction concerning genuine price agreements inform classification without deciding every restraint. Atlantic Richfield and Brunswick preserve the independent antitrust-injury requirement. Kodak and Brooke Group retain their own rules about evidence and competitive harm. Hubbard's divided reasoning supplies no general controlling precedent-reconsideration test.


### Coverage includes an association serving its members' commercial profit

#### California Dental Association v. Federal Trade Commission

**Docket or dockets:** No. 97-1625.
**Decided:** 1999-05-24.

##### Coverage includes an association serving its members' commercial profit

**Holding:** The FTC Act reaches a nonprofit professional association organized to carry on business for its members' profit when its activities confer proximate, substantial economic benefits on their for-profit operations. CDA satisfies that condition through its insurance, financing, commercial support and other member-business activities; nonprofit form, tax status and absence of distributions to the association do not eliminate the members'-profit branch of section 44.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's statutory-coverage opinion, unanimous.

##### The inquiry must test the restraint's actual competitive tendency and material explanation

**Holding:** An abbreviated rule-of-reason inquiry may suffice when experience and the restraint's actual character make its likely anticompetitive tendency sufficiently clear to assess both the restraint and its material competing explanation. Where professional-information disparities make those effects insufficiently obvious, fuller analysis fitted to the restraint is required; neither a fixed sequence of labels nor a complete industry or market-definition study is invariably compulsory.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's competitive-analysis opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas (five Justices).

##### Price restrictions require more examination on this record

**Holding:** The practical restrictions on fee descriptions and broad discount advertisements cannot be sustained on the abbreviated analysis employed here without adequately examining their asserted effect on the reliability of price comparison. Further inquiry must test the actual enforcement and claimed information benefits; the mere reduction of advertising does not settle the net competitive tendency.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's competitive-analysis opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas (five Justices).

##### Quality advertising is not equivalent to service output

**Holding:** Quality-advertising restrictions whose effects depend on the reliability and verifiability of professional information require fuller consideration than equating fewer advertisements with a restriction of dental-service output. The actual conditions and competitive explanation must be assessed; possible effects on demand do not themselves establish a producer-imposed service-output restriction.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's competitive-analysis opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas (five Justices).

**Limits and questions not reached:** No final liability, professional immunity, universal market-definition trial, fixed sequence of labels or measured service-output proof is required. Supported agency findings retain statutory review protection. No majority decides whether the Ninth Circuit may complete review on the existing record or must direct Commission reconsideration.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Sustain FTC coverage unanimously; vacate both price- and quality-advertising liability determinations, 5–4, and remand through the Ninth Circuit for fuller restraint-specific consideration. The four-Justice appellate-choice discussion and Stone-Zsela's Commission-only direction are separate noncontrolling remedies.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- NCAA v. Board of Regents supplies flexible inquiry fitted to established competitive tendencies; it creates no compulsory full-market procedure.
- National Society of Professional Engineers v. United States remains a bar to defending restraints on the premise that competition itself is undesirable; an information-quality explanation must be tested on its actual competitive terms.
- FTC v. Indiana Federation of Dentists is distinguished at the level of the restraint and information mechanism; its direct-effects method and separation of supported findings from legal sufficiency remain.
- Bates v. State Bar of Arizona supplies the value of truthful price information and the distinct difficulty of unverifiable professional quality claims.
- State Oil Co. v. Khan retains its vertical maximum-price holding and horizontal distinctions; its attention to competitive mechanisms informs this inquiry without conferring immunity.
- NYNEX Corp. v. Discon retains its vertical supplier-substitution holding and distinct horizontal categories; labels or improper purpose do not replace analysis of the actual mechanism.

##### Authority of the institutional remand allocation

**Authority of the judgment:** Five Justices vacate both advertising-liability components and require fuller consideration. Souter, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas would leave the Ninth Circuit to choose existing-record assessment or Commission reconsideration; Stone-Zsela requires Commission inquiry. Neither institutional direction has five votes, and the common judgment adopts neither allocation as controlling.

### This vertical supplier substitution is not a per se group boycott

#### NYNEX Corp. v. Discon, Inc., 525 U.S. 128

**Docket or dockets:** No. 96-1570
**Decided:** 1998-12-14.

##### This vertical supplier substitution is not a per se group boycott

**Holding:** A single buyer's agreement to purchase services from one supplier instead of another, without horizontal concerted exclusion or another established per se basis, is not a per se group boycott merely because it intentionally injures a rival, serves a fraudulent purpose or lacks an apparent efficiency justification. The pleaded vertical purchasing arrangement must therefore be assessed under the rule of reason on the surviving §1 competitive-effects theory; genuine horizontal restraints, actual price fixing and other established categories retain their distinct requirements.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's unanimous opinion of the Court.

##### Regulatory fraud and harm to one supplier do not replace competitive injury

**Holding:** Regulatory deception, consumer overcharges and elimination of one supplier do not by themselves establish that the challenged purchasing agreement injured the competitive process; Discon must establish the competitive injury and other elements of the theory actually pursued. The lower courts must reconsider the surviving §1 effects claim and the derivative rationale for the distinct §2 conspiracy claim without treating the rejected per se classification or fraud alone as sufficient, while leaving previously dismissed claims outside this remand.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's unanimous opinion of the Court.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The District Court's wholesale dismissal is not automatically reinstated. The distinct §2 conspiracy requirements remain, while previously dismissed vertical-price-fixing, monopolization, attempt and RICO claims are not revived. The Court finds neither competitive injury nor harmlessness and grants neither immunity for fraud nor a final damages or injunction remedy.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Klor's, Inc. v. Broadway-Hale Stores, Inc.: distinguishes the coordinated exclusionary structure supporting its group-boycott rule from this vertical supplier substitution.
- Business Electronics Corp. v. Sharp Electronics Corp.: applies the distinction between the character of the agreement and an exclusionary label or purpose; actual price-agreement and horizontal questions retain their own requirements.
- Northwest Wholesale Stationers v. Pacific Stationery & Printing Co.: preserves the need for a restraint fitting an established category before automatic boycott condemnation.
- FTC v. Indiana Federation of Dentists: preserves attention to the restraint's actual character and competition rather than extending per se treatment from a label.
- State Oil Co. v. Khan (November 4, 1997): applies its category-based reason for competitive-effects review without changing its distinct vertical maximum-price holding or granting immunity to harmful restraints.
- Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat and Atlantic Richfield Co. v. USA Petroleum Co.: preserve the independent requirement of antitrust injury; competitor loss alone is insufficient.
- Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services: preserves inquiry into actual competitive conditions without finding market power or injury here.
- Spectrum Sports v. McQuillan: preserves offense-specific elements; attempted monopolization is not merged with conspiracy to monopolize.
