## *Correctional Services Corp. v. Malesko*

### Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** *Correctional Services Corp. v. Malesko*
- **Citation or Docket:** 534 U.S. 61 (2001), No. 00-860; judgment below, 229 F.3d 374 (2d Cir. 2000).
- **October Term:** 2001.
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Merits stage, simulated opinion delivery after full briefing and argument.
- **Simulated Event Date:** November 27, 2001.
- **Question Presented:** Whether a *Bivens* cause of action for damages should be implied against a private corporation acting under color of federal law.
- **Question Granted:** Whether a *Bivens* cause of action for damages should be implied against a private corporation acting under color of federal law.
- **Legal Field:** Federal courts; implied constitutional damages remedies; *Bivens*; private federal custodial contractors.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit affirmed dismissal of the claims against the individual defendants as untimely, reversed dismissal of the *Bivens* claim against Correctional Services Corporation, and remanded. Only the corporate-defendant ruling is under review.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** Correctional Services Corporation seeks reversal and reinstatement of dismissal of the corporate *Bivens* claim. Malesko seeks affirmance of the Second Circuit’s judgment and further proceedings on that claim.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Correctional Services Corporation operated Le Marquis Community Correctional Center under contract with the Federal Bureau of Prisons. Malesko alleged that, despite a medical exemption from the facility’s stair-use policy, a CSC employee denied him elevator access, after which he climbed the stairs toward his fifth-floor room, suffered a heart attack, fell, and was injured. The District Court dismissed the corporate claim under *FDIC v. Meyer* and dismissed the individual claims as time barred; the individual dismissals were not challenged in this Court. For purposes of the granted defendant-category question, CSC did not contest that the complaint alleged an Eighth Amendment violation, but no constitutional liability, causation, or damages finding has been made.

The claims against individual employees, the statute-of-limitations ruling, and any distinct cause of action against those employees are outside the grant. The Court need not decide the ultimate Eighth Amendment merits, the measure of damages, or any state-law claim.

- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None identified.
- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** Official U.S. Reports opinion and syllabus, 534 U.S. 61; Second Circuit judgment, 229 F.3d 374; the petition question reproduced in the official opinion.

Stone extends the existing *Carlson* damages action to a private corporation that contracts to perform the Federal Government’s custodial function and acts under color of federal law in that function. The defect in extending *Meyer* to CSC is that it would convert privatization into a defendant-form exception to an established constitutional remedy and permit the Government to narrow accountability by outsourcing custody. *Meyer* remains intact because a federal agency is sovereign-affiliated and direct agency liability implicates the federal Treasury and the distinct remedial architecture governing the United States; CSC is a private, for-profit corporation outside that category. The existence of state tort remedies does not displace the federal constitutional action absent a congressionally created substitute intended to do so, and *Carlson* already rejects treating an alternative tort route as automatically exclusive. Satisfying this defendant-category rule permits the claim to proceed; it does not establish the underlying violation or damages.
- **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** A private corporation acting under color of federal law as the Government’s custodial agent is a proper *Bivens* defendant for an Eighth Amendment claim within *Carlson*’s established federal-custody context. *FDIC v. Meyer*’s bar on direct federal-agency liability does not extend to such a private corporation.
- **Stone’s Reasoning and Record Application:** *Carlson* already recognizes the substantive constitutional damages action alleged here. CSC operated the federal halfway house under a Bureau of Prisons contract and, for the granted question, is treated as acting under color of federal law while performing custody that federal employees otherwise would perform. Unlike the agency in *Meyer*, CSC possesses no sovereign status, and the judgment would run against the private corporation rather than the United States. *Richardson* further confirms that private custodial actors do not acquire public-officer immunity simply from contractual performance of incarceration. Denying the corporate action would make the availability of an established federal remedy turn on the Government’s choice between public and private administration and would leave institutional incentives untouched when the responsible individual claim is unavailable. Because the Second Circuit correctly allowed the corporate claim to proceed without deciding liability, its judgment is affirmed.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding is confined to a private corporation performing delegated federal custody and to a constitutional damages context already recognized by *Carlson*. It does not authorize direct *Bivens* liability against a federal agency, create a damages action for every private federal contractor or every new constitutional context, decide the precise rule of corporate attribution, reopen the individual claims, or establish the Eighth Amendment violation, causation, defenses, or damages.

## *Dusenbery v. United States*

### Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** *Dusenbery v. United States*
- **Citation or Docket:** 534 U.S. 161 (2002), No. 00-6567; judgment below, 223 F.3d 422 (6th Cir. 2000).
- **October Term:** 2001.
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Merits stage, simulated opinion delivery after full briefing and argument.
- **Simulated Event Date:** January 8, 2002.
- **Question Presented:** Whether the FBI’s certified-mail notice to a known federal prisoner at the institution where he was confined was reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise him of the administrative forfeiture of his cash.
- **Question Granted:** Whether the FBI’s notice to Dusenbery of the intended administrative forfeiture of the cash satisfied the Fifth Amendment’s notice requirement.
- **Legal Field:** Fifth Amendment procedural due process; administrative forfeiture; notice to a person in federal custody.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for the United States in the civil equitable action challenging notice of the cash forfeiture, 223 F.3d 422.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** Dusenbery seeks reversal of the summary judgment and equitable relief restoring a lawful opportunity to contest the forfeiture. The United States seeks affirmance.
- **Material Facts and Record:** While Dusenbery was confined at FCI Milan, the FBI sent forfeiture notices by certified mail to him at the institution, to the residence where he had been arrested, and to an address in the town where his mother lived, and it published notice. A prison mailroom officer signed the certified receipt for the institutional letter and described a general process under which certified mail was logged, removed from the mailroom by an inmate’s Unit Team, and distributed during mail call. The surviving record contained no inmate acknowledgment, no delivery log, no testimony from the Unit Team or caseworker responsible for the mailroom-to-prisoner transfer, and no witness who knew whether the letter reached Dusenbery; the relevant logs had been destroyed under the then-applicable one-year retention policy.

The Court need not decide ownership of the cash, whether it was traceable to illegal drug activity, the automobile or other seized property, or whether due process invariably requires actual receipt. - **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None identified.
- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** Official U.S. Reports opinion and syllabus, 534 U.S. 161; Sixth Circuit judgment, 223 F.3d 422; the statutory notice provision then codified at 19 U.S.C. § 1607(a).

#### Historical Supreme Court comparator

- **Historical Outcome and Vote:** Affirmed, 5-4.
- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote the Court’s opinion, joined by Justices O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas. Justice Ginsburg dissented, joined by Justices Stevens, Souter, and Breyer.
- **Historical Holding:** The historical Court applied *Mullane* rather than the general *Mathews* framework and held that certified mail to the prison, combined with the institution’s described mail procedures, was reasonably calculated to notify Dusenbery; actual receipt was not required.

#### A. Law entering the case

##### Issue 1: Reliability of notice routed through Government custody

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** *City of West Covina v. Perkins*, Question 1, holds that timely notice identifying seized property and the responsible custodian need not also provide personalized instructions about a published, generally available recovery procedure; it expressly leaves open a challenge to the actual adequacy, accessibility, obstruction, or operation of the notice process. *United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property*, Question 1, confirms that property deprivation requires constitutionally adequate notice and an opportunity to be heard, subject to the distinct rule governing pre-seizure process for real property. Surviving *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.* requires a method reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise the interested person and permit objections. No controlling simulated holding resolves the internal-delivery step when the Government knows the recipient’s exact location and itself controls the custodial institution.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Standards and Tests, **Post-seizure notice and published recovery procedures**: notice identifying seized property and the responsible custodian need not include personalized instructions about an accessible published remedy, but the rule does not excuse an actually inadequate, obstructed, misleading, or inaccessible notice process. No reusable entry specifies the safeguards required for the mailroom-to-prisoner delivery step.
- **Other Governing Law:** The Fifth Amendment; 19 U.S.C. § 1607(a) as then in effect; *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*; *Mennonite Board of Missions v. Adams*; *Robinson v. Hanrahan*; *Houston v. Lack*.
- **Other Authority Used:** *Weng v. United States*, 137 F.3d 709 (2d Cir. 1998), and *United States v. One Toshiba Color Television*, 213 F.3d 147 (3d Cir. 2000) (en banc), are used only as persuasive, date-eligible authority concerning reliable delivery to a prisoner and the Government’s obligation to establish the procedure on which it relies. *United States v. #### B. Stone’s approved position

- **Stone’s Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse the judgment of the Sixth Circuit and remand.
- **Stone’s Remedy and Remand Instruction:** The District Court shall vacate summary judgment for the United States and enter judgment for Dusenbery on the constitutional adequacy of notice, then determine the equitable relief lawfully available to restore a meaningful opportunity to contest the cash forfeiture. Stone orders neither immediate return of the cash nor a decision on the forfeiture merits.

##### Issue 1: Reliability of notice routed through Government custody

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the preserved notice question. He does not require proof that Dusenbery actually received the letter and does not decide ownership, traceability, or the ultimate forfeiture. The undisputed summary-judgment record is sufficient to decide whether the procedure the Government established was constitutionally adequate.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** What must the Government establish when it sends forfeiture notice to a known person confined in an institution the Government controls and relies on institutional staff to carry the notice from the mailroom to the prisoner.
- **Stone’s Legal Position:** Stone applies *Mullane* differently to this record and clarifies, without creating an actual-receipt requirement, what the existing reasonableness standard demands in Government custody. When the Government knows the intended recipient’s precise location, controls that location, and controls the only practical internal channel by which the legal notice can reach him, it must establish a reliable procedure reasonably calculated to carry the notice from institutional receipt to the prisoner. The Government, as the party constitutionally obliged to give notice and the summary-judgment movant relying on its own process, bears that showing. A prisoner’s signature, documented refusal, witnessed delivery, or another comparably reliable safeguard can satisfy the requirement, but no single form is mandatory. Failure to establish a reliable internal-delivery procedure defeats the Government’s notice defense; it does not make actual receipt an element.
- **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** When the United States seeks to forfeit property belonging to a known person in its custody, certified delivery to the institution is not enough unless the Government establishes a reliable internal procedure reasonably calculated to carry the notice from the institution’s point of receipt to the prisoner. Actual receipt is not required, and no particular acknowledgment method is constitutionally mandatory. On this record, the Government did not establish an adequate mailroom-to-prisoner procedure.
- **Stone’s Reasoning and Record Application:** *Mullane* asks whether the chosen means are those a person genuinely desirous of informing the addressee would reasonably adopt. The FBI knew that Dusenbery was confined at FCI Milan, and the Bureau of Prisons controlled his location and his only practical access to incoming legal mail. The Government proved that its letter reached the prison mailroom and that staff ordinarily logged certified mail, but the proof stopped before the legally decisive transfer to Dusenbery. The only witness worked in the mailroom, did not know the Unit Team’s actual delivery practices, could not say the notice was delivered, and identified no acknowledgment or retained record from the prisoner-facing stage. No responsible Unit Team member or caseworker supplied the missing proof. The extra mailings to the arrest residence and an address associated with his mother, and publication, do not cure that failure because the Government knew his actual controlled location and established no basis for treating those other channels as reliably calculated to reach him. A modest acknowledgment or equivalent delivery record was feasible and would materially improve reliability without demanding actual receipt in every case. The Government therefore failed to show constitutionally adequate notice, so the Sixth Circuit’s affirmance of summary judgment must be reversed.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding creates no universal actual-receipt rule, no signature-only rule, and no general service code for persons outside Government custody. A different internal process may suffice if the Government establishes its reliability.
