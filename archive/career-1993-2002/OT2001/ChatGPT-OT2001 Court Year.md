### *Correctional Services Corp. v. Malesko*

Argued October 1, 2001 | Decided November 27, 2001  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit; judgment below: dismissal of the claim against Correctional Services Corporation reversed and the case remanded, 229 F.3d 374 (2d Cir. 2000). ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/534/61/))  
Area of Law: Federal courts; *Bivens*; private federal custody

Questions Presented:

1. Whether a *Bivens* cause of action for damages should be implied against a private corporation acting under color of federal law.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 5-4.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports affirmance | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would reverse | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would reverse | Would confine *Bivens*, *Davis*, and *Carlson* to their established defendant categories and factual settings |

#### Justice Stone's Position

Vote: to affirm.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court's opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Private corporate defendant in an established federal-custody damages context

Holding and operative rule: A private corporation acting under color of federal law as the Federal Government's custodial agent is a proper *Bivens* defendant for an Eighth Amendment claim within the federal-custody context already recognized by *Carlson v. Green*. The direct federal-agency bar established by *FDIC v. Meyer*, decided February 23, 1994, does not extend to such a private corporation.

Reasoning: *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, already recognizes a federal damages action for an Eighth Amendment violation committed in federal custody. Correctional Services Corporation contracted with the Bureau of Prisons to perform that custodial function and, for the granted question, acted under color of federal law. Unlike the agency in *FDIC v. Meyer*, decided February 23, 1994, CSC possesses no sovereign status, and a judgment against it does not operate as direct liability against the United States or the federal Treasury. Extending the agency bar to CSC would permit the Government to narrow an established constitutional remedy by changing the organizational form through which custody is administered. State tort actions and administrative grievances remain available where their requirements are met, but Congress has not made either route an exclusive substitute for the federal constitutional action. The Second Circuit therefore correctly allowed the corporate claim to proceed. Constitutional liability, corporate attribution, causation, defenses, and damages remain unresolved. ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/534/61/))

#### Concurrences

No concurrences filed.

#### Dissents

O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas: *Bivens* is directed principally at deterring individual federal officers, and the availability of state tort remedies, administrative review, and prospective relief counsels against creating corporate liability. She would extend the caution governing new implied remedies to the private corporate defendant and reverse.

Scalia, joined by Thomas: Even when the rationale of an existing *Bivens* decision might logically reach a new defendant, the judicially implied action should not be enlarged. He would confine *Bivens*, *Davis*, and *Carlson* to the precise settings already recognized.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Carlson v. Green*: extended; the established federal-custody Eighth Amendment damages action reaches a private corporation performing delegated federal custody under color of federal law.

*FDIC v. Meyer*, decided February 23, 1994: distinguished; the prohibition on direct federal-agency liability does not create immunity for a private corporate federal custodian.

*Richardson v. McKnight*, decided June 23, 1997: followed; contractual performance of a custodial function does not confer sovereign or public-officer status on a competitive, for-profit private actor.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit is affirmed, and the case is remanded for ordinary proceedings on the claim against Correctional Services Corporation without reopening the time-barred claims against the individual defendants.

Effect: Malesko may attempt to prove the alleged Eighth Amendment violation, corporate attribution, causation, and damages against CSC. Private corporations performing delegated federal custody under color of federal law are subject to the same defendant-category rule when the constitutional damages context is already established by *Carlson*.

### *United States v. Knights*

Argued November 6, 2001 | Decided December 10, 2001  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; judgment below: suppression of evidence affirmed, 219 F.3d 1138 (9th Cir. 2000). ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/534/112/))  
Area of Law: Fourth Amendment; probation searches

Questions Presented:

1. Is a warrantless police search reasonable when it is authorized by a valid probation condition, supported by reasonable suspicion, and undertaken to investigate a new crime rather than for a distinct probationary purpose?

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9-0.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: No Justice.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence | Souter | No other Justice | Supports reversal and remand | Reserves broader application of the subjective-purpose rule to reasonable-suspicion searches not otherwise justified by the circumstances present here |

#### Justice Stone's Position

Vote: to reverse and remand.  
Public role: Court author.  
Announced ground: a search conducted within a valid probation condition and supported by objective reasonable suspicion is reasonable under the Fourth Amendment, and the officer's additional purpose of investigating a new crime does not create an independent constitutional defect.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Investigatory search under a probation condition

Holding and operative rule: A warrantless search conducted within a valid probation condition and supported by objective reasonable suspicion is not unreasonable merely because its immediate purpose is investigation of a new crime.

Reasoning: Knights had advance notice of a written condition authorizing probation and law-enforcement officers to search his apartment. That condition materially reduced his reasonable expectation of privacy, while his status as a probationer strengthened the Government's legitimate interest in detecting renewed criminal conduct and protecting potential victims. The detective possessed individualized suspicion based on the contents of the truck associated with Knights, the removed padlock, surveillance observations, and the pattern of arson and vandalism. Those circumstances made reasonable suspicion sufficient and rendered a warrant unnecessary for the search actually performed. *Whren v. United States*, decided June 12, 1996, confirms that an objectively justified law-enforcement action does not become unreasonable merely because an officer also seeks evidence of another offense. *Griffin v. Wisconsin*, 483 U.S. 868, sustained one kind of probation search under the special-needs doctrine; it did not make that route exclusive. Consent, suspicionless authority, harassment, third-party premises, and searches outside the probation condition are not decided. ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/534/112/))

#### Concurrences

Souter: The valid condition, the probationer's notice, and individualized suspicion are sufficient to decide the case. He reserves whether *Whren*'s treatment of subjective intention should extend without qualification to other searches resting only on reasonable suspicion.

#### Dissents

No dissents filed.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Whren v. United States*, decided June 12, 1996: applied; an otherwise reasonable probation search does not become invalid because the officer's immediate purpose is ordinary criminal investigation.

*Griffin v. Wisconsin*: distinguished; the special-needs basis sustaining a probation search does not foreclose ordinary Fourth Amendment reasonableness analysis.

Probation searches under a disclosed condition: established; a search within the condition and supported by reasonable suspicion is reasonable without a warrant on the circumstances presented.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit is reversed, and the case is remanded for proceedings in which the evidence may not be suppressed on the ground that the search served an ordinary criminal-investigation purpose.

Effect: The Government may use the evidence recovered from Knights's apartment, subject to any remaining issue not foreclosed by the Court's holding. The decision authorizes no suspicionless, arbitrary, harassing, undisclosed, out-of-scope, or third-party search.

### *Dusenbery v. United States*

Argued October 29, 2001 | Decided January 8, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit; judgment below: summary judgment for the United States affirmed, 223 F.3d 422 (6th Cir. 2000). ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/534/161/))  
Area of Law: Due process; administrative forfeiture; notice

Questions Presented:

1. Whether the FBI's notice to Dusenbery of the intended administrative forfeiture of the cash satisfied the Fifth Amendment's notice requirement.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 5-4.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone, Stevens, Souter, Breyer | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would affirm | Complete opinion |

#### Justice Stone's Position

Vote: to reverse and remand.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court's opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Internal delivery of notice to a known federal prisoner

Holding and operative rule: When the United States seeks to forfeit property belonging to a known person in its custody, certified delivery to the institution is insufficient unless the Government establishes a reliable internal procedure reasonably calculated to carry the notice from the institution's point of receipt to the prisoner. Actual receipt and any particular form of acknowledgment are not constitutionally mandatory.

Reasoning: *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*, 339 U.S. 306, requires means that a person genuinely desirous of informing the interested party would reasonably adopt under all the circumstances. The FBI knew Dusenbery's exact location, and the Bureau of Prisons controlled both that location and the only practical channel by which the forfeiture notice could reach him. The Government established that the certified letter arrived at the prison mailroom and was entered in a log, but its proof ended before the prisoner-facing stage. The mailroom witness did not know the Unit Team's actual delivery practices, no Unit Team member or caseworker testified, and no acknowledgment, refusal record, or delivery log established a reliable transfer from staff to Dusenbery. The additional mailings and publication did not cure that defect because the Government knew where Dusenbery was confined. A signature, documented refusal, witnessed delivery, or another comparably reliable procedure could satisfy due process, but the Constitution requires no single method and no proof of actual receipt. ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/534/161/))

#### Concurrences

No concurrences filed.

#### Dissents

O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas: Certified mail to the prison, together with evidence of the institution's ordinary mail-distribution process, was reasonably calculated to provide notice under *Mullane*. Due process does not require a recipient signature, a retained delivery record, or proof that every internal step succeeded.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*: clarified; when Government controls the known recipient's confinement and internal mail channel, it must establish a reliable procedure extending through the prisoner-facing delivery stage.

*City of West Covina v. Perkins*, decided January 13, 1999: distinguished; identification of property and the responsible custodian does not excuse an actually unestablished or unreliable delivery process.

*United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property*, decided December 13, 1993: followed; constitutionally adequate notice and a meaningful opportunity to contest remain prerequisites to a property deprivation.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to vacate summary judgment for the United States, enter judgment for Dusenbery on the adequacy of notice, and determine the equitable relief lawfully available to restore a meaningful opportunity to contest the cash forfeiture.

Effect: Dusenbery is entitled to a lawful opportunity to challenge the forfeiture, not an immediate adjudication of ownership, traceability, or entitlement to return of the cash. The Government may use a different internal-delivery procedure in future custodial cases if it establishes that procedure's reliability.

### *Toyota Motor Manufacturing, Kentucky, Inc. v. Williams*

Argued November 7, 2001 | Decided January 8, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit; judgment below: summary judgment for Toyota reversed and partial summary judgment entered for Williams on disability, 224 F.3d 840 (6th Cir. 2000). ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/534/184/))  
Area of Law: Americans with Disabilities Act; disability definition

Questions Presented:

1. How should a court determine whether an impairment substantially limits the major life activity of performing manual tasks?

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9-0.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: No Justice.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stone | No other Justice | Concurs in the judgment | Individualized, activity-wide assessment without adopting categorical severe-restriction or permanent-impact formulations |

#### Justice Stone's Position

Vote: to reverse and remand.  
Public role: concurrence in the judgment.  
Announced ground: the Sixth Circuit improperly defined the major life activity through selected duties of one assembly-line job. Stone would require an individualized assessment of manual activity as a whole in the claimant's actual circumstances, treating both work and nonwork evidence as relevant without making a universal severe-restriction or permanence formula controlling.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Substantial limitation in performing manual tasks

Holding and operative rule: To be substantially limited in performing manual tasks, an impairment must prevent or severely restrict activities of central importance to most people's daily lives, and the impairment's impact must be permanent or long term. Inability to perform selected duties of one specialized job does not by itself establish disability. (joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer)

Reasoning: The statutory terms substantial and major require more than proof that an employee cannot perform a particular set of workplace assignments. The relevant major life activity is manual activity at an appropriately general level, and the inquiry asks how the claimant actually performs tasks central to ordinary daily life. Williams presented evidence of restrictions involving gripping, overhead work, tool use, and repetitive assembly duties, but the Sixth Circuit treated the range of manual tasks associated with Toyota's assembly line as sufficient and discounted evidence concerning personal care, household activity, and other daily functions. That approach improperly converted the requirements of one employer's production process into the statutory major life activity. *Bragdon v. Abbott*, decided June 25, 1998, and *Sutton v. United Air Lines*, decided June 22, 1999, require an individualized evaluation rather than a diagnosis-based or job-specific conclusion. The judgment granting Williams partial summary judgment must therefore be reversed. Her ultimate disability, qualification, essential job functions, and requested accommodation remain open. ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/534/184/))

#### Concurrences

Stone: The Sixth Circuit erred because selected assembly-line duties cannot define manual activity as a whole. He would remand under that individualized, activity-wide principle without making severe restriction or permanent or long-term impact a universal formulation for every manual-task claim.

#### Dissents

No dissents filed.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Bragdon v. Abbott*, decided June 25, 1998: clarified; the claimant must identify and prove substantial limitation in the major life activity itself rather than in a task grouping defined by one employer.

*Sutton v. United Air Lines*, decided June 22, 1999: extended; the occupational-breadth principle now also prevents selected duties of one specialized job from defining the major life activity of performing manual tasks.

ADA manual-task disability: established; the Court's eight-Justice rule requires severe restriction of activities central to most people's daily lives and permanent or long-term impact.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit is reversed, and the case is remanded for reassessment of Williams's claimed disability under the Court's activity-wide standard.

Effect: Williams's partial summary judgment on disability is vacated. The lower courts must evaluate her actual manual functioning beyond the selected assembly-line duties and separately address any remaining qualification, essential-function, and accommodation questions.

### *Great-West Life & Annuity Insurance Co. v. Knudson*

Argued October 1, 2001 | Decided January 8, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; judgment below: dismissal of the reimbursement action affirmed, 208 F.3d 221 (9th Cir. 2000). ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/534/204/))  
Area of Law: ERISA; equitable relief

Questions Presented:

1. Does ERISA § 502(a)(3) authorize a fiduciary to obtain a personal reimbursement judgment when the identified settlement proceeds are not in the beneficiary's possession?

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 5-4.  
Supporting: Stone, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas. Opposing: Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stone, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas | Supports affirmance | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Stevens | No other Justice | Would reverse | Joins Ginsburg's dissent and adds a separate statutory observation |
| Dissent | Ginsburg | Stevens, Souter, Breyer | Would reverse | Complete opinion |

#### Justice Stone's Position

Vote: to affirm.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court's opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Personal reimbursement from general assets

Holding and operative rule: Section 502(a)(3) permits recovery of specifically identifiable property through a recognized equitable lien, constructive trust, or comparable equitable remedy, but it does not authorize an ordinary personal reimbursement judgment payable from a defendant's general assets.

Reasoning: Section 502(a)(3) authorizes appropriate equitable relief, which refers to categories of relief typically available in equity rather than every remedy that enforces an equitable-sounding obligation. Equitable restitution ordinarily acts upon particular property or identifiable proceeds in the defendant's possession. Great-West did not seek the return of a fund held by the Knudsons. Most settlement proceeds had been directed to a special-needs trust or used for fees and expenses, and Great-West sought to impose personal liability for the reimbursement amount from general assets. That is the substance of a legal action to enforce a contractual obligation to pay money. *Mertens v. Hewitt Associates*, 508 U.S. 248, therefore controls the statutory classification. *Harris Trust and Savings Bank v. Salomon Smith Barney Inc.*, decided June 12, 2000, involved historically equitable recovery against a transferee possessing plan property and does not create a general damages remedy. Claims against the trust or another holder, an already attached lien, a plan offset, and relief under another ERISA provision are not decided. ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/534/204/))

#### Concurrences

No concurrences filed.

#### Dissents

Stevens: He joins Ginsburg's account of the historically equitable character of the requested enforcement and finds it difficult to attribute to Congress a remedial gap that leaves an acknowledged plan obligation without an effective federal remedy.

Ginsburg, joined by Stevens, Souter, and Breyer: The action seeks enforcement of an equitable reimbursement obligation within a statute authorizing appropriate equitable relief, and the historical division between law and equity should not be applied to defeat that relief. She would reverse and permit the claim to proceed subject to the plan terms and equitable defenses.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Mertens v. Hewitt Associates*: followed; § 502(a)(3) does not authorize a personal money judgment merely because the underlying obligation is equitable in character.

*Harris Trust and Savings Bank v. Salomon Smith Barney Inc.*, decided June 12, 2000: distinguished; traced recovery from a transferee possessing plan property differs from personal liability payable from general assets.

Individualized equitable relief under § 502(a)(3): clarified; the requirements of the particular equitable remedy, including identification and tracing of property where applicable, remain indispensable.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit is affirmed.

Effect: The dismissal of Great-West's claim for personal reimbursement from the Knudsons' general assets remains in force. The judgment does not resolve a claim against the special-needs trust or another holder of identifiable proceeds, an existing lien, a plan offset, or relief authorized by another statutory provision.

### *Kelly v. South Carolina*

Argued November 26, 2001 | Decided January 9, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of South Carolina; judgment below: conviction and death sentence affirmed, 343 S.C. 350, 540 S.E.2d 851 (2001). ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/534/246/))  
Area of Law: Capital sentencing; due process; parole ineligibility

Questions Presented:

1. Did due process require an effective opportunity to tell the jury that Kelly was ineligible for parole when the State portrayed him as a continuing danger and life without parole was the only noncapital sentence?

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 6-3.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Kennedy | No other Justice | Would affirm | Would treat the State's presentation as character and prison-conduct evidence rather than an argument of future dangerousness within *Simmons* |
| Dissent | Thomas | Scalia | Would affirm | Rejects the constitutional parole-information rule and its application |

#### Justice Stone's Position

Vote: to reverse the death sentence and remand while leaving the conviction undisturbed.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court's opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Future dangerousness and parole ineligibility

Holding and operative rule: When the State's evidence and argument materially invite a capital jury to act on the defendant's continuing danger, and life without parole is the sole noncapital option available to that jury, due process requires an effective and accurate means of communicating parole ineligibility.

Reasoning: *Simmons v. South Carolina*, decided June 17, 1994, prevents the State from asking a jury to choose death because of feared future violence while withholding the legally fixed fact necessary to assess the alternative sentence. *Shafer v. South Carolina*, decided March 20, 2001, establishes that the relevant sentencing alternatives are those before the jury at the actual penalty-selection stage. Kelly's jury could choose only death or life without parole. South Carolina introduced evidence of violent custodial conduct, elicited testimony concerning Kelly's continuing desire to kill, and described him in closing argument as dangerous, bloody, and a butcher. The practical thrust of that presentation was not confined to the historical murder; it invited the jury to act on continuing danger. Due process does not turn on the prosecutor's use of the phrase future dangerousness, nor does it require a prior jury question. Because the trial court denied any effective and accurate means of conveying parole ineligibility, the death sentence cannot stand. The conviction is not implicated. ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/534/246/))

### *Porter v. Nussle*

Argued January 14, 2002 | Decided February 26, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit; judgment below: dismissal reversed and the excessive-force action allowed to proceed without administrative exhaustion, 224 F.3d 95 (2d Cir. 2000). ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep534/usrep534516/usrep534516.pdf))  
Area of Law: Prison Litigation Reform Act; administrative exhaustion; prisoner civil rights

Questions Presented:

1. Does 42 U.S.C. § 1997e(a) apply to a prisoner's federal claim arising from a single episode of allegedly excessive force?

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9-0.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: No Justice.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |

#### Justice Stone's Position

Vote: to reverse and remand.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court's opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Exhaustion for a particular episode of prison life

Holding and operative rule: Section 1997e(a) applies to every federal action brought by a prisoner about prison life, whether the action challenges general conditions or a particular episode, including an alleged use of excessive force. Exhaustion extends only to administrative remedies that were genuinely available in practical operation.

Reasoning: The enacted phrase actions with respect to prison conditions is not confined to policies affecting the prison population as a whole. An officer's alleged assault occurs within prison life, and an episode-versus-condition distinction would make coverage depend on pleading choices rather than the statutory subject. Administrative review can give correctional officials prompt notice, permit investigation and corrective action, identify staff misconduct, and create a record even when the process cannot award the full judicial remedy. *Booth v. Churner*, decided May 29, 2001, already establishes that remedial inadequacy does not excuse exhaustion, while preserving the separate requirement that the process be genuinely available. The Court therefore resolves statutory coverage but leaves for remand whether Connecticut supplied an accessible process, whether officials obstructed it, and whether Nussle timely and properly completed any process that was available. The Eighth Amendment merits are not reached. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep534/usrep534516/usrep534516.pdf))

#### Concurrences

No concurrences filed.

#### Dissents

No dissents filed.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Booth v. Churner*, decided May 29, 2001: clarified; the exhaustion command reaches all federal actions about prison life, including a particular alleged use of excessive force, while retaining the genuine-availability boundary.

42 U.S.C. § 1997e(a): established; coverage does not turn on whether the complaint describes a general condition or an isolated episode.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to apply § 1997e(a) and determine whether an administrative remedy was genuinely available and properly exhausted.

Effect: Nussle's excessive-force claim is not adjudicated on the merits. If a genuinely available process remained unexhausted, the action may not proceed on the present record; if the process was unavailable or properly completed, ordinary proceedings may continue.

### *Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N.A.*

Argued January 15, 2002 | Decided February 26, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit; judgment below: dismissal of the discrimination complaint affirmed, 5 Fed. Appx. 63 (2d Cir. 2001). ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep534/usrep534506/usrep534506.pdf))  
Area of Law: Federal civil procedure; Rule 8 pleading; employment discrimination

Questions Presented:

1. Must an employment-discrimination complaint plead facts establishing every element of the *McDonnell Douglas* prima facie case?

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9-0.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: No Justice.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |

#### Justice Stone's Position

Vote: to reverse and remand.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court's opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Evidentiary framework and Rule 8 pleading

Holding and operative rule: An employment-discrimination plaintiff need not plead facts establishing every element of the *McDonnell Douglas* prima facie case. A complaint governed by Rule 8 is sufficient when its factual allegations provide fair notice of the claim and the grounds on which it rests.

Reasoning: *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), allocates evidentiary burdens when discrimination is proved indirectly; it is not a pleading code, and its precise formulation varies with the factual setting. Rule 8 requires a short and plain statement, while Rule 9 identifies matters for which particularity is required. A court may not create another heightened category by transposing an evidentiary presumption into the complaint. Swierkiewicz identified the asserted age and national-origin discrimination, the responsible supervisor, his demotion and discharge, and the younger French employee who assumed his responsibilities. Those allegations gave Sorema fair notice and stated claims capable of relief. The Court does not treat that factual detail as a mandatory checklist for every discrimination complaint, protect a pleading made only of labels, alter the ultimate burden of proof, or guarantee unrestricted discovery. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep534/usrep534506/usrep534506.pdf))

#### Concurrences

No concurrences filed.

#### Dissents

No dissents filed.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973): clarified; its prima facie framework governs evidentiary proof rather than Rule 8 pleading.

*Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.*, decided June 12, 2000: followed; the evidentiary allocation remains flexible and must not be converted into a rigid substantive formula.

*Leatherman v. Tarrant County Narcotics Intelligence and Coordination Unit*, 507 U.S. 163 (1993): extended; absent a governing rule or statute, federal courts may not impose a claim-specific heightened pleading standard.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to reinstate the complaint and conduct ordinary proceedings under the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure.

Effect: Swierkiewicz may proceed beyond Rule 12. The judgment establishes no discrimination, discovery entitlement, qualification finding, or ultimate liability.

### *S.S. ex rel. Jervis v. McMullen*

Argued November 7, 2001 | Decided March 26, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit; judgment below: dismissal of the § 1983 substantive-due-process complaint affirmed en banc, 225 F.3d 960 (8th Cir. 2000). ([Justia Law](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/225/960/499756/))  
Area of Law: Fourteenth Amendment; child welfare; substantive due process

Questions Presented:

1. Whether the alleged affirmative return of a child from state custody to a known, specific danger states a substantive-due-process claim and, if so, whether the responsible officials are entitled to qualified immunity at the pleading stage.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 7-2.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: Scalia, Thomas.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would affirm | Complete opinion |

#### Justice Stone's Position

Vote: to reverse and remand.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court's opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Affirmative return of a child to a known grave danger

Holding and operative rule: Child-welfare officials violate substantive due process when, with time to deliberate, they affirmatively use state custody or placement authority to return a child into a known, specific, serious danger, materially increase the child's vulnerability through that placement, and act with deliberate indifference that causes the resulting harm. Promise and reliance are not required when the claim rests on the direct use of placement authority rather than an assurance that induced private forbearance.

Reasoning: *DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services*, 489 U.S. 189 (1989), creates no general constitutional duty to protect a child from private violence and does not constitutionalize an unsuccessful protective judgment. The complaint here alleges a different use of governmental power. Missouri held S.S. in legal custody for more than two years; the responsible officials allegedly knew that her father repeatedly permitted access by an identified convicted child molester, received professional and hotline warnings, and learned that the offender personally objected to limits on his contact. The officials nevertheless allegedly used their placement authority to request and obtain the legal return, after which the identified offender assaulted S.S. On a Rule 12 motion, those allegations describe an affirmative transfer that could materially increase exposure to a specific danger after ample deliberation, not merely a passive failure to protect. *Pinder v. Johnson*, decided May 21, 1996, *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, decided May 26, 1998, and *City of Florence v. Chipman*, decided June 1, 1998, require deliberate indifference in this deliberative setting and preserve defendant-specific knowledge, action, causation, and culpability. ([Justia Law](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/225/960/499756/))

##### Question 2: Qualified immunity at the pleading stage

Holding and operative rule: An official is not entitled to qualified immunity merely because no earlier decision reproduced the precise family-placement facts. Immunity must be assessed separately for each defendant from the complete body of governing law, the plaintiff-supported allegations concerning knowledge and authority, personal participation, causation, and the obviousness of the alleged danger.

Reasoning: *Elder v. Holloway*, decided February 23, 1994, and the review method clarified by *Saucier v. Katz*, decided June 18, 2001, reject a categorical demand for a materially identical precedent, but fair notice must be measured from law existing when the officials acted. By the August 22, 1996 custody return, *DeShaney* had distinguished passive nonprotection from affirmative creation or intensification of vulnerability; *Pinder v. Johnson*, decided May 21, 1996, had recognized liability for deliberate affirmative use of governmental authority that materially increases a specific danger; and *K.H. ex rel. Murphy v. Morgan*, 914 F.2d 846 (7th Cir. 1990), and *Gregory v. City of Rogers*, 974 F.2d 1006 (8th Cir. 1992), supplied additional placement-specific and state-danger notice. *City of Florence v. Chipman*, decided June 1, 1998, confirms the governing direct-exposure rule on the merits but does not contribute to the 1996 fair-notice inquiry. The Eighth Circuit therefore could not grant categorical immunity solely by treating every parental return as constitutional inaction. The Court does not decide immunity finally for McMullen, Jacoby, or Barnett because their respective knowledge, placement authority, participation, causation, and the complete date-eligible notice record remain to be tested individually. ([Justia Law](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/225/960/499756/))

#### Concurrences

No concurrences filed.

#### Dissents

Scalia, joined by Thomas: *DeShaney* controls a State's return of a child to a parent, and the complaint alleges at most a gravely mistaken reunification decision rather than a constitutional use of force. They would also find no clearly established federal rule that placed the asserted custody-return theory beyond debate.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services*, 489 U.S. 189 (1989): distinguished; a return from state custody is not invariably equivalent to inaction when officials allegedly use placement authority to intensify a newly identified, specific danger.

*Pinder v. Johnson*, decided May 21, 1996: clarified; inducement and reliance remain elements of the promise route but are not universal elements of direct affirmative placement.

*City of Florence v. Chipman*, decided June 1, 1998: extended; the direct forced-exposure rule reaches an affirmative child-placement decision satisfying knowledge, material intensification, deliberate indifference, and causation.

*County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, decided May 26, 1998: applied; deliberate indifference may satisfy substantive-due-process culpability when officials have genuine time to investigate and deliberate.

*Elder v. Holloway*, decided February 23, 1994, and *Saucier v. Katz*, decided June 18, 2001: followed; qualified immunity uses the complete body of law and does not categorically require a fact-twin case.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to vacate the Rule 12 dismissal and conduct defendant-specific proceedings on knowledge, affirmative placement authority, personal participation, culpability, causation, and qualified immunity.

Effect: S.S. has stated a substantive-due-process claim on the pleaded facts, but no defendant has been found liable and no damages have been awarded. Negligence, poor investigation, and an unsuccessful reunification decision remain insufficient without the controlling elements.

Argued November 27, 2001 | Decided March 27, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Ohio; judgment below: judgment permitting a nonemergency judicial medication route affirmed, 90 Ohio St. 3d 176, 736 N.E.2d 10 (2000). ([FindLaw](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/oh-supreme-court/1389605.html))  
Area of Law: Fourteenth Amendment; involuntary medication; civil commitment

Questions Presented:

1. Whether due process permits emergency antipsychotic medication on current medical findings and nonemergency medication of an incapacitated civil patient without a present-danger finding, and what findings, proof, decisionmaker, and review are constitutionally required.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 9-0.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: No Justice.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports affirmance | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | Thomas | Concurs in the judgment | Would affirm without making prior judicial authorization, clear-and-convincing proof, and periodic judicial review universal federal requirements |

#### Justice Stone's Position

Vote: to affirm.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court's opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Temporary emergency medication

Holding and operative rule: A qualified physician may authorize temporary antipsychotic medication without prior judicial approval only on current, individualized findings of imminent serious harm, medical appropriateness, and the absence of an effective less intrusive means. Emergency authority ends when the emergency ends, and continued or recurrent medication requires prompt neutral review. (joined by Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer)

Reasoning: A civilly committed patient retains a substantial liberty interest in avoiding unwanted antipsychotic medication, while the State retains responsibility to prevent imminent serious harm and furnish medically appropriate care. A genuine emergency can make prior judicial process impracticable, but it does not erase the need for a current patient-specific medical basis or convert temporary intervention into an ordinary treatment path. *Washington v. Harper*, 494 U.S. 210 (1990), and *United States v. Weston*, decided June 18, 2001, require the State to identify the actual rationale, establish medical appropriateness, and consider effective less intrusive alternatives. Once imminent danger no longer exists, the emergency justification ends. Any continuation or repeated use must therefore move through a neutral process capable of testing whether the asserted emergency, need, and medical fit remain.

##### Question 2: Nonemergency medication of an incapacitated civil patient

Holding and operative rule: Outside an emergency, prior judicial authorization is required. The State must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the patient lacks capacity to give or withhold informed consent, the proposed medication is medically appropriate and its expected benefits outweigh its risks and side effects, and no equally effective less intrusive treatment exists; the patient must receive counsel or equivalent assistance, notice, an opportunity to present and test evidence, and periodic neutral review. (joined by Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer)

Reasoning: Involuntary commitment does not itself establish treatment-decision incapacity, and a capable patient retains the right to refuse even a choice others regard as unwise. When capacity is absent, the State's parens-patriae interest can support patient-centered treatment without a separate present-danger finding, but the severity, cognitive effects, and possible side effects of antipsychotic medication require a neutral adjudication and heightened proof before the intrusion. The Ohio procedure places incapacity, benefit relative to risk, and the absence of an equally effective less intrusive treatment before a court; it provides representation, an independent examination, notice, participation, presentation and cross-examination of witnesses, and later review supported by competent medical evidence. Those protections satisfy the federal floor. The Court neither authorizes indefinite medication nor decides a competence-restoration rationale, and medication authority ends if capacity returns or a required predicate ceases. ([FindLaw](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/oh-supreme-court/1389605.html))

#### Concurrences

Scalia, joined by Thomas: Ohio's judgment should be affirmed because due process permits medically appropriate treatment under a neutral professional process for an involuntarily committed patient. They would not constitutionalize prior judicial authorization, clear-and-convincing proof, or periodic judicial review as universal federal requirements.

#### Dissents

No dissents filed.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Washington v. Harper*, 494 U.S. 210 (1990): clarified; emergency dangerousness may support temporary medication, but emergency authority ends with the current danger and does not establish a continuing nonemergency route.

*Riggins v. Nevada*, 504 U.S. 127 (1992): followed; necessity, medical appropriateness, side effects, and less intrusive alternatives remain material to involuntary medication.

*United States v. Weston*, decided June 18, 2001: extended; its rationale-specific and individualized-medical-need requirements now govern emergency and nonemergency medication of an involuntarily committed civil patient.

*Addington v. Texas*, 441 U.S. 418 (1979): applied; clear-and-convincing proof governs the nonemergency incapacity and treatment predicates stated by the Court.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Supreme Court of Ohio is affirmed under the federal constitutional construction stated by this Court.

Effect: Ohio may use the emergency and nonemergency routes sustained here, but no order authorizes indefinite medication of Steele. Emergency authority ends with the emergency, and any continuing order must rest on current judicial findings, meaningful participation, heightened proof, and periodic neutral review.

Holding and operative rule: The Commissioner may require both the qualifying impairment and the resulting inability to engage in substantial gainful activity to last, or be expected to last, at least twelve months. A claimant may apply before twelve months have elapsed and establish the required expected duration.

Reasoning: The Act defines disability through an inability to work by reason of a medically determinable impairment and then attaches the twelve-month language to the impairment. That syntax does not unambiguously prohibit treating the impairment-caused inability as an integrated disability state subject to the same duration requirement. The Commissioner's notice-and-comment regulation was issued through delegated force-of-law authority and reasonably reconciles the linked impairment and inability terms while supplying an administrable duration rule. *Wisconsin Department of Health and Family Services v. Blumer*, decided February 20, 2002, and *United States v. Mead Corp.*, decided June 18, 2001, require the Court to enforce any fixed statutory boundary, confirm the force-of-law exercise, and then accept a reasonable construction within genuine ambiguity. Walton returned to substantial gainful activity before twelve months elapsed, so the Fourth Circuit could not reject the regulation merely because the impairment itself continued. The rule does not require a claimant to wait twelve months before filing or prevent proof based on expected duration. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep535/usrep535212/usrep535212.pdf))

#### Concurrences

Scalia: The notice-and-comment regulation itself carries delegated legal force and reasonably resolves the ambiguity, which is sufficient. He would not assign additional weight to the longevity of earlier informal agency materials or to congressional reenactment without first establishing that those materials satisfy the force-of-law gateway. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep535/usrep535212/usrep535212.pdf))

#### Dissents

No dissents filed.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*United States v. Mead Corp.*, decided June 18, 2001: followed; notice-and-comment regulations issued through delegated authority receive controlling weight within genuine statutory ambiguity.

*Wisconsin Department of Health and Family Services v. Blumer*, decided February 20, 2002: followed; formal legal force and informal persuasive weight remain distinct.

*Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 (1984): applied; the Commissioner's integrated duration construction is reasonable within the statutory ambiguity.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to apply the valid duration regulation and conduct any remaining proceedings consistent with it.

Effect: Walton may not obtain benefits under the Fourth Circuit's rejected impairment-only duration theory. Claimants remain free to apply before twelve months and prove that both the impairment and resulting inability are expected to satisfy the statutory period.

### *Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition*

Argued October 30, 2001 | Decided April 16, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; judgment below: the CPPA's appears-to-be and conveys-the-impression provisions held facially overbroad and unconstitutional, 198 F.3d 1083 (9th Cir. 1999). ([Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/00-795.ZS.html))  
Area of Law: First Amendment; child pornography; overbreadth

Questions Presented:

1. May the Federal Government prohibit sexually explicit images that appear to depict minors when no real child was used in their production?

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 7-2.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: O'Connor, Scalia.

#### Justice Stone's Position

Vote: to affirm both judgment components.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court's opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Images that appear to depict minors without actual-child exploitation

Holding and operative rule: A nonobscene image falls within *New York v. Ferber*'s categorical child-pornography rule only when its production exploits an actual minor. Realistic appearance alone does not remove protected expression from the First Amendment, and § 2256(8)(B) is substantially overbroad. (joined by Stone, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer)

Reasoning: *Miller v. California*, 413 U.S. 15 (1973), permits regulation of obscenity under its work-as-a-whole requirements. *New York v. Ferber*, 458 U.S. 747 (1982), permits categorical regulation of actual-child pornography because production abuses a child, circulation preserves and compounds that abuse, and the distribution market is intrinsically linked to the underlying crime. Virtual images and performances by adults record no such crime and create no victim through production. The Government's asserted concerns that such material may encourage later offenses, assist grooming, or make proof of an actual child's use more difficult are contingent and do not justify suppressing a substantial body of nonobscene expression. The existing affirmative defense also excludes possession and wholly computer-generated images, leaving protected speech exposed. The First Amendment does not permit the Government to prohibit lawful expression merely to ease prosecution of unlawful expression. ([Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/00-795.ZS.html))

##### Question 2: Material presented as child pornography

Holding and operative rule: Section 2256(8)(D) is substantially overbroad because it makes sexually explicit material criminal according to how it is advertised, promoted, presented, described, or distributed and extends the taint to persons who did not make the representation. A law confined to fraud, solicitation, obscenity, or a properly defined offer to provide unlawful material presents a different question. (joined by Stone, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer)

Reasoning: The conveys-the-impression provision does not require that an image depict an actual child, appear to depict a child, or otherwise fall within *Ferber* or *Miller*. Its operation can depend on a title, trailer, description, or representation made earlier in a distribution chain, while possession becomes criminal in the hands of a recipient who neither made nor knew of the marketing claim. Pandering may bear on whether material is obscene under *Ginzburg v. United States*, 383 U.S. 463 (1966), and Congress may punish fraud or an offer to transact in unlawful child pornography through a properly confined offense. Section 2256(8)(D), however, criminalizes protected material itself at a breadth not fitted to those theories. Its invalid applications are substantial in relation to its legitimate reach. ([Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/00-795.ZS.html))

#### Concurrences

Thomas: The Government showed no actual case in which a virtual-image defense had defeated prosecution of actual-child pornography, so the asserted enforcement need remained speculative. He leaves open a future, narrowly drawn restriction or adequate affirmative defense if technological change makes proof of actual-child exploitation impracticable. ([Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/00-795.ZC.html))

O'Connor, joined by Scalia as to Part II: She agrees that § 2256(8)(D) and the application of § 2256(8)(B) to youthful-looking adults are invalid, but would preserve § 2256(8)(B) for wholly computer-generated images virtually indistinguishable from photographs of actual children. Scalia joins her defense of that narrowed virtual-image prohibition. ([Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/00-795.ZX.html))

#### Dissents

Scalia: He would construe the appears-to-be provision to reach only hard-core images virtually indistinguishable from actual-child pornography and the conveys-the-impression provision to reach knowing pandering. On those narrowing constructions, he would reverse rather than invalidate either provision facially. ([Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/00-795.ZD.html))

#### Doctrinal Effect

*New York v. Ferber*, 458 U.S. 747 (1982): declined to extend beyond material whose production exploits an actual child.

*Miller v. California*, 413 U.S. 15 (1973): reaffirmed; nonobscene expression does not lose protection because a single image appears to depict a minor.

*Ginzburg v. United States*, 383 U.S. 463 (1966): distinguished; the relevance of pandering to obscenity does not authorize criminalizing protected material in every recipient's hands.

Overbreadth doctrine: applied; the Government may not suppress a substantial amount of protected virtual expression merely to facilitate enforcement against actual-child exploitation.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit is affirmed, and the challenged provisions may not be enforced at the unconstitutional breadth adjudicated.

Effect: Laws directed to actual-child exploitation, obscenity, morphing that uses an identifiable child, solicitation, fraud, or a properly confined offer or pandering offense remain available. The decision does not immunize unlawful conduct involving real children or foreclose a materially narrower future statute.

### *United States v. Craft*

Argued January 14, 2002 | Decided April 17, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit; judgment below: federal tax lien held not to attach to the taxpayer’s rights in property held by the entirety, 233 F.3d 358 (6th Cir. 2000). ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep535/usrep535274/usrep535274.pdf))  
Area of Law: Federal tax liens; property; tenancy by the entirety

Questions Presented:

1. Does a federal tax lien attach to a delinquent taxpayer’s state-law rights in property held with a spouse as tenants by the entirety?

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 6-3.  
Supporting: Stone, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: Stevens, Scalia, Thomas.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O’Connor | Stone, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would affirm | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Thomas | Stevens, Scalia | Would affirm | Complete opinion |

#### Justice Stone’s Position

Vote: to reverse and remand.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court’s opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Federal attachment to state-created entireties rights

Holding and operative rule: Section 6321 attaches to a delinquent spouse’s valuable and individually enforceable rights in entireties property, even though state law prevents unilateral alienation and ordinary individual-creditor process. State law identifies the taxpayer’s rights, while federal law determines whether those rights constitute property or rights to property under the federal lien statute.

Reasoning: Michigan law did not give Don Craft a separately alienable fractional estate, but it did give him present rights of use, exclusion, possession, survivorship, receipt of shared proceeds, and participation in any conveyance or encumbrance. Those rights carried substantial economic value and legal control. Under *Drye v. United States*, federal classification does not turn on the label State law places on the aggregate estate or on whether state creditors may seize it. Conversely, federal law does not create new state-law incidents of ownership. It attaches the lien to the rights that Michigan actually recognized. Treating the entireties label as dispositive would allow States to exclude valuable ownership rights from a federal collection statute whose breadth Congress did not make dependent on state creditor remedies. The case concerns attachment alone. Valuation, allocation between the spouses, priority, enforcement, and any forced-sale authority remain separate questions.

#### Concurrences

No concurrences filed.

#### Dissents

Scalia, joined by Thomas: Michigan recognizes an indivisible estate held by the marital unit rather than separate property owned by each spouse. Federal law may classify state-created rights, but it may not disaggregate the State’s unitary estate into an individual interest that state law itself refuses to recognize.

Thomas, joined by Stevens and Scalia: The traditional incidents of tenancy by the entirety and § 6321’s reliance on state-created ownership require a separately transferable or creditor-reachable interest before an individual federal lien may attach. He would affirm the Sixth Circuit.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Drye v. United States*: followed; state law identifies the taxpayer’s legal rights, while federal law independently determines whether those rights constitute property or rights to property under § 6321.

*United States v. National Bank of Commerce*: reaffirmed; the federal tax-lien statute reaches valuable state-created control and beneficial rights without adopting state creditor limitations.

*United States v. Rodgers*: clarified; attachment of a lien, valuation of the attached rights, and any later forced-sale remedy are distinct inquiries.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to recognize attachment of the federal lien to Don Craft’s state-created rights and to resolve valuation, priority, allocation, and enforcement under the governing remedial law.

Effect: The federal lien attaches to Don Craft’s cognizable rights in the former entireties property and its escrowed proceeds. The judgment does not extinguish Sandra Craft’s separate rights, determine the value of Don’s interest, establish lien priority, or authorize a forced sale.

### *Michigan v. Environmental Protection Agency*

Argued November 26, 2001 | Decided April 23, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit; judgment below: the NOx SIP Call upheld in its principal respects, with discrete state-specific and procedural components vacated or remanded, 213 F.3d 663 (D.C. Cir. 2000). ([Justia Law](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/213/663/532662/))  
Area of Law: Clean Air Act; administrative law; federalism

Questions Presented:

1. Whether EPA’s NOx SIP Call lawfully defines and allocates significant interstate contribution through record-supported modeling and cost-effectiveness while preserving state implementation choice, and the proper disposition of unsupported or inadequately noticed state-specific components.

#### Judgment

Affirmed in part, vacated in part, and remanded, 7-2.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: Scalia, Thomas.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting | Opposing | Relief or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Core significant-contribution methodology and statewide-budget authority | Affirmed, 7-2 | Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia, Thomas | EPA’s core authority and methodology remain sustained within the statutory and record-based limits stated by the Court. |
| Unsupported state-specific and technical components | Affirmed, 9-0 | Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | No Justice | The lower court’s set-aside or remand of components lacking adequate record support remains in force. |
| Material notice defects and remedy | Vacated in part and remanded, 9-0 | Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | No Justice | Materially inadequately noticed components must be vacated pending lawful administrative proceedings rather than left operative on remand. |

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Supports affirmance in part, vacatur in part, and remand | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would vacate and remand | Would reject EPA’s use of cost-effectiveness to define the required amount of interstate emission reduction while agreeing that unsupported and inadequately noticed components cannot stand |

#### Justice Stone’s Position

Vote: to affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court’s opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Causal contribution and comparative cost-effectiveness

Holding and operative rule: Clean Air Act § 110 requires EPA first to identify a measurable causal contribution from an upwind State to downwind nonattainment. After establishing that interstate linkage, EPA may use comparative cost-effectiveness to determine the portion reasonably required to be eliminated and to coordinate regionally effective reductions; cost alone cannot create the contribution Congress required.

Reasoning: The statutory phrase contribute significantly contains both a causal predicate and an evaluative judgment about the contribution’s significance. EPA therefore may not include a State merely because emission reductions there are inexpensive. Air-quality modeling and the administrative record must establish that emissions from the State materially reach and affect downwind nonattainment. Once that predicate is established, however, a uniform highly cost-effective control threshold can identify practical reductions, avoid demanding elimination of every molecule traced across state lines, and coordinate an interstate pollution problem that cannot be addressed adequately through isolated source-by-source comparisons. The D.C. Circuit correctly sustained that general methodology because EPA began with modeled interstate contribution and used cost at the subsequent allocation stage. The agency remains responsible for explaining the threshold, its relationship to statutory objectives, and its operation for each included State. ([Justia Law](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/213/663/532662/))

##### Question 2: Statewide budgets and implementation choice

Holding and operative rule: EPA may establish a record-supported statewide emissions outcome without dictating the State’s implementation plan when the State remains free to select sources, controls, trading arrangements, schedules, and other lawful means of compliance, and a federal implementation plan supplies the statutory fallback.

Reasoning: Section 110 assigns the federal agency responsibility for identifying the interstate pollution reduction required by federal law while preserving substantial state authority over the means of achieving it. A statewide budget states the federally required emissions outcome. It does not itself compel a legislature to enact a specified program or require state administrators to operate a federally selected source-control regime. Each State may distribute reductions among sources, use lawful trading arrangements, select different control technologies, and design its own implementation plan. If a State declines or fails to submit an adequate plan, the consequence is federal implementation under the Clean Air Act rather than an order compelling the State to administer federal law. The budget framework therefore does not violate the anti-commandeering principle, provided the federal outcome is itself lawfully derived and adequately supported. ([Justia Law](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/213/663/532662/))

##### Question 3: Record support, notice, and remedial consequence

Holding and operative rule: Every state-specific inclusion and budget must rest on adequately explained record evidence, and a material technical definition, control assumption, or modeling premise may not be adopted without legally sufficient notice and opportunity for comment. Unsupported components must remain set aside or be remanded, while materially inadequately noticed components must be vacated pending lawful proceedings. (joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer)

Reasoning: Administrative review does not permit a court to supply missing modeling, infer an unexplained statewide extrapolation, or accept a post hoc technical premise that the agency did not expose to public testing. The rulemaking record must support each State’s inclusion and the calculation of its budget. Where the modeling supports only part of a State, an assumption remains unexplained, or the agency materially changes a definition or control premise without adequate notice, the resulting component fails ordinary Administrative Procedure Act review. The D.C. Circuit properly rejected the state-specific components for which the record was insufficient. It erred only insofar as it allowed materially notice-defective components to remain operative during remand. Because affected parties never received the required opportunity to address those premises before adoption, vacatur is the appropriate disposition while EPA conducts lawful corrective proceedings. ([Justia Law](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/213/663/532662/))

#### Concurrences

No concurrences filed.

#### Dissents

Scalia, joined by Thomas: Section 110 ties the required prohibition to the magnitude and causal significance of interstate contribution, not to the comparative price of controls after causation has been shown. They would vacate the core cost-based allocation methodology while agreeing that unsupported and inadequately noticed components fail administrative-law review.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Wisconsin Department of Health and Family Services v. Blumer*, decided February 20, 2002: followed; administrative expertise and interpretation do not excuse compliance with the statute’s fixed causal boundary.

*Ragsdale v. Wolverine World Wide, Inc.*, decided March 19, 2002: followed; delegated administrative authority does not permit an agency to replace an enacted predicate with a different rule of its own creation.

*Barnhart v. Walton*, decided March 27, 2002: clarified; deference within statutory ambiguity remains subject to reasoned explanation, adequate notice, and record support.

*Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Association v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co.*: followed; the agency must explain the relevant technical choices and connect them to the administrative record.

*New York v. United States* and *Printz v. United States*: distinguished; a federal emissions outcome coupled with state choice of implementation and a federal fallback does not compel state legislative or administrative machinery.

Clean Air Act § 110(a)(2)(D): established; measurable interstate contribution must precede cost analysis, but comparative cost-effectiveness may then assist in quantifying the portion reasonably required to be eliminated.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit is affirmed in part and vacated in part, and the case is remanded with instructions to preserve EPA’s core significant-contribution and statewide-budget framework, maintain the set-aside or remand of unsupported state-specific components, and vacate materially notice-defective components pending lawful administrative proceedings.

Effect: The NOx SIP Call is not invalidated in its entirety. EPA may continue to administer its lawful core framework, but it must correct unsupported modeling, unexplained assumptions, and notice defects before the affected state-specific components may operate.

### *Tahoe-Sierra Preservation Council, Inc. v. Tahoe Regional Planning Agency*

Argued January 7, 2002 | Decided April 23, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; judgment below: categorical-taking challenge to the temporary development moratoria rejected, 216 F.3d 764 (9th Cir. 2000). ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep535/usrep535302/usrep535302.pdf))  
Area of Law: Takings Clause; land use; temporary moratoria

Questions Presented:

1. Is a temporary development moratorium imposed while a comprehensive land-use plan is prepared automatically a categorical taking requiring compensation?

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 7-2.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: Scalia, Thomas.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports affirmance | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would reverse | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Thomas | Scalia | Would reverse | Would reject the Court’s temporal parcel-as-a-whole analysis and treat a complete prohibition during the moratorium as a compensable taking |

#### Justice Stone’s Position

Vote: to affirm.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court’s opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Temporary moratorium and categorical taking

Holding and operative rule: A finite planning moratorium is not a categorical taking merely because it prevents development during the moratorium. The property interest and its temporal duration are considered as a whole rather than divided into a separately compensable segment consisting only of the restricted period.

Reasoning: *Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council* addresses the extraordinary case in which regulation permanently eliminates all economically beneficial or productive use of the relevant property interest. The owners’ theory would convert every temporary land-use restriction into a total taking by defining the relevant interest as the precise period during which development was prohibited. That temporal severance is inconsistent with the parcel-as-a-whole principle and would subject ordinary permitting delays, planning intervals, and temporary injunctions to an automatic compensation rule. A moratorium may impose a serious burden and remains subject to the fact-specific inquiry associated with *Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City*. The owners, however, presented only the categorical theory for review. The Court therefore does not adjudicate an independent *Penn Central* claim or immunize a functionally permanent, deliberately prolonged, bad-faith, or otherwise unlawful restriction.

#### Concurrences

No concurrences filed.

#### Dissents

Scalia, joined by Thomas: A government prohibition that eliminates every lawful development use for a defined period takes the use of the property during that period. The temporary character of the deprivation affects compensation, not whether a categorical taking occurred.

Thomas, joined by Scalia: The parcel-as-a-whole rule has no adequate textual basis when it permits government to avoid compensation by combining a completely restricted temporal segment with unrestricted future years. He would reverse and permit valuation of the temporary total deprivation.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council*: declined to extend; a finite development moratorium is not converted into a permanent total deprivation by isolating the restricted period.

*Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City*: followed; temporary regulatory burdens ordinarily remain subject to the complete fact-specific inquiry when that claim is properly presented.

*Palazzolo v. Rhode Island*: followed; the categorical inquiry evaluates the property interest as a whole rather than a strategically isolated segment.

*First English Evangelical Lutheran Church of Glendale v. County of Los Angeles*: distinguished; the case concerns the remedy after a taking has been established and does not determine whether a temporary regulation is categorically a taking.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit rejecting the categorical-taking claim is affirmed.

Effect: The owners receive no compensation under the categorical theory presented. The judgment does not resolve an independently preserved *Penn Central* claim or protect an indefinite, functionally permanent, bad-faith, physically appropriative, or exaction-based restriction.

### *US Airways, Inc. v. Barnett*

Argued December 4, 2001 | Decided April 29, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; judgment below: seniority-system conflict denied presumptive effect in the reasonable-accommodation inquiry, 228 F.3d 1105 (9th Cir. 2000). ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/535/391/))  
Area of Law: Americans with Disabilities Act; reasonable accommodation; seniority

Questions Presented:

1. Does the ADA ordinarily require reassignment that conflicts with an established seniority system, and what workplace-specific showing can make the reassignment reasonable?

#### Judgment

Vacated and remanded, 5-4.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Breyer. Opposing: Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy | Supports vacatur and remand | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence | Stevens | No other Justice | Supports vacatur and remand | Emphasizes that the practical administration of the seniority system, rather than its formal title, determines the strength of employee expectations |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would reverse | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Souter | Ginsburg | Would affirm | Complete opinion |

#### Justice Stone’s Position

Vote: to vacate and remand.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court’s opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Reassignment that conflicts with a seniority system

Holding and operative rule: An employer that proves the existence of a genuine, consistently administered seniority system and an actual conflict between that system and the requested reassignment ordinarily establishes that the reassignment is not reasonable. The employee may rebut that showing through workplace-specific proof that exceptions, unilateral employer discretion, inconsistent administration, or other circumstances materially weaken the system or the expectations it creates.

Reasoning: The ADA identifies reassignment to a vacant position as a possible accommodation, but it does not make reassignment automatically controlling over every workplace rule. A genuine seniority system creates expectations among employees concerning job selection, retention, and advancement. Requiring an exception ordinarily disrupts more than the employer’s administrative preference because it alters the position of other employees who have organized workplace choices around the system. The system is not conclusive, however. A nominal policy, one routinely disregarded through exceptions, or one materially subject to unilateral employer alteration may create little settled reliance. The employer therefore carries the initial burden of proving a genuine system, consistent administration, and an actual conflict. The employee may then identify special circumstances showing that the requested exception remains reasonable in the particular workplace. If that rebuttal succeeds, the ordinary accommodation inquiry continues. Undue hardship remains a separate defense on which the employer bears the statutory burden.

#### Concurrences

Stevens: The presumption rests on demonstrated workplace reliance, not the employer’s choice to call a rule a seniority system. Frequent exceptions, inconsistent enforcement, or broad unilateral discretion may show that no material expectation would be impaired.

#### Dissents

Scalia, joined by Thomas: The ADA requires removal of workplace barriers but does not authorize preferences that displace the rights of other employees under a neutral seniority system. They would treat an actual seniority conflict as categorically unreasonable and direct judgment for US Airways.

Souter, joined by Ginsburg: Reassignment to a vacant position is facially reasonable because Congress expressly identified it as an accommodation. The seniority system should be considered through the employer’s undue-hardship defense rather than through a presumption that shifts an additional burden to the employee.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Trans World Airlines, Inc. v. Hardison*: distinguished; seniority expectations remain legally significant, but the ADA does not create an absolute seniority bar.

*Albertson’s, Inc. v. Kirkingburg*: distinguished; an externally binding federal safety qualification differs from an employer-created seniority system whose actual administration must be examined.

ADA reasonable accommodation: established; a genuine seniority conflict ordinarily defeats facial reasonableness, subject to an employee’s workplace-specific rebuttal and the employer’s separate undue-hardship burden.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit is vacated, and the case is remanded with instructions to determine whether US Airways maintained a genuine and consistently administered seniority system, whether Barnett’s requested reassignment actually conflicted with it, whether workplace-specific circumstances rebut the ordinary consequence of that conflict, and whether undue hardship is established.

Effect: Barnett is not awarded the mailroom position, and US Airways does not receive final judgment from this Court. The ADA requires neither creation of a vacancy nor displacement of an incumbent, and a nominal or inconsistently administered system receives no conclusive protection.

### *Thompson v. Western States Medical Center*

Argued February 26, 2002 | Decided April 29, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; judgment below: FDAMA’s advertising restrictions held unconstitutional and nonseverable from the pharmacy-compounding exemptions, 238 F.3d 1090 (9th Cir. 2001). ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep535/usrep535357/usrep535357.pdf))  
Area of Law: First Amendment; commercial speech; pharmaceutical regulation

Questions Presented:

1. May Congress condition the federal exemption for compounded drugs on pharmacists’ refraining from advertising or promoting particular compounded products?

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 6-3.  
Supporting: Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas. Opposing: Stevens, Ginsburg, Breyer.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O’Connor | Stone, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Supports affirmance | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence | Thomas | No other Justice | Supports affirmance | Emphasizes the Government’s failure to demonstrate why direct limits on compounding scale and distribution would be inadequate |
| Dissent | Breyer | Stevens, Ginsburg | Would reverse | Complete opinion |

#### Justice Stone’s Position

Vote: to affirm.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court’s opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Advertising condition on the compounding exemption

Holding and operative rule: Congress may directly regulate pharmacy compounding through lawful rules governing manufacturing scale, prescriptions, safety, distribution, and evasion of drug approval. It may not condition the statutory exemption on silence about truthful and lawful compounding when the Government has not shown that the speech restriction materially advances its interests with a reasonable fit in light of those direct alternatives.

Reasoning: The challenged provisions regulate truthful, nonmisleading commercial speech concerning lawful compounded drugs. The Government has substantial interests in preserving the distinction between traditional compounding and large-scale manufacturing, protecting patients, and maintaining the drug-approval system. The advertising ban nevertheless suppresses information rather than regulating directly the conduct said to create the danger. Congress could impose prescription requirements, production or batch limits, restrictions on interstate distribution, safety standards, or other nonspeech conditions fitted to the asserted risks. The record does not establish why those approaches would be ineffective or why pharmacists must be prohibited from communicating truthful information about products they may lawfully provide. The restriction therefore fails the governing commercial-speech inquiry. Because the Government sought review only of the First Amendment ruling and respondents filed no cross-petition concerning severability, the Court affirms the judgment without adopting or revising the Ninth Circuit’s separate nonseverability reasoning.

#### Concurrences

Florida* does not require dismissal because the Telecommunications Act supplies no detailed exclusive remedy covering the same continuing enforcement duty and prospective relief on this record. The case therefore may proceed against the responsible commissioners without resolving the ultimate compensation dispute or authorizing relief for completed payments.

Kennedy: A specialized federal remedy can displace § 1331 or *Young* when Congress makes the alternative route exclusive and provides meaningful review of the same duty and relief. The present statute does not do so, and the judgment authorizes no retrospective monetary recovery.

Argued February 19, 2002 | Decided May 20, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Alabama; judgment below: suspended term of imprisonment imposed after an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction invalidated, 851 So. 2d 96 (Ala. 2000). ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep535/usrep535654/usrep535654.pdf))  
Area of Law: Sixth Amendment; right to counsel; suspended sentences

Questions Presented:

1. May an indigent defendant receive a suspended jail sentence following an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction?

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 6-3.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Breyer | Supports affirmance | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Scalia | Kennedy, Thomas | Would reverse | Complete opinion |

#### Justice Stone’s Position

Vote: to affirm.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court’s opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Counsel before imposition of suspended imprisonment

Holding and operative rule: An indigent defendant may not receive a term of imprisonment, including a term suspended on probation, unless counsel was provided or validly waived during the prosecution that established guilt. Activation of the suspended term constitutes imprisonment for the original offense rather than punishment arising solely from the later revocation proceeding.

Reasoning: *Scott v. Illinois* permits an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction when the court imposes no imprisonment. Shelton’s adjudication did more. It imposed a thirty-day jail term and postponed its execution on stated conditions. If probation later were revoked, the State would imprison Shelton under the authority created by the uncounseled judgment of guilt, without a new adjudication of the elements of the underlying offense. Counsel at a later revocation hearing could contest whether probation was violated, but could not cure constitutional defects in the original guilt proceeding or retry the misdemeanor charge. Labeling the sentence suspended therefore does not remove it from the actual-imprisonment boundary. The conviction and severable monetary consequences remain valid because the right recognized here attaches to the custodial sentence. A freestanding probation disposition unaccompanied by a suspended jail term is not before the Court.

#### Concurrences

No concurrences filed.

#### Dissents

Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas: No imprisonment occurs when the uncounseled conviction and suspended sentence are entered. Because any later confinement follows a separate, counseled determination that the defendant violated probation, they would reverse and leave the suspended term intact.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Scott v. Illinois*: extended; a suspended jail term is imprisonment imposed for purposes of the right to counsel because the uncounseled judgment itself authorizes later incarceration.

*Argersinger v. Hamlin*: followed; actual imprisonment may not rest on a prosecution in which the indigent defendant lacked counsel or a valid waiver.

*Nichols v. United States*: distinguished; consideration of a valid fine-only conviction under a rebuttable sentencing scheme does not authorize direct execution of a custodial term imposed by the uncounseled judgment itself.

*Gagnon v. Scarpelli*: distinguished; process at a probation-revocation hearing cannot cure the absence of counsel in the prosecution that established guilt and imposed imprisonment.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Supreme Court of Alabama invalidating the suspended term of imprisonment is affirmed.

Effect: Shelton’s thirty-day suspended jail term may not stand. The misdemeanor conviction and any severable fine or other noncustodial consequence remain intact, and the judgment does not require counsel in every fine-only prosecution or govern imprisonment for a separately counseled new offense.

Argued January 8, 2002 | Decided May 28, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit; judgment below: prosecution-history estoppel treated as imposing a complete bar to equivalents following a patentability-related narrowing amendment, 234 F.3d 558 (Fed. Cir. 2000). ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep535/usrep535722/usrep535722.pdf))  
Area of Law: Patent law; doctrine of equivalents; prosecution-history estoppel

Questions Presented:

1. Does every patentability-related narrowing amendment extinguish all doctrine-of-equivalents protection for the amended element?

#### Judgment

Vacated and remanded, 8-1.  
Supporting: Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: Stone.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports vacatur and remand | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Stone | No other Justice | Concurs in part and dissents in part | Joins the rejection of a complete bar and the particular-equivalent rebuttal rule; would affirm rather than remand |

#### Justice Stone’s Position

Vote: to affirm.  
Public role: concurrence in part and dissent in part.  
Announced ground: Stone agrees that a patentability-related narrowing amendment creates a rebuttable presumption concerning the particular equivalent rather than a complete bar. He would affirm because the developed prosecution histories identify the amended sealing-ring and sleeve limitations, and Festo supplied no legally sufficient record basis showing that the accused substitutes were unforeseeable, tangential to the amendments, or otherwise not reasonably claimable.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Scope of surrender after a narrowing amendment

Holding and operative rule: A patentability-related narrowing amendment does not impose a complete bar on every equivalent to the amended element. It creates a rebuttable presumption that the amendment surrendered the particular equivalent asserted, which the patentee may overcome by showing that the equivalent was not reasonably within the surrender because it was unforeseeable, only tangentially related to the amendment’s rationale, or otherwise not reasonably describable in the claim at the time. (joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer)

Reasoning: Patent claims must give the public reliable notice, and a patentee who narrows a claim to obtain allowance may not recapture through equivalence the same territory surrendered during prosecution. A complete bar, however, assumes that the amended language perfectly anticipates and describes every later insubstantial substitute. That assumption would eliminate much of the doctrine of equivalents even where the reason for the amendment has no meaningful relation to the accused substitute. The proper inquiry concerns the particular equivalent. A patentability-related amendment presumptively surrenders the territory between the broader and narrower formulations, but the patentee may show that a skilled drafter could not reasonably have been expected to claim the substitute, that the substitute was unforeseeable, or that the amendment’s reason was merely tangential to it. These are applications of a single scope-of-surrender inquiry rather than an exhaustive list of formal elements.

##### Question 2: Application to Festo’s asserted equivalents

Holding and operative rule: The Federal Circuit’s complete-bar judgment cannot stand, but the existence and legal sufficiency of evidence rebutting surrender of the one two-way sealing ring and nonmagnetizable sleeve must be determined under the new particular-equivalent standard on remand.

Reasoning: The patents were narrowed during prosecution to add the one-way sealing-ring limitation and, for one patent, the magnetizable-sleeve limitation. SMC’s accused device differs at those same elements. The Federal Circuit treated the amendments as eliminating every possible equivalent and therefore had no occasion to apply the rebuttable inquiry adopted today or to determine the significance of the prosecution histories under that rule. The majority declines to make those record-sensitive determinations in the first instance. The case must return so the Federal Circuit can determine whether Festo has identified evidence that either accused substitute was unforeseeable, tangential to the reason for amendment, or otherwise not reasonably claimable, and, if the presumption is rebutted, whether the ordinary element-specific requirements of equivalence are satisfied.

#### Concurrences

Stone: He agrees with the Court’s rejection of the complete bar and with its particular-equivalent presumption. He would affirm because Festo bears the rebuttal burden and the developed record identifies no legally sufficient basis for excluding either accused substitute from the amendments’ surrender.

#### Dissents

No dissents filed.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Warner-Jenkinson Co. v. Hilton Davis Chemical Co.*: clarified; a known patentability-related narrowing amendment creates a rebuttable presumption of surrender directed to the particular equivalent asserted.

*Graver Tank & Manufacturing Co. v. Linde Air Products Co.*: reaffirmed; the doctrine of equivalents remains available on an element-by-element basis when prosecution history and the other legal limits do not bar it.

*Exhibit Supply Co. v. Ace Patents Corp.*: clarified; prosecution-history estoppel protects public reliance on actual surrender without converting every narrowing amendment into an absolute bar.

Federal Circuit complete-bar rule: overruled; patentability-related amendment does not extinguish all possible equivalents to the amended element.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit is vacated, and the case is remanded for application of the particular-equivalent rebuttal rule and, if the presumption is overcome, the ordinary doctrine-of-equivalents inquiry.

Effect: No infringement finding is made. Festo may attempt to rebut surrender of the asserted sealing-ring and sleeve equivalents, while SMC may rely on the prosecution histories, prior art, claim limitations, and every other preserved defense.

### *Federal Maritime Commission v. South Carolina State Ports Authority*

Argued February 25, 2002 | Decided May 28, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit; judgment below: privately initiated FMC adjudication categorically barred by state sovereign immunity, 243 F.3d 165 (4th Cir. 2001). ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/535/743/))  
Area of Law: State sovereign immunity; administrative adjudication; federal shipping law

Questions Presented:

1. Does state sovereign immunity categorically bar an FMC adjudication initiated by a private complainant alleging that a state port authority violated federal shipping law?

#### Judgment

Vacated and remanded, 7-2.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: Scalia, Thomas.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone | Souter, Ginsburg in full; Stevens and Breyer as to Parts I and II; O’Connor and Kennedy as to Parts I and III | Supports vacatur and remand | Complete opinion (Souter, Ginsburg); Parts I and II (Stevens, Breyer); Parts I and III (O’Connor, Kennedy) |
| Concurrence | O’Connor | Kennedy | Supports vacatur and remand | Would resolve prospective enforcement through entity classification and the officer route rather than direct Article I adjudication against an arm of the State |
| Concurrence | Stevens | Breyer | Supports vacatur and remand | Would permit both prospective compliance adjudication and congressionally authorized compensatory relief without the remedial-autonomy limitation adopted in Part III |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would affirm | Complete opinion |

#### Justice Stone’s Position

Vote: to vacate and remand.  
Public role: Court author.  
Announced ground: A private party’s initiation of an FMC complaint does not itself determine sovereign immunity, entity status, liability, or remedy. The adjudication must separate prospective enforcement of the federal shipping obligation from retrospective compensation and must classify the Port Authority through its legal and fiscal reality before selecting the available remedial route.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Categorical treatment of a privately initiated agency proceeding

Holding and operative rule: A private party’s initiation of an FMC complaint does not by itself convert every component of the administrative proceeding into a forbidden private damages action or establish that sovereign immunity bars the proceeding in full. The tribunal must identify the federal obligation, the Port Authority’s legal and fiscal status, the relief requested, and the source authorizing each remedy.

Reasoning: The Fourth Circuit treated the resemblance between an FMC adjudication and civil litigation as dispositive. That approach combines questions the governing sovereign-immunity decisions keep separate. A federal obligation may bind a State or state-related enterprise even when a particular forum or remedy is unavailable. Prospective compliance and retrospective reparations do not rest on identical constitutional and statutory foundations. Entity status also cannot be assumed from the Port Authority’s public title because the record must establish operational control, fiscal independence, responsibility for judgments, and the actual source of payment. Private initiation identifies who invoked the Commission’s process, but it does not itself determine whether the Commission is enforcing a federal obligation prospectively, awarding compensation retrospectively, or proceeding against an entity sufficiently separate from the State. The categorical dismissal therefore cannot stand.

##### Question 2: Prospective federal compliance adjudication

Holding and operative rule: When Congress acts within its enumerated authority and unmistakably authorizes the Commission to determine prospective compliance with federal shipping law, a state port participating in interstate shipping may not defeat that prospective federal adjudication solely because a private complainant supplied the initiating complaint. (joined by Stone, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer)

Reasoning: The legal force of the prospective obligation comes from federal law and the authority Congress assigned to the Commission, not from the private complainant. A private filing may furnish information and trigger a lawful process without converting the Commission’s forward-looking compliance determination into retrospective private damages. Simulated *Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida* already recognizes that Congress may unmistakably authorize a constitutionally permissible direct-State action for prospective compliance under a valid Article I power. The Commission may therefore determine whether federal shipping law requires future nondiscriminatory treatment when the statute validly and clearly grants that authority. The agency must still prove the statutory predicates and remain within the prospective remedy Congress supplied. Injunctive compliance authority does not authorize reparations, punitive sanctions, compelled property transfers, or any other retrospectively compensatory relief.

##### Question 3: Reparations and entity-specific remedial classification

Holding and operative rule: Retrospective reparations are neither automatically available nor categorically barred on the present record. The lower court must determine whether the Port Authority is an arm of the State or a qualifying commercial state enterprise through its operational and fiscal reality and must then identify an independently lawful compensatory route consistent with remedial autonomy and the federal fallback. (joined by Stone, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg)

Reasoning: The record does not establish who bears responsibility for judgments, whether Port funds are legally and practically separate from the state treasury, how the State controls operations, or whether the Port functions as a commercially distinct enterprise in the interstate shipping market. Those facts are necessary under *Hess v. Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp.* and the simulation’s commercial-state-enterprise rule. If the Port Authority is sufficiently separate or qualifies under that rule, ordinary compensatory consequences may be available within the statute. If it is an arm of the State outside that category, the federal obligation remains, but retrospective compensation must proceed through an independently authorized state or federal remedial route rather than by assumption. The Commission and reviewing court may not resolve the gap by merging prospective compliance with reparations or by treating the complainant’s participation as either consent or automatic prohibition.

#### Concurrences

O’Connor, joined by Kennedy: The categorical judgment must be vacated because the Port Authority’s status and the requested remedies were not analyzed separately. They would preserve prospective enforcement through responsible officers if the entity is an immune arm of the State and would not join a direct Article I administrative adjudication against that entity.

Stevens, joined by Breyer: Congress may authorize the Commission to adjudicate prospective compliance and award ordinary compensation for a violation of valid federal shipping law. They would not condition congressionally authorized reparations on the remedial-autonomy and federal-fallback analysis stated in Part III.

#### Dissents

Scalia, joined by Thomas: A coercive agency adjudication initiated and prosecuted by a private complainant against an arm of the State is the functional equivalent of the private suit forbidden by sovereign immunity. They would affirm the categorical dismissal and leave federal enforcement to the United States or to a properly confined prospective officer action.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida*: extended; unmistakably authorized prospective compliance under a valid Article I power may be adjudicated administratively even when a private complaint initiates the federal process.

*Hess v. Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp.*: followed; the legal and fiscal reality of the entity, including control, judgment responsibility, and source of payment, must be established before arm-of-the-State consequences are assigned.

*College Savings Bank v. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board*: followed; participation in a regulated interstate market does not itself create constructive waiver, while entity status, federal obligation, and remedial route remain separate.

*Lapides v. Board of Regents of University System of Georgia*, decided May 13, 2002: distinguished; the Port Authority did not voluntarily remove the matter or otherwise invoke the federal forum.

*Verizon Maryland Inc. v. Public Service Commission of Maryland*, decided May 20, 2002: followed; properly confined prospective officer relief remains available for ongoing federal-law violations and is distinct from retrospective entity liability.

Federal administrative adjudication against state-related entities: established; private initiation alone neither bars the proceeding in full nor supplies authority for every requested remedy.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit is vacated, and the case is remanded with instructions to determine the Port Authority’s legal and fiscal status, identify the statutory source and scope of prospective compliance authority, and analyze separately the independently available route for any retrospective compensation.

Effect: The FMC complaint is not dismissed categorically, but no Shipping Act violation, reparations award, or immunity classification is established. Prospective compliance may proceed only within valid and unmistakable statutory authority, while compensation depends on the Port Authority’s proven status and an independently lawful remedial route.

### *McKune v. Lile*

Argued November 28, 2001 | Decided June 10, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit; judgment below: summary judgment for Lile and injunctive relief against the challenged penalties affirmed, 224 F.3d 1175 (10th Cir. 2000). ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/536/24/))  
Area of Law: Fifth Amendment; self-incrimination; prison rehabilitation

Questions Presented:

1. May a prison impose materially harsher custody and substantial privilege losses on an inmate who refuses to disclose potentially prosecutable sexual history without immunity?

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 5-4.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports affirmance | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | O’Connor | No other Justice | Would reverse | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Kennedy | Scalia, Thomas | Would reverse | Complete opinion |

#### Justice Stone’s Position

Vote: to affirm.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court’s opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Compelled admissions under threat of harsher custody

Holding and operative rule: A prison may encourage rehabilitative disclosure, but it may not condition avoidance of materially harsher custody and substantial loss of established privileges on admissions carrying a real and appreciable prosecution risk unless use-and-derivative-use immunity or equivalent protection removes that risk.

Reasoning: The privilege against self-incrimination applies when truthful answers may furnish evidence or a link in the chain of evidence for a possible prosecution. Kansas required Lile to disclose charged and uncharged sexual conduct while preserving the possibility that the information could be used criminally. Refusal automatically changed his custody classification and curtailed visitation, earnings, work opportunities, family support, canteen access, personal property, and other established privileges. Those consequences were not merely the loss of a possible discretionary benefit. They were direct, automatic legal consequences attached to invocation of the privilege. *Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard*, decided March 25, 1998, involved an optional clemency interview in which silence forfeited only a chance to obtain discretionary mercy and produced no new punishment or entitlement loss. Kansas remains free to operate a treatment program, require therapeutic participation that creates no real prosecution hazard, provide adequate immunity, or use incentives that do not amount to a substantial penalty. ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/536/24/))

#### Concurrences

No concurrences filed.

#### Dissents

O’Connor: The reductions in privileges and transfer within the prison system do not impose pressure comparable to the loss of employment, professional status, or political rights in the Court’s penalty cases. She would reverse without adopting a general rule equating Fifth Amendment compulsion with the due-process standard for atypical prison hardship.

Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas: Kansas’s program serves a legitimate rehabilitative purpose, and the transfer and privilege adjustments are ordinary incidents of prison administration rather than unconstitutional compulsion. They would permit the State to retain the possibility of later prosecution without supplying immunity.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard*, decided March 25, 1998: distinguished; loss of a discretionary opportunity for mercy differs from an automatic material worsening of custody and established privileges.

*Ohio v. Reiner*, decided March 19, 2001: applied; a profession of innocence does not eliminate the privilege when truthful disclosure creates a real and appreciable prosecution risk.

*Sandin v. Conner*: declined to extend; the due-process classification of ordinary prison hardship does not define Fifth Amendment compulsion.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit is affirmed.

Effect: Kansas may not impose the challenged transfer and privilege losses on Lile for refusing the potentially incriminating disclosures without adequate use-and-derivative-use protection or an equivalent safeguard. The judgment does not invalidate sex-offender treatment, ordinary therapeutic requirements, or incentives unconnected to a real prosecution risk and substantial legal penalty.

### *National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Morgan*

Argued January 9, 2002 | Decided June 10, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; judgment below: related discriminatory acts outside the charge-filing period allowed to support recovery under a continuing-violation theory, 232 F.3d 1008 (9th Cir. 2000). ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep536/usrep536101/usrep536101.pdf))  
Area of Law: Title VII; charge-filing periods; hostile work environment

Questions Presented:

1. How does Title VII’s charge-filing period apply to independently actionable discrete decisions and to a hostile environment composed of repeated conduct over time?

#### Judgment

Affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded, 6-3.  
Supporting: Stone, Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting | Opposing | Relief or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Independently actionable discrete acts | Reversed and remanded, 9-0 | Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | No Justice | Relief may not be awarded on a discrete act for which no timely charge was filed, although the act may have any background-evidence role governing law permits. |
| Hostile-work-environment claim | Affirmed and remanded, 6-3 | Stone, Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy | The claim may include earlier conduct if a timely act contributed to the same actionable hostile environment. |

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | Stone, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer in full; O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy as to the discrete-act portion | Supports affirmance in part, reversal in part, and remand | Complete opinion (Stone, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer); discrete-act portion (O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy) |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | O’Connor | Scalia, Kennedy as to the hostile-environment limitation; Breyer as to the discovery-rule discussion | Concurs in part and dissents in part | Complete separate writing with joins as stated |

#### Justice Stone’s Position

Vote: to affirm in part, reverse in part, and remand.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court’s opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Timeliness of discrete employment actions

Holding and operative rule: Each independently actionable discriminatory or retaliatory act supports relief only if a charge concerning that act was timely filed. An untimely discrete act may be considered only in any background-evidence role otherwise permitted by law. (joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer)

Reasoning: A termination, refusal to hire, denial of transfer, disciplinary decision, or comparable discrete action occurs when the particular employment decision is made and communicated. Each act is independently capable of supporting a Title VII claim, so thematic relation to later conduct does not restart its filing period or merge it into another actionable event. The Ninth Circuit’s broader continuing-violation approach therefore cannot preserve liability for untimely discrete acts. Title VII does not prohibit the use of earlier conduct as relevant background evidence when the rules of evidence and the substantive claim permit it. Nor does the ruling eliminate waiver, estoppel, equitable tolling, or another properly established equitable doctrine. It simply prevents a timely charge concerning one act from creating substantive liability for a separate act whose own statutory filing period expired. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep536/usrep536101/usrep536101.pdf))

##### Question 2: Timeliness of a cumulative hostile environment

Holding and operative rule: A hostile work environment constitutes one alleged unlawful employment practice composed of repeated conduct over time. Earlier contributing acts may be considered when at least one timely act forms part of the same actionable environment.

Reasoning: Unlike a discrete decision, a hostile environment ordinarily develops through accumulated intimidation, ridicule, insult, or other discriminatory conduct whose combined operation may satisfy the severe-or-pervasive standard. The court must determine whether the alleged incidents genuinely form the same environment, considering their nature, frequency, participants, setting, continuity, and any intervening event that ended or materially separated the earlier condition. Thematic similarity alone is insufficient. When a timely act contributes to the same actionable environment, the charge encompasses the complete practice, including earlier contributing conduct. The ruling does not alter the objective and subjective hostility requirements, employer attribution, causation, available defenses, or proof of damages. Waiver, estoppel, equitable tolling, and laches also remain available where their requirements are established. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep536/usrep536101/usrep536101.pdf))

#### Concurrences

O’Connor, joined by Scalia and Kennedy on the hostile-environment issue and by Breyer on the discovery-rule discussion: She agrees that untimely discrete acts cannot support relief but would also limit hostile-environment liability to conduct within the filing period, subject to ordinary equitable doctrines. She would not permit one timely component to revive liability for remote earlier components of the environment.

#### Dissents

No separate dissents filed.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Harris v. Forklift Systems, Inc.*, decided November 9, 1993: clarified; the cumulative nature of a hostile environment also determines how the charge-filing provision identifies the relevant unlawful employment practice.

*Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services, Inc.*, decided March 4, 1998: followed; the same severe-or-pervasive standard governs the timely and earlier components alleged to form one environment.

Title VII continuing-violation doctrine: limited; relatedness does not preserve untimely discrete acts, while a genuinely unitary hostile environment remains one cumulative practice.

*Zipes v. Trans World Airlines, Inc.*: reaffirmed; the charge-filing period remains nonjurisdictional and subject to applicable equitable doctrines.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit is affirmed as to the hostile-work-environment timing rule, reversed as to untimely discrete acts, and the case is remanded for claim-specific proceedings under the rules stated by the Court.

Effect: Morgan may not obtain relief for an independently actionable act that was not timely charged. He may rely on earlier conduct as part of the hostile-environment claim only if the lower courts determine that it formed one actionable environment with at least one timely contributing act, subject to the ordinary merits, attribution, causation, equitable-defense, and remedial requirements.

### *Carey v. Saffold*

Argued February 27, 2002 | Decided June 17, 2002  
On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; judgment below: the full interval between California state habeas petitions treated as tolled under 28 U.S.C. § 2244(d)(2), 250 F.3d 1262 (9th Cir. 2001). ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep536/usrep536214/usrep536214.pdf))  
Area of Law: AEDPA; statutory tolling; state collateral review

Questions Presented:

1. Is AEDPA’s limitations period tolled between levels of California collateral review, and when does unreasonable delay end pending status?

#### Judgment

Vacated and remanded, 5-4.  
Supporting: Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Opposing: Stone, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stone as to the interlevel-pendency and California functional-process propositions; Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg in full | Supports vacatur and remand | Complete opinion (Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg); interlevel-pendency and functional-process propositions (Stone) |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Stone | No other Justice | Concurs in part and dissents in part | Agrees with the interlevel rules but would affirm on the record presented |
| Dissent | Kennedy | Scalia, Thomas | Would reverse | Complete opinion |

#### Justice Stone’s Position

Vote: to affirm.  
Public role: concurrence in part and dissent in part.  
Announced ground: an ordinary interval needed to pursue the next level of California collateral review may remain pending, but the interval here should be treated as tolled because no authoritative California rule or unambiguous state-court determination establishes that Saffold filed too late.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Ordinary intervals between levels of collateral review

Holding and operative rule: An application may remain pending during the ordinary interval between a lower state court’s denial and a timely filing seeking the next level of collateral review. (joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer)

Reasoning: Section 2244(d)(2) protects the period during which the State’s ordinary collateral-review process remains underway. In a system using a formal appeal, that process ordinarily includes the time allowed to seek the next level of review. Excluding the corresponding interval would force protective federal filings before the State had a fair opportunity to complete its own review and would make tolling turn on the mechanics used by a particular State. Pending status nevertheless depends on use of the ordinary review sequence and a timely next filing. A repetitive, abandoned, extraordinary, or clearly untimely application does not remain pending merely because the prisoner later submits another petition.

##### Question 2: California’s successive-original-petition procedure

Holding and operative rule: California’s use of successive original petitions rather than formal appeals does not remove ordinary interlevel intervals from pending status. Federal law looks to how the state procedure functions, not the label attached to each filing. (joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer)

Reasoning: California directs a prisoner to proceed from the superior court to the court of appeal and then to the state supreme court through new original petitions. Functionally, that process supplies a single ordinary round of collateral review comparable to appellate systems used elsewhere. Treating every new petition as unrelated would deny tolling solely because California selected a different procedural form. The State may define and enforce reasonable timeliness requirements, and federal courts must respect an authoritative state determination that a petition fell outside them. California’s indeterminate timeliness standard may complicate the federal inquiry, but that difficulty does not permit the federal court to disregard the actual structure of the state process. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep536/usrep536214/usrep536214.pdf))

##### Question 3: Timeliness of Saffold’s particular filing

Holding and operative rule: A state petition remains pending during an interlevel interval only if the next petition was timely under state law. State-court language addressing the merits does not by itself establish timeliness, and the present record requires reconsideration of whether Saffold’s approximately four-and-one-half-month delay remained within California’s ordinary review process.

Reasoning: A state court may discuss the merits while also regarding a filing as procedurally late, and California’s unexplained or summary dispositions do not always reveal which ground controlled. The Ninth Circuit therefore placed too much weight on the state supreme court’s merits language. At the same time, the record does not establish that California had an authoritative fixed deadline or that the state supreme court unambiguously rejected Saffold’s filing as untimely. The case must return for consideration of California law, any special circumstances bearing on the delay, and whether certification to the state supreme court would assist. The Court sets no universal number of permissible days and does not hold that the interval was untimely. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep536/usrep536214/usrep536214.pdf))

#### Concurrences

Stone: He agrees that ordinary interlevel intervals may remain pending and that California’s successive-petition procedure must be treated functionally. He would affirm because no authoritative state rule or unambiguous state-court determination establishes that Saffold’s interval was untimely.

#### Dissents

Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas: Each California original petition is a separate application, so the period after denial of one petition and before filing another is not time during which an application remains pending. They would reverse and exclude the full interpetition interval from statutory tolling.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Artuz v. Bennett*, decided November 7, 2000: clarified; proper filing and pending status remain distinct, and an ordinary timely interlevel interval may be part of the pending period.

*O’Sullivan v. Boerckel*: applied by functional analogy; one complete ordinary state-review round may include intervals between levels, but only when the next filing complies with state timeliness law.

AEDPA interlevel tolling: established; procedure is evaluated by practical function rather than formal labels, without creating a universal permissible-delay period.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit is vacated, and the case is remanded for a state-law timeliness determination consistent with the Court’s functional pending-status rule.

Effect: The Ninth Circuit must determine whether Saffold’s delay remained within California’s timely ordinary review process, considering any special circumstances and authoritative state law. The Court neither declares the federal petition timely nor excludes the interval automatically.

Affirmed, 8-1.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |

Vote: to affirm.

##### Question 1: Constitutional work performed by the production-material element

Holding and operative rule: The fact that a disk, videotape, camera, film, computer, or other incidental production material previously crossed state lines does not by itself establish federal authority over otherwise self-contained local possession. Reasoning: A jurisdictional phrase does not become constitutionally sufficient merely by reciting an interstate fact. Most modern production materials have moved through commerce at some time, and their remote historical movement may bear no meaningful relation to the possession Congress seeks to regulate. *Fraternal Order of Police v. United States*, decided March 13, 2000, requires an offense-specific element to establish a real interstate connection or to identify how the regulated conduct fits within a valid market regime. Treating earlier manufacture outside the State as independently conclusive would reproduce the attenuated reasoning rejected in *United States v. Lopez*, decided April 26, 1995. The Government must therefore identify a recognized Commerce Clause route beyond the incidental medium’s historical travel. ([Justia Law](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/234/326/603589/))

Holding and operative rule: Congress may include intrastate possession of actual-child pornography within a valid national prohibition on interstate production, receipt, sale, shipment, and distribution when possession is sufficiently integrated with acquisition, retention, copying, or redistribution that excluding it would create a material evasion route or materially impair enforcement of the interstate-market regime. Angle’s conviction falls within that rule.

Reasoning: Congress validly regulates interstate trafficking in material whose production exploits actual children. Possession is commonly the retained endpoint of acquisition and receipt, and reproducible files and recordings function as inventory for copying and redistribution. A categorical local-possession exemption would leave a substantial gap between prohibited receipt or distribution and continued retention after the commercial or interstate transaction became difficult to prove. That mechanism is materially tighter than a generalized assertion that possession increases demand or creates downstream social costs. Angle’s record illustrates the integrated class: he communicated with and ordered videotapes from an interstate supplier, used interstate communications, possessed an additional child-pornography videotape, and retained numerous reproducible or recoverable files on computer media in his residence. His separate offenses are not substituted for the possession element, but the record confirms that the possession adjudicated here was neither self-contained local creation nor isolated private custody disconnected from acquisition, copying, or trafficking. No concurrences filed.

Affirmed, 6-3.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Insurance-saving-clause classification

Holding and operative rule: Illinois’s requirement that an insured HMO submit specified medical-necessity disputes to independent physician review is directed specifically toward insurance and alters the insurer-insured relationship. It therefore falls within ERISA’s insurance saving clause.

Reasoning: The statute governs insured HMOs in their capacity as providers of insurance coverage, not employers or health-care providers generally. It regulates the terms under which covered medical benefits are determined and assigns an independent physician a defined role in resolving medical necessity. The requirement thereby changes the insurer’s obligation to the insured and affects the transfer and administration of insured risk. Its application to an HMO combining insurance and medical-delivery features does not change that classification. The case does not involve a self-funded plan, and ERISA’s deemer clause remains fully operative for such plans. Illinois therefore may apply the review requirement to the insured arrangement before the Court. ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/536/355/case.pdf))

##### Question 2: Relationship to ERISA civil enforcement

Holding and operative rule: The independent-review requirement supplies a saved insurance rule governing the benefit determination but creates no alternative cause of action, extra-contractual damages remedy, or substitute judicial forum. Enforcement remains within ERISA’s civil-remedial structure.

Reasoning: Independent review is materially different from a state-law damages action supplementing § 502. The physician determines only the medical-necessity issue specified by the insurance law and does not possess free-ranging authority to construe the plan, award damages, or adjudicate an independent legal claim. If judicial enforcement becomes necessary, the beneficiary still proceeds through ERISA’s authorized route and receives only the relief ERISA permits. The state rule may affect which medical benefit is contractually due, just as another saved insurance mandate may define coverage, without creating a new federal remedy. Nothing in the ruling authorizes punitive damages, consequential damages, or another form of extra-contractual relief. ([Justia Law](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/536/355/case.pdf))

No concurrences filed.

Thomas, joined by Scalia and Kennedy: The binding independent-review mechanism functions as an alternative adjudicatory process and interferes with ERISA’s nationally uniform civil-enforcement scheme. They would hold the requirement preempted despite its insurance focus.

#### Judgment

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Utah's standing

Holding and operative rule: A State has Article III standing when the challenged census methodology is substantially likely to cost it a congressional seat and correction of the methodology would redress that representational injury. (joined by Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer)

Reasoning: Utah alleged a concrete apportionment consequence rather than an abstract interest in lawful census administration. The challenged imputation increased North Carolina's population more than Utah's and affected the final allocation of the last House seat. The injury is traceable to the methodology because the apportionment totals incorporated the imputed entries, and it is redressable because a judgment requiring lawful recalculation would change the totals used for apportionment if Utah prevailed. The standing rule follows the voter-injury and representational-consequence principles established in *Department of Commerce v. United States House of Representatives; Clinton v. Glavin*, decided January 25, 1999. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep536/usrep536452/usrep536452.pdf))

##### Question 2: Statutory classification of hot-deck imputation

Holding and operative rule: Hot-deck imputation is not the statistical method known as sampling within 13 U.S.C. § 195. It completes individual missing entries by drawing an inference from nearby actual enumeration data rather than selecting a sample and extrapolating from that sample to an unenumerated population. (joined by Stone, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer)

Reasoning: Sampling and imputation perform different work. Sampling selects a representative subset and uses information gathered from that subset to estimate characteristics of a larger population. Hot-deck imputation begins only after the Bureau has attempted direct enumeration of the particular address, identifies an unresolved entry, and uses information from the closest comparable enumerated unit to complete that entry. The method may involve statistical judgment, but § 195 does not prohibit every methodology containing inference or estimation. The statute's reference to the statistical method known as sampling does not reach this limited last-resort completion procedure. The Court therefore need not rely on the broader statutory authorization of supplementary sampling already recognized in *Department of Commerce v. United States House of Representatives; Clinton v. Glavin*. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep536/usrep536452/usrep536452.pdf))

##### Question 3: Actual Enumeration

Holding and operative rule: A limited, technically supported imputation method used after direct enumeration efforts fail satisfies the Census Clause when it remains directed to determining actual population and bears a reasonable relationship to a complete and accurate apportionment count.

Reasoning: The Constitution requires an actual enumeration but entrusts Congress with directing its manner. The Bureau mailed census forms, conducted follow-up visits, and used imputation only for a small residue of unresolved addresses. The method relied on actual census information, reduced the substantial inaccuracy that would result from assigning zero population to every unresolved occupied unit, and contained no feature suggesting partisan allocation or manipulation. Under *Wisconsin v. O'Connor: Utah has standing, but she would classify hot-deck imputation as sampling prohibited by § 195 and reverse without reaching the constitutional question.

Scalia: Utah's asserted loss of a seat depends on numerous legal and mathematical steps and does not establish a judicially redressable injury sufficient for Article III standing.

*Department of Commerce v. United States House of Representatives; Clinton v. Glavin*, decided January 25, 1999: followed; a concrete apportionment consequence supplies standing, and authorized census methodology remains valid when reasonably directed to actual population.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |

Vote: to reverse the sentence and remand.  
Public role: joins the Opinion of the Court in full.  
Announced ground: he announces no ground beyond the Court's opinion.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Question 1: Fact increasing a mandatory minimum

Holding and operative rule: Other than a prior conviction, a fact that raises the legally prescribed minimum punishment is an offense element that must be charged, submitted to a jury, and proved beyond a reasonable doubt.

Reasoning: The constitutional allocation turns on the legal effect of the fact rather than the legislature's label. Before a finding of brandishing, the jury's verdict authorized a mandatory minimum of five years. New Jersey*, decided June 26, 2000, therefore cannot be confined to the upper endpoint without preserving the same relabeling device its offense-element principle rejects. *McMillan v. Pennsylvania* is overruled to the extent it permits a nonrecidivist fact to raise a mandatory minimum without indictment and jury determination. No concurrences filed.

Kennedy, joined by O'Connor and Scalia: *McMillan* draws a valid distinction between a fact enlarging the maximum punishment and one regulating judicial discretion within the maximum. They would leave brandishing as a sentencing factor and affirm.

Breyer: He would not extend *Apprendi* to mandatory minimums because doing so transfers additional charging control to prosecutors, complicates sentencing administration, and further limits legislative choice in structuring punishment.

#### Doctrinal Effect

*Apprendi v. New Jersey*, decided June 26, 2000: extended; the offense-element rule reaches nonrecidivist facts that raise either endpoint of the legally prescribed sentencing range.

*McMillan v. Pennsylvania*: overruled in part; judicial factfinding may not raise a mandatory minimum through a nonrecidivist offense fact.

*Almendarez-Torres v. United States*, decided March 24, 1998: distinguished; its maximum-increasing prior-conviction holding remains intact, and the present decision does not alter the treatment of prior convictions.

Judicial sentencing factfinding: clarified; judges may find facts used to select punishment within the range authorized by the indictment and verdict or plea.

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to vacate the brandishing-based sentence and conduct resentencing or any other proceeding independently permitted by law.

Effect: Harris's § 924(c) conviction remains intact, but the seven-year mandatory minimum may not rest on the judge-found brandishing fact. #### Doctrinal Effect

*Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, decided March 4, 1998: distinguished; resolving a legal contention that itself determines whether § 3742(a)(1)'s statutory condition exists is not an assumption of jurisdiction to decide unrelated merits.

*Koon v. United States; Powell v. United States*, decided June 13, 1996: followed; a legal misunderstanding of departure authority is reviewable, but an understood discretionary refusal is not reviewable absent an independent statutory ground.

*Kyles v. Whitley*, decided April 19, 1995, and *Giglio v. United States*, 405 U.S. 150: declined to extend; their trial-fairness disclosure rules do not establish a categorical pre-plea timetable for material impeachment information.

*United States v. Mezzanatto*, decided January 18, 1995: followed; trial-related protections are not categorically nonwaivable, subject to ordinary requirements of knowledge and voluntariness and to case-specific objections based on fraud, coercion, or lack of understanding.

#### Ruling Beyond the Court

Mandate: The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit is reversed; that court shall leave the District Court's sentence undisturbed.

Effect: Ruiz receives no new sentencing proceeding. The Government may condition a plea offer on a knowing and voluntary relinquishment of pre-plea impeachment disclosure, but the judgment does not decide a plea procured through material governmental misrepresentation, deliberate concealment of known information directly negating guilt or falsifying the factual basis, ineffective assistance of counsel, or another independently available claim.
