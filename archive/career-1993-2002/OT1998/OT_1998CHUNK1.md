#### Issue 1: Personal privacy of short-term invited residential users

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** Simulated *United States v. Fields* recognizes a legitimate privacy interest in a substantially screened bedroom for a permitted user and that user's invited guest even though neither held the lease. *Fields* does not decide whether a short-term visitor invited into a home solely for a commercial transaction possesses the same personal privacy interest.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** No separate reusable guest-status doctrine is registered. Simulated *Fields* supplies the closest controlling relationship-and-screening application, while the short-term commercial-guest boundary remains open.
- **Other Governing Law:** Fourth Amendment; *Katz v. United States*; *Rakas v. Illinois*; *Minnesota v. Olson*.
- **Other Authority Used:** Property ownership and overnight residence may strongly support a privacy claim, but the Fourth Amendment inquiry remains personal and turns on the claimant's relationship to the place and the privacy actually asserted there.
- **Open Point:** Whether host-authorized, short-term occupants using a substantially screened residential interior for private activity possess a personal Fourth Amendment privacy interest despite the visit's commercial purpose and brevity.

#### Issue 2: Deliberate exploitation of a narrow gap in substantially closed blinds from a lawful public vantage

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** Simulated *United States v. Fields*, together with its treatment of *United States v. Pinson*, holds that officers conduct a search when they deliberately obtain otherwise unavailable information from inside a substantially screened home by sense-enhancing technology or by entering a fenced, nonpublic vantage without lawful access. *Fields* expressly leaves ordinary unaided observation from a lawful public vantage unaffected.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** `Deliberate acquisition of otherwise unavailable home-interior information.` The current rule preserves ordinary observation from a lawful public vantage.
- **Other Governing Law:** Fourth Amendment; *Katz v. United States*; lawful-vantage and plain-view principles; *California v. Ciraolo*; *Florida v. Riley*.
- **Other Authority Used:** *Ciraolo* and *Riley* protect unaided observation of information actually exposed to lawful public observation. They do not independently resolve whether deliberate police exploitation of a narrow accidental aperture in an otherwise substantially screened home must always be treated as public exposure.
- **Open Point:** Current simulated law does not decide whether lawful physical presence is categorically sufficient when officers deliberately use a narrow gap in otherwise closed blinds to investigate the interior of a home.

#### B. Stone's approved position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the Minnesota Supreme Court's suppression judgment.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** The suppression judgment remains in force. The Minnesota courts may conduct further proceedings consistent with that judgment and with issues not resolved here. No broader suppression rule is prescribed beyond evidence derived from the unlawful observation under the ordinarily governing causation and exclusion principles.

#### Issue 1: Personal privacy of short-term invited residential users

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches respondents' personal Fourth Amendment interest because suppression cannot rest solely on Thompson's privacy. The issue was decided below and is necessary to Stone's judgment.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone extends simulated *Fields* at its unresolved guest-status boundary. Host authorization, actual private use of the residential interior, and deliberate screening of that interior are sufficient here to establish a personal privacy interest. Brief duration, commercial purpose, and the illegality of the activity do not by themselves extinguish that interest once the host has admitted the visitors to use the screened interior privately.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Short-term invited occupants who are admitted by the resident to use a substantially screened residential interior for private activity possess a personal Fourth Amendment privacy interest in that interior even when the visit is commercial and brief.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Thompson admitted Carter and Johns into the apartment and allowed them to conduct their activity inside the screened home rather than in a place held open to the public. Simulated *Fields* already rejects leasehold title as indispensable and recognizes privacy for a permitted user and an invited guest. Stone extends that relationship-based protection to these host-authorized visitors because the Fourth Amendment protects the personal privacy actually exercised in the home, not the legality or economic character of the concealed activity.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not establish privacy for a person lacking the resident's permission, a visitor remaining in an area held open to the public, or a person who knowingly exposes the relevant activity to general observation. It does not establish that every social, commercial, or momentary visitor automatically has Fourth Amendment protection throughout a residence.

#### Issue 2: Deliberate exploitation of a narrow gap in substantially closed blinds from a lawful public vantage

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches whether the officer's observation was a search. He accepts for purposes of decision that the officer remained in a place lawfully accessible to the public and does not rely on trespass, curtilage entry, or a restricted physical vantage.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone openly modifies the lawful-public-vantage boundary stated in simulated *Fields*. The prior rule leaves ordinary unaided observation from a lawful public vantage unaffected. Stone would retain that rule for information genuinely exposed to ordinary public view, but would not make lawful physical location categorically dispositive when police deliberately exploit a narrow aperture in an otherwise substantially screened home to investigate its interior. The legal defect in a categorical public-vantage rule is that it makes an occupant's substantial effort to screen the home constitutionally meaningless whenever a small accidental gap permits a determined observer to peer inside. Stone's replacement boundary is that ordinary observation of an exposed interior remains lawful, while deliberate investigative exploitation of a narrow gap in an otherwise substantially closed residential privacy barrier is a search. The technology rule, restricted-vantage rule, ordinary open-window rule, and plain-view requirement of lawful access remain untouched.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Police conduct a Fourth Amendment search when, from a lawful public location, they deliberately exploit a narrow gap in otherwise substantially closed blinds to conduct investigative observation of the screened interior of a home. Lawful physical vantage alone does not convert the screened residential interior into publicly exposed space.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The officer's lawful location is accepted rather than rewritten. The blinds substantially screened the apartment, yet the officer deliberately used the narrow opening to watch activity inside for approximately twelve to fifteen minutes. The passerby's earlier ability to glimpse activity could justify investigation and could contribute to probable cause, but it did not categorically publish the entire screened interior or authorize sustained police inspection through the same aperture. Stone therefore changes *Fields* candidly at the lawful-vantage boundary instead of falsely characterizing the officer's location as restricted. The change protects deliberate residential screening while preserving ordinary observation of what occupants actually leave open to general view.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not prohibit unaided observation through an open window or door, observation of an interior plainly exposed to ordinary passersby, or police reliance on information a passerby voluntarily reports. Duration alone does not create a search, and the decision creates no general rule that every glance through blinds requires a warrant. It addresses deliberate investigative use of a narrow aperture in an otherwise substantially closed residential privacy barrier.
