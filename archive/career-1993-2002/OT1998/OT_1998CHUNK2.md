#### Issue 1: Standing and vehicle

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone resolves Article III before the statutory and constitutional merits. The *Glavin* plaintiffs establish concrete representational injuries. The House, unlike the individual legislators in simulated *Raines*, sues as the authorized chamber itself and alleges that the challenged census method will directly determine the constitutional composition of that institution.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** A substantially likely loss or dilution of representation caused by the announced methodology supplies individual standing under ordinary Article III rules. The House also has institutional standing in this posture because the institution itself is the plaintiff and the challenged governmental method directly fixes the allocation of the seats through which its constitutionally prescribed composition exists. This does not convert ordinary legislative disagreement or lost political influence into Article III injury.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The properly supported *Glavin* plaintiffs have Article III standing because the announced methodology creates a substantially likely representational injury redressable by judgment. The House, acting as an authorized institutional plaintiff, also has standing to challenge a census method alleged to determine its composition unlawfully.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The *Glavin* record identifies specific plaintiffs facing likely interstate or intrastate representational consequences. The House asserts a different injury: not disagreement with executive policy or diminished influence, but an alleged defect in the process that determines how the House itself is constituted. Because both injuries are concrete enough for their respective plaintiffs and a judgment resolves them, Stone reaches the merits in both dockets.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Individual Members do not acquire standing merely from reduced influence, disagreement with census policy, or a generalized interest in lawful administration. The institutional holding is confined to the House itself acting through an authorized suit concerning its own constitutionally prescribed composition.

#### Issue 2: Statutory authorization for apportionment sampling

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the statutory question after resolving standing.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Section 141(a) expressly authorizes the Secretary, when taking the decennial census, to determine its form and content "including the use of sampling procedures and special surveys." Section 195 does not silently withdraw that authority for apportionment. After the 1976 amendment, § 195's "Except ... the Secretary shall" formulation removes apportionment from the provision's mandatory-use command; it does not convert the exception into a prohibition that defeats § 141(a)'s express decennial-census authorization. Statistical sampling is an important modern accuracy tool, particularly where a conventional enumeration predictably leaves a differential undercount, but its practical value does not itself create authority; § 141(a) supplies the authority.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Sections 141(a) and 195 permit, but do not require, the Secretary to use statistical sampling in determining the population for congressional apportionment. Section 195's apportionment exception means that sampling is not mandatory for that determination, not that it is forbidden.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Congress expressly added sampling procedures to the Secretary's decennial-census authority. At the same time it changed § 195 so that sampling became mandatory when feasible outside apportionment. Reading the apportionment exception as removing only that mandate gives operative effect to both provisions: sampling is required when feasible for other Title 13 functions, while the Secretary retains § 141(a) discretion in the apportionment census. The contrary reading turns an exception from a mandatory command into an unexpressed prohibition and unnecessarily narrows Congress's explicit methodological grant.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not require a particular sampling technique, authorize a method outside § 141(a), or permit the Secretary to disregard any separate statutory constraint. It holds only that §§ 141 and 195 do not categorically forbid sampling in the apportionment determination.

#### Issue 3: Enumeration Clause

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Because Stone concludes that the Census Act authorizes the methodology, the constitutional question can no longer be avoided and is reached.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Simulated *Wisconsin v. City of New York* controls. The Constitution requires an actual enumeration but expressly commits the "Manner" of that enumeration to Congress. An authorized statistical methodology is constitutional when it remains genuinely directed to determining the population and bears a reasonable relationship to achieving a complete and accurate apportionment count. The Clause does not freeze the census to face-to-face or person-by-person techniques that predate modern statistical methods. Accuracy matters because apportionment depends on relative population, and statistical sampling can be especially important when the existing method predictably undercounts identifiable populations. That importance supports the reasonableness of a lawful methodology; it does not make sampling constitutionally mandatory.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The Enumeration Clause permits Congress and the Secretary to use authorized statistical sampling as part of the decennial census when the methodology bears a reasonable relationship to accomplishing an actual enumeration for apportionment. The challenged 2000 plan satisfies that standard on the present record because it is designed to correct persistent undercount and improve the completeness and accuracy of the population totals used for representation.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The record identifies a chronic and differential undercount and a methodology designed to use statistical estimation to improve the final population totals. Simulated *Wisconsin* already rejects the proposition that the Constitution selects one judicially preferred census technique; it asks whether the chosen method remains within delegated authority and reasonably serves the enumeration. Sampling here operates as a method of determining the population, not as a substitute for the constitutional objective itself. A rule forbidding statistical correction simply because the technique estimates some unobserved cases would elevate an older administrative practice over the Clause's express assignment of methodological choice and its representational purpose.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not constitutionalize every estimate, immunize partisan manipulation, excuse a methodology disconnected from genuine population measurement, or require sampling whenever experts predict greater accuracy. A materially different method remains reviewable for statutory authorization, reasoned administration, intentional discrimination, and the reasonable-relationship requirement of simulated *Wisconsin*.
