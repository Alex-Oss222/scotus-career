## Wyoming v. Houghton

### Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** Wyoming v. Houghton
- **Citation or Docket:** 526 U.S. 295; No. 98-184; 956 P.2d 363 (Wyo. 1998)
- **October Term:** 1998
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued; merits decision pending.
- **Simulated Event Date:** April 5, 1999
- **Question Presented:** Whether probable cause to search a vehicle for contraband automatically authorizes police to search a passenger’s closed purse found in the vehicle.
- **Question Granted:** Whether probable cause to search a vehicle for contraband automatically authorizes police to search a passenger’s closed purse found in the vehicle.
- **Legal Field:** Fourth Amendment, automobile search of a passenger’s closed belongings
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Wyoming Supreme Court suppressed methamphetamine found in Houghton’s purse, holding that probable cause directed to the driver and car did not justify the passenger-container search.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** Reverse and adopt a rule permitting search of every container in the vehicle capable of holding the object of probable cause.
- **Material Facts and Record:** An officer stopped the car for traffic violations, saw a syringe in the driver’s shirt pocket, and heard the driver admit drug use. After the two female passengers were removed and frisked without finding contraband, the officer searched behind their seating area and found a closed cloth “lady’s purse.” He opened it, found Houghton’s driver’s license in a wallet, and only then did Houghton state that the purse was hers. The Wyoming Supreme Court nevertheless treated the purse, before opening, as a personal effect the officer knew or reasonably should have known belonged to one of the nonsuspect female passengers rather than the male driver.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** No additional preservation, jurisdictional, finality, or vehicle defect is identified beyond the matters stated in the issue blocks.
- **Relevant Dates:** Argued January 12, 1999; simulated opinion-delivery date April 5, 1999.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None.
- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** Official U.S. Reports or controlling lower-court opinion for the citation, record, posture, and historical disposition; Supreme Court docket and argument calendar for the supplied dates; Oyez for case metadata and oral-argument chronology.

#### Historical Supreme Court comparator

- **Historical Outcome and Vote:** Reversed, 6-3.
- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Scalia delivered the Court’s opinion; Stevens dissented joined by Souter and Ginsburg.
- **Historical Holding:** The automobile exception permits search of a passenger’s belongings found in the car when they could conceal the object of probable cause.

#### Issue 1: Independent privacy in a passenger's closed purse

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** Simulated *United States v. Wellons* holds that a person's lack of privacy in another person's vehicle does not eliminate the person's independent privacy interest in closed luggage found inside. Ownership, possession, control, and steps taken to preserve privacy govern that interest.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Independent privacy in closed luggage.
- **Other Governing Law:** Fourth Amendment; *Katz v. United States*; *Rakas v. Illinois*.
- **Other Authority Used:** A closed purse ultimately established as the passenger’s personal property is materially different from an unclaimed or commonly controlled vehicle container; the timing of specific identification remains relevant to the scope inquiry.

#### Issue 2: Scope of vehicle probable cause

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** Simulated *Pennsylvania v. Labron; Pennsylvania v. Kilgore* permits a warrantless search of a readily mobile vehicle and containers within the scope of probable cause, without a separate exigency showing. Its holding expressly leaves ownership and scope questions unresolved. Simulated *United States v. Wellons* permits a container search only when the automobile exception or another lawful authority reaches that container.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** No separate registered rule resolves whether probable cause directed to one occupant reaches a clearly passenger-owned closed container. The independent-privacy rule in simulated *Wellons* remains operative.
- **Other Governing Law:** Fourth Amendment; *United States v. Ross*; *California v. Acevedo*; ordinary probable-cause scope principles.
- **Other Authority Used:** The Government bears the burden of establishing that the warrant exception and the scope of probable cause reach the place searched.
- **Open Point:** Whether probable cause arising from the driver’s possession and admission extends to a closed purse that, before opening, the officer knew or reasonably should have known was the personal effect of a nonsuspect passenger, even though Houghton did not specifically identify it as hers until after it was opened.

#### B. Stone's approved position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the Wyoming Supreme Court's suppression judgment.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** No separate remedial instruction is required. The purse evidence remains suppressed, and the state courts may conduct any further proceedings supported by untainted evidence and issues not resolved here.

#### Issue 1: Independent privacy in a passenger's closed purse

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches Houghton's personal privacy interest because simulated *Wellons* makes it independent of her lack of control over the vehicle.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Houghton retained a personal privacy interest in the closed purse that was established as hers. Its presence in another person’s car did not merge her container privacy into the driver’s diminished automobile privacy. Her specific identification occurred after the initial opening, so Stone does not use pre-search identification of Houghton as the factual basis for suppression.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A passenger retains a personal Fourth Amendment privacy interest in a closed purse established as the passenger’s own, notwithstanding the passenger’s lack of a privacy interest in the surrounding vehicle.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The purse was closed and was ultimately established as Houghton’s. The officer did not learn Houghton’s specific ownership until after opening it, so Stone does not invent an earlier identification or personal-possession finding. Once ownership was established, simulated *Wellons* permits Houghton to challenge whether lawful automobile-search authority reached her container.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not decide an abandoned, disclaimed, unclaimed, jointly controlled, or inventory-searched container.

#### Issue 2: Scope of vehicle probable cause

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the scope of probable cause because Wyoming relies solely on the automobile exception and simulated *Labron* expressly reserved this ownership-and-scope question.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Probable cause directed to the driver does not automatically reach a closed personal container that officers know or reasonably should know belongs to a nonsuspect passenger rather than the driver. The Government must identify facts creating a fair probability that the object of the search is in that passenger’s container. Capacity to conceal the object is necessary to scope, but on Stone’s view it is not sufficient when the record objectively identifies the container as a passenger’s personal effect and the facts point only to another occupant.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** When vehicle probable cause arises from a driver’s possession and admissions, police may not search a closed purse that they know or reasonably should know is a nonsuspect passenger’s personal effect without facts connecting that passenger or the purse to the suspected contraband.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The syringe was in the driver’s shirt pocket, and the driver admitted drug use. Before opening the purse, the officer had removed and frisked the two female passengers without finding contraband and then found a closed “lady’s purse” behind their seating area. Houghton did not specifically identify it as hers until after the officer opened it and found her license, but the Wyoming Supreme Court treated the pre-opening circumstances as showing that the officer knew or reasonably should have known it was a nonsuspect passenger’s personal effect rather than the driver’s. No pre-opening fact connected either passenger or that purse to the suspected contraband. Simulated *Labron* removes a separate exigency requirement, but Stone does not read that rule to convert cause focused on the driver into cause to inspect a passenger’s objectively identifiable personal effect.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not bar a search when probable cause fairly encompasses the passenger or the container, ownership is uncertain or shared, the object is observed in the container, consent is given, a valid inventory applies, or another lawful authority reaches the property.

## In re Sealed Case

  1. Whether Federal Rule of Evidence 501 and federal common law recognize a Secret Service protective-function privilege against compelled testimony concerning information obtained while protecting the President.
  2. If such a privilege exists, whether it is absolute or qualified, and what showing of criminal investigative need is sufficient to overcome it.
- **Legal Field:** Issue 1, Rule 501 and recognition of a protective-function privilege. Issue 2, scope and overcoming standard for any recognized privilege.

#### Issue 1: Recognition of a protective-function privilege

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** *Jaffee v. Redmond*, 518 U.S. 1, recognizes a Rule 501 privilege only after examining established confidential relationships, public and private interests served by confidentiality, evidentiary costs, and administrable boundaries. Once recognized, the privilege is not subjected to ad hoc balancing in every case.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Psychotherapist-patient privilege.
- **Other Governing Law:** Federal Rule of Evidence 501; the federal grand jury's evidentiary authority; surviving authority including *United States v. Nixon*, *Trammel v. United States*, and *Branzburg v. Hayes*.
- **Other Authority Used:** Separation-of-powers principles recognize the President's protective needs but do not themselves create an evidentiary privilege for observations by protective personnel.

#### Issue 2: Absolute or qualified scope and overcoming need

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** *Jaffee* rejects routine case-by-case balancing after recognition of a categorical confidential-communications privilege, but it does not support recognition of a privilege lacking the required predicate or a privilege covering ordinary observations rather than protected communications.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Psychotherapist-patient privilege.
- **Other Governing Law:** Rule 501; ordinary grand-jury relevance and law-enforcement need; judicial authority to use sealing, protective orders, and tailored questioning where independently lawful.
- **Open Point:** No current simulated law establishes a distinct overcoming test for a Secret Service privilege because no such privilege has been recognized.

#### B. Stone's approved position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the judgment of the D.C. Circuit.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** No separate remedial instruction is required. The order compelling testimony remains enforceable subject to ordinary evidentiary objections and any lawful protective measures imposed by the supervising court.

#### Issue 1: Recognition of a protective-function privilege

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the merits. The compulsion order and Rule 501 question are properly before the Court.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies Rule 501 without creating the proposed privilege. The asserted relationship is operational rather than a historically protected confidential relationship, the proposed rule would cover observations as well as communications, and the record does not establish that ordinary compelled testimony materially impairs the Secret Service's protective function. Institutional importance cannot substitute for the legal and evidentiary predicate required to create a privilege.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Federal Rule of Evidence 501 does not recognize, on this record, a Secret Service protective-function privilege against federal grand-jury testimony concerning information obtained while protecting the President.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Privileges withhold relevant evidence and therefore require a source-grounded justification and workable boundary. The Secret Service's prediction that testimony could reduce presidential cooperation deserves serious consideration, but no record finding establishes that consequence, and the proposed contemporaneous-felony exception is poorly matched to later-discovered criminal significance. Ordinary confidentiality practices and tailored judicial protections can address genuine security concerns without creating categorical immunity from the grand jury. Rule 501 does not authorize the Court to convert an important executive function into an evidentiary privilege merely because testimony may be inconvenient or sensitive.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding is confined to federal grand-jury testimony and does not decide classified information, state secrets, executive privilege, physical security details, or a materially different proceeding and record.

#### Issue 2: Absolute or qualified scope and overcoming need

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone does not reach the conditional choice between an absolute and qualified privilege because Issue 1 rejects recognition of the asserted privilege.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** No special showing of investigative need is required to overcome a privilege that does not exist. The grand jury remains subject to ordinary relevance, legality, and court-supervision rules.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Because Rule 501 recognizes no protective-function privilege on this record, the Court establishes no absolute or qualified form and no privilege-specific overcoming standard.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Selecting an overcoming test after declining to recognize the underlying privilege would create an advisory rule. The supervising court may still protect operational security through ordinary lawful means without altering the evidentiary entitlement.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** A future claim under another recognized privilege or a materially different statutory or national-security source must be analyzed under that source's own elements and limits.

- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Unlike notice-prejudice, the agency rule does not define an insurance-policy obligation. It determines who receives claim notice for an ERISA plan and therefore governs the plan's administrative structure.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not decide voluntarily assumed agency, fact-specific actual authority, or apparent authority established under nonpreempted principles.

#### B. Stone's approved position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the injunction against California's first-year lower-benefit rule.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** California shall treat otherwise eligible bona fide residents equally without using recent arrival or the benefit level in a former State to reduce first-year benefits. Bona fide residence verification and equal prospective eligibility rules remain available.

#### Issue 1: Equal state citizenship after migration

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the facial classification applied to established bona fide residents.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** The Fourteenth Amendment makes a United States citizen a citizen of the State of bona fide residence. California may not make that current state citizenship inferior for twelve months solely by reference to recent arrival or former residence.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A bona fide new resident is entitled to equal state citizenship and may not receive lower generally available welfare benefits solely because of recent arrival or the benefit level in a former State.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The plaintiffs are current California residents who satisfy the State's ordinary eligibility rules. The reduced payment is triggered only by recent migration and the former State's benefit level, the kind of unequal treatment *Shapiro*, *Dunn*, and *Zobel* forbid.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not prevent bona fide residence verification, equal prospective requirements, or distinctions tied to services or contributions earned elsewhere.

#### Issue 2: Congressional authorization

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches Congress's asserted permission because California relies on it to defend the classification.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Congress may structure and condition a federal welfare program, but it may not authorize a State to violate the Fourteenth Amendment's equal state-citizenship guarantee.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Congress may not authorize a State to violate the Fourteenth Amendment guarantee of equal state citizenship.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The federal provision cannot lower the constitutional floor applicable to state administration. Because the state classification is unconstitutional, federal permission does not save it.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not decide whether Congress could restructure the underlying federal program through a constitutionally distinct nationwide rule.
