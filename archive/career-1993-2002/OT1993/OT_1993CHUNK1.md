- **Question Presented:** Whether the Government may seize real property for civil forfeiture without prior notice and a hearing, and whether the delay between seizure and institution of forfeiture proceedings barred the action.
- **Question Granted:** Whether the Government may seize real property for civil forfeiture without prior notice and a hearing, and whether the delay between seizure and institution of forfeiture proceedings barred the action.
- **Legal Field:** Fifth Amendment procedural due process and federal civil forfeiture, including the legal effect of statutory timing directives.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Ninth Circuit held that the warrant-based seizure of Good's home without pre-seizure notice and an opportunity to be heard violated due process and that the Government's failure to comply with the timing directives incorporated through 19 U.S.C. §§ 1602-1604 independently required dismissal even though the action was filed within 19 U.S.C. § 1621's five-year period.

The constitutional challenge is to the procedure used before seizure, not to the ultimate merits of forfeiture. The timing issue is statutory: whether noncompliance with the internal timing directives of 19 U.S.C. §§ 1602-1604 requires dismissal of a forfeiture action concededly commenced within § 1621's five-year limitations period.

#### Issue 1: Pre-seizure process for real property

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the merits. No jurisdictional or preservation defect bars review; exigency is an exception the Government must establish on the actual record.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether, absent a demonstrated exigency, the United States may seize an occupied home for civil forfeiture before notice and a hearing.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies received procedural due process. Real property is immobile, the private possessory interest in a home is acute, and a lis pendens, restraining order, or other less intrusive device ordinarily protects the Government while an adversarial hearing occurs. Immediate seizure remains available on particularized proof of exigency, not from the forfeiture label alone.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Except on a demonstrated exigency, the Fifth Amendment requires notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard before the Government seizes real property for civil forfeiture.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Good was deprived of possession and control of his home through an ex parte procedure. Unlike a vessel or automobile, the property could not be removed from the jurisdiction, and the Government identified no record-supported emergency requiring immediate occupation. The available preservation devices defeat the claimed need to seize first and hear later, so the seizure violated due process.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding concerns real property. It does not decide the procedures governing readily movable contraband or personal property, and it does not require a pre-seizure hearing where the Government proves a genuine, particularized exigency.

#### Issue 2: Effect of the statutory forfeiture timing directives

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the statutory issue. The parties agree that the forfeiture action was commenced within § 1621's five-year limitations period; the question is therefore the legal consequence of any failure to comply with §§ 1602-1604, not calculation of the limitations period.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether noncompliance with the internal timing directives of 19 U.S.C. §§ 1602-1604 requires dismissal of a forfeiture action concededly commenced within the five-year period prescribed by § 1621.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies the received rule governing statutory timing commands. Congress's use of mandatory language imposes duties on the responsible officials, but a court does not automatically convert violation of those duties into loss of governmental authority or dismissal when Congress has specified no such consequence. Sections 1602-1604 require prompt governmental action, while § 1621 separately establishes the limitations period for commencement of the forfeiture suit. Nothing in the statutory scheme makes dismissal of an otherwise timely action the consequence of violating the internal timing directives.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A forfeiture action commenced within the five-year period prescribed by 19 U.S.C. § 1621 may not be dismissed solely because responsible officials failed to comply with the timing directives of §§ 1602-1604, absent statutory language making dismissal or loss of authority the consequence of that noncompliance.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The Government waited approximately four and one-half years before commencing the action, but the parties agree that this remained within the limitations period Congress enacted. Sections 1602-1604 direct officials to report and commence proceedings with dispatch, yet Congress did not state that violation of those directives extinguishes the forfeiture claim. Treating them as an additional judicially created statute of limitations would attach a consequence Congress did not prescribe and would duplicate the function § 1621 already performs. The Ninth Circuit's contrary statutory judgment therefore cannot stand. This conclusion concerns the statutory consequence of delay and does not erase the separate constitutional defect in the manner of seizure.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not hold that §§ 1602-1604 are optional or that governmental delay can never have legal consequences. He holds only that those provisions do not themselves require dismissal of an action filed within § 1621 when Congress did not prescribe dismissal as the sanction. Any independently preserved constitutional challenge to delay remains governed by its own doctrine.

- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether Albright's surrender pursuant to an arrest warrant and the ensuing judicial prohibition on leaving Illinois constituted Fourth Amendment restraint after his release on bond, while the mere existence of the prosecution did not.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone makes a limited application and refinement of existing seizure doctrine without changing the probable-cause standard, burden of proof, or standard of review. A criminal prosecution standing alone is not a seizure. Surrender in response to an arrest warrant is a seizure because the person submits to governmental authority. Release from physical custody does not automatically end the Fourth Amendment inquiry when criminal process continues to impose a significant restraint on movement attributable to that arrest. Albright's judicially enforceable prohibition on leaving Illinois without permission was sufficiently substantial to keep him under Fourth Amendment restraint while it remained in force. Hodari D.'s statement that seizure is a single act does not control this situation because Albright did not escape governmental control; the government changed the mechanism of restraint from physical custody to binding judicial conditions. Ordinary Fourth Amendment principles, including Malley and Franks, govern any claim that an officer intentionally or recklessly caused or maintained the restraint through materially false information or without an objectively reasonable basis for seeking the warrant. The later dismissal of the charge for failure to state an Illinois offense does not itself establish absence of constitutional probable cause; that inquiry would require examination of the offense invoked, the facts supplied, and any material falsehoods or omissions.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** If lawfully reached, Albright was seized when he surrendered pursuant to the arrest warrant and remained under Fourth Amendment restraint while a court order prohibited him from leaving Illinois without permission; the mere pendency of criminal charges, standing alone, is not a seizure. This holding identifies the governing constitutional source but does not establish Fourth Amendment liability on the undeveloped claim.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Albright submitted to the warrant and was therefore intentionally restrained by governmental authority. Gerstein recognizes that pretrial liberty exists in degrees and that significant noncustodial restraints remain constitutionally relevant. The interstate-travel prohibition materially limited Albright's freedom of movement through an enforceable command arising directly from the arrest and criminal process, so release on bond did not restore him to the position of an ordinary citizen free of governmental restraint. This conclusion does not convert every prosecution, court appearance, or bond condition into a seizure. It answers the record before the Court while leaving unresolved whether Oliver lacked probable cause, supplied false information, caused the continuing restraint, or incurred damages liability. Because Albright did not present those questions as a Fourth Amendment claim, the answer does not alter Stone's vote to affirm.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not hold that every defendant released on bond remains seized, that a promise to appear or the pendency of charges alone is sufficient, or that dismissal of a charge establishes lack of probable cause. He reserves the duration and treatment of lesser conditions, favorable-termination requirements, accrual and limitations, causation, qualified immunity, and damages.

#### Issue 2: Totality analysis, proportionality, and nonmaximization

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the statutory claims. The claims remain limited to the geographic and institutional units lawfully litigated below. Because the record summary does not establish whether each district counted as an opportunity district actually functions as one under the relevant electorate and voting patterns, Stone does not make that factual determination in the first instance.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** What legal weight rough proportionality and the ability to draw additional majority-minority districts receive within the § 2 totality inquiry, and what the districts counted in a proportionality comparison must represent.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone clarifies the evidentiary role of proportionality within the existing statutory totality; he does not create a separate vote-dilution test. Section 2 protects electoral opportunity, not a racial allocation of seats. Vote dilution exists when an electoral structure, interacting with racial bloc voting and the relevant social and political conditions, leaves protected voters with less opportunity to participate effectively and elect candidates of choice. Majority-minority districts are one means of measuring or securing that opportunity, not the definition of the statutory right. Rough proportionality is relevant because it may evidence opportunity, but its weight depends on whether the districts being counted actually function as opportunities under the relevant electorate and voting patterns. Neither proportionality nor its absence decides the claim, and failure to maximize additional majority-minority districts is not itself dilution. Packing, fragmentation, crossover voting, citizenship, registration, turnout, and comparable conditions are evidence when the record makes them relevant to actual opportunity; they are not mandatory factors or a separate anti-manipulation framework.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Section 2 protects electoral opportunity rather than a racial allocation of seats. Rough proportionality may bear on that opportunity, but neither proportionality nor its absence is dispositive; the court must determine under the enacted totality whether the districts counted as opportunities function as such under the actual electorate and voting patterns. Failure to maximize additional majority-minority districts is not itself vote dilution.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The statute asks whether protected voters have less opportunity than other members of the electorate to participate in the political process and elect representatives of their choice; it does not guarantee a racial share of legislative seats. A count of nominal majority-minority districts is therefore only an imperfect measure of the protected opportunity. Apparent proportionality may overstate opportunity where packing, fragmentation, registration or citizenship patterns, turnout, or actual bloc voting prevent a counted district from functioning as an electoral opportunity. Conversely, the mathematical possibility of drawing another majority-minority district does not establish unequal opportunity where the existing structure, considered under the full statutory totality, permits effective participation and the election of candidates of choice. The district court treated the relationship between drawable districts and proportionality too conclusively, while the present record summary does not permit Stone to decide how each challenged district actually functions. Vacatur and remand are therefore required.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone creates no proportional-representation entitlement, proportionality safe harbor, maximization requirement, or independent packing or anti-manipulation test. Nothing in the holding assumes that numerical majority status is the only circumstance in which minority voters may possess meaningful electoral opportunity; the legal status of an influence-district claim is reserved because its resolution is unnecessary here.
