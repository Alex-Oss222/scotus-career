### Harris v. Forklift Systems, Inc., 510 U.S. 17 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** 92-1168.
**Decided:** November 9, 1993.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

The lower courts shall reconsider the established record under the correct hostile-environment standard. The Court makes no finding of liability.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O’Connor | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence | Scalia | No other Justice | Supports reversal and remand | Joins the Court while noting the unavoidable judgment involved in applying the statutory standard |
| Concurrence | Ginsburg | No other Justice | Supports reversal and remand | Emphasizes discriminatory alteration of working conditions rather than clinical or economic injury |

#### Holding

**Question decided:** Threshold for an actionable hostile work environment.

**Holding:** A Title VII hostile-work-environment plaintiff need not prove serious psychological injury. The discriminatory conduct must be sufficiently severe or pervasive to create an environment that is objectively hostile or abusive and that the plaintiff subjectively perceives as abusive.

**Authority:** Opinion of the Court by O’Connor, joined as stated in the Opinions table above; Complete opinion. The proposition is controlling at that stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The inquiry considers the totality of the circumstances. Relevant considerations may include frequency, severity, physical threat or humiliation, and interference with work, but the list is nonexclusive and no single consideration is indispensable. Psychological harm may be relevant evidence, but it is not an element.

**Application:** The district court found repeated sex-based humiliation but attached dispositive weight to the lack of serious psychological harm. Because that erroneous requirement may have affected its ultimate totality assessment, remand is necessary.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Ordinary incivility is not actionable merely because it is offensive. The conduct must be connected to a protected characteristic, meet the objective and subjective requirements, and be attributable to the employer under the governing liability rules.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Meritor is clarified. Serious psychological injury is not required, and the complete circumstances determine whether discriminatory conduct altered the conditions of employment by creating an objectively and subjectively abusive environment.

### Florence County School District Four v. Carter, 510 U.S. 7 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** 92-1523.
**Decided:** November 9, 1993.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg. No opposing Justice.

The reimbursement judgment remains in force, subject to the district court’s continuing equitable authority to ensure that the amount represents an appropriate and reasonable private placement.

Justice O’Connor delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg.

**Opinion of the Court:** O’Connor, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg.

#### Holding

**Question decided:** Reimbursement for a parent-selected private placement.

**Holding:** IDEA permits reimbursement when a public agency failed to offer a free appropriate public education and the parents obtained an education otherwise appropriate under the Act. The private school’s lack of state approval and failure to comply with procedures written for public-agency placements do not categorically bar relief.

**Authority:** O’Connor, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg; the proposition is controlling.

**Controlling explanation:** Parents who unilaterally reject a public placement bear the financial risk of their decision. Reimbursement becomes available only if they establish that the public offer violated IDEA and that the private education was appropriate to the child. Even then, § 1415(e)(2) leaves the court with equitable discretion to reduce or deny an unreasonable amount.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Reimbursement is not available for a placement that is merely preferred, educationally inappropriate, or unreasonably expensive. The decision does not prevent States from regulating private schools through otherwise valid laws.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Burlington is clarified. Equitable reimbursement does not require parents to recreate every procedural and approval feature governing the public program they lawfully rejected. Rowley’s substantive appropriateness requirement remains applicable to the private education.

### United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property, 510 U.S. 43 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** 92-1180.
**Decided:** December 13, 1993.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Relief or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-seizure due process | Affirmed, 6-3 | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | O’Connor, Scalia, Thomas | The warrant-based occupation of Good’s home without prior process was unconstitutional absent a demonstrated exigency. |
| Statutory timing | Reversed, 9-0 | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | No opposing vote | Noncompliance with §§ 1602 through 1604 does not require dismissal of an action timely under § 1621. |

**Judgment, mandate, and remand:**

The judgment is affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded. The forfeiture action may continue because it was commenced within § 1621. The lower courts must separately determine the lawful consequences of the unconstitutional pre-hearing seizure.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg in full; O’Connor, Scalia, and Thomas as to the statutory-timing holding | Supports both judgment components | Full due-process and timing holdings for the six-Justice coalition; timing holding for all nine |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | O’Connor | Scalia, Thomas | Agrees with the timing reversal and opposes the due-process affirmance | Would treat the ex parte warrant procedure as constitutionally adequate |

#### Question 1: Pre-seizure process for real property

**Question decided:** Pre-seizure process for real property.

**Holding:** Except upon particularized proof of exigent circumstances, the Fifth Amendment requires notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard before the Government seizes real property for civil forfeiture.

**Authority:** Opinion of the Court by Kennedy, joined as stated in the Opinions table above; Full due-process and timing holdings for the six-Justice coalition; timing holding for all nine. The proposition is controlling at that stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Government bears the obligation to establish an actual exigency justifying immediate seizure. The forfeiture label alone is insufficient. A lis pendens, restraining order, posting of process, or other less intrusive measure ordinarily preserves the Government’s interests while an adversarial hearing occurs.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The rule concerns real property. It does not determine the treatment of readily movable personal property or contraband, and it permits immediate action when the Government proves a genuine exigency.

#### Question 2: Effect of the statutory timing directives

**Question decided:** Effect of the statutory timing directives.

**Holding:** A forfeiture action filed within § 1621’s five-year limitation period may not be dismissed solely because officials failed to comply with §§ 1602 through 1604, where Congress did not make dismissal or loss of authority the consequence of noncompliance.

**Authority:** Opinion of the Court by Kennedy, joined as stated in the Opinions table above; Full due-process and timing holdings for the six-Justice coalition; timing holding for all nine. The proposition is controlling at that stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** Mandatory timing language imposes duties on responsible officials, but a court does not create a forfeiture-ending sanction that Congress omitted. The rule does not make the directives optional and does not foreclose independently preserved constitutional objections to delay.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Calero-Toledo’s allowance for immediate action against movable property does not extend categorically to an immovable home. Montalvo-Murillo and Brock are applied to reject an implied dismissal sanction for the internal forfeiture timing directives. The decision supplies the first simulated rule governing pre-seizure process for real property.

### John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank, 510 U.S. 86 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** 92-1074.
**Decided:** December 13, 1993.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 6-3. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg. Opposing: O’Connor, Kennedy, Thomas.

The genuinely guaranteed components remain outside plan-asset treatment. Proceedings may continue concerning Hancock’s conduct toward the free funds, including breach and any relief authorized by ERISA.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Souter | Supports affirmance | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Thomas | O’Connor, Kennedy | Would reverse | Would classify the contract more broadly as a guaranteed-benefit policy and reject fiduciary treatment of the free funds |

#### Holding

**Question decided:** Guaranteed-benefit exclusion and fiduciary control over free funds.

**Holding:** ERISA’s guaranteed-benefit-policy exclusion covers only those components of an insurance contract that genuinely guarantee plan benefits and allocate the corresponding investment risk to the insurer. GAC 50’s unconverted free funds do not qualify, and Hancock is a fiduciary to the extent it exercises authority or control over them.

**Authority:** Opinion of the Court by Ginsburg, joined as stated in the Opinions table above; Complete opinion. The proposition is controlling at that stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** Each materially distinct component of a mixed insurance contract must be examined separately. A component falls within the exclusion when the insurer guarantees an aggregate amount of benefits and assumes the relevant investment risk. For an accumulation pool of free funds, that ordinarily requires a guaranteed reasonable return and a contractual mechanism for conversion into defined guaranteed benefits. Corporate status as an insurer and commingling in a general account do not alone establish the exclusion.

**Application:** Hancock’s status as fiduciary follows only with respect to the authority or control it exercised over the free funds. The Court does not determine whether that authority was exercised loyally or prudently.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The decision does not make every insurer an ERISA fiduciary, include assets backing genuinely fixed benefits within plan assets, or govern a materially different annuity that guarantees returns and conversion terms during its accumulation phase.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** ERISA’s “to the extent” language now requires component-specific classification of mixed annuity contracts. Functional fiduciary status follows control over nonexcluded plan assets and does not attach categorically to every aspect of an insurer’s contract.

### Ratzlaf v. United States, 510 U.S. 135 (1994)

**Docket or dockets:** 92-1196.
**Decided:** January 11, 1994.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 6-3. Supporting: Stone, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg. Opposing: Blackmun, O’Connor, Thomas.

Ratzlaf’s conviction under 31 U.S.C. §§ 5322 and 5324 is vacated. The Government may retry him under a correct instruction if otherwise lawful and supported by admissible evidence.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Blackmun | O’Connor, Thomas | Would affirm | Would require knowledge of the reporting requirement and an intent to evade it, but not knowledge that structuring is unlawful |

#### Holding

**Question decided:** Willfulness in the anti-structuring offense.

**Holding:** Conviction under §§ 5322 and 5324 requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant knew the structuring was unlawful, in addition to proof that the defendant intended to evade the financial institution’s reporting duty.

**Authority:** Opinion of the Court by Ginsburg, joined as stated in the Opinions table above; Complete opinion. The proposition is controlling at that stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 5324’s purpose-to-evade language establishes the required object of the transaction pattern. Section 5322’s separate willfulness requirement adds knowledge that the chosen structuring conduct violates law. The Government need not prove knowledge of the precise statutory citation or every detail of the reporting scheme.

**Application:** The jury was affirmatively instructed that knowledge of unlawfulness was unnecessary. It therefore did not find every element required by the statute.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The holding creates no general ignorance-of-law defense, does not alter civil reporting obligations, and does not prevent Congress from changing the offense’s mental state through clear enacted language.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Cheek and Liparota are applied to the distinctive text and context of the currency-reporting subchapter. The decision establishes knowledge of unlawfulness as an element of the willful anti-structuring offense, without creating a general rule for every statute using the word willfully.

### Thunder Basin Coal Co. v. Reich, 510 U.S. 200 (1994)

**Docket or dockets:** 92-896.
**Decided:** January 19, 1994.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

Thunder Basin must use the Mine Act’s administrative-review route. The Court does not decide the underlying labor-law or representative-designation issues.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Blackmun | Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg in full; Scalia and Thomas except the legislative-history and portions of the constitutional-review discussion | Supports affirmance | Complete preclusion and meaningful-review rationale for seven Justices; core statutory-preclusion holding for all nine |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Scalia | Thomas | Supports affirmance | Relies on statutory text and structure, rejects reliance on legislative history, and treats the limited constitutional objection separately |

#### Holding

**Question decided:** Statutory review-channel preclusion.

**Holding:** The Mine Act’s comprehensive enforcement and review scheme precludes district-court jurisdiction over Thunder Basin’s pre-enforcement challenge because Congress’s intent to channel the dispute is fairly discernible from the statutory structure and the designated route preserves meaningful judicial review.

**Authority:** Opinion of the Court by Blackmun, joined as stated in the Opinions table above; Complete preclusion and meaningful-review rationale for seven Justices; core statutory-preclusion holding for all nine. The proposition is controlling at that stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** In determining whether a specialized statutory scheme displaces initial district-court review, a court examines the enacted review structure and asks whether the asserted claims can receive meaningful consideration through that structure. A claim closely connected to the statutory scheme ordinarily must use the designated route. District-court review remains open when the statutory route cannot provide meaningful judicial review, when the claim is genuinely collateral to the scheme, or when the process imposes an irreparable deprivation that the designated review cannot address.

**Application:** The Mine Act assigns challenges to enforcement measures to the Commission and then to a federal court of appeals, while expressly granting district-court authority in limited proceedings initiated by the Secretary. Thunder Basin’s labor-law arguments depend on interpretation of Mine Act representative provisions and can be preserved before the Commission and decided by the reviewing court. Civil penalties do not become payable before review is available, and the record shows no constitutionally intolerable prehearing deprivation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Whether the representative designations violate the National Labor Relations Act, whether the Department’s regulation is valid on the merits, and whether Thunder Basin ultimately must permit every activity attributed to the representatives.

**Application:** The Tenth Circuit’s jurisdictional judgment is affirmed.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Block and Whitney National Bank are applied and clarified. A comprehensive administrative and appellate route may impliedly preclude initial district-court review even when the statute does not expressly mention pre-enforcement suits, provided congressional channeling is evident from the structure and meaningful judicial review remains available.

### Albright v. Oliver, 510 U.S. 266 (1994)

**Docket or dockets:** 92-833.
**Decided:** January 24, 1994.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 7-2. Supporting: Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg. Opposing: Blackmun, Stevens.

No Fourth Amendment damages claim is revived or remanded.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Ginsburg | Supports affirmance | Substantive-due-process holding and reservation of materially distinct nonseizure injuries |
| Concurrence | Stone | No other Justice | Supports affirmance | Would reach the limited Fourth Amendment source-and-seizure question |
| Concurrence | Ginsburg | No other Justice | Supports affirmance | Explains the possible duration of a Fourth Amendment seizure while agreeing that liability is not presented |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Kennedy | Thomas | Supports affirmance on a separate due-process and state-remedy ground | Does not join the Court’s full source-specific rationale |
| Dissent | Stevens | Blackmun | Opposes affirmance | Would recognize a due-process restriction on prosecution without probable cause |

#### Holding

**Question decided:** Substantive due process as the asserted source.

**Holding:** A claimant may not use substantive due process to duplicate Fourth Amendment protection for injuries alleged to arise from arrest and prosecution-related restraints on the person. Albright’s pleaded substantive-due-process claim therefore fails.

**Authority:** Opinion of the Court by Souter, joined as stated in the Opinions table above; Substantive-due-process holding and reservation of materially distinct nonseizure injuries. The proposition is controlling at that stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** When the alleged injury is the arrest or restraint of a person and settled Fourth Amendment law supplies the directly relevant source, substantive due process does not create a parallel federal malicious-prosecution tort. The Court reserves an exceptional case involving a substantial injury distinct from any seizure or other specific constitutional protection.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide whether probable cause existed, whether Oliver supplied false information, whether the travel condition constituted a continuing seizure, or whether a properly pleaded Fourth Amendment, fabricated-evidence, procedural-due-process, or state-law claim would succeed.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Good remains intact: independently applicable constitutional guarantees may coexist. Albright establishes only that substantive due process does not duplicate Fourth Amendment protection for the arrest and pretrial restraints alleged here. No simulated rule is created concerning whether post-release bond conditions constitute a continuing seizure.

### National Organization for Women, Inc. v. Scheidler, 510 U.S. 249 (1994)

**Docket or dockets:** 92-780.
**Decided:** January 24, 1994.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

The clinics’ RICO claims may proceed past the economic-motive dismissal. Every remaining statutory, causal, injury, and constitutional issue remains open.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone | Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence | Souter | Kennedy | Supports reversal and remand | Emphasizes that particular RICO applications and remedies remain subject to the First Amendment |

#### Question 1: Standing of the clinic plaintiffs

**Question decided:** Standing of the clinic plaintiffs.

**Holding:** DWHO and SWHO have Article III standing at the pleading stage because each alleges injury to its own business or property fairly traceable to respondents’ asserted campaign and redressable through the relief sought.

**Authority:** Opinion of the Court by Stone, joined as stated in the Opinions table above; Complete opinion. The proposition is controlling at that stated scope.

#### Question 2: Economic motive under RICO

**Question decided:** Economic motive under RICO.

**Holding:** RICO does not require proof that the enterprise or the alleged predicate acts were motivated by profit or another economic objective.

**Authority:** Opinion of the Court by Stone, joined as stated in the Opinions table above; Complete opinion. The proposition is controlling at that stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** Ideological purpose neither establishes nor defeats liability. A civil plaintiff must prove the statutory enterprise, pattern, qualifying predicates, injury to business or property, and the required causal connection. No additional economic-motive element may be inserted into those requirements.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide whether respondents committed qualifying predicates, whether a pattern or enterprise can be proved, whether the clinics satisfy § 1964(c), or whether any particular protest activity is constitutionally protected. Peaceful advocacy, association, picketing, and persuasion cannot become racketeering merely because they accompany a political movement. The Court does not decide any concrete First Amendment defense on this record.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Turkette and Sedima are applied without adding an economic-purpose limitation. RICO liability continues to depend on the enacted elements, injury, and causation rather than the ideological or commercial objective of the enterprise.

**Question decided:** Diminishment of the Uintah Reservation.

### FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471 (1994)

**Docket or dockets:** 92-741.
**Decided:** February 23, 1994.

#### Judgment

Reversed, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

**Judgment, mandate, and remand:**

The agency damages award shall be vacated, and judgment shall be entered for the FDIC on the claim against FSLIC. The Court’s disposition does not reopen the unreviewed judgment concerning the individual official.

Justice Thomas delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg.

**Opinion of the Court:** Thomas, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg.

#### Question 1: FTCA exclusivity and the sue-and-be-sued waiver

**Question decided:** FTCA exclusivity and the sue-and-be-sued waiver.

**Holding:** Meyer’s federal constitutional claim is not cognizable under 28 U.S.C. § 1346(b), so the FTCA does not make the United States the exclusive defendant. FSLIC’s broad sue-and-be-sued clause waives the agency’s sovereign immunity from suit on the claim.

**Authority:** Thomas, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg; the proposition is controlling.

#### Question 2: Bivens liability of a federal agency

**Question decided:** Bivens liability of a federal agency.

**Holding:** Bivens does not imply a damages cause of action directly against a federal agency, even when sovereign immunity has been waived.

**Authority:** Thomas, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg; the proposition is controlling.

**Controlling explanation:** A waiver of immunity determines whether an entity may be sued. It does not create a constitutional cause of action or authorize damages. The officer remedy recognized in Bivens does not extend automatically to the employing agency.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Existing Bivens actions against individual officers are not narrowed. The Court does not decide any statutory cause of action, prospective remedy, or the due-process merits of Meyer’s discharge.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Albright’s source-specific reasoning is reinforced but not extended. Meyer establishes that constitutional right, sovereign-immunity waiver, cause of action, and remedy are separate legal questions. Bivens remains an individual-officer remedy and does not authorize direct agency damages.

### Department of Defense v. Federal Labor Relations Authority, 510 U.S. 487 (1994)

**Docket or dockets:** 92-1223.
**Decided:** February 23, 1994.

#### Judgment

Reversed, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

The Fifth Circuit’s judgment is reversed. The home-address disclosure orders may not be enforced through FOIA’s exception to the Privacy Act.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Supports reversal | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence | Souter | No other Justice | Supports reversal | Joins the Court and adds observations concerning the privacy interest |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Ginsburg | No other Justice | Supports reversal | Treats Reporters Committee as controlling while reserving disagreement with parts of its account of FOIA’s text |

#### Holding

**Question decided:** FOIA public interest and employee home addresses.

**Holding:** FOIA does not require public disclosure of federal employees’ home addresses because the addresses reveal virtually nothing about agency operations and their disclosure would constitute a clearly unwarranted invasion of personal privacy. The Privacy Act’s FOIA exception therefore does not authorize release.

**Authority:** Opinion of the Court by Thomas, joined as stated in the Opinions table above; Complete opinion. The proposition is controlling at that stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** Under Exemption 6, the legally relevant public interest is the contribution disclosure makes to public understanding of governmental activity. The identity, purpose, and private benefit of the requester do not enlarge that interest. Residential information implicates privacy through unwanted contact, loss of control over further dissemination, and the connection of named individuals to their homes.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The decision does not diminish collective-bargaining rights, bar disclosure with employee consent, decide every possible Privacy Act routine use, or prevent Congress from creating a separate disclosure mechanism with defined safeguards.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Reporters Committee is applied without alteration. A requester’s beneficial use of personal information does not count as a FOIA public interest unless disclosure itself illuminates government operations.

### Elder v. Holloway, 510 U.S. 510 (1994)

**Docket or dockets:** 92-857.
**Decided:** February 23, 1994.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

The Ninth Circuit shall reconsider qualified immunity using all materially relevant precedent, including Al-Azzawy. The Court does not decide whether the officers ultimately receive immunity.

Justice Ginsburg delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas.

**Opinion of the Court:** Ginsburg, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas.

#### Holding

**Question decided:** Legal authorities considered on qualified-immunity review.

**Holding:** A court reviewing qualified immunity must consider all materially relevant precedent available at the time of the challenged conduct, including authority not cited to the district court.

**Authority:** Ginsburg, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; the proposition is controlling.

**Controlling explanation:** Whether governing law clearly established the asserted right is a question of law reviewed de novo. **Application:** The Ninth Circuit identified Al-Azzawy as potentially relevant but declined to evaluate its legal effect. It must now determine what that decision and the rest of the governing law established concerning the asserted warrantless in-home arrest.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide whether Al-Azzawy clearly established a violation, whether the officers’ conduct amounted to an in-home arrest, whether exigent circumstances existed, or whether immunity ultimately applies.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Harlow and Anderson are clarified as requiring courts to determine clearly established law from the complete body of materially relevant authority. Failure to cite a case below does not operate as forfeiture of the law itself.

### Fogerty v. Fantasy, Inc., 510 U.S. 517 (1994)

**Docket or dockets:** 92-1750.
**Decided:** March 1, 1994.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg. No opposing Justice.

The district court shall reconsider Fogerty’s application without a plaintiff-favoring presumption and without requiring frivolousness or bad faith as a categorical condition for a prevailing defendant.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone | Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Thomas | No other Justice | Supports reversal and remand | Agrees with the evenhanded reading of § 505 but objects to its inconsistency with Christiansburg’s treatment of nearly identical language |

#### Holding

**Question decided:** Evenhanded discretionary awards under § 505.

**Holding:** Prevailing copyright plaintiffs and defendants must be evaluated under the same discretionary legal standard. Neither side is presumptively entitled to fees, and a prevailing defendant need not prove frivolousness or bad faith as a categorical prerequisite.

**Authority:** Opinion of the Court by Stone, joined as stated in the Opinions table above; Complete opinion. The proposition is controlling at that stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** A court may consider objective unreasonableness, frivolousness, motivation, compensation, deterrence, and other circumstances faithful to the Copyright Act’s purposes. These considerations are nonexclusive, no one is mandatory, and they must be applied evenhandedly. Section 505 does not establish automatic prevailing-party fee shifting.

**Application:** Fogerty prevailed but was denied fees under the legally erroneous asymmetric threshold. The trial court must exercise its own discretion under the corrected rule rather than treat reversal as an instruction to award fees.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court adopts no presumption for defendants, makes fee awards neither routine nor automatic, and does not turn the illustrative considerations into a mandatory checklist.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** The Ninth Circuit’s dual copyright-fee standard is rejected. Christiansburg is not extended to § 505, and Hensley remains relevant to reasoned discretionary fee adjudication. The decision concerns fee shifting and does not alter Campbell’s later fair-use analysis.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |

**Controlling explanation:** The instruction requirement is triggered by the combination of a prosecution argument placing future dangerousness at issue and legally fixed parole ineligibility under the available life sentence. **Application:** The jury specifically expressed concern about the meaning of life imprisonment, and the trial court’s response did not disclose parole ineligibility. The defect requires a new penalty proceeding but supplies no basis for disturbing the conviction.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The rule does not require predictions about discretionary parole when eligibility remains legally possible or contingent. **Treatment of earlier authority:** Gardner and Skipper are extended from a general right to answer material sentencing assertions to a specific instruction requirement when future dangerousness and fixed parole ineligibility coincide. | Dissent | Thomas | Scalia | Opposes the Court’s statutory judgment | Would vacate and direct dismissal because § 2 does not reach districting-based vote dilution |

#### Question 1: Preclusive effect of the state reapportionment judgment

**Question decided:** Preclusive effect of the state reapportionment judgment.

**Holding:** Florida’s expedited reapportionment judgment does not preclude the later federal § 2 claims because the rendering court expressly left Voting Rights Act challenges open and its judgment carries no greater effect in federal court.

**Authority:** Souter’s opinion, joined on this issue by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, and Ginsburg, an actual seven-Justice majority.

#### Question 2: Gingles, proportionality, and nonmaximization

**Question decided:** Gingles, proportionality, and nonmaximization.

**Holding:** The Gingles preconditions are necessary but not invariably sufficient to establish § 2 vote dilution. Rough proportionality is relevant to electoral opportunity but is neither dispositive nor a safe harbor. The ability to draw additional majority-minority districts does not by itself establish dilution, and § 2 does not require maximization.

**Authority:** Souter’s opinion, joined on the operative rule by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, and Ginsburg.

**Controlling explanation:** The court must assess whether protected voters have less opportunity than other members of the electorate to participate effectively and elect representatives of choice under the full statutory totality. Districts counted in a proportionality comparison must operate as genuine electoral opportunities. Numerical majority status is evidence, not the statutory right itself.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide whether proportionality must be measured statewide when the claims were litigated on a smaller geographic basis. It creates no proportional-representation entitlement and does not resolve the legal status of an influence-district claim.

#### Question 3: Application to the House and Senate plans

**Question decided:** Application to the House and Senate plans.

**Holding:** On the record accepted by the Court, the House districts do not violate § 2, and the decision leaving the Senate plan undisturbed is affirmed.

**Authority:** Souter’s opinion, joined on the judgment and application by Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, and Ginsburg, a six-Justice majority.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Gingles remains the threshold framework, but satisfaction of its preconditions does not end the statutory inquiry. Proportionality is relevant evidence of opportunity, not a safe harbor, and nonmaximization is not a violation. The Court’s application establishes no § 2 violation on this record; Stone’s preferred factfinding requirements remain noncontrolling beyond the Court’s more general requirement that counted districts represent effective electoral opportunities.

#### Question 1: Standard for a content-neutral speech injunction

**Question decided:** Standard for a content-neutral speech injunction.

**Holding:** A content-neutral injunction directed at identified speakers must be reviewed provision by provision, and each restraint must burden no more speech than necessary to remedy demonstrated unlawful conduct and protect the significant interests affected by that conduct.

**Authority:** Opinion of the Court by Stone, joined as stated in the Opinions table above; Stevens joins the content-neutral classification, active-concert construction, and judgments respecting the entrance and driveway zone, noise, images, and residential buffer; he does not join the Court’s governing standard or its private-property and no-approach rulings. Ladue’s attention to realistic communicative alternatives is applied in determining whether a broad geographic or communicative restriction leaves meaningful expression available.

### International Union, United Mine Workers of America v. Bagwell, 512 U.S. 821 (1994)

**Docket or dockets:** 92-1625.
**Decided:** June 30, 1994.

#### Judgment

Reversed, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

The challenged public fines are vacated. Virginia may pursue criminal contempt through constitutionally adequate proceedings or impose genuinely compensatory or coercive civil relief within lawful limits.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Blackmun | Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas in full; Ginsburg except the broader procedural discussion concerning complex injunctions | Supports reversal | Complete opinion for eight Justices; core classification and application for all nine |
| Concurrence | Scalia | No other Justice | Supports reversal | Addresses the established distinction between civil and criminal contempt |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Ginsburg | No other Justice | Supports reversal | Relies on the completed-act, noncompensatory, and nonpurgeable character of the fines while reserving the broader process-based taxonomy |

#### Holding

**Question decided:** Classification and procedure for serious contempt fines.

**Holding:** The determinate, noncompensatory, nonpurgeable fines imposed for completed violations of the labor injunction are serious criminal contempt sanctions and may be imposed only through a criminal proceeding that includes the right to a jury trial.

**Authority:** All nine Justices support the criminal classification and reversal. Blackmun’s fuller account of procedure for complex injunctions is joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas, an eight-Justice majority.

**Controlling explanation:** A contempt fine is civil when it compensates proved loss or coerces present compliance while leaving the contemnor a meaningful opportunity to avoid or purge the sanction. A fixed and unconditional fine for a completed violation is punitive. Advance announcement of the amount gives notice but does not transform punishment into civil coercion.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Courts retain summary authority over petty direct contempt necessary to maintain courtroom order. They may use per diem fines, suspended fines, or comparable sanctions that remain genuinely purgeable, and may award compensation tied to proved loss.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Gompers and Mine Workers are clarified. Civil coercion depends on a present opportunity to comply and purge, while serious determinate punishment for completed conduct requires criminal adjudication. Direct courtroom contempt and proved compensatory relief remain distinct.

### Tuilaepa v. California / Proctor v. California, 512 U.S. 967 (1994)

**Docket or dockets:** 93-5131 and 93-5161.
**Decided:** June 30, 1994.

#### Judgment

Affirmed in both cases, 8-1. Supporting: Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg. Opposing: Blackmun.

No separate remedial instruction is required in either docket.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas | Supports both affirmances | Complete opinion |

| Dissent | Blackmun | No other Justice | Opposes both affirmances | Would invalidate the selection factors and separately adheres to his broader capital-punishment position |

#### Question 1: Clarity and function of capital selection factors

**Question decided:** Clarity and function of capital selection factors.

**Holding:** California Penal Code § 190.3 factors directing the sentencer to consider the circumstances of the capital crime, other violent criminal activity, and the defendant’s age are not facially vague at the selection stage. Each identifies an understandable factual subject, and the Eighth Amendment does not require a prescribed weight for those facts.

**Authority:** Opinion of the Court by Kennedy, joined as stated in the Opinions table above; Complete opinion. The proposition is controlling at that stated scope.

**Controlling explanation:** Eligibility factors must genuinely narrow the class exposed to death and provide a principled boundary. Once eligibility has been lawfully established, selection factors may direct individualized consideration of relevant characteristics of the offense and offender. A selection factor must possess a common-sense core that ordinary jurors can understand, but it need not produce a yes-or-no answer or assign predetermined aggravating or mitigating weight.

**Application:** The instructions allowed each jury to consider the identified facts together with relevant mitigation. They did not make the challenged factors perform the scheme’s eligibility-narrowing function or require death upon a specified finding.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not approve an eligibility factor that fails to narrow, an instruction that forecloses relevant mitigation, or a label lacking an understandable core. It does not adjudicate the constitutionality of California’s capital scheme as a whole.

#### Question 2: Relationship to Romano

**Question decided:** Relationship to Romano.

**Holding:** Romano does not invalidate the challenged factors because they direct the sentencer to relevant facts concerning the present defendant and offenses rather than importing another sentencer’s irrelevant ultimate judgment.

**Authority:** Opinion of the Court by Kennedy, joined as stated in the Opinions table above; Complete opinion. The proposition is controlling at that stated scope.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** The eligibility-selection distinction is reaffirmed and clarified. Romano remains controlling when the State introduces another jury’s irrelevant death judgment; it does not require mechanical weighting of relevant individualized sentencing facts.
