### United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property, 510 U.S. 43 (1993)

Argued October 6, 1993 | Decided December 13, 1993

From: The Ninth Circuit held that the Government violated due process by seizing Good’s occupied home without prior notice and a hearing, and that failure to comply with the timing directives in 19 U.S.C. §§ 1602 through 1604 required dismissal despite commencement within § 1621’s five-year period. The questions are whether pre-seizure process was constitutionally required and whether the statutory timing directives carried an implied dismissal sanction. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510043/usrep510043.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: No earlier simulated merits holding governs. Fuentes, Mathews, and Calero-Toledo leave open whether the mobility rationale for immediate seizure extends to an occupied home. Montalvo-Murillo and Brock counsel against attaching loss of governmental authority to a missed statutory deadline when Congress supplied no such consequence.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Relief or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-seizure due process | Affirmed, 6-3 | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | O’Connor, Scalia, Thomas | The warrant-based occupation of Good’s home without prior process was unconstitutional absent a demonstrated exigency. |
| Statutory timing | Reversed, 9-0 | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | No opposing vote | Noncompliance with §§ 1602 through 1604 does not require dismissal of an action timely under § 1621. |

##### Judgment, Mandate, and Remand

The judgment is affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded. The forfeiture action may continue because it was commenced within § 1621. The lower courts must separately determine the lawful consequences of the unconstitutional pre-hearing seizure.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg in full; O’Connor, Scalia, and Thomas as to the statutory-timing holding | Supports both judgment components | Full due-process and timing holdings for the six-Justice coalition; timing holding for all nine |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | O’Connor | Scalia, Thomas | Agrees with the timing reversal and opposes the due-process affirmance | Would treat the ex parte warrant procedure as constitutionally adequate |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Pre-seizure process for real property

Holding: Except upon particularized proof of exigent circumstances, the Fifth Amendment requires notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard before the Government seizes real property for civil forfeiture.

Operative rule: The Government bears the obligation to establish an actual exigency justifying immediate seizure. The forfeiture label alone is insufficient. A lis pendens, restraining order, posting of process, or other less intrusive measure ordinarily preserves the Government’s interests while an adversarial hearing occurs.

Reasoning: Seizure displaced Good from possession and control of an occupied home through an ex parte proceeding. Real property cannot leave the jurisdiction, and the record showed no emergency that required immediate governmental occupation. The risk and severity of an erroneous deprivation therefore outweighed the limited burden of conducting a hearing first.

Limits and questions left open: The rule concerns real property. It does not determine the treatment of readily movable personal property or contraband, and it permits immediate action when the Government proves a genuine exigency.

Legal basis: Fifth Amendment Due Process Clause.

##### Effect of the statutory timing directives

Holding: A forfeiture action filed within § 1621’s five-year limitation period may not be dismissed solely because officials failed to comply with §§ 1602 through 1604, where Congress did not make dismissal or loss of authority the consequence of noncompliance.

Operative rule: Mandatory timing language imposes duties on responsible officials, but a court does not create a forfeiture-ending sanction that Congress omitted. The rule does not make the directives optional and does not foreclose independently preserved constitutional objections to delay.

Reasoning: Congress separately established a five-year filing period and did not state that failure to report or proceed “promptly” or “forthwith” extinguishes a timely forfeiture claim. Treating the directives as an additional limitations period would duplicate § 1621 and attach a sanction Congress did not enact.

Legal basis: 21 U.S.C. § 881(d) and 19 U.S.C. §§ 1602 through 1604 and 1621.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Affirm the due-process ruling; reverse the statutory-timing ruling; remand.

Public role: Joins Justice Kennedy’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone announces no ground beyond the Court opinion.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

Calero-Toledo’s allowance for immediate action against movable property does not extend categorically to an immovable home. Montalvo-Murillo and Brock are applied to reject an implied dismissal sanction for the internal forfeiture timing directives. The decision supplies the first simulated rule governing pre-seizure process for real property.

### Albright v. Oliver, 510 U.S. 266 (1994)

Argued October 12, 1993 | Decided January 24, 1994

From: The Seventh Circuit affirmed dismissal of Albright’s § 1983 complaint, which alleged a substantive-due-process right to be free from prosecution without probable cause. Albright surrendered under an arrest warrant, posted bond, and was barred from leaving Illinois without judicial permission, but he did not present an independent Fourth Amendment claim in his petition. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510266/usrep510266.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Good establishes that one constitutional provision does not invariably preclude another, but it involved an independently applicable procedural guarantee in a civil-forfeiture setting. Graham and Gerstein continue to govern whether substantive due process may duplicate protection for arrest and pretrial restraint.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 7-2. Supporting: Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg. Opposing: Blackmun, Stevens.

No Fourth Amendment damages claim is revived or remanded.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Dissent | Stevens | Blackmun | Opposes affirmance | Would recognize a due-process restriction on prosecution without probable cause |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Substantive due process as the asserted source

Holding: A claimant may not use substantive due process to duplicate Fourth Amendment protection for injuries alleged to arise from arrest and prosecution-related restraints on the person. Albright’s pleaded substantive-due-process claim therefore fails.

Operative rule: When the alleged injury is the arrest or restraint of a person and settled Fourth Amendment law supplies the directly relevant source, substantive due process does not create a parallel federal malicious-prosecution tort. The Court reserves an exceptional case involving a substantial injury distinct from any seizure or other specific constitutional protection.

Reasoning: Albright’s alleged injuries began with surrender under a warrant and continued through judicial restrictions imposed as part of pretrial release. Those are restraints of the person ordinarily addressed by the Fourth Amendment. Good does not require a contrary result because Good applied an independently established procedural guarantee and did not authorize a duplicate substantive-due-process cause of action.

Limits and questions left open: The Court does not decide whether probable cause existed, whether Oliver supplied false information, whether the travel condition constituted a continuing seizure, or whether a properly pleaded Fourth Amendment, fabricated-evidence, procedural-due-process, or state-law claim would succeed.

Legal basis: Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments and 42 U.S.C. § 1983.

Eight Justices decline to take up the unpresented Fourth Amendment source-and-seizure question. Stone alone votes to reach it. The Court therefore creates no holding concerning the constitutional character or duration of Albright’s bond conditions.

### BFP v. Resolution Trust Corp., 511 U.S. 531 (1994)

Argued December 7, 1993 | Decided May 23, 1994

From: The Ninth Circuit treated the price received at a noncollusive foreclosure sale conducted in compliance with California law as conclusively satisfying § 548(a)(2). The property sold for approximately $433,000, while BFP alleged a value exceeding $725,000. No judicial finding conclusively established the asserted market value. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511531/usrep511531.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: No simulated rule defines reasonably equivalent value in a foreclosure. Section 548 asks what value the debtor received, and no enacted foreclosure-sale safe harbor answers that federal element.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 5-4. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg. Opposing: O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas.

##### Judgment, Mandate, and Remand

The Ninth Circuit shall remand to the bankruptcy court for a transaction-specific determination of reasonably equivalent value. Avoidance may issue only after the statutory elements and an authorized remedy are established.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Ginsburg | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Scalia | O’Connor, Kennedy, Thomas | Would affirm | Would treat the compliant foreclosure price as conclusive |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Meaning of reasonably equivalent value after foreclosure

Holding: A regularly conducted, noncollusive foreclosure sale is substantial evidence of value under forced-sale conditions, but the sale price and compliance with state foreclosure law do not conclusively establish reasonably equivalent value under § 548(a)(2).

Operative rule: The party seeking avoidance retains the ordinary burden of proof. The decision creates neither a presumption nor a shifted burden of production. Relevant, nonexclusive considerations may include the foreclosure price, forced-sale conditions, exposure to potential purchasers, procedural regularity, and the value of secured debt discharged. The court may not double count the sale price and discharged debt when they describe the same economic consideration.

Reasoning: Section 548 asks whether the debtor received reasonably equivalent value, not only whether the creditor complied with state procedure. Fair market value cannot be applied mechanically to a forced sale, but forced-sale conditions cannot erase the federal element or create a safe harbor Congress did not enact. The unresolved disparity alleged here requires factfinding.

Limits and questions left open: The Court prescribes no fixed percentage, automatic avoidance rule, mandatory valuation sequence, or alteration of state foreclosure procedure.

Legal basis: 11 U.S.C. § 548(a)(2).

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Reverse and remand.

Public role: Joins Justice Souter’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone announces no additional ground.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

Section 548 now requires a transaction-specific value inquiry in mortgage-foreclosure cases. State-law compliance is weighty evidence but not a conclusive federal safe harbor. The ordinary burden remains unchanged.

### Staples v. United States, 511 U.S. 600 (1994)

Argued November 30, 1993 | Decided May 23, 1994

From: The Tenth Circuit affirmed Staples’s conviction under 26 U.S.C. § 5861(d) without requiring the jury to find that he knew his rifle possessed automatic-fire capability. Staples testified that he did not know of that characteristic, and the jury was not instructed to resolve the disputed mental-state fact. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511600/usrep511600.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Morissette, Freed, and Liparota establish the ordinary presumption of mens rea but leave open whether Congress displaced it for the characteristic separating a familiar semiautomatic rifle from a statutory machinegun.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 7-2. Supporting: Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg. Opposing: Blackmun, Stevens.

The conviction is vacated. The Government may retry Staples under a legally sufficient instruction.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | Stone, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Supports reversal | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Ginsburg | O’Connor | Supports reversal | Agrees with the required knowledge finding on a narrower statutory formulation |
| Dissent | Stevens | Blackmun | Would affirm | Would treat the offense as not requiring knowledge of automatic-fire capability |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Mens rea for the regulated characteristic

Holding: Section 5861(d) requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant knew the weapon possessed the characteristics that made it a machinegun. The Government need not prove knowledge of the statutory classification or registration requirement.

Operative rule: Silence in the statute does not displace the traditional mens rea presumption when an otherwise familiar and commonly lawful object becomes seriously criminal only because of a particular physical characteristic. Knowledge must attach to that characteristic.

Reasoning: Ordinary firearm possession is not conduct whose blameworthiness is so evident that knowledge of the regulated feature may be omitted. Automatic-fire capability is the fact that transforms Staples’s rifle into the heavily regulated weapon, and the severe potential punishment reinforces the need for a clear congressional basis before dispensing with mens rea. The jury instruction removed that disputed fact from the factfinder.

Limits and questions left open: The decision does not address weapons or contraband whose dangerous character is unmistakable from possession, and it requires no proof that a defendant knew federal law or the registration duty.

Legal basis: 26 U.S.C. §§ 5845, 5861(d), and 5871.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Reverse and remand.

Public role: Joins Justice Thomas’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone announces no additional ground.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

The traditional mens rea presumption now requires knowledge of the factual characteristic that places a weapon within the National Firearms Act’s machinegun category. Freed is limited to its materially different contraband setting and is not extended to ordinary-looking firearms.

#### Historical Comparison

The historical Court also reversed 7-2. Stone occupies Rehnquist’s place in the five-Justice Court opinion, while the judgment coalition and operative mens rea rule remain unchanged. ([Library of Congress Tile

### Hagen v. Utah, 510 U.S. 399 (1994)

Argued November 2, 1993 | Decided February 23, 1994

From: The Utah Supreme Court reversed an intermediate appellate judgment and reinstated Hagen’s conviction, holding that Congress had diminished the Uintah Indian Reservation and that Myton therefore lay outside Indian country. The question is whether the federal opening enactments altered the reservation boundary so that Utah could exercise criminal jurisdiction over an enrolled Ute whose offense occurred within the reservation’s original boundaries. ([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/510bv.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: No earlier same-term decision affects reservation diminishment. Solem v. Bartlett and related surviving authority require congressional intent to diminish, treat statutory text as the most probative evidence, resolve genuine ambiguities in favor of tribal interests, and permit consideration of contemporaneous understanding and later jurisdictional practice.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 6-3. Supporting: Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg. Opposing: Stone, Blackmun, Souter.

The state conviction remains in force.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O’Connor | Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Supports affirmance | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Blackmun | Stone, Souter | Would reverse and direct vacation of the conviction | Rejects diminishment and Utah criminal jurisdiction |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Diminishment of the Uintah Reservation

Holding: Congress diminished the Uintah Reservation when it directed that the unallotted lands be restored to the public domain and implemented that direction through the related opening enactments. Myton consequently was not Indian country, and Utah properly exercised criminal jurisdiction over Hagen.

Reasoning: The operative public-domain language, read with the complementary 1902, 1903, and 1905 enactments, manifested an express congressional purpose to terminate reservation status in the opened area. Contemporaneous executive and legislative materials understood the opening in the same manner. Subsequent population patterns, governmental administration, and jurisdictional practice confirmed that understanding, although they did not independently create the boundary change.

Limits and questions left open: No particular verbal formula is indispensable to diminishment, but congressional purpose remains necessary and ambiguities continue to be resolved in favor of tribal interests. The decision determines reservation status and criminal jurisdiction in the opened area; it does not adjudicate ownership of particular parcels or displace federal and tribal authority that survives outside the diminished lands.

Legal basis: The federal opening enactments and 18 U.S.C. §§ 1151 and 1153.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Reverse and remand with instructions to vacate the conviction and dismiss the state prosecution.

Public role: Joins Justice Blackmun’s dissent in full.

Announced ground: Stone concludes that the enactments changed the disposition and ownership of unallotted lands without clearly terminating reservation status. Later demographics and state practice cannot supply the congressional decision required to transfer criminal jurisdiction.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

Solem’s diminishment framework is applied without categorical alteration. The Court holds that the statutory public-domain language and surrounding enactments satisfy its congressional-intent requirement for the opened Uintah lands.

#### Historical Comparison

The historical Court affirmed 7-2, with Chief Justice Rehnquist joining O’Connor’s opinion and Blackmun and Souter dissenting. Stone instead joins the historical dissenters, reducing the affirmance coalition to 6-3 without changing the controlling rule or judgment. ([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/510bv.pdf))

### Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569 (1994)

Argued November 9, 1993 | Decided March 7, 1994

From: The Sixth Circuit reversed summary judgment for 2 Live Crew, treated the commercial character of its parody as presumptively unfair, found the amount copied excessive, and presumed market injury. The question is whether a commercial parody may constitute fair use and how the four statutory factors apply when the new work comments on the copyrighted original. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510569/usrep510569.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Section 107 requires consideration of four nonexclusive factors. Sony and Harper & Row recognize the relevance of commercial purpose and market injury but do not establish a categorical rule governing transformative parody.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

The Sixth Circuit shall reconsider fair use under the complete statutory inquiry and permit focused development of evidence concerning any cognizable derivative market.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence | Kennedy | No other Justice | Supports reversal and remand | Emphasizes that parody must genuinely comment on the original rather than use parody as a label for ordinary appropriation |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Commercial parody under the statutory fair-use inquiry

Holding: A commercial parody may qualify as fair use. Commerciality creates no presumption of unfairness, and market harm may not be presumed merely because the parody was sold for profit.

Operative rule: The four § 107 factors must be weighed together in light of copyright’s purposes. Under the first factor, courts consider whether the new work adds expression, meaning, or message and uses the original for a different function. The greater the transformation, the less dispositive commerciality becomes. Under the third factor, the amount copied is evaluated in relation to the parodic purpose, including the need to make the original recognizable. Under the fourth factor, the relevant injury is substitution in a market copyright law protects, not lost demand caused by criticism or ridicule.

Reasoning: The new song used recognizable elements of Oh, Pretty Woman as the target of comment and then departed from the original’s lyrics and musical character. The Sixth Circuit gave commercial purpose controlling force and treated copying necessary to identify the target as ordinary duplication. It also presumed market injury despite the markedly different function of the two works.

Application: The record did not conclusively establish the effect on any legitimate market for authorized rap derivatives, and the amount of musical copying required further consideration under the correct standard. The Court therefore does not enter final judgment that the parody was fair use.

Limits and questions left open: Humor alone does not make a work a parody. The new work must reasonably be perceived as commenting on the original, and a parodist remains subject to the complete statutory inquiry.

Legal basis: 17 U.S.C. § 107.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Reverse and remand.

Public role: Joins Justice Souter’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone announces no ground beyond the Court opinion.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

Sony and Harper & Row are clarified. Commercial use remains relevant but does not create a controlling presumption against transformative parody, and presumed market harm does not replace evidence of substitution in a cognizable market.

#### Historical Comparison

The historical Court reached the same unanimous judgment through Souter’s opinion and Kennedy’s concurrence. Stone occupies Rehnquist’s place in the unanimous Court without changing the statutory rule or remand. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510569/usrep510569.pdf))

### Victor v. Nebraska / Sandoval v. California, 511 U.S. 1 (1994), Nos. 92-8894 and 92-9049

Argued January 18, 1994 | Decided March 22, 1994

From: The Nebraska and California Supreme Courts separately upheld murder convictions and rejected challenges to reasonable-doubt instructions containing phrases such as moral certainty, substantial doubt, and abiding conviction. The companion cases require separate judgments and ask whether each charge, read as a whole, permitted conviction on proof below the constitutional standard. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511001/usrep511001.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Winship requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every element. Cage invalidated an instruction whose combined references to grave uncertainty, substantial doubt, and moral certainty created an impermissibly low threshold. Estelle requires challenged language to be assessed in the context of the complete charge.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Relief or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Victor v. Nebraska, No. 92-8894 | Affirmed, 7-2 | Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Blackmun, Souter | Victor’s conviction survives the instructional challenge. |
| Sandoval v. California, No. 92-9049 | Affirmed, 8-1 | Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Blackmun | Sandoval’s conviction survives the instructional challenge. |

All nine Justices agree that Sandoval’s reasonable-doubt instruction satisfies the constitutional burden. Blackmun’s opposition to the Sandoval judgment rests solely on his separate capital-punishment position.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O’Connor | Stone, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas in full; Blackmun and Souter as to Sandoval’s instruction; Ginsburg as to Sandoval and the specified portions of the Victor analysis | Supports both affirmances | Complete opinion for six Justices; differing partial joins as stated |
| Concurrence | Kennedy | No other Justice | Supports both affirmances | Warns against continued use of archaic formulations |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Ginsburg | No other Justice | Supports both affirmances | Agrees the instructions were adequate while proposing a clearer model formulation |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Blackmun | Souter as to Victor’s instruction challenge only | Supports rejection of Sandoval’s instructional claim; opposes Victor’s affirmance and separately opposes Sandoval’s capital judgment | Victor instruction dissent and Blackmun’s separate capital ground |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Constitutional standard for reasonable-doubt instructions

Holding: Neither the Nebraska nor California charge, read as a whole, created a reasonable likelihood that the jury applied a burden lower than proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

Operative rule: The Constitution prescribes no fixed wording for a reasonable-doubt instruction. Reviewing courts ask whether there is a reasonable likelihood that the jury understood the complete charge to permit conviction on insufficient proof. Disputed phrases must be assessed with the surrounding directions concerning evidence, the prosecution’s burden, and the need for a durable conviction of guilt.

Reasoning: In each case the charge repeatedly connected the required certainty to the evidence and required an abiding conviction of guilt. Unlike Cage, the instructions did not combine the challenged phrases in a manner reasonably likely to require only grave uncertainty or an unusually large doubt before acquittal.

##### Sandoval’s instruction

Holding: Sandoval’s instruction was constitutionally adequate despite its references to moral evidence and moral certainty.

Reasoning: The surrounding language required an abiding conviction formed from the evidence and excluded conviction based on mere probability. All nine Justices agree on this instructional conclusion.

Authority of the proposition: O’Connor’s opinion, joined on this issue by every participating Justice, is unanimous on the reasonable-doubt question.

##### Victor’s instruction

Holding: Victor’s instruction was constitutionally adequate because its references to substantial doubt and moral certainty were confined and clarified by the charge as a whole.

Reasoning: The instruction distinguished reasonable doubt from conjecture, directed exclusive reliance on the evidence, and required a settled conviction after full and impartial consideration. The combined charge therefore did not reproduce Cage’s constitutionally defective formulation.

Authority of the proposition: O’Connor’s opinion, joined on the judgment and operative analysis by Stone, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Ginsburg, supplies a seven-Justice majority.

Limits and questions left open: The Court does not endorse moral-certainty language. A similar phrase may be unconstitutional when its context fails to anchor the required certainty in the evidence or combines it with language materially lowering the prosecution’s burden.

Legal basis: The Due Process Clause and the Sixth Amendment jury guarantee.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Affirm in Victor; affirm in Sandoval.

Public role: Joins Justice O’Connor’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone announces no separate ground. The Court opinion’s criticism of archaic language preserves his position that trial courts should discontinue formulations that add ambiguity without improving the instruction.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

Winship’s burden remains unchanged. Cage is distinguished rather than narrowed: its result depends on the combined operation of the defective phrases, while Victor and Sandoval confirm that ambiguous wording is evaluated through the reasonable-likelihood inquiry applied to the whole charge.

#### Historical Comparison

The simulation preserves the corrected historical judgments: Victor is affirmed 7-2 and Sandoval 8-1, with the Sandoval instructional issue unanimous. Stone replaces Rehnquist among the full joiners of O’Connor’s opinion. ([Library of Congress Tile

### J.E.B. v. Alabama ex rel. T.B., 511 U.S. 127 (1994)

Argued November 2, 1993 | Decided April 19, 1994

From: In a state paternity and child-support action, Alabama used nine of its ten peremptory challenges against male prospective jurors, producing an all-female jury. J.E.B. objected before the jury was sworn, but the Alabama courts declined to extend Batson beyond race. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511127/usrep511127.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Batson prohibits purposeful racial discrimination in peremptory challenges. Powers permits a litigant to raise the excluded jurors’ rights, and Edmonson treats the exercise of peremptories in civil litigation as state action. Existing equal-protection decisions subject intentional sex classifications to heightened review.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 7-2. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg. Opposing: Scalia, Thomas.

The Alabama courts shall apply the ordinary peremptory-challenge framework. If the State cannot provide genuine sex-neutral explanations sufficient to defeat the showing of purposeful discrimination, the judgment must be vacated and the matter retried before a lawfully selected jury.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Blackmun | Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence | O’Connor | No other Justice | Supports reversal | Emphasizes the limited application of the rule to governmental use of sex as a proxy |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Kennedy | No other Justice | Supports reversal | Grounds the result in equal citizenship and the individual juror’s right not to be excluded by stereotype |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would affirm | Rejects extension of Batson to sex-based peremptories |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Sex discrimination in peremptory challenges

Holding: The Equal Protection Clause prohibits a party’s purposeful use of peremptory challenges to exclude prospective jurors because of sex. The prohibition applies in a civil enforcement action conducted through state judicial process.

Operative rule: A challenger must first establish circumstances supporting an inference of purposeful sex discrimination. The striking party must then articulate a genuine sex-neutral explanation. The trial court determines whether purposeful discrimination occurred, with the ultimate burden of persuasion remaining on the challenger.

Reasoning: Government may not assume that men or women will decide a dispute in a particular way solely because of sex. Jury service is an exercise of governmental authority and a significant incident of citizenship. Exclusion based on stereotyped assumptions both injures the excluded juror and undermines public confidence in adjudication.

Application: Alabama’s use of nearly every available strike against men in a case where the asserted justification depended on assumptions about sex and family obligations supports the required initial inference. Any individualized explanations must be tested under the ordinary framework on remand.

Limits and questions left open: The decision does not abolish peremptory challenges, treat disproportionate impact alone as proof of purpose, or bar strikes based on individual answers, relationships, experiences, or demeanor that are not proxies for sex.

Legal basis: Fourteenth Amendment Equal Protection Clause.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Reverse and remand.

Public role: Joins Justice Blackmun’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone announces no ground beyond the Court opinion.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

Batson’s procedural framework now governs purposeful sex discrimination as well as racial discrimination in jury selection. Powers and Edmonson are applied without changing their rules concerning third-party standing and state action.

The historical Court reversed 6-3. Chief Justice Rehnquist dissented separately and joined Scalia’s dissent, while Stone joins the Court opinion, producing a 7-2 judgment. The governing prohibition and burden sequence otherwise remain materially unchanged. ([Library of Congress Tile

### C & A Carbone, Inc. v. Town of Clarkstown, 511 U.S. 383 (1994)

Argued December 7, 1993 | Decided May 16, 1994

From: Clarkstown required all nonhazardous solid waste within the town to pass through a designated privately operated transfer station. The station charged a tipping fee used to finance the facility, and the ordinance prevented Carbone from sending processed residue directly to out-of-state disposal sites. New York courts upheld the ordinance and enjoined Carbone’s competing route. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511383/usrep511383.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Philadelphia v. New Jersey and Hughes prohibit protectionist regulation of interstate commerce. Pike governs genuinely evenhanded measures whose incidental burdens are disproportionate. No existing rule permits a municipality to reserve a local processing market for a selected private operator merely because local and out-of-state competitors are excluded alike.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 7-2. Supporting: Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg. Opposing: Blackmun, Souter.

The state courts shall enter judgment barring enforcement against Carbone of the requirement that all waste be processed through the favored private station.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone, Stevens, Scalia, Thomas, Ginsburg | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence in the judgment | O’Connor | No other Justice | Supports reversal | Would invalidate the ordinance under a somewhat different Commerce Clause formulation |
| Dissent | Souter | Blackmun | Would affirm | Treats the arrangement as a permissible local financing and waste-management measure |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Exclusive local processing requirement

Holding: Clarkstown’s ordinance discriminates against interstate commerce by reserving the local waste-processing market for a designated private facility and excluding competing processors and interstate disposal routes.

Operative rule: A law is discriminatory when its practical operation directs commerce to a favored local private enterprise, even if disfavored local firms are excluded alongside out-of-state firms. Such a law is invalid unless the government establishes a legitimate local objective that cannot adequately be served through reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives.

Reasoning: The relevant commerce includes the service of processing and disposing of waste. Clarkstown used regulatory power to ensure that the chosen local station received the processing business and fee before waste could enter competing channels. Uniform health and safety requirements, direct taxes, municipal bonds, contracts, and other nondiscriminatory financing devices remained available.

Limits and questions left open: The decision does not prevent nondiscriminatory regulation of waste, condemn municipal ownership, or decide a materially different ordinance directing waste to a genuinely public facility operating under distinct governmental-market circumstances.

Legal basis: Commerce Clause.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Reverse and remand for judgment barring enforcement of the exclusive requirement.

Public role: Joins Justice Kennedy’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone announces no ground beyond the Court opinion.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

Philadelphia and Hughes are applied to discrimination in a service market rather than only to the physical movement of goods. Pike does not govern because the ordinance is discriminatory in operation, not merely an evenhanded law with incidental interstate effects.

#### Historical Comparison

The historical Court reversed 6-3, with Chief Justice Rehnquist joining Blackmun and Souter in dissent. Stone joins Kennedy’s Court opinion, producing a 7-2 judgment while preserving the historical substantive rule. ([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/511bv.pdf))

### Custis v. United States, 511 U.S. 485 (1994)

([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/511bv.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Gideon, Burgett, and Tucker prohibit custodial reliance on convictions obtained after a complete denial of counsel. Lewis permits Congress to attach consequences to an outstanding conviction without requiring collateral review in every later proceeding. No prior simulated decision establishes a general ACCA sentencing forum for fact-intensive attacks on predicate convictions.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 6-3. Supporting: Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg. Opposing: Blackmun, Stevens, Souter.

Custis’s ACCA sentence remains in force.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone | O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Supports affirmance | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Souter | Blackmun, Stevens | Would reverse and permit a broader class of constitutional challenges at sentencing | Complete dissent |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Collateral attack on ACCA predicates

Holding: A defendant may challenge an ACCA predicate during federal sentencing when the conviction was obtained after a complete denial of the Gideon right to counsel. Other constitutional challenges to an outstanding state conviction must first succeed through an authorized direct or collateral proceeding.

Operative rule: The federal sentencing court determines whether an existing conviction qualifies under the statute. It does not conduct collateral trials concerning ineffective assistance, involuntary pleas, or comparable fact-intensive claims unless Congress has supplied that review procedure. A conviction showing complete absence of counsel receives different treatment under the categorical rule established by Gideon, Burgett, and Tucker.

Reasoning: Section 924(e) focuses on the existence of prior convictions and contains no broad collateral-review mechanism. Litigation of plea colloquies and attorney performance would require records, witnesses, and factfinding more appropriately available in the convicting jurisdiction. Limits and questions left open: Custis may seek direct or collateral relief in the jurisdictions that entered the convictions. If a predicate is later vacated, he may pursue whatever correction of the federal sentence governing law then permits. The Court does not decide the proper procedure or disposition of such a later request.

Legal basis: 18 U.S.C. §§ 921(a)(20) and 924(e), and the Sixth Amendment right to counsel.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Affirm.

Public role: Author of the Opinion of the Court.

Announced ground: The Court opinion states Stone’s complete public ground.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

Burgett and Tucker retain full force for convictions obtained through complete denial of counsel. They are not extended to require adjudication of every alleged constitutional defect during ACCA sentencing. The decision concerns the permissible forum and does not determine the later custodial weight of an otherwise valid uncounseled fine-only misdemeanor.

### Waters v. Churchill, 511 U.S. 661 (1994)

Argued December 1, 1993 | Decided May 31, 1994

From: The Seventh Circuit reversed summary judgment for public-hospital officials because witnesses disputed what Churchill had said during a meal-break conversation. The case asks whether constitutional liability turns only on a later factfinder’s reconstruction of the speech or may instead depend on the factual understanding genuinely and reasonably held by the public employer when it acted. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511661/usrep511661.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Pickering and Connick balance protected public-employee speech against the government’s legitimate interests as employer. Mt. Healthy separately requires attention to the actual reason for an adverse action. Existing law does not determine which factual version of disputed speech supplies the premise for that analysis.

#### Judgment

Vacated and remanded, 7-2. Supporting: Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg. Opposing: Blackmun, Stevens.

The Seventh Circuit shall determine whether the hospital actually relied on a factual understanding that was genuinely held and objectively reasonable in light of information reasonably available when it acted. It shall then apply Pickering and Connick to the speech as permissibly understood, while addressing pretext, individual liability, and qualified immunity separately.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion announcing the judgment; plurality opinion | O’Connor | Stone, Souter, Ginsburg | Supports vacatur and remand | Genuine-belief and objective-reasonableness rule; no independent investigative procedure; pretext and remand |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | Kennedy, Thomas | Supports vacatur and remand on a narrower motive ground | Rejects an objective-reasonableness requirement but agrees that no independent investigative procedure exists and that pretext remains open |
| Dissent | Stevens | Blackmun | Would affirm the Seventh Circuit | Would judge the employer’s action against the speech a factfinder determines was actually made |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Permissible factual basis for public-employee discipline

Holding: A public employer does not violate the First Amendment merely because its factual understanding of disputed employee speech later proves mistaken. It may act on a version it genuinely believed when that belief was objectively reasonable in light of information reasonably available to the decisionmaker.

Operative rule: The employer must establish actual reliance on a genuinely held factual understanding. That understanding must have an objectively reasonable basis. The scope, neutrality, and character of the employer’s inquiry are relevant evidence of actual belief, reasonableness, and pretext, but the First Amendment prescribes no particular number of interviews, mini-hearing, or other independent investigative procedure. Once the permissible factual premise is identified, Pickering and Connick apply to the speech as the employer reasonably understood it.

Reasoning: Public employers frequently must make personnel decisions before litigation can establish disputed workplace facts with certainty. Requiring factual infallibility would impair effective administration, but accepting an asserted belief without objective support would permit protected expression to be punished through pretext or reckless indifference to contrary information.

Application: Conflicting accounts, evidence of management’s prior reaction to Churchill’s criticism, the information available to the final decisionmaker, and alleged selective reliance create disputes concerning genuine belief, objective reasonableness, and actual motive. Summary judgment cannot be entered until those disputes are resolved.

Limits and questions left open: The rule does not protect discipline based on a fabricated reason, an asserted belief the employer did not hold, or an objectively unsupported factual premise. It creates no constitutional workplace-hearing code and does not decide qualified immunity or damages liability for any individual official.

Legal basis: First and Fourteenth Amendments.

Authority of the proposition: No single opinion controls the entire rationale. The plurality and Scalia concurrence, seven Justices in total, agree that a genuinely held, objectively reasonable belief is sufficient to permit reliance on the reported speech and that no independently prescribed investigative procedure exists. The plurality and the Stevens dissent, six Justices in total, agree that an objectively unreasonable mistake cannot itself supply a constitutionally adequate justification. Those cross-cutting majorities establish the stated factual-predicate rule. The judgment and remand are supported by all seven Justices outside the dissent.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Vacate and remand.

Public role: Joins Justice O’Connor’s plurality opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone agrees that constitutional liability turns on the factual understanding actually and reasonably held by the employer, not automatically on a later reconstruction of the conversation. He also joins the limitation treating the employer’s inquiry as evidence rather than as an independent constitutional procedure.

#### Material Separate Writings

Scalia’s concurrence, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, would make genuine reliance and pretext controlling without requiring objective reasonableness. Under that broader view, an honestly held mistake would not violate the First Amendment merely because the employer’s factfinding was careless. That proposition is noncontrolling.

Stevens’s dissent, joined by Blackmun, would place the risk of factual mistake on the public employer and evaluate the discipline against the speech the factfinder determines was actually made. That proposition is noncontrolling.

### Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738 (1994)

Argued January 10, 1994 | Decided June 6, 1994

From: Nichols received no imprisonment for a prior uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, making the conviction valid under Scott. At a later federal sentencing, the Guidelines assigned a criminal-history point to the conviction and increased his presumptive imprisonment range. Nichols preserved the constitutional challenge to that use but did not claim under § 4A1.3 that the resulting category overstated his criminal history. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511738/usrep511738.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Scott permits a misdemeanor conviction without appointed counsel when no imprisonment is imposed. Custis, decided May 23, limits collateral attacks on outstanding predicates at federal sentencing but does not decide what custodial weight a valid fine-only uncounseled conviction may receive. Baldasar produced a judgment against later imprisonment but no single majority rationale.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 5-4. Supporting: O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas. Opposing: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Ginsburg.

Nichols’s sentence remains in force.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plurality opinion | O’Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Supports affirmance | Would permit consideration of any valid fine-only uncounseled conviction in later sentencing and categorically displace Baldasar |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Souter | No other Justice | Supplies the narrow judgment-supporting ground | Permits use under this Guidelines scheme because the prior conviction’s role is rebuttable and individualized departure remains available |
| Dissent | Stone | Blackmun, Stevens, Ginsburg | Would vacate and remand for resentencing | Rejects automatic or presumptive custodial weight resting on the uncounseled adjudication itself |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Use of Nichols’s prior conviction under the federal Guidelines

Holding: Nichols’s valid uncounseled misdemeanor conviction may be included in the Guidelines calculation because the resulting criminal-history assessment is not conclusive and the Guidelines provide a meaningful mechanism for contesting the conviction’s reliability and sentencing significance.

Operative rule: A sentencing scheme may consider a valid fine-only uncounseled misdemeanor when the defendant has a reasonable opportunity to challenge the accuracy and relevance of the information and the sentencing court possesses authority to correct a criminal-history category that materially overstates the defendant’s conduct or risk. The decision does not validate a scheme that gives such a conviction automatic, irrebuttable, or conclusive custodial effect.

Reasoning: Section 4A1.3 permits downward departure when the calculated category significantly overrepresents the seriousness of the defendant’s record or likelihood of recidivism. The prior conviction therefore supplies a presumptive item of sentencing information rather than an unreviewable command. Traditional sentencing practice permits consideration of broadly relevant information when the defendant can contest its accuracy and significance.

Application: Nichols did not preserve a claim that his category overstated his history or that § 4A1.3 relief was wrongly denied. His preserved argument demanded categorical exclusion of the conviction, which the controlling rationale rejects.

Limits and questions left open: The Court does not decide whether an uncounseled fine-only conviction may automatically require increased imprisonment under a scheme lacking individualized correction. Scott remains intact, and the validity of the original conviction is not open to collateral retrial here.

Legal basis: Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments and the federal Sentencing Guidelines.

Authority of the proposition: Souter’s concurrence is the controlling rule under Marks. It is a true logical subset of the O’Connor plurality’s broader rationale: every use permitted under Souter’s scheme-specific rule would also be allowed under the plurality’s categorical rule, while the concurrence expressly reserves applications the plurality would decide. No majority adopts the plurality’s proposed categorical rule.

##### Treatment of Baldasar

No majority categorically overrules Baldasar. The plurality would overrule it, Souter concludes that its fragmented opinions yielded no controlling rationale governing this case, and the four dissenters would preserve its protection against custodial use. The present decision establishes only the narrower Guidelines rule stated above.

Public role: Authors the dissent, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, and Ginsburg.

Announced ground: Stone accepts the continuing validity of the fine-only conviction under Scott but would forbid the uncounseled adjudication itself from automatically or presumptively increasing imprisonment. ### Farmer v. Brennan, 511 U.S. 825 (1994)

Argued January 12, 1994 | Decided June 6, 1994

From: Farmer alleged that federal prison officials transferred and housed her in a high-security male penitentiary’s general population despite a known vulnerability to sexual assault, after which another prisoner raped her. The lower courts entered summary judgment for the officials under a standard that effectively demanded direct proof of actual knowledge. ([Library of Congress Tile

](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511825/usrep511825.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Estelle and Wilson require deliberate indifference rather than negligence for Eighth Amendment prison-condition liability. Helling confirms that exposure to a substantial future risk may be actionable. Existing law leaves the precise knowledge requirement and permissible means of proof unresolved.

#### Judgment

Vacated and remanded, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

The lower courts shall apply the subjective-awareness standard, permit ordinary circumstantial proof, and determine separately for each defendant whether that official knew of and disregarded the risk or responded reasonably.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Ginsburg | Supports vacatur and remand | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence | Blackmun | No other Justice | Supports the judgment | Would recognize broader responsibility for systemic prison conditions |
| Concurrence | Stevens | No other Justice | Supports the judgment | Explains his adherence to the Court’s precedent concerning the subjective component |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Thomas | No other Justice | Supports vacatur and remand | Questions the broader constitutional basis for prison-condition claims but accepts the disposition |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Knowledge and disregard of a serious custodial risk

Holding: A prison official violates the Eighth Amendment when the official knows that an inmate faces a substantial risk of serious harm and consciously disregards that risk by failing to take reasonable protective measures.

Operative rule: The claimant must prove an objectively substantial risk and the defendant’s actual awareness of it. Awareness may be proved through direct or circumstantial evidence. A factfinder may infer knowledge when the risk was so obvious that a denial of awareness is not credible, but obviousness is evidence rather than an irrebuttable presumption. An official who responds reasonably is not liable merely because the measures fail to prevent injury.

Reasoning: Deliberate indifference requires more than negligence but does not require a purpose to cause harm. A subjective recklessness standard distinguishes punishment from an inadvertent failure to perceive danger while allowing knowledge to be established through ordinary factfinding rather than an official’s admission.

Application: Evidence concerning Farmer’s vulnerability, institutional history, conditions at the penitentiary, and information available to individual officials could support an inference of awareness. The officials remain free to show that they lacked the requisite knowledge or took reasonable protective action. Those questions cannot be resolved under a rule requiring direct admission.

Limits and questions left open: The decision imposes no strict liability for inmate assault and does not constitutionalize every housing or classification mistake. Knowledge, conscious disregard, causation, and each defendant’s own role must be established separately. Qualified immunity and damages remain distinct inquiries.

Legal basis: Eighth Amendment Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Vacate and remand.

Public role: Joins Justice Souter’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone announces no ground beyond the Court opinion.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

Estelle and Wilson are clarified. Deliberate indifference requires subjective awareness, but circumstantial evidence and the obviousness of a risk may establish that awareness. A reasonable response defeats liability even when serious harm occurs.

#### Historical Comparison

The historical Court also vacated and remanded unanimously in the judgment. Stone replaces Rehnquist in Souter’s eight-Justice Court opinion. The official historical topology also included separate concurrences by Blackmun and Stevens, and a concurrence in the judgment by Thomas. ([Library of Congress Tile

Preventing both an instruction and corrective argument deprived Simmons of a meaningful opportunity to answer a material sentencing premise and created an unacceptable risk that the death sentence rested on misinformation.

Application: The jury specifically expressed concern about the meaning of life imprisonment, and the trial court’s response did not disclose parole ineligibility. The defect requires a new penalty proceeding but supplies no basis for disturbing the conviction.

Limits and questions left open: The rule does not require predictions about discretionary parole when eligibility remains legally possible or contingent. ### Central Bank of Denver, N.A. v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A., 511 U.S. 164 (1994)

Argued November 30, 1993 | Decided April 19, 1994

From: The Tenth Circuit reversed summary judgment for Central Bank and permitted a private aiding-and-abetting theory under § 10(b) to proceed. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511164/usrep511164.pdf))

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Relief or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Categorical availability of private aiding-and-abetting liability | Tenth Circuit affirmed, 5-4 | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | A private plaintiff is not categorically barred from pursuing culpable secondary assistance. |
| Tenth Circuit’s broader recklessness formulation | Vacated, 5-4 | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | The claim must satisfy the narrower knowledge, responsibility, assistance, and causation requirements stated by the Court. |

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone | Blackmun, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | Supports affirmance in part, vacatur in part, and remand | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Kennedy | O’Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Would reverse and direct judgment for Central Bank on the secondary-liability theory | Would confine the private action categorically to primary violators |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Limited private liability for culpable assistance

Holding: A private § 10(b) plaintiff may pursue aiding-and-abetting liability by proving a completed primary violation, the defendant’s knowledge of that violation or conscious recklessness approaching actual knowledge, and substantial assistance that materially facilitated the violation. An omission qualifies only when the defendant had a defined, transaction-specific responsibility to act.

Operative rule: The plaintiff retains the ordinary burdens of proving transaction causation, loss causation, and damages. Professional status, negligence, proximity to the transaction, or silence unconnected to a defined responsibility is insufficient. The secondary defendant’s state of mind and assistance must be established independently of the primary violator’s misconduct.

Reasoning: The established private action would be materially incomplete if a participant could knowingly employ a defined transaction role to facilitate a completed securities fraud while escaping solely because another actor made the primary misstatement. At the same time, secondary liability cannot become professional negligence by another name. The heightened state-of-mind requirement, material-assistance requirement, and transaction-specific limitation on omissions preserve that boundary.

Application: Central Bank’s appraisal delay can support further proceedings only if the plaintiffs prove the primary fraud, the required state of mind, a defined responsibility to act, material facilitation, and the ordinary causal and damages requirements. The existing record does not itself establish those propositions.

Limits and questions left open: The Court does not adjudicate an unpresented primary-liability theory, change the elements governing primary violators, or impose liability for ordinary negligence or passive association.

Legal basis: Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act, Rule 10b-5, and the established implied private action.

Vote: Affirm the rejection of a categorical bar, vacate the broader liability formulation, and remand.

Public role: Author of the Opinion of the Court.

Announced ground: The Court opinion states Stone’s complete public ground.

#### Material Separate Writings

Kennedy’s dissent, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, and Thomas, would hold that the implied private action reaches only a defendant who personally commits the manipulative or deceptive conduct prohibited by § 10(b). Culpable assistance by another actor would remain outside the private action absent an express congressional remedy.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

Hochfelder’s scienter requirement is preserved and applied to secondary assistance through the Court’s heightened knowledge formulation. Blue Chip Stamps and Chiarella remain limits on the implied action but do not establish a categorical immunity for a defendant who knowingly and materially facilitates a completed primary violation.

### Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244 (1994)

Argued October 13, 1993 | Decided April 26, 1994

From: The Fifth Circuit declined to apply the new compensatory-damages, punitive-damages, and jury-trial provisions of § 102 of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 to a pending Title VII case based on pre-enactment conduct. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511244/usrep511244.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Harris confirms that actionable harassment need not cause serious psychological injury, but Landgraf’s granted question assumes the prior merits findings and concerns only the temporal reach of the new remedies. Bowen, Bradley, and Kaiser leave unresolved a general sequence for reconciling statutory silence and retroactive effect.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 8-1. Supporting: Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg. Opposing: Blackmun.

The new § 102 remedies and accompanying jury-trial provision do not govern Landgraf’s pre-enactment claim.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg | Supports affirmance | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | Kennedy, Thomas | Supports affirmance | Agrees on nonretroactivity through a different account of the governing interpretive principles |
| Dissent | Blackmun | No other Justice | Would reverse | Would apply the new remedial provisions to the pending case |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Temporal reach of newly enacted remedies

Holding: Section 102’s compensatory-damages, punitive-damages, and associated jury-trial provisions do not apply to conduct completed before enactment because Congress did not clearly prescribe that reach and application would attach new legal consequences to past conduct.

Operative rule: A court first asks whether Congress clearly prescribed the statute’s temporal reach. If the enactment contains no controlling command, the court determines whether applying it would attach new legal consequences to completed conduct, including by increasing liability, impairing an existing right, or imposing a new duty. When such retroactive effect would result, the statute does not govern absent clear congressional intent. A genuinely procedural provision may apply to a pending matter when doing so does not alter substantive liability or completed legal consequences. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511244/usrep511244.pdf))

Reasoning: Section 102 made legal damages available for past conduct that previously carried only the earlier Title VII remedial consequences. That increased exposure is substantive in operation regardless of the remedial label. The jury-trial entitlement accompanies the newly created damages claim and therefore does not operate independently here.

Limits and questions left open: Congress remains free to prescribe retroactive operation through clear language. The decision does not bar application of genuinely procedural provisions to pending proceedings and does not decide § 101’s separate effect.

Legal basis: The Civil Rights Act of 1991 and the traditional presumption against statutory retroactivity.

Announced ground: Stone announces no ground beyond the Court opinion.

([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511571/usrep511571.pdf))

#### Judgment

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Dissent | Kennedy | O’Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Would affirm the Sixth Circuit | Would treat patient-care direction satisfying the listed statutory criteria as action in the employer’s interest |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Professional direction and statutory supervisory authority

Holding: Section 2(11) permits the Board to distinguish genuine management authority from professional or technical direction incidental to an employee’s own skilled work. The Board permissibly classified Heartland’s nurses as protected employees rather than supervisors.

Operative rule: Work does not become supervisory merely because it benefits the enterprise or includes skilled direction of less-experienced employees. The Board may examine whether the asserted authority derives from professional competence in performing the service or instead aligns the employee with management through genuine labor-management prerogatives. Reasoning: Reading “in the interest of the employer” to include everything that advances the enterprise would perform almost no limiting work because ordinary employees are hired to advance the enterprise. Congress simultaneously excluded supervisors and protected professional employees. The Board’s distinction reconciles those provisions by focusing on whether authority identifies the worker with management rather than merely with competent performance of the service.

Application: The nurses spent only a small portion of their time directing aides, generally followed established assignment patterns, and referred nonroutine matters to the administrator or director of nursing. Those findings support the Board’s conclusion that the nurses did not exercise genuine front-line management authority. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511571/usrep511571.pdf))

Limits and questions left open: Nurses, professionals, and technical employees may qualify as supervisors when they independently exercise genuine management prerogatives. The decision does not exempt a profession as a class.

Legal basis: NLRA §§ 2(3), 2(11), and 2(12).

The dissent would reject the Board’s management-alignment distinction.

Congress sought to preserve free local broadcasting and address cable operators’ structural bottleneck control, not to promote a preferred message. Cable operators nevertheless exercise protected editorial judgment, so the less demanding broadcast-scarcity standard does not apply. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep512/usrep512622/usrep512622.pdf))

##### Legislative predictions and the remaining fit inquiry

Holding: Congress had an adequate legislative basis for concluding that preserving free local broadcasting is important, that cable bottleneck power could threaten local stations, and that carriage protection could materially alleviate that threat. Those predictive judgments need not be reproved as adjudicative facts or established by a trial-level preponderance. Summary judgment nevertheless cannot stand because the record does not adequately permit an independent assessment of the actual magnitude and distribution of displaced cable programming and whether the carriage obligation is reasonably fitted to the established interests.

Reasoning: Legislatures may act on reasonable forecasts in complex and developing markets without compiling a trial record. Judicial respect for those forecasts does not eliminate independent constitutional review of the burden imposed on protected editorial selection. The unresolved question is therefore the degree and distribution of compelled displacement, not whether Congress must establish market collapse or reconstruct its legislative findings at trial.

Limits and questions left open: The Court neither sustains nor invalidates the statute on the merits. It creates no requirement that every must-carry scheme contain a system-specific exemption, and cable systems remain free to pursue properly supported as-applied challenges.

Legal basis: First Amendment and §§ 4 and 5 of the Cable Television Consumer Protection and Competition Act of 1992.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Vacate and remand.

Public role: Joins Justice Kennedy’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone joins the content-neutral classification, intermediate-scrutiny rule, acceptance of Congress’s supported general predictions, and the limited remand concerning editorial burden and constitutional fit.

##### Standard for a content-neutral speech injunction

Holding: A content-neutral injunction directed at identified speakers must be reviewed provision by provision, and each restraint must burden no more speech than necessary to remedy demonstrated unlawful conduct and protect the significant interests affected by that conduct.

Reasoning: Equity must prevent an enjoined party from evading a valid order through agents or collaborators. That enforcement principle does not authorize guilt by association. ### FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471 (1994)

Argued October 4, 1993 | Decided February 23, 1994

From: The Ninth Circuit affirmed a $130,000 damages judgment against FSLIC, whose successor was the FDIC. Meyer alleged that his summary discharge deprived him of a constitutionally protected employment interest without notice or a hearing. The court below held that the FTCA did not make the claim exclusive, that FSLIC’s sue-and-be-sued clause waived immunity, and that a constitutional damages action could proceed directly against the agency. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510471/usrep510471.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Albright distinguishes the existence of a constitutional source from the availability of a particular damages action. #### Judgment

Reversed, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

##### Judgment, Mandate, and Remand

The agency damages award shall be vacated, and judgment shall be entered for the FDIC on the claim against FSLIC. The Court’s disposition does not reopen the unreviewed judgment concerning the individual official.

Justice Thomas delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg.

#### Decision of the Court

##### FTCA exclusivity and the sue-and-be-sued waiver

Holding: Meyer’s federal constitutional claim is not cognizable under 28 U.S.C. § 1346(b), so the FTCA does not make the United States the exclusive defendant. FSLIC’s broad sue-and-be-sued clause waives the agency’s sovereign immunity from suit on the claim.

Reasoning: Section 1346(b) requires liability analogous to that of a private person under the law of the place, meaning state law. A Fifth Amendment claim arises under federal law and has no state-law private-person analogue. Section 2679(a) therefore does not displace the agency’s otherwise unrestricted waiver. Immunity is removed, but the waiver does not itself supply a substantive cause of action.

Legal basis: 28 U.S.C. §§ 1346(b) and 2679(a), and FSLIC’s organic sue-and-be-sued clause.

##### Bivens liability of a federal agency

Holding: Bivens does not imply a damages cause of action directly against a federal agency, even when sovereign immunity has been waived.

Operative rule: A waiver of immunity determines whether an entity may be sued. It does not create a constitutional cause of action or authorize damages. The officer remedy recognized in Bivens does not extend automatically to the employing agency.

Reasoning: Bivens developed as a remedy against responsible federal officers and operated alongside personal deterrence and qualified immunity. Direct agency liability would bypass that structure and create a substantially different fiscal remedy against the Federal Government. The constitutional merits of Meyer’s discharge therefore need not be decided.

Limits and questions left open: Existing Bivens actions against individual officers are not narrowed. The Court does not decide any statutory cause of action, prospective remedy, or the due-process merits of Meyer’s discharge.

Legal basis: The Fifth Amendment and the judicially implied remedy recognized in Bivens.

Vote: Reverse and direct entry of judgment for the FDIC on the agency claim.

Public role: Joins Justice Thomas’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone announces no ground beyond the Court opinion.

### Davis v. United States, 512 U.S. 452 (1994)

Argued March 29, 1994 | Decided June 24, 1994

From: After knowingly waiving his Miranda rights, Davis spoke with naval investigators for approximately 90 minutes and then said, “Maybe I should talk to a lawyer.” The agents stopped substantive questioning, asked whether he was requesting counsel, and received a clear denial before continuing. Questioning ended when Davis later made an unequivocal request for a lawyer. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep512/usrep512452/usrep512452.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Miranda and Edwards require interrogation to cease after an invocation of counsel but leave open the required response when a reasonable officer understands a statement as a genuine but ambiguous possible request.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

Davis’s conviction remains in force because the investigators used the clarification procedure now required.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Ginsburg | Supports affirmance | Mandatory pause and neutral-clarification rule; application to Davis |
| Concurrence in the judgment | O’Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Supports affirmance | Would require questioning to cease only after an unambiguous request and would leave clarification as recommended practice |
| Separate concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | No other Justice | Supports affirmance | Joins the clear-invocation ground and separately addresses the statutory status of voluntary confessions |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Ambiguous possible request for counsel after waiver

Holding: When a reasonable officer would understand a suspect’s words as a genuine but ambiguous possible request for counsel, substantive interrogation must pause while officers ask neutral questions directed solely to whether counsel is being requested.

Operative rule: If the suspect clearly invokes counsel, interrogation must cease under Edwards. If the suspect clearly disclaims an invocation, substantive questioning may resume. Clarification may not become persuasion, argument, discouragement of counsel, or questioning about the offense. A passing reference that no reasonable officer would understand as a possible request does not activate the clarification requirement.

Reasoning: A suspect should not lose the Miranda choice merely because the desire for counsel is expressed imperfectly, while officers should not be required to treat every reference to a lawyer as a completed invocation. Neutral clarification protects the suspect’s choice and gives investigators a practical, objective method for resolving uncertainty.

Application: Davis’s statement reasonably raised uncertainty about whether he wanted counsel. The investigators immediately paused, asked whether he was requesting a lawyer, received a clear denial, and only then resumed. They stopped when he later invoked unequivocally. His statements therefore were not obtained in violation of the governing rule.

Limits and questions left open: The Court prescribes no required wording for clarification and does not alter the rule that a clear invocation immediately ends interrogation until counsel is present or the suspect lawfully reinitiates communication.

Legal basis: Fifth Amendment privilege against compelled self-incrimination and the safeguards established by Miranda and Edwards.

Authority of the proposition: Justice Souter’s opinion, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, and Ginsburg, is an actual five-Justice Opinion of the Court. O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas agree only with the judgment and would not require clarification.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Affirm.

Public role: Joins Justice Souter’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone agrees that genuine ambiguity requires a pause and neutral clarification, and that the investigators complied with that requirement here.

#### Material Separate Writings

O’Connor’s concurrence in the judgment, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas, would retain a clear-invocation threshold and would not make clarification constitutionally mandatory. That proposition is noncontrolling.

Scalia’s separate concurrence agrees with the clear-invocation position and additionally questions reliance on judicially created safeguards without considering the governing federal confession statute. That additional proposition is noncontrolling.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

Edwards is extended to require neutral clarification when the suspect’s statement creates a genuine, objectively reasonable uncertainty about whether counsel is wanted. A clear request still ends interrogation immediately, and the decision does not treat every mention of a lawyer as an invocation.

#### Historical Comparison

The historical Court unanimously affirmed, but five Justices adopted the clear-invocation rule and treated clarification as good practice rather than a requirement. Stone joins the four historical Souter concurrence Justices, making the pause-and-clarify rule controlling while preserving the unanimous judgment. ### Ratzlaf v. United States, 510 U.S. 135 (1994)

Argued November 1, 1993 | Decided January 11, 1994

From: The Ninth Circuit affirmed Ratzlaf’s conviction after the jury was instructed that he had to know of the bank-reporting requirement and intend to evade it, but did not have to know that arranging transactions below $10,000 was itself unlawful. The record showed deliberate structuring of cashier’s-check purchases after casino personnel explained the reporting threshold. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510135/usrep510135.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: No earlier same-term holding resolves the question. Cheek and Liparota recognize that statutory context may require knowledge of illegality when Congress uses willfulness and the underlying conduct is not inherently blameworthy.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 6-3. Supporting: Stone, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg. Opposing: Blackmun, O’Connor, Thomas.

Ratzlaf’s conviction under 31 U.S.C. §§ 5322 and 5324 is vacated. The Government may retry him under a correct instruction if otherwise lawful and supported by admissible evidence.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Blackmun | O’Connor, Thomas | Would affirm | Would require knowledge of the reporting requirement and an intent to evade it, but not knowledge that structuring is unlawful |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Willfulness in the anti-structuring offense

Holding: Conviction under §§ 5322 and 5324 requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant knew the structuring was unlawful, in addition to proof that the defendant intended to evade the financial institution’s reporting duty.

Operative rule: Section 5324’s purpose-to-evade language establishes the required object of the transaction pattern. Section 5322’s separate willfulness requirement adds knowledge that the chosen structuring conduct violates law. The Government need not prove knowledge of the precise statutory citation or every detail of the reporting scheme.

Reasoning: Reading willfulness as no more than intentional evasion would make it duplicate the mental state already stated in § 5324. Structuring otherwise lawful transactions is not conduct whose illegality is self-evident. Congress’s use of the same willfulness term throughout the reporting subchapter, together with ordinary criminal fair-notice principles, requires knowledge of the legal prohibition.

Application: The jury was affirmatively instructed that knowledge of unlawfulness was unnecessary. It therefore did not find every element required by the statute.

Limits and questions left open: The holding creates no general ignorance-of-law defense, does not alter civil reporting obligations, and does not prevent Congress from changing the offense’s mental state through clear enacted language.

Legal basis: 31 U.S.C. §§ 5322 and 5324, and the Fifth Amendment requirement that every criminal element be proved beyond a reasonable doubt.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Reverse and remand.

Public role: Joins Justice Ginsburg’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone announces no ground beyond the Court opinion.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

Cheek and Liparota are applied to the distinctive text and context of the currency-reporting subchapter. The decision establishes knowledge of unlawfulness as an element of the willful anti-structuring offense, without creating a general rule for every statute using the word willfully.

#### Historical Comparison

The historical Court also reversed, but by a 5-4 vote. Chief Justice Rehnquist joined Blackmun’s dissent; Stone instead joins Ginsburg’s opinion, producing a 6-3 judgment while preserving the historical statutory rule and remedy. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510135/usrep510135.pdf))

Research sources: Official U.S. Reports for the transactions, jury instruction, statutory provisions, judgment, historical lineup, and separate opinions. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510135/usrep510135.pdf))

### Department of Defense v. Federal Labor Relations Authority, 510 U.S. 487 (1994)

Argued November 8, 1993 | Decided February 23, 1994

From: The Fifth Circuit enforced FLRA orders requiring federal agencies to provide unions with bargaining-unit employees’ names and home addresses. The court counted the unions’ legitimate collective-bargaining use as a public interest favoring disclosure under FOIA Exemption 6 and therefore concluded that the Privacy Act’s FOIA exception permitted release. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510487/usrep510487.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Reporters Committee limits FOIA’s public interest to information that materially illuminates government operations. Department of State v. Ray requires that interest to be weighed against the privacy invasion caused by disclosure.

#### Judgment

Reversed, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

The Fifth Circuit’s judgment is reversed. The home-address disclosure orders may not be enforced through FOIA’s exception to the Privacy Act.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Supports reversal | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence | Souter | No other Justice | Supports reversal | Joins the Court and adds observations concerning the privacy interest |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Ginsburg | No other Justice | Supports reversal | Treats Reporters Committee as controlling while reserving disagreement with parts of its account of FOIA’s text |

#### Decision of the Court

##### FOIA public interest and employee home addresses

Holding: FOIA does not require public disclosure of federal employees’ home addresses because the addresses reveal virtually nothing about agency operations and their disclosure would constitute a clearly unwarranted invasion of personal privacy. The Privacy Act’s FOIA exception therefore does not authorize release.

Operative rule: Under Exemption 6, the legally relevant public interest is the contribution disclosure makes to public understanding of governmental activity. The identity, purpose, and private benefit of the requester do not enlarge that interest. Residential information implicates privacy through unwanted contact, loss of control over further dissemination, and the connection of named individuals to their homes.

Reasoning: The unions’ bargaining objective is lawful and significant under federal labor law, but it is not the transparency interest FOIA serves. Once the request is evaluated as disclosure to the public rather than delivery to a preferred user, the addresses contribute negligible information about agency conduct while surrendering personal residential data.

Limits and questions left open: The decision does not diminish collective-bargaining rights, bar disclosure with employee consent, decide every possible Privacy Act routine use, or prevent Congress from creating a separate disclosure mechanism with defined safeguards.

Legal basis: 5 U.S.C. §§ 552(b)(6), 552a, and 7114(b)(4).

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Reverse.

Public role: Joins Justice Thomas’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone announces no additional ground.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

Reporters Committee is applied without alteration. A requester’s beneficial use of personal information does not count as a FOIA public interest unless disclosure itself illuminates government operations.

#### Historical Comparison

The historical Court reached the same unanimous judgment. Stone replaces Chief Justice Rehnquist among the eight Justices joining Thomas’s opinion; Souter’s separate concurrence and Ginsburg’s concurrence in the judgment remain. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510487/usrep510487.pdf))

Research sources: Official U.S. Reports for the requested records, statutory interaction, Exemption 6 balance, judgment, and historical opinion structure. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510487/usrep510487.pdf))

### Elder v. Holloway, 510 U.S. 510 (1994)

Argued January 10, 1994 | Decided February 23, 1994

From: The Ninth Circuit affirmed qualified immunity for officers who surrounded Elder’s home and ordered him outside before arresting him without a warrant. Although the court identified its own potentially relevant precedent, United States v. Al-Azzawy, it refused to consider that decision because Elder had not cited it to the district court. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510510/usrep510510.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Harlow, Mitchell, and Anderson make the clearly established character of a right an objective legal question. No prior simulated decision permits an appellate court to disregard governing law because a party’s research below was incomplete.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

The Ninth Circuit shall reconsider qualified immunity using all materially relevant precedent, including Al-Azzawy. The Court does not decide whether the officers ultimately receive immunity.

Justice Ginsburg delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Legal authorities considered on qualified-immunity review

Holding: A court reviewing qualified immunity must consider all materially relevant precedent available at the time of the challenged conduct, including authority not cited to the district court.

Operative rule: Whether governing law clearly established the asserted right is a question of law reviewed de novo. Parties remain responsible for preserving claims and the factual premises on which those claims depend, but they do not bind a court to an incomplete statement of controlling law.

Reasoning: Qualified immunity asks what the body of law objectively communicated to a reasonable official. That answer cannot depend on whether a litigant located every relevant decision in the trial court. Ordinary principles allowing courts to determine and apply law therefore govern the immunity inquiry.

Application: The Ninth Circuit identified Al-Azzawy as potentially relevant but declined to evaluate its legal effect. It must now determine what that decision and the rest of the governing law established concerning the asserted warrantless in-home arrest.

Limits and questions left open: The Court does not decide whether Al-Azzawy clearly established a violation, whether the officers’ conduct amounted to an in-home arrest, whether exigent circumstances existed, or whether immunity ultimately applies.

Legal basis: 42 U.S.C. § 1983 and the legal standard governing qualified immunity.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Reverse and remand.

Public role: Joins Justice Ginsburg’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone announces no ground beyond the Court opinion.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

Harlow and Anderson are clarified as requiring courts to determine clearly established law from the complete body of materially relevant authority. Failure to cite a case below does not operate as forfeiture of the law itself.

#### Historical Comparison

The historical Court also reversed and remanded unanimously through Justice Ginsburg’s opinion. Stone occupies Chief Justice Rehnquist’s place without changing the judgment, rule, or scope of remand. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510510/usrep510510.pdf))

Research sources: Official U.S. Reports for the arrest circumstances, treatment of Al-Azzawy, de novo review rule, judgment, and historical unanimity. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510510/usrep510510.pdf))

### Powell v. Nevada, 511 U.S. 79 (1994)

Argued February 22, 1994 | Decided March 30, 1994

From: Powell was arrested without a warrant on November 3, 1989. A magistrate did not determine probable cause until November 7, and Powell made incriminating statements that day. His conviction remained on direct review when County of Riverside v. McLaughlin established its general 48-hour benchmark, but the Nevada Supreme Court declined to apply McLaughlin because the arrest predated that decision. ([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/511bv.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Griffith requires new criminal-procedure rules to apply to all cases still pending on direct review. McLaughlin treats a probable-cause determination more than 48 hours after a warrantless arrest as presumptively unreasonable, subject to proof of a qualifying emergency or other extraordinary circumstance.

#### Judgment

Vacated and remanded, 8-1. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg. Opposing: Thomas.

The Nevada Supreme Court shall apply McLaughlin, address any adequate preservation rule, determine whether the State overcomes the presumption of unreasonable delay, and decide the authorized remedy and harmlessness questions. The Court orders neither release nor a new trial on the present record.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Supports vacatur and remand | Complete opinion |
| Dissent | Thomas | No other Justice | Would affirm | Would not require the Nevada court to apply McLaughlin in the circumstances presented |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Application of McLaughlin to a nonfinal conviction

Holding: McLaughlin applies to Powell because his criminal judgment was not final when that decision was announced.

Reasoning: Griffith forbids selective prospective application of a new rule to one defendant while withholding it from another whose case remains on direct review. The fact that Powell’s arrest occurred earlier does not permit the Nevada court to deny him the same governing constitutional rule.

Application: The approximately four-day interval exceeds McLaughlin’s 48-hour benchmark and is presumptively unreasonable. The state court must determine under the full record whether extraordinary circumstances justified the delay.

Limits and questions left open: The Court does not decide whether Powell adequately preserved every remedial theory, whether his November 7 statements must be suppressed, whether any constitutional error was harmless, or whether release, retrial, or resentencing is authorized.

Legal basis: Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments, Griffith v. Kentucky, and County of Riverside v. McLaughlin.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Vacate and remand.

Public role: Joins Justice Ginsburg’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone agrees that Griffith requires application of McLaughlin but that preservation, justification, causation, remedy, and harmlessness remain distinct questions for the Nevada courts.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

Griffith is applied without modification. McLaughlin’s rule governs nonfinal cases, but application of the rule does not itself prescribe suppression or another automatic remedy.

#### Historical Comparison

The historical Court vacated and remanded 7-2, with Chief Justice Rehnquist joining Thomas’s dissent. Stone joins Ginsburg’s opinion, producing an 8-1 judgment while leaving the rule and remand materially unchanged. ([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/511bv.pdf))

Research sources: Official U.S. Reports for the arrest and probable-cause chronology, direct-review posture, Griffith analysis, judgment, and historical lineup. ([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/511bv.pdf))

### Stansbury v. California, 511 U.S. 318 (1994)

Argued March 30, 1994 | Decided April 26, 1994

From: Four plainclothes officers transported Stansbury from his home to a police station late at night and questioned him without Miranda warnings. The California Supreme Court treated the point at which investigators began focusing suspicion on him as materially important to determining custody. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511318/usrep511318.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Miranda custody depends on the objective circumstances manifested to the person being questioned. Beckwith, Mathiason, Berkemer, and Beheler reject suspect status or investigative focus as independently dispositive.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

The California Supreme Court shall determine whether Stansbury was in custody during any part of the unwarned interview under the correct objective standard, then apply the governing suppression, derivative-evidence, and harmless-error rules.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Per curiam Opinion of the Court | The Court | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence | Blackmun | No other Justice | Supports reversal and remand | Joins the per curiam opinion and states an additional capital-punishment ground |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Objective custody under Miranda

Holding: An officer’s undisclosed belief that a person is or is not a suspect is irrelevant to Miranda custody. An officer’s words or conduct may be considered only insofar as they would affect how a reasonable person in the interviewee’s position understood the freedom to end the encounter and leave.

Operative rule: Custody turns on the objective circumstances manifested to the person questioned and whether those circumstances impose restraint comparable to formal arrest. Communicated suspicion is relevant but not automatically dispositive. An undisclosed view may be used to test an officer’s credibility concerning what occurred, but it does not itself enter the custody standard.

Reasoning: Miranda addresses the coercive setting perceived by the person being questioned, not the government’s unexpressed investigative judgment. A person cannot respond to an intention that was never communicated. The California court’s focus inquiry therefore relied on a legally irrelevant consideration.

Application: The remaining facts, including late-night transportation, stationhouse questioning, the officers’ conduct, and what Stansbury was told, require an objective assessment by the state court in the first instance.

Limits and questions left open: Stationhouse questioning is not automatically custodial, communicated suspect status is not conclusive, and the Court does not decide whether Stansbury was in custody or what evidence must be suppressed.

Legal basis: Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments and Miranda v. Arizona.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Reverse and remand.

Public role: Joins the per curiam opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone announces no additional ground.

#### Material Separate Writings

Blackmun’s concurrence joins the Court’s Miranda ruling and states that he would also vacate the death sentence under his separate view that capital punishment cannot be administered consistently with the Constitution. That additional proposition is noncontrolling. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511318/usrep511318.pdf))

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

The received objective custody rule is clarified. Investigative focus and private police suspicion have no independent legal significance unless manifested in circumstances that affect a reasonable person’s perception of freedom.

#### Historical Comparison

The historical Court issued the same per curiam reversal and remand, accompanied by Blackmun’s concurrence. Stone’s substitution for Chief Justice Rehnquist does not change the judgment or custody rule. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511318/usrep511318.pdf))

Research sources: Official U.S. Reports for the interview circumstances, objective-custody rule, remand, and Blackmun’s concurrence. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511318/usrep511318.pdf))

### Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Insurance Co. of America, 511 U.S. 375 (1994)

Argued March 1, 1994 | Decided May 16, 1994

From: The parties settled a removed federal action and filed a Rule 41(a)(1)(ii) stipulation dismissing it with prejudice. The dismissal neither incorporated the settlement nor retained jurisdiction to enforce it. When a later contract dispute arose, the district court entered an enforcement order in the closed case, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed on an inherent-power theory. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511375/usrep511375.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Federal courts possess only jurisdiction conferred by Constitution and statute. Ancillary jurisdiction may resolve factually interdependent matters or enable a court to manage its proceedings and effectuate its decrees, but convenience alone supplies no jurisdiction.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

The settlement-enforcement order shall be vacated. The proceeding must be dismissed for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction unless an independent federal jurisdictional basis is established.

Justice Scalia delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Ancillary jurisdiction to enforce a settlement after dismissal

Holding: A federal district court lacks ancillary jurisdiction to enforce a settlement following a Rule 41(a)(1)(ii) dismissal when the dismissal neither incorporates the settlement terms nor expressly retains enforcement jurisdiction. An independent jurisdictional basis is then required.

Operative rule: Enforcement remains ancillary when the dismissal order makes compliance with the settlement part of the federal decree or expressly retains jurisdiction over the agreement. Otherwise, the alleged breach presents an independent contract controversy governed through a court possessing its own jurisdiction.

Reasoning: The later dispute concerns contractual rights and facts distinct from the claims dismissed. Because the federal order did not command performance of the settlement, enforcement is not necessary to vindicate or administer that order. Familiarity with the parties and efficiency cannot create subject-matter jurisdiction.

Limits and questions left open: The parties may pursue a state contract action or a separate federal action supported by diversity or another jurisdictional grant. A future dismissal order may incorporate settlement terms or retain jurisdiction when lawfully requested.

Legal basis: Article III, the federal jurisdictional statutes, ancillary-jurisdiction principles, and Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 41(a)(1)(ii).

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Reverse and remand.

Public role: Joins Justice Scalia’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone announces no ground beyond the Court opinion.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

The decision supplies the first same-term rule governing federal enforcement of settlements after dismissal. A prior federal action does not itself create continuing jurisdiction over a later contract dispute.

#### Historical Comparison

The historical Court reached the same unanimous judgment through Scalia’s opinion. Stone occupies Chief Justice Rehnquist’s place without changing the jurisdictional rule or remand. ([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/511bv.pdf))

Research sources: Official U.S. Reports for the dismissal, absence of retained jurisdiction, ancillary-jurisdiction analysis, judgment, and historical unanimity. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511375/usrep511375.pdf))

### Digital Equipment Corp. v. Desktop Direct, Inc., 511 U.S. 863 (1994)

Argued February 22, 1994 | Decided June 6, 1994

From: Desktop obtained an order vacating a settlement-based dismissal and reviving its underlying suit against Digital. Digital sought immediate appellate review, asserting a contractual right not to stand trial, but the Tenth Circuit dismissed the appeal for lack of a final collateral order. ([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/511bv.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Cohen and its successors confine collateral-order review to a small category of conclusive, separate rulings involving important rights that would be effectively unreviewable after final judgment. Kokkonen confirms that an unincorporated settlement ordinarily creates private contractual rights rather than a continuing federal decree.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

Digital’s interlocutory appeal remains dismissed. It may challenge the settlement ruling after final judgment or use another authorized route for interlocutory review.

Justice Souter delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Ginsburg.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Collateral-order appeal from refusal to enforce a settlement

Holding: An order vacating a settlement-based dismissal or refusing to enforce a settlement is not immediately appealable under the collateral-order doctrine.

Reasoning: The order may conclusively determine the settlement issue and remain separate from the merits, but the asserted private contractual interest in avoiding trial does not possess the public and legal importance required to overcome § 1291’s final-judgment rule. The enforceability issue can be reviewed after final judgment, and ordinary contract remedies can address any breach.

Application: Digital’s asserted loss is the burden of continued litigation after denial of its settlement defense. Treating that burden as sufficient would permit immediate appeal from a broad range of contract and procedural rulings and fragment federal litigation.

Limits and questions left open: The decision does not govern constitutional or statutory immunities whose value would be destroyed by trial itself. It leaves available § 1292(b), final review, and mandamus in an extraordinary case satisfying its independent requirements.

Legal basis: 28 U.S.C. § 1291 and the collateral-order doctrine.

#### Justice Stone’s Announced Position

Vote: Affirm.

Public role: Joins Justice Souter’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone announces no additional ground.

#### Effect on Existing Simulated Law

Cohen’s exception is applied narrowly. Kokkonen’s treatment of an unincorporated settlement as an independent contract dispute reinforces, but does not independently decide, the conclusion that settlement enforcement lacks the exceptional status required for immediate appeal.

#### Historical Comparison

The historical Court also affirmed unanimously through Souter’s opinion. Stone replaces Chief Justice Rehnquist without changing the finality rule, judgment, or available alternative review channels. ([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/511bv.pdf))

Research sources: Official U.S. Reports for the settlement dispute, interlocutory posture, collateral-order analysis, judgment, and historical unanimity. ([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/511bv.pdf))

### Romano v. Oklahoma, 512 U.S. 1 (1994)

Argued March 22, 1994 | Decided June 13, 1994

From: During the penalty phase of Romano’s trial for the Sarfaty murder, Oklahoma introduced the judgment from his separate Thompson murder case, including the fact that another jury had already sentenced him to death. Romano offered to stipulate to the conviction. The Oklahoma Court of Criminal Appeals acknowledged that the sentence itself was irrelevant but affirmed the new death judgment. ([Library of Congress Tile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep512/usrep512001/usrep512001.pdf))

#### Decision of the Court

##### Prior death sentence in an independent capital sentencing

Holding: When a capital jury must independently choose between life and death, the State may not introduce the fact that another jury has already sentenced the defendant to death when that prior sentence is irrelevant to any valid aggravating circumstance. Admission of the official death judgment over objection undermines the reliability and independent responsibility of the sentencing decision and requires a new penalty hearing.

Operative rule: The State may prove a valid prior conviction and admissible facts concerning the underlying offense. It may not use another sentencer’s ultimate life-or-death judgment as aggravating evidence unless that sentence itself is made legally relevant by a valid issue not present here.

Reasoning: The prior conviction established historical facts potentially relevant to aggravation. The prior death sentence established only another jury’s normative conclusion that Romano should be executed. Its natural force was to suggest that execution had already been authoritatively approved and might occur regardless of the present jury’s independent choice. That message strikes at the responsibility protected by Caldwell and the heightened reliability required in capital sentencing.

Application: The State deliberately introduced the judgment despite Romano’s offer to stipulate to the conviction. General instructions describing the jury’s formal responsibility did not explain why an existing execution judgment had been placed before it or neutralize the institutional authority of that document.

Limits and questions left open: The Court does not create a general federal evidence code for capital proceedings, bar proof of prior convictions or underlying conduct, or decide a case in which the prior sentence itself bears on a valid statutory issue.

Legal basis: Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments.

Vote: Leave the conviction in place, reverse the death sentence, and remand for a new penalty proceeding.

Public role: Joins Justice Ginsburg’s opinion in full.

Announced ground: Stone agrees that an irrelevant official death judgment from another case creates an intolerable risk that the present jury will confirm an existing result rather than exercise independent sentencing responsibility.

##### Guaranteed-benefit exclusion and fiduciary control over free funds

### Fogerty v. Fantasy, Inc., 510 U.S. 517 (1994)

Argued December 8, 1993 | Decided March 1, 1994

From: After a jury rejected Fantasy’s copyright-infringement claim, the Ninth Circuit affirmed denial of attorney’s fees to prevailing defendant John Fogerty. Its dual standard generally favored prevailing copyright plaintiffs but required prevailing defendants to establish frivolousness or bad faith. The Court reviews the legal standard governing § 505 discretion, not Fogerty’s ultimate entitlement or the amount of any award. ([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/510bv.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Section 505 authorizes a court, in its discretion, to award reasonable attorney’s fees to “the prevailing party.” Alyeska supplies the American Rule background, while Christiansburg and Hensley concern different statutory settings or the exercise of fee discretion. No earlier simulated copyright decision establishes an asymmetric fee threshold.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg. No opposing Justice.

The district court shall reconsider Fogerty’s application without a plaintiff-favoring presumption and without requiring frivolousness or bad faith as a categorical condition for a prevailing defendant.

#### Opinions

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone | Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Supports reversal and remand | Complete opinion |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Thomas | No other Justice | Supports reversal and remand | Agrees with the evenhanded reading of § 505 but objects to its inconsistency with Christiansburg’s treatment of nearly identical language |

#### Decision of the Court

##### Evenhanded discretionary awards under § 505

Holding: Prevailing copyright plaintiffs and defendants must be evaluated under the same discretionary legal standard. Neither side is presumptively entitled to fees, and a prevailing defendant need not prove frivolousness or bad faith as a categorical prerequisite.

Operative rule: A court may consider objective unreasonableness, frivolousness, motivation, compensation, deterrence, and other circumstances faithful to the Copyright Act’s purposes. These considerations are nonexclusive, no one is mandatory, and they must be applied evenhandedly. Section 505 does not establish automatic prevailing-party fee shifting.

Reasoning: The statute refers symmetrically to “the prevailing party” and supplies no textual basis for a defendant-specific threshold. Copyright serves the public both by protecting valid rights and by preserving lawful access to expression, so a successful defense may clarify the boundaries of protection as materially as a successful infringement action. At the same time, Congress’s use of “may,” against the American Rule background, preserves case-specific discretion and forecloses an automatic British Rule. ### Livadas v. Bradshaw, 512 U.S. 107 (1994)

Argued April 26, 1994 | Decided June 13, 1994

From: California law required immediate payment of wages upon discharge and imposed a waiting-time penalty for willful delay. The Labor Commissioner refused to process Livadas’s claim solely because she was covered by a collective-bargaining agreement containing an arbitration clause. Her statutory entitlement depended on California law and calendar dates; the agreement supplied only the undisputed wage rate used to calculate the penalty. The Ninth Circuit reversed summary judgment for Livadas after characterizing the dispute as an issue of state-law interpretation rather than interference with a federal labor right. ([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/512bv.pdf))

Controlling simulated law before decision: Nash and Golden State prohibit States from imposing penalties on conduct protected by federal labor law. Lingle and related § 301 decisions distinguish claims requiring interpretation of a collective-bargaining agreement from claims that merely consult an agreement for an undisputed fact. Golden State II permits § 1983 enforcement when federal labor law protects a concrete individual interest against state interference and Congress has not displaced the remedy.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9-0. Supporting: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg. No opposing Justice.

The lower courts shall determine the appropriate declaratory, prospective, and other lawful relief. Immunity, official-capacity monetary relief, and the permissible remedial scope remain separate questions.

Justice Souter delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Ginsburg.

#### Decision of the Court

##### Federal preemption of the nonenforcement policy

Holding: The NLRA preempts a state policy that categorically withholds enforcement of otherwise available, nonwaivable wage protections solely because an employee is covered by a collective-bargaining agreement containing an arbitration clause.

Operative rule: A State may administer neutral enforcement priorities and may decline to adjudicate a claim genuinely displaced by federal law. It may not make completion of federally protected collective bargaining, or agreement to arbitration, the condition for losing an enforcement service available to otherwise similarly situated employees.

Reasoning: The policy forced employees to choose between the benefits of collective bargaining and the State’s ordinary enforcement of minimum wage protections. Its validity turns on actual operation, not on whether California Labor Code § 229 could be administered constitutionally in another manner. The categorical withdrawal directly burdened federal labor rights and was not justified by any actual contractual dispute or federal requirement to arbitrate Livadas’s independent statutory claim. ([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/512bv.pdf))

Limits and questions left open: The decision does not invalidate § 229 on its face, preempt every state distinction involving union representation, or prevent neutral enforcement priorities.

Legal basis: Supremacy Clause and the National Labor Relations Act.

##### Section 301 and the independent wage-penalty claim

Holding: LMRA § 301 does not preempt an independent state wage-payment claim when liability requires no interpretation of a collective-bargaining agreement and the agreement is consulted only for an undisputed wage rate used to calculate relief.

Reasoning: California law, not the collective-bargaining agreement, supplied Livadas’s right, the employer’s duty, and the elements of liability. The calendar established the delay, and the undisputed wage rate supplied arithmetic. Federal uniformity in contract interpretation is not implicated merely because a court looks to an agreement for a number whose meaning no party contests. ([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/512bv.pdf))

Limits and questions left open: Section 301 may govern when liability arises from the agreement, a contractual term is genuinely disputed, or resolution requires interpretation rather than reference.

Legal basis: 29 U.S.C. § 185 and California Labor Code §§ 201 and 203.

##### Section 1983 remedy for state interference with the federal labor right

Holding: Livadas may invoke § 1983 against an appropriate state official to redress the State’s interference with her concrete federal right to complete collective bargaining and agree to arbitration without governmental penalty. Congress has not displaced that cause of action.

Reasoning: Golden State II establishes that not every preemption claim supports § 1983, but it permits relief when federal labor law protects an individual interest against state abridgment, the governmental duty is judicially administrable, and Congress has not supplied an exclusive alternative remedy for state interference. Livadas’s asserted right and the Commissioner’s corresponding obligation satisfy those conditions. ([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/512bv.pdf))

Limits and questions left open: The holding does not make every federal preemption objection actionable under § 1983, waive state sovereign immunity, authorize damages against the State, or predetermine the proper defendant and form of relief.

Legal basis: 42 U.S.C. § 1983 and the federal labor rights protected by the NLRA.

Announced ground: Stone announces no ground beyond the Court opinion.
