## Article III and Federal Courts

### Specialized statutory review-channel preclusion

**Governing question:** When a comprehensive federal administrative-review scheme displaces initial district-court jurisdiction over a claim connected to that scheme.

**Current rule:** A specialized statutory scheme precludes initial district-court review when congressional intent to channel the dispute is fairly discernible from the enacted structure and the designated route preserves meaningful judicial review. A closely connected claim ordinarily must use that route.

**Sequence or decisionmaker:** Examine the enacted review structure first, then determine whether the asserted claim can receive meaningful consideration through the designated administrative and appellate sequence.

**Exceptions and limits:** Initial district-court review remains available when the statutory route cannot provide meaningful judicial review, the claim is genuinely collateral to the scheme, or the process imposes an irreparable deprivation the designated review cannot address.

**First controlling authority:** Thunder Basin Coal Co. v. Reich, 510 U.S. 200 (1994), decided January 19, 1994, Statutory review-channel preclusion; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Thunder Basin Coal Co. v. Reich, 510 U.S. 200 (1994), decided January 19, 1994, Statutory review-channel preclusion; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Thunder Basin Coal Co. v. Reich, 510 U.S. 200 (1994), January 19, 1994.

### Federal constitutional claims under the FTCA and agency waivers

**Governing question:** Whether a federal constitutional claim falls within the Federal Tort Claims Act and whether a federal agency’s sue-and-be-sued clause removes immunity.

**Current rule:** A federal constitutional claim is not cognizable under 28 U.S.C. § 1346(b) because it arises under federal rather than state law and lacks the required private-person state-law analogue. The FTCA therefore does not make the United States the exclusive defendant on that claim. A broad agency sue-and-be-sued clause may waive the agency’s sovereign immunity, but the waiver does not itself create a substantive cause of action.

**Controlling construction:** Sections 1346(b) and 2679(a) do not convert a federal constitutional claim into an FTCA claim or make the FTCA exclusive when § 1346(b) is not satisfied.

**Exceptions and limits:** A separate cause of action and authorized remedy remain necessary even when immunity is waived.

**First controlling authority:** FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471 (1994), decided February 23, 1994, FTCA exclusivity and the sue-and-be-sued waiver; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471 (1994), decided February 23, 1994, FTCA exclusivity and the sue-and-be-sued waiver; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471 (1994), February 23, 1994.

### Constitutional damages actions against federal agencies

**Governing question:** Whether the officer damages remedy recognized in Bivens extends directly to a federal agency.

**Current rule:** Bivens does not imply a damages cause of action directly against a federal agency, even when the agency’s sovereign immunity has been waived. The judicially implied remedy remains directed to responsible federal officers rather than the employing agency.

**Consequence or recurring remedy:** A constitutional damages claim brought directly against the federal agency must be dismissed absent an independent statutory cause of action.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule does not narrow existing Bivens actions against individual officers or foreclose an otherwise authorized statutory or prospective remedy.

**First controlling authority:** FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471 (1994), decided February 23, 1994, Bivens liability of a federal agency; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471 (1994), decided February 23, 1994, Bivens liability of a federal agency; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471 (1994), February 23, 1994.

### Qualified-immunity review uses the complete body of law

**Governing question:** What legal authorities an appellate court must consider when deciding whether a right was clearly established.

**Current rule:** Because clearly established law is an objective legal question reviewed de novo, a court must consider all materially relevant precedent available at the time of the challenged conduct, including authority not cited to the district court. A party’s incomplete legal research does not bind the court to an incomplete statement of governing law.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule does not excuse failure to preserve the claim itself or the factual premises on which it depends.

**First controlling authority:** Elder v. Holloway, 510 U.S. 510 (1994), decided February 23, 1994, Legal authorities considered on qualified-immunity review; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Elder v. Holloway, 510 U.S. 510 (1994), decided February 23, 1994, Legal authorities considered on qualified-immunity review; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Elder v. Holloway, 510 U.S. 510 (1994), February 23, 1994.

### Ancillary jurisdiction to enforce settlements after dismissal

**Governing question:** When a federal court may enforce a settlement after a Rule 41(a)(1)(ii) dismissal.

**Current rule:** A federal district court lacks ancillary jurisdiction to enforce a settlement after a Rule 41(a)(1)(ii) dismissal when the dismissal neither incorporates the settlement terms nor expressly retains enforcement jurisdiction. Enforcement remains ancillary when the federal dismissal makes compliance part of the decree or expressly retains jurisdiction; otherwise an independent jurisdictional basis is required.

**Consequence or recurring remedy:** Without incorporation, retained jurisdiction, or an independent federal jurisdictional ground, the enforcement proceeding must be dismissed.

**Exceptions and limits:** The parties may pursue an independent state contract action or a separate federal action supported by diversity or another jurisdictional grant.

**First controlling authority:** Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Insurance Co. of America, 511 U.S. 375 (1994), decided May 16, 1994, Ancillary jurisdiction to enforce a settlement after dismissal; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Insurance Co. of America, 511 U.S. 375 (1994), decided May 16, 1994, Ancillary jurisdiction to enforce a settlement after dismissal; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Insurance Co. of America, 511 U.S. 375 (1994), May 16, 1994.

### Collateral-order review of settlement-enforcement rulings

**Governing question:** Whether an order vacating a settlement-based dismissal or refusing to enforce a settlement is immediately appealable.

**Current rule:** An order vacating a settlement-based dismissal or refusing to enforce a settlement is not immediately appealable under the collateral-order doctrine. The private contractual interest in avoiding continued litigation lacks the exceptional public and legal importance required to overcome the final-judgment rule, and the ruling can be reviewed after final judgment.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule does not govern constitutional or statutory immunities whose value would be destroyed by trial. Section 1292(b), final review, and extraordinary mandamus remain available when their independent requirements are met.

**First controlling authority:** Digital Equipment Corp. v. Desktop Direct, Inc., 511 U.S. 863 (1994), decided June 6, 1994, Collateral-order appeal from refusal to enforce a settlement; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Digital Equipment Corp. v. Desktop Direct, Inc., 511 U.S. 863 (1994), decided June 6, 1994, Collateral-order appeal from refusal to enforce a settlement; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Digital Equipment Corp. v. Desktop Direct, Inc., 511 U.S. 863 (1994), June 6, 1994.

### Habeas channeling of conviction-invalidating § 1983 damages claims

**Governing question:** When a claimant in custody may pursue § 1983 damages on a theory that would necessarily invalidate an outstanding conviction or sentence.

**Current rule:** While a claimant remains in custody and a habeas remedy is legally available, a § 1983 damages claim whose success would necessarily establish the invalidity of an outstanding conviction or sentence may not proceed while that judgment remains outstanding. The restriction is procedural and temporary, not jurisdictional, and a genuinely separable claim that would not imply invalidity remains outside it.

**Consequence or recurring remedy:** The conviction-invalidating damages claim is dismissed without prejudice while the outstanding judgment and legally available habeas channel remain.

**Open question:** The Court left open the treatment of a claimant for whom habeas is no longer legally available through no fault of the claimant.

**First controlling authority:** Heck v. Humphrey, 512 U.S. 477 (1994), decided June 24, 1994, Coordination of § 1983 damages with an outstanding conviction; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Heck v. Humphrey, 512 U.S. 477 (1994), decided June 24, 1994, Coordination of § 1983 damages with an outstanding conviction; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Heck v. Humphrey, 512 U.S. 477 (1994), June 24, 1994.

### Capital habeas counsel and prepetition stay authority

**Governing question:** When a capital federal postconviction proceeding begins for appointment of counsel and related stay authority.

**Current rule:** A capital prisoner’s motion requesting appointment of federal habeas counsel commences the represented postconviction proceeding for purposes of 21 U.S.C. § 848(q)(4)(B). Once that right is invoked, the same proceeding is pending for purposes of 28 U.S.C. § 2251, and the district court may stay execution in its sound discretion when reasonably necessary to make the representation effective.

**Sequence or decisionmaker:** The court appoints qualified counsel, then determines whether and for how long a stay is reasonably necessary to permit investigation, preparation, and filing of a meaningful first federal petition.

**Exceptions and limits:** A stay is not automatic or indefinite. Inexcusable delay or disregard of available procedures may justify denial, and the decision excuses no later procedural default.

**First controlling authority:** McFarland v. Scott, 512 U.S. 849 (1994), decided June 30, 1994, Commencement of the represented capital proceeding; established the commencement rule; the companion stay component is supplied by the same decision’s stay-authority holding.

**Controlling authorities:** McFarland v. Scott, 512 U.S. 849 (1994), decided June 30, 1994, Commencement of the represented capital proceeding; supplies the commencement rule; the companion stay component is supplied by the same decision’s stay-authority holding.

**Present operation effective:** McFarland v. Scott, 512 U.S. 849 (1994), June 30, 1994.

## Due Process

### Pre-seizure process for civil forfeiture of real property

**Governing question:** What process is required before the Government seizes real property for civil forfeiture.

**Current rule:** Except upon particularized proof of exigent circumstances, the Fifth Amendment requires notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard before the Government seizes real property for civil forfeiture.

**Burdens and proof:** The Government bears the obligation to establish an actual exigency justifying immediate seizure.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule concerns real property and permits immediate action on proof of a genuine exigency. It does not decide the treatment of readily movable personal property or contraband.

**First controlling authority:** United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property, 510 U.S. 43 (1993), decided December 13, 1993, Pre-seizure process for real property; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property, 510 U.S. 43 (1993), decided December 13, 1993, Pre-seizure process for real property; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property, 510 U.S. 43 (1993), December 13, 1993.

### Specific constitutional source and substantive-due-process nonduplication

**Governing question:** Whether substantive due process supplies a parallel claim when a specific constitutional provision directly governs the alleged arrest or personal restraint.

**Current rule:** A claimant may not use substantive due process to duplicate Fourth Amendment protection for injuries alleged to arise from arrest and prosecution-related restraints on the person. When settled Fourth Amendment law supplies the directly relevant source, substantive due process does not create a parallel federal malicious-prosecution tort.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule leaves open a materially distinct nonseizure injury and does not decide a properly pleaded Fourth Amendment, fabricated-evidence, procedural-due-process, or state-law claim.

**First controlling authority:** Albright v. Oliver, 510 U.S. 266 (1994), decided January 24, 1994, Substantive due process as the asserted source; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Albright v. Oliver, 510 U.S. 266 (1994), decided January 24, 1994, Substantive due process as the asserted source; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Albright v. Oliver, 510 U.S. 266 (1994), January 24, 1994.

### Whole-charge review of reasonable-doubt instructions

**Governing question:** How a reviewing court determines whether a reasonable-doubt instruction lowered the prosecution’s burden.

**Current rule:** The Constitution prescribes no fixed wording for a reasonable-doubt instruction. A reviewing court asks whether there is a reasonable likelihood that the jury understood the complete charge to permit conviction on proof below reasonable doubt, evaluating disputed phrases in the context of the directions concerning evidence, the prosecution’s burden, and the required abiding conviction of guilt.

**Exceptions and limits:** The decision does not endorse archaic moral-certainty language; similar wording may be unconstitutional when context fails to anchor the required certainty in the evidence or combines phrases that materially lower the burden.

**First controlling authority:** Victor v. Nebraska / Sandoval v. California, 511 U.S. 1 (1994), decided March 22, 1994, Constitutional standard for reasonable-doubt instructions; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Victor v. Nebraska / Sandoval v. California, 511 U.S. 1 (1994), decided March 22, 1994, Constitutional standard for reasonable-doubt instructions; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Victor v. Nebraska / Sandoval v. California, 511 U.S. 1 (1994), March 22, 1994.

### Meaningful judicial review of punitive-damages amounts

**Governing question:** What postverdict procedure a State must provide when it authorizes punitive damages.

**Current rule:** A State that authorizes punitive damages must provide meaningful judicial review of the amount, including review of whether the award rests on legally permissible punitive considerations and remains within the range a rational jury could derive from the evidence. Review need not be de novo or use a fixed verbal formula, but it must be capable of correcting an amount outside the rational range supported by the record.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule creates no numerical ratio, proof burden, or mandatory remittitur formula and leaves the State discretion to select a constitutionally adequate corrective procedure.

**First controlling authority:** Honda Motor Co. v. Oberg, 512 U.S. 415 (1994), decided June 24, 1994, Procedural safeguard against arbitrary punitive amounts; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Honda Motor Co. v. Oberg, 512 U.S. 415 (1994), decided June 24, 1994, Procedural safeguard against arbitrary punitive amounts; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Honda Motor Co. v. Oberg, 512 U.S. 415 (1994), June 24, 1994.

## Equal Protection

### Sex discrimination in peremptory challenges

**Governing question:** Whether a party may use peremptory challenges to exclude prospective jurors because of sex.

**Current rule:** The Equal Protection Clause prohibits purposeful use of peremptory challenges to exclude prospective jurors because of sex, including in civil litigation conducted through state judicial process.

**Burdens and proof:** The ultimate burden of persuasion remains on the challenger.

**Sequence or decisionmaker:** The challenger must establish circumstances supporting an inference of purposeful sex discrimination. The striking party must then articulate a genuine sex-neutral explanation, and the trial court determines whether purposeful discrimination occurred.

**Exceptions and limits:** Disproportionate impact alone does not prove purpose, and the rule does not bar strikes based on individualized answers, relationships, experiences, or demeanor that are not proxies for sex.

**First controlling authority:** J.E.B. v. Alabama ex rel. T.B., 511 U.S. 127 (1994), decided April 19, 1994, Sex discrimination in peremptory challenges; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** J.E.B. v. Alabama ex rel. T.B., 511 U.S. 127 (1994), decided April 19, 1994, Sex discrimination in peremptory challenges; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** J.E.B. v. Alabama ex rel. T.B., 511 U.S. 127 (1994), April 19, 1994.

## Elections and Voting

### Objective comparator for governing-body-size claims under Voting Rights Act § 2

**Governing question:** When a challenge to the size of a governing body is cognizable under Voting Rights Act § 2.

**Current rule:** A governing-body-size claim is cognizable under § 2 when the plaintiff identifies a concrete and workable alternative structure grounded in objective law, governmental practice, history, or a closely comparable institution. Comparator sufficiency permits the statutory comparison but does not itself prove vote dilution.

**Burdens and proof:** The plaintiff retains the existing burden to establish the Gingles predicates and liability under the statutory totality.

**Exceptions and limits:** The comparator requirement is a comparative merits prerequisite, not an Article III bar, and it creates no right to proportional representation.

**Unresolved interaction or authority boundary:** The judgment vacating the particular liability finding had no controlling rationale under Marks; the majority-supported comparator rule remains controlling.

**First controlling authority:** Holder v. Hall, 512 U.S. 874 (1994), decided June 30, 1994, Cognizability and the five-member comparator; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Holder v. Hall, 512 U.S. 874 (1994), decided June 30, 1994, Cognizability and the five-member comparator; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Holder v. Hall, 512 U.S. 874 (1994), June 30, 1994.

### Voting Rights Act § 2 totality, proportionality, and nonmaximization

**Governing question:** How Gingles preconditions, proportionality, and the ability to draw additional majority-minority districts bear on § 2 vote-dilution liability.

**Current rule:** The Gingles preconditions are necessary but not invariably sufficient to establish § 2 vote dilution. The court must determine under the full statutory totality whether protected voters have less opportunity than other members of the electorate to participate effectively and elect representatives of choice. Rough proportionality is relevant evidence but neither dispositive nor a safe harbor, and the ability to draw additional majority-minority districts does not by itself establish dilution because § 2 does not require maximization.

**Exceptions and limits:** Districts counted in a proportionality comparison must operate as genuine electoral opportunities. The rule creates no proportional-representation entitlement and leaves influence-district claims unresolved.

**First controlling authority:** Johnson v. De Grandy, 512 U.S. 997 (1994), decided June 30, 1994, Gingles, proportionality, and nonmaximization; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Johnson v. De Grandy, 512 U.S. 997 (1994), decided June 30, 1994, Gingles, proportionality, and nonmaximization; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Johnson v. De Grandy, 512 U.S. 997 (1994), June 30, 1994.

## First Amendment

### Public-employee discipline based on a reasonable factual understanding

**Governing question:** Which factual version of disputed employee speech a public employer may use when applying the public-employee speech doctrine.

**Current rule:** A public employer may act on a version of disputed speech it genuinely believed when that factual understanding was objectively reasonable in light of information reasonably available to the decisionmaker. The inquiry into how the employer investigated is evidence of actual belief, reasonableness, and pretext, not an independent First Amendment hearing code. Pickering and Connick then apply to the speech as permissibly understood.

**Burdens and proof:** The employer must establish actual reliance on a genuinely held and objectively reasonable factual understanding.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule does not protect a fabricated reason, an asserted belief the employer did not hold, an objectively unsupported factual premise, or pretext.

**First controlling authority:** Waters v. Churchill, 511 U.S. 661 (1994), decided May 31, 1994, Permissible factual basis for public-employee discipline; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Waters v. Churchill, 511 U.S. 661 (1994), decided May 31, 1994, Permissible factual basis for public-employee discipline; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Waters v. Churchill, 511 U.S. 661 (1994), May 31, 1994.

### Near-total elimination of the residential-sign medium

**Governing question:** When a municipality may broadly prohibit signs displayed at a resident’s own home.

**Current rule:** A municipality violates the First Amendment when it nearly eliminates residential signs and leaves no realistic equivalent for the inexpensive, location-linked expression conveyed by a sign at one’s own home. Other media are not adequate substitutes when they cannot communicate the same personal message from the residence itself.

**Exceptions and limits:** A municipality may impose reasonable content-neutral rules governing size, number, placement, duration, illumination, and safety, and no one has a right to place a sign on another person’s property.

**First controlling authority:** City of Ladue v. Gilleo, 512 U.S. 43 (1994), decided June 13, 1994, Near-total elimination of residential signs; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** City of Ladue v. Gilleo, 512 U.S. 43 (1994), decided June 13, 1994, Near-total elimination of residential signs; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** City of Ladue v. Gilleo, 512 U.S. 43 (1994), June 13, 1994.

### Cable must-carry classification and intermediate scrutiny

**Governing question:** What First Amendment scrutiny governs content-neutral cable must-carry obligations and how legislative predictions and editorial burdens are reviewed.

**Current rule:** Cable must-carry obligations keyed to broadcast status and transmission method rather than viewpoint or program subject are content neutral and receive intermediate scrutiny. The government must establish an important or substantial interest unrelated to suppressing expression and a reasonable fit that does not burden substantially more protected editorial discretion than necessary; least-restrictive-means analysis is not required. Courts may respect adequately supported legislative predictions in complex markets while independently reviewing the actual magnitude and distribution of compelled editorial displacement and the fit between that burden and the established interests.

**Burdens and proof:** The government bears the burden of establishing the substantial interest and reasonable fit.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule does not apply the broadcast-scarcity standard to cable and permits properly supported as-applied challenges.

**First controlling authority:** Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC, 512 U.S. 622 (1994), decided June 27, 1994, Classification and scrutiny of must-carry; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC, 512 U.S. 622 (1994), decided June 27, 1994, Classification and scrutiny of must-carry; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC, 512 U.S. 622 (1994), June 27, 1994.

### Provision-specific review of content-neutral speech injunctions

**Governing question:** What fit is required for a content-neutral injunction directed at identified speakers and enforced by contempt.

**Current rule:** A content-neutral injunction directed at identified speakers must be reviewed provision by provision. Each restraint must be connected to demonstrated unlawful conduct and burden no more speech than necessary to remedy that conduct and protect the significant interests affected by it. The injunction need not use the least restrictive conceivable wording, but contempt-backed relief may not exceed what is necessary to provide complete lawful protection.

**Exceptions and limits:** Misconduct supporting one provision does not automatically justify another, and a fixed geographic or personal-distance rule requires findings showing that the restriction is necessary.

**First controlling authority:** Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc., 512 U.S. 753 (1994), decided June 30, 1994, Standard for a content-neutral speech injunction; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc., 512 U.S. 753 (1994), decided June 30, 1994, Standard for a content-neutral speech injunction; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc., 512 U.S. 753 (1994), June 30, 1994.

### Binding nonparties through active concert in speech injunctions

**Governing question:** When a speech injunction may bind a person who was not named as a party.

**Current rule:** A speech injunction may bind a nonparty with actual notice who actively coordinates with, aids, or participates with a named enjoined person in conduct the injunction lawfully prohibits. It may not reach a person merely because of shared views, organizational affiliation, presence at the same event, or independently chosen parallel advocacy.

**Burdens and proof:** Enforcement against a nonparty requires proof of actual notice and coordinated participation in prohibited conduct.

**First controlling authority:** Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc., 512 U.S. 753 (1994), decided June 30, 1994, Persons acting in active concert or participation; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc., 512 U.S. 753 (1994), decided June 30, 1994, Persons acting in active concert or participation; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc., 512 U.S. 753 (1994), June 30, 1994.

**Related current rules:** Provision-specific review of content-neutral speech injunctions, under First Amendment, supplies the validity and fit requirements for the underlying injunction.

### Truthful professional credentials and potentially misleading advertising

**Governing question:** When a regulator may prohibit or qualify truthful use of a professional license or bona fide private credential.

**Current rule:** A regulator may not punish truthful use of an active professional license or bona fide private credential without proof that the representation is false, inherently misleading, actually deceptive, or presents a real risk of deception materially alleviated by the restriction. Unsupported speculation that a private certification implies state approval is insufficient. When a real potential for confusion is shown, a proportionate disclosure ordinarily must be considered before prohibition.

**Burdens and proof:** The regulator bears the burden of establishing the asserted deception risk and the material effectiveness and proportionality of the response.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule does not protect a revoked, inactive, falsely described, or fabricated credential, and it permits supported disclosure requirements identifying the certifying body or other material qualification.

**First controlling authority:** Ibanez v. Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation, Board of Accountancy, 512 U.S. 136 (1994), decided June 13, 1994, Truthful use of the CFP designation; established the private-credential rule, together with the same decision’s unanimous active-license holding.

**Controlling authorities:** Ibanez v. Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation, Board of Accountancy, 512 U.S. 136 (1994), decided June 13, 1994, Truthful use of the CFP designation; supplies the private-credential rule, together with the same decision’s unanimous active-license holding.

**Present operation effective:** Ibanez v. Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation, Board of Accountancy, 512 U.S. 136 (1994), June 13, 1994.

### Religion-neutral allocation of civil governmental authority

**Governing question:** When government may allocate ordinary civil authority to a religiously homogeneous community.

**Current rule:** Government may accommodate religious needs and organize municipalities or school districts under neutral laws, but it may not specially allocate civil authority to a religiously identified community without neutral, generally applicable criteria available on equal terms to comparable religious and secular communities.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule does not invalidate a religiously homogeneous municipality formed under neutral incorporation law, bar religious citizens from ordinary political authority, deny equal public services, or forbid accommodations administered through neutral eligibility and secular public control.

**First controlling authority:** Board of Education of Kiryas Joel Village School District v. Grumet, 512 U.S. 687 (1994), decided June 27, 1994, Special allocation of public-school authority to a religious enclave; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Board of Education of Kiryas Joel Village School District v. Grumet, 512 U.S. 687 (1994), decided June 27, 1994, Special allocation of public-school authority to a religious enclave; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Board of Education of Kiryas Joel Village School District v. Grumet, 512 U.S. 687 (1994), June 27, 1994.

## Commerce Clause

### Origin-based charges and compensatory-burden defense

**Governing question:** When a state charge triggered by out-of-state origin survives dormant Commerce Clause review as compensatory.

**Current rule:** A charge triggered expressly by interstate origin is presumptively invalid. A compensatory defense requires identification of an actual intrastate tax or burden imposed on a substantially equivalent in-state event for the same governmental purpose; generalized estimates of additional public costs do not substitute for that corresponding burden.

**Burdens and proof:** The State bears the demanding burden of justifying the facial discrimination and establishing the substantially equivalent intrastate burden.

**Exceptions and limits:** The State may impose uniform charges, regulate genuine health or environmental risks evenhandedly, and recover demonstrable costs through nondiscriminatory measures.

**First controlling authority:** Oregon Waste Systems, Inc. v. Department of Environmental Quality, 511 U.S. 93 (1994), decided April 4, 1994, Origin-based waste surcharge; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Oregon Waste Systems, Inc. v. Department of Environmental Quality, 511 U.S. 93 (1994), decided April 4, 1994, Origin-based waste surcharge; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Oregon Waste Systems, Inc. v. Department of Environmental Quality, 511 U.S. 93 (1994), April 4, 1994.

### Operational discrimination favoring a local private enterprise

**Governing question:** When a formally local routing requirement discriminates against interstate commerce by reserving a market for a favored private enterprise.

**Current rule:** A law is discriminatory when its practical operation directs commerce to a favored local private enterprise, even if disfavored local firms are excluded alongside out-of-state firms. Such a law is invalid unless the government establishes a legitimate local objective that cannot adequately be served through reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives.

**Burdens and proof:** The government bears the burden of showing both the legitimate local objective and the inadequacy of reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule does not condemn evenhanded health and safety regulation or decide a materially different arrangement involving a genuinely public facility and distinct governmental-market circumstances.

**First controlling authority:** C & A Carbone, Inc. v. Town of Clarkstown, 511 U.S. 383 (1994), decided May 16, 1994, Exclusive local processing requirement; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** C & A Carbone, Inc. v. Town of Clarkstown, 511 U.S. 383 (1994), decided May 16, 1994, Exclusive local processing requirement; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** C & A Carbone, Inc. v. Town of Clarkstown, 511 U.S. 383 (1994), May 16, 1994.

### Linked market assessment and local-producer subsidy

**Governing question:** When a facially evenhanded market assessment becomes discriminatory because of a formally linked local-only distribution.

**Current rule:** A State discriminates against interstate commerce when it links an otherwise evenhanded market assessment to distribution of the entire resulting fund exclusively to in-state producers competing in the assessed market, so that the integrated program shifts the economic burden away from local producers and onto their interstate competitors. Formally linked components are evaluated as one economic arrangement.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule does not invalidate an evenhanded tax merely because revenue enters the general treasury, prohibit local subsidies financed from general revenues, or constitutionalize ordinary public-spending choices.

**First controlling authority:** West Lynn Creamery, Inc. v. Healy, 512 U.S. 186 (1994), decided June 17, 1994, Combined assessment and in-state producer subsidy; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** West Lynn Creamery, Inc. v. Healy, 512 U.S. 186 (1994), decided June 17, 1994, Combined assessment and in-state producer subsidy; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** West Lynn Creamery, Inc. v. Healy, 512 U.S. 186 (1994), June 17, 1994.

## Property and Takings

### Essential nexus and rough proportionality for adjudicative property exactions

**Governing question:** What relationship must exist between an adjudicative permit exaction of a property interest and a proposed development’s impacts.

**Current rule:** A permit condition compelling dedication of a property interest must bear an essential nexus and rough proportionality, in nature and extent, to impacts attributable to the proposed development. Government must make an individualized determination connecting the demanded property to the development’s attributable impact; mathematical precision and least-restrictive-means analysis are not required.

**Burdens and proof:** The government bears the obligation to justify the compelled dedication through the required individualized connection.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule governs adjudicative exactions of property interests, not ordinary zoning restrictions or generally applicable taxes and fees that do not function as compelled conveyances.

**First controlling authority:** Dolan v. City of Tigard, 512 U.S. 374 (1994), decided June 24, 1994, Nexus and proportionality of compelled land dedications; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Dolan v. City of Tigard, 512 U.S. 374 (1994), decided June 24, 1994, Nexus and proportionality of compelled land dedications; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Dolan v. City of Tigard, 512 U.S. 374 (1994), June 24, 1994.

## Bankruptcy

### Reasonably equivalent value after mortgage foreclosure

**Governing question:** How a bankruptcy court determines reasonably equivalent value after a regularly conducted mortgage foreclosure.

**Current rule:** A regularly conducted, noncollusive foreclosure sale is substantial evidence of value under forced-sale conditions, but the sale price and compliance with state foreclosure law do not conclusively establish reasonably equivalent value under 11 U.S.C. § 548(a)(2). The court must conduct a transaction-specific inquiry using relevant evidence such as the price, forced-sale conditions, exposure to purchasers, procedural regularity, and secured debt discharged, without double counting equivalent consideration.

**Burdens and proof:** The party seeking avoidance retains the ordinary burden of proof. No presumption or shifted burden of production arises from the foreclosure sale alone.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule prescribes no fixed percentage, automatic avoidance rule, mandatory valuation sequence, or alteration of state foreclosure procedure.

**First controlling authority:** BFP v. Resolution Trust Corp., 511 U.S. 531 (1994), decided May 23, 1994, Meaning of reasonably equivalent value after foreclosure; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** BFP v. Resolution Trust Corp., 511 U.S. 531 (1994), decided May 23, 1994, Meaning of reasonably equivalent value after foreclosure; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** BFP v. Resolution Trust Corp., 511 U.S. 531 (1994), May 23, 1994.

## Copyright

### Commercial parody under the fair-use factors

**Governing question:** How the four statutory fair-use factors apply to a commercial work that comments on the copyrighted original.

**Current rule:** A commercial parody may qualify as fair use. The four factors must be weighed together in light of copyright’s purposes. Transformative use reduces the force of commerciality; the amount copied is judged in relation to the need to make the target recognizable; and market injury concerns substitution in a market copyright law protects, not lost demand caused by criticism or ridicule. Commerciality and market harm are not presumptively dispositive.

**Exceptions and limits:** Humor alone does not establish parody. The new work must reasonably be perceived as commenting on the original and remains subject to the complete statutory inquiry.

**First controlling authority:** Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569 (1994), decided March 7, 1994, Commercial parody under the statutory fair-use inquiry; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569 (1994), decided March 7, 1994, Commercial parody under the statutory fair-use inquiry; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569 (1994), March 7, 1994.

### Evenhanded discretionary attorney-fee awards under § 505

**Governing question:** What legal standard governs a Copyright Act attorney-fee request by a prevailing plaintiff or defendant.

**Current rule:** Prevailing copyright plaintiffs and defendants are evaluated under the same discretionary standard. Neither side is presumptively entitled to fees, and a prevailing defendant need not prove frivolousness or bad faith as a categorical prerequisite. Courts may consider objective unreasonableness, frivolousness, motivation, compensation, deterrence, and other circumstances faithful to copyright’s purposes, applying all considerations evenhandedly.

**Exceptions and limits:** The considerations are nonexclusive, no single one is mandatory, and § 505 does not establish automatic prevailing-party fee shifting.

**First controlling authority:** Fogerty v. Fantasy, Inc., 510 U.S. 517 (1994), decided March 1, 1994, Evenhanded discretionary awards under § 505; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Fogerty v. Fantasy, Inc., 510 U.S. 517 (1994), decided March 1, 1994, Evenhanded discretionary awards under § 505; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Fogerty v. Fantasy, Inc., 510 U.S. 517 (1994), March 1, 1994.

## Statutory Interpretation

### No implied claim-ending sanction for missed statutory timing directives

**Governing question:** When a court may attach dismissal or loss of governmental authority to violation of a statutory timing command.

**Current rule:** Mandatory timing language imposes duties on responsible officials, but a court does not create a claim-ending dismissal sanction or loss of governmental authority that Congress omitted, particularly when Congress separately enacted an express limitations period. The absence of that sanction does not make the timing directives optional or foreclose independently preserved constitutional objections to delay.

**Exceptions and limits:** A different result follows when Congress expressly supplies dismissal, loss of authority, or another consequence.

**First controlling authority:** United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property, 510 U.S. 43 (1993), decided December 13, 1993, Effect of the statutory timing directives; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property, 510 U.S. 43 (1993), decided December 13, 1993, Effect of the statutory timing directives; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property, 510 U.S. 43 (1993), December 13, 1993.

### Presumption against retroactive legal consequences

**Governing question:** How a court determines the temporal reach of a statute enacted while a case is pending.

**Current rule:** A court first asks whether Congress clearly prescribed the statute’s temporal reach. If not, the court determines whether applying the enactment would attach new legal consequences to completed conduct, including increased liability, impairment of an existing right, or imposition of a new duty. A provision with such retroactive effect does not govern absent clear congressional intent. A genuinely procedural provision may apply to a pending matter when it does not alter substantive liability or completed legal consequences.

**Sequence or decisionmaker:** First identify a controlling temporal command; only if none exists evaluate retroactive effect.

**Exceptions and limits:** Congress may prescribe retroactive operation through clear language.

**First controlling authority:** Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244 (1994), decided April 26, 1994, Temporal reach of newly enacted remedies; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244 (1994), decided April 26, 1994, Temporal reach of newly enacted remedies; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244 (1994), April 26, 1994.

## Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act

### No economic-motive element in RICO

**Governing question:** Whether RICO requires the enterprise or alleged predicate acts to pursue profit or another economic objective.

**Current rule:** RICO contains no economic-motive element. Ideological purpose neither establishes nor defeats liability; a civil plaintiff must prove the enacted enterprise, pattern, qualifying predicates, injury to business or property, and required causal connection.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule does not decide whether particular conduct satisfies the predicates or pattern, and protected advocacy, association, picketing, and persuasion do not become racketeering merely because they accompany a political movement.

## Securities Regulation

### Private liability for culpable assistance to a § 10(b) violation

**Governing question:** When a private § 10(b) plaintiff may impose liability on a defendant who materially assists another actor’s completed securities fraud.

**Current rule:** A private § 10(b) plaintiff may pursue aiding-and-abetting liability by proving a completed primary violation, the secondary defendant’s knowledge of that violation or conscious recklessness approaching actual knowledge, and substantial assistance that materially facilitated it. An omission qualifies only when the defendant had a defined, transaction-specific responsibility to act.

**Burdens and proof:** The plaintiff retains the ordinary burdens of proving the primary violation, the secondary defendant’s state of mind and material assistance, transaction causation, loss causation, and damages.

**Exceptions and limits:** Professional status, negligence, proximity to the transaction, passive association, or silence unconnected to a defined responsibility is insufficient.

## Criminal Law and Sentencing

### Willfulness in the federal anti-structuring offense

**Governing question:** What mental state the willfulness requirement adds to the federal anti-structuring offense.

**Current rule:** Conviction under 31 U.S.C. §§ 5322 and 5324 requires proof that the defendant knew the structuring was unlawful, in addition to proof that the defendant intended to evade the financial institution’s reporting duty. The Government need not prove knowledge of the precise statutory citation or every detail of the reporting scheme.

**Burdens and proof:** The Government must prove the required knowledge of unlawfulness beyond a reasonable doubt.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule is tied to the text and context of the currency-reporting subchapter and creates no general ignorance-of-law defense.

**First controlling authority:** Ratzlaf v. United States, 510 U.S. 135 (1994), decided January 11, 1994, Willfulness in the anti-structuring offense; established the current rule.

### Mens rea for a regulated characteristic of an otherwise familiar object

**Governing question:** Whether statutory silence displaces mens rea for the physical characteristic that makes an otherwise familiar object seriously criminal.

**Current rule:** When an otherwise familiar and commonly lawful object becomes seriously criminal only because of a particular physical characteristic, statutory silence does not displace the traditional mens rea presumption. For 26 U.S.C. § 5861(d), the Government must prove that the defendant knew the weapon possessed the characteristic making it a machinegun, though not that the defendant knew the statutory classification or registration duty.

**Burdens and proof:** The Government must prove knowledge of the regulated physical characteristic beyond a reasonable doubt.

**Exceptions and limits:** The decision does not govern unmistakable contraband or objects whose dangerous character is evident from possession.

**First controlling authority:** Staples v. United States, 511 U.S. 600 (1994), decided May 23, 1994, Mens rea for the regulated characteristic; established the current rule.

### Collateral attack on Armed Career Criminal Act predicates

**Governing question:** Which constitutional defects in an outstanding state conviction may be litigated during federal ACCA sentencing.

**Current rule:** A defendant may challenge an ACCA predicate during federal sentencing when the conviction was obtained after a complete denial of the Gideon right to counsel. Other constitutional challenges to an outstanding state conviction, including ineffective assistance and involuntary-plea claims, must first succeed through an authorized direct or collateral proceeding.

**Sequence or decisionmaker:** The federal sentencing court determines whether an existing conviction qualifies; fact-intensive collateral challenges proceed in the convicting jurisdiction unless Congress supplies a broader sentencing-stage review mechanism.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule leaves open the procedure and disposition when a predicate is later vacated.

**First controlling authority:** Custis v. United States, 511 U.S. 485 (1994), decided May 23, 1994, Collateral attack on ACCA predicates; established the current rule.

### Use of valid uncounseled fine-only misdemeanors in later sentencing

**Governing question:** When a valid uncounseled misdemeanor that resulted in no imprisonment may affect a later custodial sentence.

**Current rule:** A sentencing scheme may consider a valid fine-only uncounseled misdemeanor when the defendant has a meaningful opportunity to challenge the information’s accuracy and sentencing significance and the court has authority to correct a criminal-history category that materially overstates the defendant’s conduct or risk. The decision does not validate automatic, irrebuttable, or conclusive custodial effect.

**Burdens and proof:** The defendant must invoke the available individualized correction mechanism to preserve a claim that the resulting category materially overstates the record.

**Unresolved interaction or authority boundary:** No majority categorically overruled Baldasar. The current rule is limited to a scheme with meaningful rebuttal and individualized correction.

## Criminal Procedure

### Objective custody under Miranda

**Governing question:** What role an officer’s undisclosed suspicions or communicated views play in determining Miranda custody.

**Current rule:** Miranda custody turns on the objective circumstances manifested to the person questioned and whether those circumstances impose restraint comparable to formal arrest. An officer’s undisclosed belief that the person is or is not a suspect is legally irrelevant. Communicated suspicion is relevant only insofar as it would affect a reasonable person’s understanding of the freedom to end the encounter and leave, and it is not automatically dispositive.

**Exceptions and limits:** An undisclosed view may be used to test credibility concerning what occurred, but it does not itself enter the custody standard.

**First controlling authority:** Stansbury v. California, 511 U.S. 318 (1994), decided April 26, 1994, Objective custody under Miranda; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Stansbury v. California, 511 U.S. 318 (1994), decided April 26, 1994, Objective custody under Miranda; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Stansbury v. California, 511 U.S. 318 (1994), April 26, 1994.

### Neutral clarification of an ambiguous request for counsel

**Governing question:** What officers must do when a reasonable officer would understand a suspect’s words as a genuine but ambiguous possible request for counsel after a Miranda waiver.

**Current rule:** Substantive interrogation must pause while officers ask neutral questions directed solely to whether counsel is being requested. A clear invocation ends interrogation under Edwards; a clear disclaimer permits substantive questioning to resume. Clarification may not become persuasion, argument, discouragement of counsel, or questioning about the offense.

**Exceptions and limits:** A passing reference that no reasonable officer would understand as a possible request does not activate the clarification requirement.

**First controlling authority:** Davis v. United States, 512 U.S. 452 (1994), decided June 24, 1994, Ambiguous possible request for counsel after waiver; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Davis v. United States, 512 U.S. 452 (1994), decided June 24, 1994, Ambiguous possible request for counsel after waiver; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Davis v. United States, 512 U.S. 452 (1994), June 24, 1994.

### Civil and criminal classification of contempt fines

**Governing question:** When a contempt fine is civil rather than criminal and what process a serious criminal fine requires.

**Current rule:** A contempt fine is civil when it compensates proved loss or coerces present compliance while leaving the contemnor a meaningful opportunity to avoid or purge the sanction. A fixed, determinate, noncompensatory, nonpurgeable fine for completed misconduct is punitive and therefore criminal. Serious criminal contempt fines may be imposed only through criminal proceedings that include the right to a jury trial.

**Exceptions and limits:** Courts retain summary authority over petty direct contempt necessary to maintain courtroom order and may use genuinely purgeable coercive sanctions or compensation tied to proved loss.

**First controlling authority:** International Union, United Mine Workers of America v. Bagwell, 512 U.S. 821 (1994), decided June 30, 1994, Classification and procedure for serious contempt fines; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** International Union, United Mine Workers of America v. Bagwell, 512 U.S. 821 (1994), decided June 30, 1994, Classification and procedure for serious contempt fines; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** International Union, United Mine Workers of America v. Bagwell, 512 U.S. 821 (1994), June 30, 1994.

## Eighth Amendment

### Deliberate indifference to a substantial custodial risk

**Governing question:** What knowledge and response establish an Eighth Amendment violation based on a serious risk of harm in custody.

**Current rule:** A prison official violates the Eighth Amendment when the official knows that an inmate faces an objectively substantial risk of serious harm and consciously disregards it by failing to take reasonable protective measures. Actual awareness may be proved directly or circumstantially, and a factfinder may infer knowledge from an obvious risk, but obviousness is evidence rather than an irrebuttable presumption.

**Burdens and proof:** The claimant must prove the objectively substantial risk, each defendant’s actual awareness, conscious disregard, causation, and individual role.

**Exceptions and limits:** An official who responds reasonably is not liable merely because the measures fail, and negligence or a classification mistake alone is insufficient.

**First controlling authority:** Farmer v. Brennan, 511 U.S. 825 (1994), decided June 6, 1994, Knowledge and disregard of a serious custodial risk; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Farmer v. Brennan, 511 U.S. 825 (1994), decided June 6, 1994, Knowledge and disregard of a serious custodial risk; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Farmer v. Brennan, 511 U.S. 825 (1994), June 6, 1994.

## Double Jeopardy

### Punitive taxes imposed after criminal punishment

**Governing question:** When an assessment denominated a tax constitutes a second punishment for Double Jeopardy purposes.

**Current rule:** A tax designation receives substantial but not conclusive weight. A levy constitutes punishment when its legal design and practical operation depart sharply from ordinary revenue collection and tie the assessment to a completed criminal offense and prosecution. A separate punitive assessment may not follow criminal punishment for the same conduct.

**Exceptions and limits:** No single unusual feature is dispositive. The rule does not regulate ordinary tax rates, bar taxation of unlawful businesses, or classify remedial charges, interest, investigation costs, or conventional civil assessments as punishment merely because they follow misconduct.

**First controlling authority:** Department of Revenue of Montana v. Kurth Ranch, 511 U.S. 767 (1994), decided June 6, 1994, Punitive post-conviction drug tax; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Department of Revenue of Montana v. Kurth Ranch, 511 U.S. 767 (1994), decided June 6, 1994, Punitive post-conviction drug tax; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Department of Revenue of Montana v. Kurth Ranch, 511 U.S. 767 (1994), June 6, 1994.

## Capital Sentencing

### Exclusion of another sentencer’s irrelevant death judgment

**Governing question:** Whether a capital jury independently choosing life or death may be told that another jury already sentenced the defendant to death.

**Current rule:** When a capital jury must independently choose between life and death, the State may not introduce the fact that another jury already imposed a death sentence when that prior sentence is irrelevant to a valid aggravating issue. The prior conviction and admissible underlying conduct may be proved, but another sentencer’s ultimate life-or-death judgment may not be used as aggravating evidence merely to suggest that execution has already been authoritatively approved.

**Consequence or recurring remedy:** Admission over objection requires a new penalty proceeding when it undermines the reliability and independent responsibility of the sentencing decision.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule leaves open a case in which the prior sentence itself bears on a valid statutory issue.

**First controlling authority:** Romano v. Oklahoma, 512 U.S. 1 (1994), decided June 13, 1994, Prior death sentence in an independent capital sentencing; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Romano v. Oklahoma, 512 U.S. 1 (1994), decided June 13, 1994, Prior death sentence in an independent capital sentencing; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Romano v. Oklahoma, 512 U.S. 1 (1994), June 13, 1994.

### Parole-ineligibility instruction when future dangerousness is argued

**Governing question:** When due process requires a capital jury to be instructed that a life sentence carries no possibility of parole.

**Current rule:** When the prosecution places future dangerousness at issue and the only noncapital sentence makes the defendant legally ineligible for parole, due process requires an accurate judicial instruction that a life sentence carries no possibility of parole.

**Consequence or recurring remedy:** Failure to give the instruction requires a new penalty proceeding when the jury was left with a materially false implication about eventual release.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule does not require predictions about discretionary parole when legal eligibility remains possible or contingent and does not dictate the weight the jury must assign to parole ineligibility.

**First controlling authority:** Simmons v. South Carolina, 512 U.S. 154 (1994), decided June 17, 1994, Parole-ineligibility instruction when future dangerousness is argued; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Simmons v. South Carolina, 512 U.S. 154 (1994), decided June 17, 1994, Parole-ineligibility instruction when future dangerousness is argued; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Simmons v. South Carolina, 512 U.S. 154 (1994), June 17, 1994.

### Eligibility narrowing and understandable selection factors

**Governing question:** What clarity and guidance the Eighth Amendment requires of factors used after a defendant has been made death eligible.

**Current rule:** Eligibility factors must genuinely narrow the class exposed to death and provide a principled boundary. Once eligibility has lawfully been established, selection factors may direct individualized consideration of relevant characteristics of the offense and offender. A selection factor must possess a common-sense core ordinary jurors can understand, but it need not yield a yes-or-no answer or carry predetermined aggravating or mitigating weight.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule does not approve a nonnarrowing eligibility factor, an instruction that forecloses relevant mitigation, or a label lacking an understandable core. Another sentencer’s irrelevant ultimate judgment remains governed by the separate rule excluding a prior death sentence.

**First controlling authority:** Tuilaepa v. California / Proctor v. California, 512 U.S. 967 (1994), decided June 30, 1994, Clarity and function of capital selection factors; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** Tuilaepa v. California / Proctor v. California, 512 U.S. 967 (1994), decided June 30, 1994, Clarity and function of capital selection factors; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** Tuilaepa v. California / Proctor v. California, 512 U.S. 967 (1994), June 30, 1994.

## Labor and Employment

### Title VII hostile work environment without serious psychological injury

**Governing question:** What level of discriminatory workplace conduct and injury is required for a Title VII hostile-work-environment claim.

**Current rule:** A plaintiff need not prove serious psychological injury. The discriminatory conduct must be sufficiently severe or pervasive to create an environment that is objectively hostile or abusive and that the plaintiff subjectively perceives as abusive. The inquiry considers the totality of the circumstances, including nonexclusive considerations such as frequency, severity, physical threat or humiliation, and interference with work.

**Exceptions and limits:** Psychological harm is relevant evidence but not an element. Ordinary incivility is not actionable merely because it is offensive, and the conduct must be connected to a protected characteristic and attributable to the employer under governing liability rules.

**First controlling authority:** Harris v. Forklift Systems, Inc., 510 U.S. 17 (1993), decided November 9, 1993, Threshold for an actionable hostile work environment; established the current rule.

### Professional direction versus statutory supervisory authority

**Governing question:** When skilled direction of other workers makes a professional employee a supervisor under NLRA § 2(11).

**Current rule:** Work does not become supervisory merely because it benefits the enterprise or includes skilled direction of less-experienced employees. The Board may distinguish professional or technical direction incidental to the employee’s own skilled service from genuine labor-management authority aligning the employee with management, including independent authority over hiring, discharge, discipline, promotion, compensation, grievances, or comparable matters.

**Exceptions and limits:** Professionals may qualify as supervisors when they independently exercise genuine management prerogatives; no profession is categorically exempt.

### NLRA preemption of state withdrawal of generally available protections

**Governing question:** Whether a State may withhold an otherwise available, nonwaivable labor protection solely because an employee is covered by a collective-bargaining agreement.

**Current rule:** The NLRA preempts a state policy that categorically withholds otherwise available, nonwaivable wage protections solely because an employee is covered by a collective-bargaining agreement containing an arbitration clause. A State may administer neutral enforcement priorities and decline a claim genuinely displaced by federal law, but it may not make exercise of federally protected collective bargaining the condition for losing a generally available enforcement service.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule does not preempt every state distinction involving union representation or invalidate a statute that can be administered neutrally.

### Section 301 interpretation-versus-consultation boundary

**Governing question:** When LMRA § 301 preempts an independent state-law claim that refers to a collective-bargaining agreement.

**Current rule:** Section 301 does not preempt an independent state-law claim when liability arises from state law, no contractual term is genuinely disputed, and the agreement is consulted only for an undisputed factual input such as a wage rate. **Exceptions and limits:** The existence of a collective-bargaining agreement alone does not trigger preemption.

### Medicare prohibition on redistributed educational costs

**Governing question:** Whether 42 C.F.R. § 413.85(c) permits a patient-care institution to obtain Medicare reimbursement for educational costs previously borne by an affiliated educational institution.

**Current rule:** Section 413.85(c), independently interpreted, bars Medicare reimbursement for educational costs previously borne by an affiliated educational institution and later shifted to the patient-care institution. ## Administrative Law

### Delegated power to modify does not authorize elimination

**Governing question:** How far an agency may alter a statutory requirement under a delegated power to modify it.

**Current rule:** A delegated power to modify permits bounded alteration, including adjustments to form, content, timing, and genuinely limited exceptions. It does not authorize an agency to eliminate a central statutory requirement for nearly an entire regulated sector or replace the enacted regime with a fundamentally different model. Deference does not extend authority beyond a meaning the statutory text can bear.

**Exceptions and limits:** The agency may make incremental adjustments and grant genuinely limited waivers consistent with the statutory delegation.

**First controlling authority:** MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co., 512 U.S. 218 (1994), decided June 17, 1994, Scope of the power to modify tariff-filing requirements; established the current rule.

**Controlling authorities:** MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co., 512 U.S. 218 (1994), decided June 17, 1994, Scope of the power to modify tariff-filing requirements; supplies the current rule.

**Present operation effective:** MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co., 512 U.S. 218 (1994), June 17, 1994.
