## 31. FDIC v. Meyer

### Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** FDIC v. Meyer
- **Citation or Docket:** 510 U.S. 471 (1994); Supreme Court No. 92-741; useful lower-court citation: 944 F.2d 562 (9th Cir. 1991).
- **October Term:** 1993.
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Merits case, argued or submitted, entering the simulated decision event.
- **Simulated Event Date:** February 23, 1994.
- **Question Presented:** Whether the Federal Tort Claims Act makes a constitutional damages claim against a sue-and-be-sued federal agency exclusive to the United States, and, if not, whether a Bivens damages action may be implied directly against the agency.
- **Question Granted:** Whether the Federal Tort Claims Act makes a constitutional damages claim against a sue-and-be-sued federal agency exclusive to the United States, and, if not, whether a Bivens damages action may be implied directly against the agency.
- **Legal Field:** Federal courts and remedies, sovereign immunity, the FTCA, and implied constitutional damages actions.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Ninth Circuit affirmed a $130,000 jury judgment against the Federal Savings and Loan Insurance Corporation, holding that the FTCA did not make the claim exclusive, the agency's sue-and-be-sued clause waived immunity, and a damages action could proceed directly against the agency.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** The FDIC, as FSLIC's statutory successor, asks the Court to reverse the damages judgment.
- **Material Facts and Record:** FSLIC became receiver for a failed thrift and summarily discharged Meyer, a senior officer. Meyer alleged that state law gave him a property interest in continued employment and that the discharge without notice or hearing violated the Fifth Amendment. The jury found the individual official immune but returned a damages verdict against FSLIC.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** Sovereign immunity and the existence of a cause of action precede any constitutional merits question. The Court did not grant review of Meyer's separate challenge to the individual official's qualified immunity.

- **Public Sources:** Official U.S. Reports, 510 U.S. 471 (1994), including syllabus and opinions; 944 F.2d 562 (9th Cir. 1991); Supreme Court docket No. 92-741; the official statutory or constitutional provisions identified below.

#### Historical Supreme Court comparator

- **Historical Outcome and Vote:** Reversed unanimously, 9-0.
- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Justice Thomas wrote for a unanimous Court.
- **Historical Holding:** The constitutional claim was not cognizable under 28 U.S.C. § 1346(b), so the FTCA did not displace FSLIC's broad sue-and-be-sued waiver; nevertheless, Bivens did not supply a damages cause of action directly against a federal agency.

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the cause-of-action question. Its negative answer disposes of the agency claim, so he does not adjudicate whether Meyer's discharge violated due process.

- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone declines to extend Bivens from responsible officers to a federal agency. - **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Bivens does not imply a damages cause of action directly against a federal agency, even when the agency's sovereign immunity has been waived.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Meyer has established a forum and an immunity waiver, but neither supplies the missing cause of action. Agency liability would bypass the officer-liability structure and expose the Treasury through a judicially created remedy of broad scope. That institutional choice belongs to Congress. Because the cause of action is absent, the damages judgment must be reversed without an advisory due-process ruling.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not narrow existing Bivens actions against individual federal officers, decide any statutory cause of action, or affect prospective relief available through an authorized channel.

#### Issue 1: Interpretation of Medicare's anti-redistribution regulation

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the legal interpretation. The regulation's text and structure resolve the reimbursement dispute without making automatic deference to the agency necessary to the judgment.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether § 413.85(c), independently interpreted, bars the claimed costs and what noncontrolling weight the Secretary's interpretation may receive.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone independently determines the regulation's meaning and affirms because the text excludes the disputed redistribution of costs. He rejects the proposition that ambiguity automatically transfers interpretive control to the agency and proposes instead that an agency's interpretation may receive persuasive weight according to nonexclusive considerations including the authority of the speaker, relevant technical expertise, consistency or an adequate explanation for change, and compatibility with fair notice. Those considerations are not formal elements, and weakness in one does not automatically invalidate an otherwise persuasive reading. Because the regulation independently resolves this case, that broader proposed rule does not alter the judgment.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Independently interpreted, § 413.85(c) bars Medicare reimbursement for educational costs previously borne by the affiliated medical school and later shifted to the patient-care institution; the Secretary's interpretation confirms but does not control that result.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The regulation states that Medicare will not participate in increased costs resulting from redistribution from educational institutions to patient-care institutions. The hospital seeks reimbursement for costs the affiliated medical college previously bore, placing the claim within the prohibition under the regulation's text, structure, and program function. The Secretary's authoritative and consistent understanding, informed by administration of the reimbursement system and compatible with fair notice, supports the same reading, but the court's independent interpretation supplies the judgment.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not announce a rigid four-element deference test or hold that every agency interpretation must satisfy every listed consideration. Agencies retain delegated policy discretion, and their technical judgments may earn substantial persuasive weight; they do not receive automatic control over the meaning of their own binding text.
