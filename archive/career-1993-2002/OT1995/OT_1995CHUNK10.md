# Montana v. Egelhoff

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** *Montana v. Egelhoff*
- **Citation or Docket:** 518 U.S. 37; No. 95-566.
- **October Term:** 1995.
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Plenary merits review of a state criminal judgment.
- **Simulated Event Date:** June 13, 1996.
- **Question Presented:** Whether due process permits Montana to retain purposeful or knowing homicide as an element while categorically forbidding the jury to consider voluntary-intoxication evidence offered to negate that element.
- **Question Granted:** Whether Montana Code § 45-2-203 is a lawful definition of criminal responsibility or an unconstitutional exclusion of evidence directly bearing on an element the State must prove.
- **Legal Field:** Criminal law, due process, mens rea, evidence, and legislative definition of offenses.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Montana Supreme Court reversed Egelhoff’s deliberate-homicide convictions because the statute prevented consideration of relevant intoxication evidence on purpose and knowledge.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** Montana seeks reversal and reinstatement; Egelhoff seeks affirmance.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Egelhoff was found in a vehicle with two shooting victims and a high blood-alcohol level. He was charged with deliberate homicide, which required purposeful or knowing conduct. The jury was instructed that voluntary intoxication could not be considered in deciding whether he possessed the required mental state. The evidence of intoxication was substantial and directly relevant to the contested element.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** Montana’s authority to define the offense is distinct from its authority to exclude evidence while retaining an element. The Court reviews the enacted text and actual instructions, not a hypothetical replacement offense.
- **Relevant Dates:** Argument, March 20, 1996; decision, June 13, 1996.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** Two homicide convictions arise from the same trial.
- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** Official syllabus and opinions, 518 U.S. 37; Montana Code §§ 45-2-203 and 45-5-102.

### Historical Supreme Court comparator

- **Historical Outcome and Vote:** Reversed, 5-4.
- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Justice Scalia announced the judgment for four Justices; Justice Ginsburg concurred in the judgment on an offense-definition rationale; four Justices dissented.
- **Historical Holding:** The historical judgment sustained the statute, with no single five-Justice rationale beyond the characterization that Montana could assign criminal responsibility without giving intoxication exculpatory force.
- **Historical Authority:** Fractured authority; Justice Ginsburg’s narrower offense-definition rationale supported the judgment.
- **Historical Sources:** 518 U.S. 37.

## Section II: Entering law and Stone's position

**Approval Status:** Draft for express approval; this Section II is not controlling or frozen until approved.

### A. Law entering the case

- **Current Through:** Law effective immediately before the June 13, 1996 simulated event, including all earlier effective simulated decisions.
- **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** No completed earlier OT1995 decision materially changes the offense-definition, retained-element, or evidentiary question presented here.
- **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** None identified.

#### Issue 1: Retained mental-state elements and exclusion of intoxication evidence

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** *Ratzlaf v. United States*, *Staples v. United States*, and *United States v. X-Citement Video* require proof of enacted mens rea and operative facts; *Victor v. Nebraska / Sandoval v. California* preserves proof beyond a reasonable doubt for every retained element.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** **Mens rea for operative facts distinguishing lawful conduct from serious crime** and **Whole-charge review of reasonable-doubt instructions**.
- **Other Governing Law:** The Due Process Clause; *In re Winship*; *Patterson v. New York*; *Chambers v. Mississippi*; *Crane v. Kentucky*; state authority to define offenses and defenses; ordinary evidentiary authority.
- **Other Authority Used:** The Montana Supreme Court's authoritative construction of §§ 45-2-203 and 45-5-102, its determination that deliberate homicide continued to require purposeful or knowing conduct, its holding that intoxication evidence was relevant to those retained elements, and the actual jury instruction forbidding consideration of that evidence.
- **Open Point:** None material to the state-law classification. The Montana Supreme Court authoritatively resolved that classification in the judgment under review.

### B. Stone's position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** **Affirm the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court.** Egelhoff's deliberate-homicide convictions remain reversed.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** The case shall return for a new trial if Montana elects to retry Egelhoff. At any retrial under the same retained purposeful-or-knowing elements, the jury may not be categorically forbidden from considering competent voluntary-intoxication evidence offered to negate those elements. Montana remains free prospectively to redefine culpability so voluntary intoxication has no exculpatory relevance, subject to ordinary constitutional limits.

#### Issue 1: Retained mental-state elements and exclusion of intoxication evidence

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the constitutional merits. The Montana Supreme Court, as the authoritative expositor of Montana law, already determined that deliberate homicide continued to require purposeful or knowing conduct and that voluntary-intoxication evidence was relevant to whether those elements were proved. Stone therefore does not remand for a state-law classification that has already occurred.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** A State may define an offense so voluntary intoxication does not reduce or negate culpability. But where the State's highest court authoritatively holds that purpose or knowledge remains an element and that intoxication evidence is relevant to that retained element, the State may not simultaneously require proof beyond a reasonable doubt and categorically prevent the jury from considering competent evidence offered to negate it in a manner that relieves the prosecution of the burden *Winship* assigns. Montana defended § 45-2-203 as a substantive allocation of culpability; that characterization cannot displace the Montana Supreme Court's binding construction of the offense actually tried.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** When state law retains purpose or knowledge as an element of deliberate homicide and treats voluntary intoxication as relevant to whether that element existed, due process does not permit a categorical instruction forbidding the jury to consider competent intoxication evidence offered to negate the retained element. On the authoritative Montana construction and instructions in this case, Egelhoff is entitled to a new trial.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The Constitution creates no general intoxication defense and leaves Montana substantial authority to define crimes and culpability. The decisive point is narrower. Montana required the jury to find purposeful or knowing homicide, while its highest court held that Egelhoff's intoxication evidence was relevant to that very mental-state inquiry. The trial instruction nevertheless directed the jury not to consider intoxication when deciding whether the State proved purpose or knowledge. That combination prevents meaningful adversarial testing of an element the State retained and permits conviction without allowing the jury to consider competent evidence directly bearing on reasonable doubt. Because the Montana Supreme Court already resolved the antecedent classification and reversed the convictions, the correct Supreme Court disposition for Stone is affirmance, not another remand for classification.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not establish a freestanding constitutional intoxication defense, require admission of cumulative, unreliable, or otherwise lawfully excludable evidence, prevent Montana from prospectively redefining culpability, or decide a materially different statute under which intoxication is genuinely irrelevant to the offense as authoritatively defined.

# Rise v. Oregon

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** *Rise v. Oregon*
- **Citation or Docket:** 59 F.3d 1556 (9th Cir. 1995), No. 93-35642.
- **October Term:** 1995.
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Granted merits review of a § 1983 challenge to a convicted-offender DNA databank.
- **Simulated Event Date:** June 14, 1996, simulation-assigned opinion-delivery date.
- **Question Presented:** Whether Oregon may require persons convicted of murder and specified sexual offenses to provide a blood sample for a restricted DNA identification databank, including persons convicted before enactment.
- **Question Granted:** Does a statute requiring convicted offenders to provide blood samples for permanent DNA identification and inclusion in a law-enforcement database constitute an unreasonable search under the Fourth Amendment, or otherwise violate Due Process or the Ex Post Facto Clause?
- **Legal Field:** Fourth Amendment special needs, bodily intrusion, convicted-person identification, informational safeguards, and ex post facto punishment.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Ninth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for Oregon.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** Rise and the other plaintiffs seek reversal and an injunction; Oregon seeks affirmance.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Oregon required one medically acceptable blood draw from persons convicted of murder or enumerated sexual offenses, subject to a health exception and exemption for an adequate existing sample. The resulting DNA profile was placed in an identification databank available to specified justice-system users. The law restricted analysis for genetic predispositions. Most plaintiffs were convicted before enactment; one plaintiff’s attempted-murder conviction was not a statutory predicate and raised an individual implementation issue.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** The facial and as-applied claims are distinct. A blood draw is a search. The record must distinguish the standardized extraction and identification profile from retention of biological material and later secondary analysis. Individual liability requires personal involvement.
- **Relevant Dates:** Ninth Circuit decision, July 24, 1995; simulated decision, June 14, 1996.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** Multiple convicted plaintiffs challenge the same statutory program; Milligan presents a separate coverage and personal-involvement issue.
- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** 59 F.3d 1556; Oregon Laws 1991, chapter 669; operative statutes described in the opinion.

### Historical Supreme Court comparator

- **Historical Outcome and Vote:** No historical Supreme Court merits decision. The Ninth Circuit affirmance is the historical anchor.
- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Panel majority affirmed; Judge D. W. Nelson dissented from the Fourth Amendment ruling.
- **Historical Holding:** The restricted one-time DNA blood-draw program was reasonable and nonpunitive.
- **Historical Authority:** Lower-court historical anchor.
- **Historical Sources:** 59 F.3d 1556.

## Section II: Entering law and Stone's position

**Approval Status:** Draft for express approval; this Section II is not controlling or frozen until approved.

### A. Law entering the case

- **Current Through:** Law effective immediately before the June 14, 1996 simulated event, including all earlier effective simulated decisions.
- **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** No completed earlier OT1995 decision materially changes the DNA-search, pre-extraction process, or ex post facto questions presented here.
- **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** None identified.

#### Issue 1: Suspicionless DNA collection for ordinary law enforcement

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** *Vernonia School District 47J v. Acton* permits suspicionless testing only in a non-law-enforcement special-needs program and expressly reserves programs directed to ordinary criminal investigation. *Schmerber v. California* establishes that a compelled blood draw is a Fourth Amendment search.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** **Suspicionless drug testing of public-school athletes** is limited to its non-law-enforcement setting and does not create a general convicted-person exception. The warrant requirement and its recognized exceptions otherwise govern bodily extraction for criminal investigation.
- **Other Governing Law:** The Fourth Amendment; *Schmerber*; warrant, consent, probable-cause, and recognized-exception doctrine.
- **Other Authority Used:** The compelled blood draw, retention of a DNA identification profile and biological material, future-crime investigation purpose, predicate convictions, and statutory use restrictions.
- **Open Point:** Whether conviction alone creates a suspicionless ordinary-law-enforcement exception for bodily extraction and retained genetic identification.

#### Issue 2: Pre-extraction procedural due process for covered persons

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** Procedural due process identifies the protected interest and deprivation first, then asks under *Mathews v. Eldridge* what procedure is required to address the actual risk of erroneous governmental action. Predeprivation process is ordinarily required when a material predicate is genuinely disputed and advance correction is feasible; the required form depends on the error the procedure must catch.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** **Specific constitutional source and substantive-due-process nonduplication** keeps the reasonableness of the bodily search within the Fourth Amendment. Procedural due process separately governs only whether an erroneous application of statutory coverage or another material predicate requires a meaningful opportunity for correction before the bodily intrusion occurs.
- **Other Governing Law:** The Due Process Clause; *Mathews v. Eldridge*; final criminal judgments establishing predicate convictions; Oregon's statutory coverage and exemption provisions.
- **Other Authority Used:** The covered plaintiffs did not identify a genuine dispute concerning their qualifying convictions or another material statutory predicate before extraction. The Ninth Circuit separately adjudicated and rejected their demand for a hearing before every covered blood draw.
- **Open Point:** A materially different case in which a covered status, identity, health exception, adequate existing sample, or another legally operative predicate is genuinely disputed may require a pre-extraction opportunity to obtain correction.

#### Issue 3: Retrospective application and Milligan's noncoverage claim

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** *California Department of Corrections v. Morales* asks whether a retrospective change creates a sufficient risk of increased punishment. Individual § 1983 liability requires a responsible defendant and causal connection.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** **Retrospective changes to parole-hearing frequency** supplies the ex post facto risk framework by analogy. Ordinary procedural-due-process principles govern a mistaken application of the statute to a person outside its coverage.
- **Other Governing Law:** The Ex Post Facto Clause, procedural due process, state coverage provisions, and defendant-specific § 1983 principles.
- **Other Authority Used:** The statute's regulatory purpose, absence of a sentence change, Milligan's nonqualifying conviction, the disciplinary process he received, and the lack of a supported connection between the named defendants and the mistaken demand directed to him.
- **Open Point:** The due-process merits of a claim against an official actually responsible for knowingly or persistently applying the statute to a noncovered person remain outside this record.

### B. Stone's position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** **Affirm in part, reverse in part, and remand.** Reverse the Fourth Amendment judgment as to the covered plaintiffs. Affirm the judgment rejecting the covered plaintiffs' facial demand for a separate pre-extraction hearing on this record. Affirm rejection of the Ex Post Facto claim. Affirm judgment for the named defendants on Milligan's claim because the required personal involvement and causation were not established.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** The district court shall determine whether covered plaintiffs' samples have been drawn or profiles retained and enter only the declaratory or prospective relief necessary to prevent unconstitutional collection or use under Stone's Fourth Amendment holding. No separate procedural-due-process relief is required for covered plaintiffs on this record. Destruction, segregation, or nonuse may be ordered only if properly sought and necessary under the governing remedial source. Judgment remains for the named defendants on Milligan's present claim; a properly pleaded claim against an actually responsible official remains governed by ordinary procedural-due-process and § 1983 rules.

#### Issue 1: Suspicionless DNA collection for ordinary law enforcement

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the Fourth Amendment merits. Conviction does not waive the Amendment or itself supply a warrant exception.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** A blood draw is a bodily search, and retained genetic identification presents a substantial informational intrusion. Oregon's program is directed to solving ordinary future crimes, so the special-needs exception recognized in *Vernonia* is unavailable. A State may collect such material through a warrant supported by probable cause, valid consent, or another recognized Fourth Amendment exception. The importance of crime solving and a person's conviction status do not create a free-standing suspicionless law-enforcement exception.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A State may not compel a suspicionless blood draw and retain a DNA identification profile from a convicted person for ordinary future law enforcement without a warrant, probable cause, consent, or another recognized Fourth Amendment exception.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The program's central object is comparison of convicted persons' profiles against evidence from future crimes. That is ordinary criminal investigation, not a need beyond law enforcement. The extraction enters the body, and the retained material can reveal substantially more than conventional booking identifiers. Statutory limits reduce misuse risk but do not create constitutional authority for the initial suspicionless search.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not prohibit fingerprints, photographs, lawful booking records, collection under a warrant or consent, testing for a genuine non-law-enforcement medical purpose, or use of DNA independently obtained through lawful means.

#### Issue 2: Pre-extraction procedural due process for covered persons

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the independently preserved procedural claim because the grant expressly includes Due Process and the Ninth Circuit entered a separate judgment on the claimed right to a hearing before extraction. He keeps the procedural question separate from whether the search itself is reasonable under the Fourth Amendment.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Due process does not require a formal adversarial hearing before every blood draw from a person whose statutory coverage is established by an existing final conviction and who raises no genuine dispute concerning identity, coverage, or another legally operative predicate. Under *Mathews*, the bodily interest is substantial, but a hearing adds little reliability where the only relevant coverage fact is established by an official judgment and no contrary factual claim is presented. If a person timely identifies a genuine and material coverage or exemption error that can be corrected before extraction, due process requires a meaningful predeprivation opportunity to obtain that correction; the Constitution does not prescribe a trial-type hearing when a record check or other simpler procedure will reliably resolve the issue.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Covered persons are not constitutionally entitled to a separate formal hearing before every DNA blood draw when statutory coverage rests on an undisputed final conviction and no other material predicate is genuinely contested. Due process does require a meaningful pre-extraction opportunity to correct a timely raised, material coverage or exemption error when advance correction is feasible.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The covered plaintiffs did not dispute that they had convictions falling within the statute or identify a concrete coverage or exemption error requiring adjudication before extraction. A mandatory evidentiary hearing would therefore repeat facts already fixed by final judgments without materially reducing error. The existence of Milligan's separate mistaken-coverage episode shows why due process cannot be reduced to the proposition that error is impossible, but it does not establish that every covered person needs a hearing. The required procedure follows the actual source of possible error: a simple and timely correction channel is sufficient for an objective record mistake, while a genuinely disputed predicate may require more.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not hold that prison officials may ignore a timely claim of noncoverage, mistaken identity, a legally operative health exception, an adequate existing sample, or another material statutory predicate. He does not decide the precise form of process required for a genuinely disputed predicate not presented by the covered plaintiffs here.

#### Issue 3: Retrospective application and Milligan's noncoverage claim

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the ex post facto question and the defendant-specific basis of Milligan's due-process claim.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** The statute is regulatory in stated purpose and operation and does not increase the punishment attached to the predicate convictions, so retrospective application is not ex post facto punishment. Milligan's attempted-murder conviction did not place him within the statute, and the mistaken demand therefore implicated a distinct procedural concern. But personal relief under § 1983 requires proof that the defendant sued caused, maintained, or was legally responsible for that deprivation. The supplied record does not connect the named defendants whose judgment is before the Court to the mistaken order.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The statute is not ex post facto punishment. Milligan cannot obtain relief against the named defendants without establishing their personal responsibility for the mistaken demand, and the Court therefore does not decide the due-process liability of an official actually responsible for applying the statute to him.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The law changes identification administration rather than sentence length, offense elements, or parole eligibility. That conclusion does not cure the separate Fourth Amendment defect. Milligan's noncoverage demonstrates that administrative error can occur, but the particular defendants before the Court cannot be held merely because they occupied positions within the correctional system. Defendant-specific causation remains an independent requirement.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not authorize collection from a noncovered person, foreclose a properly supported procedural-due-process or § 1983 claim against an actually responsible official, or decide damages and qualified immunity for a defendant not established on this record.

# Gray v. Netherland

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** *Gray v. Netherland*
- **Citation or Docket:** 518 U.S. 152; No. 95-6510.
- **October Term:** 1995.
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Plenary federal habeas review limited to penalty-phase notice and Brady issues.
- **Simulated Event Date:** June 20, 1996.
- **Question Presented:** Whether Gray may obtain federal habeas relief based on late disclosure and alleged prosecutorial misrepresentation concerning evidence of other murders introduced at his capital sentencing.
- **Question Granted:** Whether the notice claim is barred as a new rule, whether the Brady claim is procedurally defaulted, and whether a distinct misrepresentation claim was preserved and exhausted.
- **Legal Field:** Habeas corpus, capital sentencing, due process, prosecutorial disclosure, exhaustion, default, and retroactivity.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Fourth Circuit reversed a district-court grant of habeas relief on the notice theory and rejected the Brady claim.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** Gray seeks a new penalty proceeding; the warden seeks affirmance of the denial of relief.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Before trial, Virginia stated that it would use Gray’s admissions to inmates concerning the Sorrell murders if the case reached sentencing. On the day of conviction, the prosecution disclosed that it would also present photographs, an investigating detective, and a medical examiner. Defense counsel objected to surprise and sought exclusion but did not request a continuance. In habeas, Gray separately alleged inadequate notice, suppression of favorable Sorrell evidence, and prosecutorial misrepresentation about the evidence to be used.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** The three theories are distinct. Exhaustion and procedural default must be resolved claim by claim. A new-rule bar cannot substitute for deciding whether an older, separately preserved misrepresentation principle governs. Cause and prejudice, if asserted, require record support.
- **Relevant Dates:** Argument, April 15, 1996; decision, June 20, 1996.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None.
- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** Official syllabus and opinions, 518 U.S. 152; state and federal habeas records summarized there.

### Historical Supreme Court comparator

- **Historical Outcome and Vote:** Vacated and remanded, 5-4.
- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote for the Court; Justice Ginsburg dissented with three Justices.
- **Historical Holding:** The Brady claim was defaulted; the misrepresentation claim required preservation analysis; the notice claim was separately characterized.
- **Historical Authority:** Opinion of the Court.
- **Historical Sources:** 518 U.S. 152.

## Section II: Entering law and Stone's position

**Approval Status:** Draft for express approval; this Section II is not controlling or frozen until approved.

### A. Law entering the case

- **Current Through:** June 29, 1995, including *Kyles v. Whitley* and current habeas gateway rules.
- **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** None completed at drafting.
- **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** None identified.

#### Issue 1: New advance-notice theory

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** No controlling authority at the time Gray's conviction became final established a general constitutional right to item-by-item advance notice of all nonstatutory capital-sentencing evidence.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** No exact registered standard. *Teague v. Lane* bars federal habeas relief resting on a new rule unless a recognized exception applies.
- **Other Governing Law:** *Gardner v. Florida*, capital due process, and habeas nonretroactivity.
- **Other Authority Used:** The notice theory as presented, the finality date, and the absence of controlling authority establishing the requested general rule.
- **Open Point:** None for the notice-only theory.

#### Issue 2: Suppression and affirmative prosecutorial deception

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** *Kyles v. Whitley* governs cumulative materiality of suppressed favorable evidence. Existing due process prohibits knowing prosecutorial deception. Procedural default, cause, prejudice, and exhaustion remain claim-specific thresholds.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** **Cumulative Brady materiality and prosecution-team responsibility** and **Actual innocence as a gateway to defaulted trial claims** when properly invoked.
- **Other Governing Law:** *Brady v. Maryland*, *Mooney v. Holohan*, *Raley v. Ohio*, *Wainwright v. Sykes*, and *Murray v. Carrier*.
- **Other Authority Used:** The prosecution's statements about the Sorrell evidence, the timing and content of disclosure, Gray's state and federal pleadings, and any connection between alleged deception and failure to present the claim earlier.
- **Open Point:** Whether Gray fairly preserved distinct suppression and affirmative-deception claims, and whether any default is excused, remain unresolved.

### B. Stone's position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm as to the notice-only theory; vacate and remand as to the suppression and deception claims.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** The notice-only theory may not support habeas relief. The Fourth Circuit shall classify the separately pleaded suppression and affirmative-deception claims, determine preservation, exhaustion, default, cause, prejudice, and materiality under their own doctrines, and order a new penalty proceeding only if a surviving claim satisfies its governing standard.

#### Issue 1: New advance-notice theory

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone resolves the habeas nonretroactivity threshold and does not supply an advisory merits rule.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** A general right to advance notice of every item of nonstatutory penalty evidence was not dictated by precedent when Gray's judgment became final. Federal habeas therefore may not create that rule on this record.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The notice-only theory is unavailable on federal habeas under *Teague*.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Existing cases protected against secret evidence, suppression, and deception, but they did not establish the broad item-by-item notice rule Gray seeks. The novelty bar applies to that new proposition and not automatically to separate old-law claims arising from some of the same events.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone creates no universal witness-list or criminal-discovery right and expresses no view on a prospectively enacted notice requirement.

#### Issue 2: Suppression and affirmative prosecutorial deception

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone does not decide the underlying merits until the lower court determines which distinct claims were fairly presented and whether any default is excused.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** A suppression claim and a claim that the prosecution affirmatively misrepresented the evidence it intended to present are not converted into a new notice theory merely because they concern late-disclosed penalty evidence. Each must be adjudicated under its existing source, elements, and habeas thresholds. State concealment or deception may establish cause only if it actually prevented timely presentation.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Separately preserved suppression and affirmative-deception claims must be classified and adjudicated under their own existing doctrines rather than rejected as a new advance-notice rule.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The novelty bar attaches to the new notice proposition, not to *Brady* or *Mooney* claims that require different proof and legal consequences. The record does not permit the Court to decide preservation, cause, cumulative materiality, or the truth and effect of the alleged representation in the first instance.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone orders a new penalty proceeding only if a surviving claim satisfies its governing prejudice or materiality standard.
