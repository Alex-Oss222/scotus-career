# Board of County Commissioners of Bryan County v. Brown

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** Board of County Commissioners of Bryan County v. Brown
- **Citation or Docket:** 520 U.S. 397 (1997); No. 95-1100
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued merits case on review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit.
- **Simulated Event Date:** April 28, 1997.
- **Question Presented:** Whether a county may be held liable under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 for a single hiring decision by a final policymaker when the hired deputy later uses excessive force.
- **Question Granted:** Whether Sheriff Moore's decision to hire Deputy Burns without more searching review was the moving force behind Jill Brown's constitutional injury and reflected the culpability required for municipal liability.
- **Legal Field:** Section 1983, municipal liability, excessive force, and causation.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Fifth Circuit affirmed a jury verdict against Bryan County. The county had stipulated that Sheriff Moore was its final policymaker for sheriff-department hiring, and the jury found the county liable on the theory that Moore was deliberately indifferent in hiring Burns.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** Bryan County asks the Court to reverse and direct judgment in its favor. Brown seeks affirmance of the municipal-liability judgment.
- **Material Facts and Record:** After a traffic stop, Reserve Deputy Stacy Burns pursued the vehicle, dragged Jill Brown from it, and caused serious knee injuries. Sheriff Moore had hired Burns, his nephew, after obtaining but not closely reviewing records showing driving violations and misdemeanor convictions for assault and battery, resisting arrest, and public drunkenness. Oklahoma law did not disqualify a misdemeanor offender from service, and Moore initially withheld authority to carry a weapon or drive a patrol car.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** The county preserved judgment-as-a-matter-of-law objections to fault and causation. The deputy's underlying use of excessive force is treated as established for purposes of the municipal claim. The Court should not decide Sheriff Moore's individual liability, which was dismissed before trial.
- **Relevant Dates:** Fifth Circuit decision September 8, 1995; rehearing denied November 13, 1995; argued November 5, 1996; decided April 28, 1997.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None.
- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** Official U.S. Reports, 520 U.S. 397; Brown v. Bryan County, 67 F.3d 1174 (5th Cir. 1995); trial stipulation and jury instructions reproduced in the official opinion.

### Historical Supreme Court comparator

- **Historical Outcome and Vote:** Vacated and remanded, 5-4.
- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Justice O'Connor delivered the Opinion of the Court, joined by the Chief Justice and Justices Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas. Justice Souter dissented, joined by Justices Stevens and Breyer. Justice Breyer separately dissented, joined by Justices Stevens and Ginsburg.
- **Historical Holding:** A facially lawful single hiring decision creates municipal liability only when inadequate scrutiny reflects deliberate indifference to a plainly obvious consequence that the hire would commit the particular constitutional violation and the decision directly causes the injury. The record did not satisfy that standard.
- **Historical Authority:** Opinion of the Court.
- **Historical Sources:** Board of County Commissioners v. Brown, 520 U.S. 397 (1997).

#### Issue 1: Municipal liability for a final policymaker's single hire

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the county's preserved sufficiency challenge. The underlying excessive-force violation and Sheriff Moore's final policymaking authority are treated as established for this issue.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** When a final policymaker's single hiring decision may constitute deliberate municipal action that directly causes a later constitutional injury.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** A final policymaker's single hire may support liability when the policymaker consciously disregards a substantial and highly predictable, applicant-specific risk that conferring the particular governmental authority will produce the kind of constitutional injury that occurs, and the hiring decision directly moves that injury. The standard does not require practical inevitability, which would collapse deliberate indifference into intentional authorization, but it requires much more than negligent screening, favoritism, or general unsuitability.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A final policymaker's single hiring decision may support municipal liability when the policymaker consciously disregards a substantial and highly predictable, applicant-specific risk that conferring the particular governmental authority will produce the kind of constitutional injury that occurs, and the decision directly moves that injury. This record permits that finding.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Moore conferred coercive arrest authority after obtaining but not meaningfully examining a record that included assault and battery, resistance to arrest, public drunkenness, and repeated lawbreaking. The jury could find that the risk closely corresponded to the authority granted and the excessive-force injury suffered, that Moore consciously disregarded it, and that the hire was the moving force. That is municipal fault by the County's own final policymaker, not respondeat superior.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Negligent screening, general unsuitability, or prior misconduct unrelated to the authority later abused is insufficient. The plaintiff must still prove an underlying violation, final policymaking authority, deliberate indifference, and direct causation. Individual qualified immunity cannot be transferred to the municipality.

# Pic-A-State Pennsylvania, Inc. v. Reno

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** Pic-A-State Pennsylvania, Inc. v. Reno
- **Citation or Docket:** 76 F.3d 1294 (3d Cir. 1996); No. 95-7137; simulated Supreme Court merits review
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued merits case on review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit.
- **Simulated Event Date:** April 28, 1997, simulation-assigned.
- **Question Presented:** Whether the Interstate Wagering Amendment to 18 U.S.C. § 1301, which prohibits a business from knowingly transmitting interstate information to procure interests in another State's lottery absent an interstate agreement, exceeds Congress's Commerce Clause power.
- **Question Granted:** Whether Congress may prohibit Pic-A-State's interstate computer-assisted procurement of out-of-state lottery interests.
- **Legal Field:** Commerce Clause, federal criminal law, lotteries, and pre-enforcement review.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Third Circuit affirmed dismissal of Pic-A-State's declaratory and injunctive action, holding the challenge ripe and the amendment a valid regulation of interstate commerce.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** Pic-A-State asks the Court to reverse, declare the amendment unconstitutional, and enjoin federal enforcement. The United States seeks affirmance.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Pic-A-State took Pennsylvania customer orders for interests in other States' lottery tickets. Agents bought and retained the physical tickets in the lottery State, while Pic-A-State transmitted computer-generated receipts and procurement information across state lines. Congress amended § 1301 specifically to reach that transaction unless the relevant States had authorized it by agreement. Pic-A-State ceased operations after enactment and filed this action three days later.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** The amendment directly targeted Pic-A-State's existing business, imposed criminal penalties on resumption, and caused immediate cessation. The pre-enforcement dispute is ripe and Pic-A-State has standing. No prosecution is pending, so Younger abstention is inapplicable.
- **Relevant Dates:** Amendment enacted September 13, 1994; complaint filed September 16, 1994; Third Circuit argued November 13, 1995 and decided February 13, 1996; Supreme Court argument December 4, 1996; simulated decision April 28, 1997.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None.

- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Published panel opinion by Judge Scirica, joined by Judge Becker and District Judge Cohill.
- **Historical Holding:** The Interstate Wagering Amendment regulates an interstate lottery-procurement business and is rationally related to Congress's objectives of controlling interstate gambling and preserving state lottery policies and revenues.

#### Issue 1: Pre-enforcement standing and ripeness

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone holds the challenge concrete and ripe. The statute forced Pic-A-State to stop the business Congress specifically targeted, resumption would expose it to serious criminal penalties, and the Government did not disavow enforcement.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** A predominantly legal pre-enforcement challenge is justiciable when the statute directly targets the plaintiff's existing conduct, serious penalties attach to resumption, and the Government has not disavowed enforcement.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Pic-A-State's predominantly legal pre-enforcement challenge is fit for adjudication and presents a concrete Article III controversy.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The injury is neither hypothetical nor dependent on a discretionary chain. The company ceased its existing operation because the new offense expressly reached it, and the threatened penalty made resumption impracticable.

#### Issue 2: Commerce power and the interstate-agreement exception

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the enacted offense and the legal effect of its interstate-agreement structure.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether § 1301 regulates interstate commerce itself and whether Congress may authorize state restrictions otherwise barred only by the dormant Commerce Clause.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** The Amendment regulates a knowing interstate transmission in a commercial transaction to procure an interest in another State's lottery. It therefore reaches interstate commerce itself and need not be sustained by aggregating substantial effects. The interstate element performs real work. Congress may also unmistakably authorize state restrictions otherwise barred only by the dormant Commerce Clause, and § 1301's federal prohibition coupled with its interstate-agreement exception supplies that authorization.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The Interstate Wagering Amendment regulates interstate commercial transmissions used to procure out-of-state lottery interests and therefore lies within Congress's commerce power. Its express interstate-agreement structure may authorize state restrictions that the dormant Commerce Clause alone otherwise would forbid.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Pic-A-State's business used interstate communications to consummate the economic substance of an out-of-state lottery purchase while leaving the paper ticket elsewhere. Congress regulated that cross-border procurement mechanism, not generalized gambling effects. The agreement exception permits States to alter the federally imposed rule by mutual authorization and thereby removes only the dormant implication.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not permit Congress to federalize local gambling through generalized effects, validate a nominal interstate element unrelated to the transaction, or authorize States to violate constitutional provisions other than the dormant Commerce Clause.

# Richards v. Wisconsin

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** Richards v. Wisconsin
- **Citation or Docket:** 520 U.S. 385 (1997); No. 96-5955
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued merits case on review of the Supreme Court of Wisconsin.
- **Simulated Event Date:** April 28, 1997.
- **Question Presented:** Whether the Fourth Amendment permits a blanket exception to the knock-and-announce requirement whenever officers execute a warrant in a felony drug investigation, and whether the entry here was reasonable.
- **Question Granted:** Whether Wisconsin's categorical drug-felony exception is constitutional and whether case-specific circumstances justified the officers' no-knock entry.
- **Legal Field:** Fourth Amendment, execution of search warrants, knock and announce, and exigent circumstances.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Wisconsin Supreme Court affirmed denial of suppression. It retained a categorical rule that felony drug warrants always justify unannounced entry.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** Richards asks the Court to reverse and suppress the seized cocaine and cash. Wisconsin seeks affirmance.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Officers obtained a warrant to search Richards's motel room for drugs, but the magistrate deleted requested no-knock authorization. At 3:40 a.m. an officer dressed as a maintenance worker knocked; Richards opened the chained door, saw a uniformed officer, and slammed it. After a brief interval officers identified themselves while forcing entry and found Richards attempting escape and cocaine hidden above bathroom ceiling tiles.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** The suppression issue is preserved. The Court may evaluate circumstances arising at execution even though the magistrate declined advance no-knock authorization. No remedial question beyond suppression is independently presented if the entry was reasonable.
- **Relevant Dates:** Wisconsin Supreme Court decision June 12, 1996; argued March 24, 1997; decided April 28, 1997.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None.
- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** Official U.S. Reports, 520 U.S. 385; State v. Richards, 201 Wis. 2d 845, 549 N.W.2d 218 (1996); suppression-hearing findings.

### Historical Supreme Court comparator

- **Historical Outcome and Vote:** Affirmed, 9-0.
- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Justice Stevens delivered the Opinion of the Court for a unanimous Court.
- **Historical Holding:** The Fourth Amendment permits no blanket drug-felony exception. Unannounced entry requires reasonable suspicion that announcement would be dangerous, futile, or inhibit effective investigation, and the circumstances here satisfied that standard.
- **Historical Authority:** Opinion of the Court.
- **Historical Sources:** Richards v. Wisconsin, 520 U.S. 385 (1997).

#### Issue 1: Case-specific justification for unannounced entry

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** Simulated Wilson v. Arkansas holds that knock and announce forms part of Fourth Amendment reasonableness, ordinarily requires announcement, and may be excused by case-specific circumstances indicating danger, futility, escape, or destruction of evidence. Wilson expressly left the quantum of justification open.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Knock and announce as a component of reasonableness requires case-specific grounds and rejects categorical offense-based exceptions. The present case must state the operational threshold without disturbing Wilson's purposes.
- **Other Governing Law:** The Fourth Amendment; the warrant requirement; ordinary suppression principles.
- **Other Authority Used:** Maryland v. Buie is persuasive on reasonable suspicion as a workable protective threshold. The trial court's findings concerning recognition and easily disposable evidence supply the application.

### B. Stone's approved position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the judgment while rejecting Wisconsin's categorical exception.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** The evidence remains admissible because the particular entry was reasonable. Wisconsin may not rely on a felony-drug category alone in future cases.

#### Issue 1: Case-specific justification for unannounced entry

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches both the categorical rule and the case-specific application. The suppression record supports review without new factfinding.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone rejects Wisconsin's blanket exception and adopts reasonable suspicion for dispensing with announcement. Officers must possess particular facts supporting a reasonable suspicion that announcement would create danger, be futile, permit escape, or materially risk destruction of evidence. The threshold is lower than probable cause but cannot rest solely on the charged offense.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The Fourth Amendment does not permit a categorical no-knock exception for felony drug investigations. The entry here was reasonable because Richards recognized police, immediately closed and secured the door, and the officers reasonably suspected that further delay would permit destruction of readily disposable drugs or escape.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** A category-wide exception would make Wilson's protection vanish whenever officials selected a drug offense and would substitute legislative or police generalization for judicial review of the entry actually made. At execution, however, the factual setting changed from the one before the magistrate. Richards's apparent recognition and abrupt retreat made additional announcement substantially futile and created a concrete destruction risk. The officers' brief response was tied to that development rather than to a per se rule.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The disposable character of drugs alone does not justify every unannounced entry. Advance denial of no-knock authorization does not forbid officers from responding to later exigent facts, and advance authorization does not excuse review of the actual execution.

#### Issue 1: Governmental use of identified medical information

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** Sheets v. Salt Lake County protects against deliberate public dissemination of highly intimate information entrusted to government for a limited purpose when the disclosure serves no connected governmental function. Hodge v. Jones addresses confidential retention without new investigative use or consequential disclosure.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** The existing limited confidentiality protection requires a genuine and proportionate connection between governmental use of identified intimate information and the public function asserted.

### B. Stone's approved position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse the Third Circuit's direction to enter judgment for the defendants under Rule 50 and remand.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** The district court's denial of judgment as a matter of law stands. The Third Circuit shall address any preserved Rule 59 and damages issues it did not reach. No ungranted municipal-liability, personal-immunity, or damages question is decided by this Court.

#### Issue 1: Governmental use of identified medical information

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the constitutional standard and applies it under Rule 50's verdict-protective posture. He does not decide the ungranted liability and remedy questions identified above.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether access to and internal use of identified prescription information must remain proportionately connected to the actual public function asserted, and whether the evidence permitted the jury's finding.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Identifiable prescription records, especially records revealing HIV status, fall within the Fourteenth Amendment's limited protection against unjustified governmental acquisition, use, and disclosure of highly intimate information. The existing confidentiality balance asks whether personally identifying access is genuinely and proportionately connected to the public function asserted, considering sensitivity, the need for identifiers rather than aggregate or coded data, who receives access, and the safeguards actually in place. SEPTA's interest justified utilization monitoring, but it did not establish justification as a matter of law for continued identified review.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Governmental access to and internal use of identified prescription information must remain proportionately connected to an actual public function, with access and safeguards fitted to the information's sensitivity. The evidence permitted the jury to find that SEPTA exceeded that need when it continued to use Doe's identified prescription record beyond a function that required only de-identified utilization data.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** A reasonable jury could find that employee names were unnecessary for the relevant utilization monitoring, that Pierce deliberately examined and researched the identified prescriptions after their sensitivity became apparent, and that no established safeguard limited unnecessary identified review. The Third Circuit could not replace those verdict-supported inferences with its own characterization of the intrusion as minimal.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not bar aggregate or coded auditing, identified review after a concrete fraud or eligibility concern, disclosures required by law, or confidential retention alone. It does not decide damages, personal immunity, municipal liability, or private-employer duties.

#### Issue 1: Section 1465 coverage, targeted electronic distribution, and receiving-community standards

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the convictions arising from the Memphis downloads and shipments. The controlled membership record permits a narrow disposition without deciding the architecture of the open Internet.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether § 1465 reaches obscene GIF files transmitted through an interstate computer-and-telephone system and whether a distributor that screens a subscriber, knows the subscriber's Tennessee location, and authorizes delivery may be prosecuted and judged in the receiving community.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies the enacted terms and existing obscenity and venue law to targeted electronic delivery. Section 1465's references to transport, distribution, pictures, images, electrical transcriptions, and other covered matter reach GIF image files knowingly transmitted through an interstate computer-and-telephone system; applying those general terms to a later transmission method is application, not statutory amendment. The Thomases knowingly approved a Memphis member and transmitted files at his request through interstate facilities. The receiving district was both a place of completed distribution and a community whose standards the defendants could anticipate and avoid by withholding access. No national standard is required on these facts.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Section 1465 reaches obscene image files knowingly transported for distribution through an interstate computer-and-telephone system. When operators knowingly authorize delivery to a subscriber in a known district, venue is proper there and that receiving community supplies Miller's geographic community standards.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Section 1465 uses general terms covering images and electrical transmissions rather than a closed list of physical media, so the interstate delivery method falls within the enacted offense. The files did not simply exist on a server for unknown users. Membership, payment, application review, address information, a confirming call, and individual passwords made each distribution a controlled transaction. Memphis was where the material was received and the statutory offense completed. The operators could protect themselves by declining the subscription or limiting distribution. Applying local standards here therefore does not impose unknowable liability for accidental global reach.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not decide an open website, an anonymous network, unavoidable routing, or a publisher unable to identify or control the recipient's jurisdiction. It does not alter the national serious-value component of Miller or protect entrapment, selective prosecution, or evidentiary claims not before the Court.
