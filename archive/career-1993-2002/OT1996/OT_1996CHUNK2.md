# Atherton v. Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** Atherton v. Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation
- **Citation or Docket:** 519 U.S. 213 (1997); No. 95-928; useful lower-court citation: 57 F.3d 1231 (3d Cir. 1995).
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued merits case on review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit.
- **Simulated Event Date:** January 14, 1997.
- **Question Presented:** Whether federal common law supplies a nationwide standard of care for officers and directors of federally chartered savings associations, and how 12 U.S.C. § 1821(k) interacts with state law.
- **Question Granted:** Whether state law or a federal common-law negligence rule governs the FDIC receiver's claims, subject to § 1821(k)'s gross-negligence provision.
- **Legal Field:** Federal courts, federal common law, financial institutions, and statutory interpretation.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Third Circuit applied a federal common-law standard of ordinary negligence to claims brought by the FDIC as receiver for City Federal Savings Bank.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** Atherton asks the Court to reverse and require application of New Jersey law, which he argues supplies a more protective business-judgment standard. The FDIC seeks affirmance of a nationwide federal negligence standard.
- **Material Facts and Record:** After City Federal failed, its federal receiver sued former officers and directors for gross negligence, simple negligence, and breach of fiduciary duty arising from allegedly imprudent loans. The institution was federally chartered and insured, but the asserted duties were traditional corporate-governance duties. Section 1821(k) expressly authorizes liability for gross negligence and preserves other applicable law.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** The receiver's standing and federal jurisdiction are not disputed. The case presents choice of governing law, not whether the alleged conduct satisfies any particular standard. No factual finding of liability is before the Court.
- **Relevant Dates:** Argued November 4, 1996; decided January 14, 1997.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None.
- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** Official U.S. Reports, 519 U.S. 213, for the record, dates, judgment, and historical comparator; Atherton v. FDIC, 57 F.3d 1231 (3d Cir. 1995), for the judgment under review; 12 U.S.C. § 1821(k); New Jersey corporate law identified below.

### Historical Supreme Court comparator

- **Historical Outcome and Vote:** Vacated and remanded, 9-0.
- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Justice Breyer delivered the Opinion of the Court. Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg joined in full. Justices O'Connor, Scalia, and Thomas joined except to the extent the opinion relied on legislative history. Justice O'Connor, joined by Justices Scalia and Thomas, concurred in part and concurred in the judgment.
- **Historical Holding:** State law ordinarily governs the standard of care for officers and directors of federally chartered savings institutions. Section 1821(k) creates a federal gross-negligence floor and preserves stricter state standards; no special federal common-law ordinary-negligence rule is justified.
- **Historical Authority:** Opinion of the Court.
- **Historical Sources:** Atherton v. FDIC, 519 U.S. 213 (1997).

#### Issue 1: Governing standard of care

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** O'Melveny & Myers v. FDIC, 512 U.S. 79 (1994), holds that state law governs an FDIC receiver's state-law claims and defenses unless enacted federal law or a specific and significant conflict with an identifiable federal policy requires displacement. No simulated holding creates a general federal corporate-governance law for federally chartered institutions.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** No separately titled Standards and Tests entry is registered for O'Melveny's federal-common-law rule, but the holding controls directly.
- **Other Governing Law:** 12 U.S.C. § 1821(k); federal thrift-charter provisions; New Jersey law governing director and officer duties; Erie and Kimbell Foods principles.
- **Other Authority Used:** The historical Atherton opinion is date-eligible persuasive authority on the interaction between § 1821(k) and preserved state standards.
- **Open Point:** Whether § 1821(k)'s final sentence preserves state law stricter than gross negligence or instead preempts it is not separately answered in the supplied registers.

### B. Stone's approved position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Vacate the judgment and remand.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** The court of appeals shall direct application of New Jersey law subject to § 1821(k)'s federal gross-negligence floor. The lower courts shall decide breach, causation, defenses, and remedy under the correct sources.

#### Issue 1: Governing standard of care

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the statutory and federal-common-law merits. He does not decide whether Atherton was negligent or whether New Jersey law ultimately imposes liability.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether federal chartering and insurance justify a judge-made uniform negligence rule, and what legal effect § 1821(k) gives state law.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies O'Melveny and the enacted text. Federal chartering, nationwide supervision, and receivership convenience do not themselves authorize federal common law. Section 1821(k) makes gross negligence actionable under federal law and expressly preserves liability under other applicable law. The best reading is a federal floor, not a ceiling: state law may impose ordinary negligence or another stricter standard, while a State may not reduce liability below the federal gross-negligence minimum.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** State law governs the ordinary standard of care for officers and directors of a federally chartered savings association unless a specific federal rule displaces it. Section 1821(k) establishes gross negligence as a minimum federal basis of liability and preserves stricter applicable state law.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The asserted duties concern lending judgment, fiduciary conduct, and corporate governance, fields traditionally supplied by state law. The FDIC identifies no operational conflict that requires a federal ordinary-negligence rule. Section 1821(k) does not merely mention gross negligence; its preservation clause confirms that other law continues to operate. Reading the section as a ceiling would erase that clause and create the broad preemption Congress did not write. The lower court therefore used the wrong source of law.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not prevent Congress or a valid federal regulation from supplying a specific duty, decide the standard for federal enforcement actions resting on distinct statutory obligations, or resolve New Jersey's business-judgment rule.

# Elizabeth G. Curtis v. School Committee of Falmouth

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** Elizabeth G. Curtis v. School Committee of Falmouth
- **Citation or Docket:** 420 Mass. 749, 652 N.E.2d 580 (1995); historical certiorari petition No. 95-617; simulated Supreme Court merits review.
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued merits case on review of the Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts.
- **Simulated Event Date:** January 21, 1997, simulation-assigned.
- **Question Presented:** Whether a voluntary public-school condom-availability program, without parental opt-out or notification, violates parents' liberty to direct upbringing, familial privacy, or free exercise of religion.
- **Question Granted:** Whether the Federal Constitution requires parental veto or notification when junior-high and high-school students may voluntarily obtain condoms from a nurse or vending machine.
- **Legal Field:** Family liberty, public education, free exercise, and student health policy.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts affirmed summary judgment for the school committee, holding that the voluntary program imposed no coercive constitutional burden and required neither opt-out nor parental notification.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** The parent and student plaintiffs ask the Court to reverse and enjoin the program unless parents may bar participation and receive notice when a student obtains a condom.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Falmouth made condoms available in grades seven through twelve. Junior-high students could request them from a nurse after counseling; high-school students could request them from a nurse or buy them from restroom vending machines. Participation, counseling, and literature were voluntary. The program emphasized abstinence as the only certain method of avoiding sexually transmitted disease and contained no parental opt-out or notice requirement.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** The federal claims were preserved. State-law medical-consent and moral-education claims were waived below and are not before the Court. The challenge is facial to the program's stated operation; no student was compelled to obtain a condom or undergo treatment.
- **Relevant Dates:** Civil action commenced May 22, 1992; Supreme Judicial Court argued March 7, 1995, and decided July 17, 1995; historical certiorari denied January 8, 1996; simulated Supreme Court argument November 4, 1996; simulated decision January 21, 1997.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None.
- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** Curtis v. School Committee of Falmouth, 420 Mass. 749, 652 N.E.2d 580 (1995), for the program, procedural history, court composition, and judgment; the superintendent's January 2, 1992 memorandum; school-program record.

### Historical Supreme Court comparator

- **Historical Outcome and Vote:** No historical Supreme Court merits comparator. The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court unanimously affirmed, and certiorari was denied January 8, 1996, in No. 95-617, reported at 516 U.S. 1067.
- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Chief Justice Liacos wrote for a unanimous court consisting of Chief Justice Liacos and Justices Wilkins, Abrams, O'Connor, and Greaney.
- **Historical Holding:** Because the program was entirely voluntary and imposed no penalty or compelled participation, it did not substantially burden parental liberty, familial privacy, or religious exercise.

#### Issue 1: Parental liberty and familial privacy

### B. Stone's approved position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the judgment of the Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** No separate remedial instruction is required.

#### Issue 1: Parental liberty and familial privacy

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the facial merits. Waived state-law consent claims and hypothetical medical services are outside the case.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether mere availability of condoms in a compulsory-school setting is itself coercive interference with parental direction.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies the received family-liberty cases without enlarging them. Parents retain primary authority to instruct their children on morality, religion, and sexual conduct. The State does not displace that authority by making a nonprescription preventive item voluntarily available, where students may ignore the program, no belief is demanded, and no benefit or discipline turns on participation. Compulsory attendance does not make every optional resource compulsory.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A voluntary school condom-availability program does not violate parental liberty or familial privacy merely because it lacks a parental opt-out or notice provision, when students are not required to obtain condoms, receive counseling, read materials, or affirm any message.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The challenged program changes availability, not legal authority within the family. Parents remain free to instruct their children not to participate, and the school imposes no sanction for compliance with that instruction. The condoms are not an abortion, sterilization, medication, or other procedure that itself changes bodily status. The program's presence in school may offend or make parental instruction more difficult, but constitutional interference requires more than proximity to an option the parent rejects.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not decide confidential medical treatment, prescription drugs, compelled sex education, distribution to a child unable to consent, or a program that affirmatively undermines parental authority through penalty or deception.

#### Issue 2: Free exercise

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the free-exercise merits. The plaintiffs identify sincere religious objections but no required religiously prohibited act.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether exposure to and voluntary access to a school program substantially burdens religious exercise.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies the substantial-burden threshold. Government neither requires students to obtain condoms nor conditions schooling, grades, discipline, or benefits on approval of the program. The school does not regulate belief or worship. Offense and peer influence do not become legal compulsion without state-created pressure tied to a consequence.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The program imposes no substantial burden on free exercise because participation is voluntary and no student or parent is penalized for religiously motivated refusal.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The Constitution protects the family from coerced observance and targeted disability, not from every public program inconsistent with private faith. The record contains no state demand that the plaintiffs endorse premarital sex, use contraception, or surrender a benefit. The free-exercise claim therefore ends at the burden threshold.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not approve religious targeting, compelled participation in sexual instruction, denial of an exemption from a mandatory practice, or a program that conditions a benefit on conduct forbidden by faith.

# Ingalls Shipbuilding, Inc. v. Director, Office of Workers' Compensation Programs

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** Ingalls Shipbuilding, Inc. v. Director, Office of Workers' Compensation Programs
- **Citation or Docket:** 519 U.S. 248 (1997); No. 95-1081
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued merits case on review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit.
- **Simulated Event Date:** February 18, 1997.
- **Question Presented:** Whether an injured worker's spouse is a 'person entitled to compensation' under LHWCA § 33(g) before the worker's death, and whether the Director of OWCP is a proper respondent in a court-of-appeals review proceeding.
- **Question Granted:** Whether Mrs. Yates forfeited future death benefits by settling third-party claims before her husband's death without employer approval, and whether the Director may participate as respondent under Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 15(a).
- **Legal Field:** Workers' compensation, statutory interpretation, and administrative appellate procedure.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Fifth Circuit affirmed the Benefits Review Board's ruling that the spouse was not yet a person entitled to death benefits and permitted the Director to appear as respondent.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** Ingalls asks the Court to reverse both rulings and bar the widow's death-benefit claim.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Jefferson Yates was exposed to asbestos while working for Ingalls and later settled disability claims. His wife joined third-party settlements releasing present consortium and possible future wrongful-death claims without Ingalls's approval. She later became a widow and sought LHWCA death benefits. Before her husband's death, she might have predeceased or divorced him, or he might have died from a nonwork cause.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** Mrs. Yates's entitlement is determined at the time of the third-party settlements. The Director's party status is a procedural question under Rule 15(a), not Article III standing in the strict sense because the private parties remain adverse. No issue of the amount of benefits is before the Court.
- **Relevant Dates:** Argued November 12, 1996; decided February 18, 1997.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** The caption includes related Ingalls petitioners and the Yates claim; no separately controlled docket requires a different judgment.
- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** Official U.S. Reports, 519 U.S. 248; LHWCA §§ 9 and 33; Benefits Review Board and Fifth Circuit records.

### Historical Supreme Court comparator

- **Historical Outcome and Vote:** Affirmed. The compensation issue was unanimous; the Director-participation issue was 7-2.
- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Justice O'Connor delivered the unanimous portions and the Court's opinion on participation, joined there by the Chief Justice, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Justice Scalia, joined by Thomas, dissented from the participation ruling.
- **Historical Holding:** A spouse is not a person entitled to death benefits before the worker dies, so predeath settlements do not trigger § 33(g)'s approval forfeiture. The Department of Labor, represented by the Director, may be named respondent under Rule 15(a).
- **Historical Authority:** Opinion of the Court at each stated scope.
- **Historical Sources:** Ingalls Shipbuilding, Inc. v. Director, OWCP, 519 U.S. 248 (1997).

### B. Stone's approved position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the Fifth Circuit's judgment.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** Mrs. Yates's death-benefit claim may proceed without a § 33(g) forfeiture based on the predeath settlements. The Director may remain a respondent. Amount, causation, and any other defenses remain for the agency process.

#### Issue 1: Predeath spouse and § 33(g)

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the statutory merits. The future-benefit question is ripe because Ingalls asserts the settlements as a present forfeiture defense.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether Mrs. Yates satisfied the legal prerequisites for death benefits when she signed the settlements before her husband's death.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies the statutory phrase at the time of settlement. A person is entitled when the legal prerequisites for the benefit are satisfied, not merely when future events could make entitlement likely. Before Mr. Yates died from a work-related cause, Mrs. Yates had no death-benefit claim and might never have acquired one. The phrase 'would be entitled' compares settlement value to compensation once a person already entitled enters the settlement; it does not convert every potential future beneficiary into a present one.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** An injured worker's spouse is not a 'person entitled to compensation' for LHWCA death benefits before the worker's work-related death. A predeath third-party settlement therefore does not trigger § 33(g)'s employer-approval forfeiture for the later death-benefit claim.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The statute uses entitlement, not expectancy. Mrs. Yates's present consortium claim and contingent wrongful-death release do not establish the distinct federal prerequisites for LHWCA death benefits. Ingalls's double-recovery concern is a policy argument that cannot add a forfeiture Congress did not write. Other offset provisions remain available only within their text.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not govern a settlement entered after the worker's death, an already vested disability claim, or an employer's right to an offset expressly authorized elsewhere.

#### Issue 2: Director as respondent

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the procedural issue because both private parties remain adverse and the Director participated below.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether Rule 15(a)'s agency-respondent requirement permits the Department to appear through its designated litigating officer.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies the Rule to the Department whose statutory program and internal adjudicative components produced the review proceeding. The Secretary may designate the Director to represent the Department. Respondent status does not make the Director the author of the Board's decision or require defense of it; it permits the Department to state its position within an existing Article III controversy.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The Director of OWCP, as the Secretary's designated representative of the Department of Labor, is a proper respondent under Rule 15(a) in review of a Benefits Review Board order when a private party has invoked appellate jurisdiction.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The LHWCA divides adjudication and administration within one Department and separately authorizes the Secretary's litigation designation. Rule 15(a) requires the agency to be named, and the Director is the Department's lawful representative. Newport News limits who may initiate review; it does not bar participation once another party creates the appeal.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not decide whether the Director may initiate an appeal in a setting Newport News forbids or whether an agency representative alone can preserve a controversy after all private adversity disappears.

# Uhlrig v. Harder

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** Uhlrig v. Harder
- **Citation or Docket:** 64 F.3d 567 (10th Cir. 1995); No. 94-3190; simulated Supreme Court merits review.
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued merits case on review of summary judgment in an individual-capacity § 1983 action.
- **Simulated Event Date:** February 18, 1997, simulation-assigned.
- **Question Presented:** Whether state mental-health administrators affirmatively created or intensified a danger to a hospital therapist by closing a secure forensic unit and delegating patient reassignment, and whether they may be held personally liable for her murder by a patient.
- **Question Granted:** Whether the record supports a due-process danger-creation claim against the named administrators and whether qualified immunity bars damages.
- **Legal Field:** Substantive due process, state-created danger, causation, and qualified immunity.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Tenth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for the administrators, finding no conscience-shocking reckless danger creation and qualified immunity.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** Uhlrig's estate asks the Court to reverse and permit a jury to decide whether the administrators knowingly exposed a defined group of therapists to a substantial danger.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Budget constraints led officials to close a secure forensic ward at Topeka State Hospital. Clinical teams, not the named senior officials, assigned Kenneth Waddell to general units. Waddell had a violent history and later raped a patient. Therapist Stephanie Uhlrig received general and specific warnings, had access to his file, and agreed to work with him. He killed her after an off-grounds outing.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** Claims against Kansas, derivative family claims, and state-law tort claims are not before the Court. Only the estate's individual-capacity § 1983 claim remains. Defendant-specific knowledge, affirmative conduct, causation, and clearly established law must remain separate.
- **Relevant Dates:** Relevant administrative actions in 1991; killing February 23, 1992; Tenth Circuit decision August 30, 1995; argued November 5, 1996; simulated decision February 18, 1997.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None.
- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** Uhlrig v. Harder, 64 F.3d 567 (10th Cir. 1995), for the hospital closure, placement and warning record, panel, and judgment.

### Historical Supreme Court comparator

- **Historical Outcome and Vote:** No historical Supreme Court merits comparator. The historical anchor is the Tenth Circuit's affirmance of summary judgment.
- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Judge Ebel wrote for a panel joined by Judge Kelly and Senior District Judge Howard C. Bratton, sitting by designation.
- **Historical Holding:** The evidence did not show a substantial immediate risk, reckless conscious disregard, or conscience-shocking conduct by the named administrators; qualified immunity also applied.

#### Issue 1: Affirmative creation or intensification of private danger

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** Pinder v. Johnson, 54 F.3d 1169 (simulated decision May 21, 1996), holds that due process reaches a narrow sequence in which an official knows of a specific imminent threat, induces abandonment of self-protection, affirmatively uses authority to make the person materially more vulnerable, acts with deliberate indifference, and causes the harm. It creates no general duty to protect.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Affirmative official creation or intensification of a private danger. The claimant must establish a specific imminent threat, affirmative inducement, reasonable reliance altering self-protection, deliberate official use of authority, material increased vulnerability, deliberate indifference, and causation.
- **Other Governing Law:** Fourteenth Amendment; 42 U.S.C. § 1983; DeShaney and Collins; qualified-immunity rules in Elder, Johnson v. Jones, and Behrens.
- **Other Authority Used:** The historical Uhlrig opinion is persuasive on the record but its free-standing five-factor formula does not replace Pinder's controlling rule.

### B. Stone's approved position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the Tenth Circuit's judgment.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** No separate remedial instruction is required. The judgment concerns only the federal individual-capacity claim in this appeal.

#### Issue 1: Affirmative creation or intensification of private danger

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the constitutional merits. Because no constitutional violation is established, he does not reach qualified immunity.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether the named administrators affirmatively induced reliance or personally made Uhlrig more vulnerable to a specific imminent threat.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies Pinder without converting negligent administration into constitutional liability. Closing the unit and delegating placement were affirmative policy acts, but the record does not show a concrete assurance to Uhlrig, induced abandonment of available protection, concealment, or a defendant-specific decision directing Waddell into her care. She was warned of general and specific risk, trained for violent patients, and had access to his file. The named officials used a clinical placement process rather than deliberately overriding a known immediate threat to her.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The record does not establish the affirmative inducement, material increase in vulnerability, deliberate indifference, and defendant-specific causation required by Pinder. The estate therefore has no substantive-due-process damages claim against the named administrators.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The death is grave, and the decision to close a specialized ward may have been negligent or poor policy. Due process does not make senior officials insurers of every later act by a patient. Pinder requires government to create the specific vulnerability through an affirmative sequence, not merely to regulate a dangerous institution imperfectly. The warnings and delegated clinical process defeat the claimed inducement and concealment, while the absence of personal placement decisions defeats causal responsibility. Summary judgment was proper.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not protect an official who conceals a known imminent threat, affirmatively orders an employee into a uniquely dangerous encounter after promising safety, or personally overrides clinical safeguards with deliberate indifference.

# Auer v. Robbins

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** Auer v. Robbins
- **Citation or Docket:** 519 U.S. 452 (1997); No. 95-897
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued merits case on review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit.
- **Simulated Event Date:** February 19, 1997.
- **Question Presented:** Whether the Department of Labor's salary-basis regulation validly applies to public employees, how the phrase 'subject to reduction' should be interpreted, and what weight courts give an agency's interpretation of its own regulation.
- **Question Granted:** Whether St. Louis police sergeants and a lieutenant are salaried executive employees exempt from FLSA overtime under 29 C.F.R. § 541.118(a).
- **Legal Field:** Administrative law, statutory interpretation, labor law, and agency interpretation of regulations.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Eighth Circuit held that all petitioners satisfied the salary-basis and duties tests and were exempt from overtime.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** The officers ask the Court to reverse and award further proceedings on overtime. The police commissioners seek affirmance.
- **Material Facts and Record:** The police manual authorized disciplinary sanctions that could include pay deductions, but such deductions were not the general practice. One deduction implicated the salary-basis rule. The Secretary interpreted 'subject to reduction' to require either an actual practice of improper deductions or a significant likelihood created by a clear and particularized policy, and recognized a reimbursement or correction window for isolated improper deductions.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** The Secretary participated as amicus and presented an authoritative agency interpretation. The case permits independent construction of the regulation and does not require deciding damages before liability. The duties question is preserved.
- **Relevant Dates:** Argued December 10, 1996; decided February 19, 1997.

- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** Official U.S. Reports, 519 U.S. 452; 29 U.S.C. §§ 207 and 213; 29 C.F.R. §§ 541.1 to 541.3 and 541.118; St. Louis Police Department Manual.

### Historical Supreme Court comparator

Assuming the positive-subscription policy created a state-law interest, failure to preserve a challenge to available postdeprivation remedies defeats the procedural-due-process claim.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The program may have been crude and the alleged policy violation may support state-law relief. Federal liability still requires an identified constitutional deprivation. Listening is not compelled profession, public-school enrollment is not a waiver of parental instruction outside school, and compulsory attendance does not make every idea a bodily or religious imposition. The unpreserved challenge to state remedies prevents the Court from converting the local policy dispute into a federal procedural judgment.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not authorize compelled profession or participation, bodily intrusion, religious establishment or targeting, discrimination because of sex, pretextual curricular action, or disregard of state law without the remedies that source provides.

#### Issue 2: Title IX

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the missing statutory element and does not reach severity or pervasiveness.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Neither the presentation nor the alleged later ridicule was imposed on the plaintiffs because of sex. Sexual subject matter, without sex-based targeting, exclusion, or hostility, is not Title IX discrimination.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The complaint fails under Title IX because it alleges no discrimination, exclusion, or hostile educational condition imposed on the plaintiffs because of sex.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The presentation was delivered to the student audience generally, and the named students were not selected for the skits. The missing because-of-sex element disposes of the claim without announcing a broader recipient-liability rule.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not govern sex-specific humiliation, school-authorized sexual targeting, or deliberate indifference to known sex-based harassment.

#### Issue 1: Doctrine of equivalents and prosecution-history estoppel
