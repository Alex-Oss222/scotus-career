# Wright v. MetroHealth Medical Center

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** Wright v. MetroHealth Medical Center
- **Citation or Docket:** 58 F.3d 1130 (6th Cir. 1995); No. 94-3548; simulated Supreme Court merits review
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued merits case on review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit.
- **Simulated Event Date:** March 3, 1997, simulation-assigned.
- **Question Presented:** Whether a public hospital's anti-nepotism policy, as applied to a nurse and a contract helicopter pilot who married while working in the same emergency-flight unit, directly and substantially interferes with the fundamental right to marry or otherwise violates equal protection and due process.
- **Question Granted:** Whether MetroHealth's transfer policy imposed an unconstitutional condition on marriage and whether the federal judgment sustaining the policy should stand.
- **Legal Field:** Personal liberty, equal protection, public employment, and rational-basis review.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Sixth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for MetroHealth on the federal constitutional claim and affirmed dismissal of the pendent state-law claims.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** The Wrights ask the Court to reverse, subject the policy to strict scrutiny, enjoin its application to them, and restore their state-law claims. MetroHealth seeks affirmance.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Martha Wright, a MetroHealth registered nurse, and John Wright, a helicopter pilot employed by MetroHealth's contractor, worked in the hospital's LifeFlight unit. After they announced their engagement, MetroHealth invoked a policy barring immediate family members from positions requiring close interaction and offered Martha a comparable transfer; when she declined, MetroHealth asked the contractor to move John. The policy did not bar the marriage, terminate either spouse, or reduce pay as written, and the parties arranged schedules so they did not fly together while litigation proceeded.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** The federal constitutional claim was resolved on summary judgment. No live claim challenges a completed discharge, because neither plaintiff was terminated. The Court should not decide the merits of the state tort and employment claims beyond the federal disposition necessary to resolve supplemental-jurisdiction consequences.
- **Relevant Dates:** Sixth Circuit argued May 22, 1995; decided July 13, 1995; rehearing denied September 1, 1995; Supreme Court argument November 12, 1996; simulated decision March 3, 1997.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None.
- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** Wright v. MetroHealth Medical Center, 58 F.3d 1130 (6th Cir. 1995); the hospital nepotism policy reproduced in the opinion; district-court summary-judgment materials.

### Historical Supreme Court comparator

- **Historical Outcome and Vote:** No historical Supreme Court merits comparator. The historical anchor is the Sixth Circuit's affirmance.
- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Published panel opinion by Judge Milburn, joined by Judge Batchelder and District Judge Todd.
- **Historical Holding:** The policy did not directly and substantially interfere with marriage and was rationally related to avoiding conflicts of interest and workplace-morale problems.
- **Historical Authority:** Lower-court judgment only.
- **Historical Sources:** Wright, 58 F.3d 1130.

### B. Stone's approved position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the Sixth Circuit's judgment.

#### Issue 1: Anti-nepotism policy and the right to marry

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the constitutional merits. The record identifies the policy, the offered transfer, and the absence of a legal bar to marriage; no additional factfinding is required to classify the burden.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether requiring one spouse to leave a particular work unit, while preserving employment and pay, directly and substantially interferes with the decision to marry.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies received doctrine without creating a new test. The policy affects where the spouses may work together, not whether they may marry or remain married. It therefore receives rational-basis review. Avoiding conflicts in a small, high-pressure medical-flight team and preserving confidence in assignment and safety decisions are legitimate objectives. A rule requiring separation within the unit is rationally related to those objectives even if MetroHealth evaluates close proximity case by case.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A public-employer policy requiring one spouse to transfer from a work unit in which the spouses must interact closely does not directly and substantially burden marriage when it creates no legal obstacle to marriage, preserves employment and comparable pay, and regulates only the spouses' simultaneous assignment. On this record, the policy survives rational-basis review.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The Wrights married despite the policy, and nothing in the rule required divorce, denied civil status, or made marriage practically unavailable. The transfer consequence is real, but it is an employment consequence of the same kind received doctrine treats as indirect. MetroHealth need not prove that every married pair will compromise operations. It may make a reasonable line-drawing judgment that close, recurring interaction in LifeFlight creates risks of perceived favoritism, divided loyalty, and impaired team coordination. Selective application based on actual proximity is not irrational on this record.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not approve a policy adopted to deter marriage, a rule that automatically discharges one spouse, sex-based administration, selective enforcement resting on animus, or a transfer materially inferior in pay or responsibility.

# Brown v. Polk County

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** Brown v. Polk County
- **Citation or Docket:** 61 F.3d 650 (8th Cir. 1995) (en banc); No. 93-3313; simulated Supreme Court merits review
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued merits case on review of an en banc judgment of the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit.
- **Simulated Event Date:** March 17, 1997, simulation-assigned.
- **Question Presented:** Whether a county may broadly prohibit a supervisory employee from religious witnessing, counseling, proselytizing, prayer, and possession of religious items at work in order to avoid establishment concerns, and whether religion was an impermissible factor in his discharge under Title VII.
- **Question Granted:** Whether the workplace directives and the religion-based component of Isaiah Brown's termination violated the First Amendment and Title VII.
- **Legal Field:** Free exercise, speech, public employment, establishment, and employment discrimination.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Eighth Circuit, sitting en banc, affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded. It held the broad religious-expression restrictions unconstitutional and reversed the judgment on Brown's statutory religious-discrimination claims while leaving other rulings intact.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** Polk County asks the Court to reverse the constitutional and Title VII rulings. Brown seeks affirmance and appropriate declaratory, injunctive, and employment relief on remand.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Isaiah Brown directed Polk County's information-services department and supervised about fifty employees. An investigation found that a secretary had typed Bible-study notes, employees sometimes prayed in Brown's office before work and at department meetings, and Brown referred to Christian beliefs and work-ethic passages. The county ordered him to cease any activity that could be considered proselytizing, witnessing, or counseling and later directed removal of all religious items, including a Bible kept in a desk. The administrator later testified that the religion-related reprimand was one factor in Brown's discharge, along with budget and supervisory concerns.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** The case follows a trial record. The constitutional restrictions, Title VII causation, municipal responsibility, individual liability, and remedy must remain distinct. Conduct preceding the Civil Rights Act of 1991 is governed by the law then applicable under Landgraf.
- **Relevant Dates:** Eighth Circuit submitted April 13, 1995; decided July 31, 1995; rehearing denied September 21, 1995; Supreme Court argument November 13, 1996; simulated decision March 17, 1997.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None.
- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** Brown v. Polk County, 61 F.3d 650 (8th Cir. 1995) (en banc); Brown v. Polk County, 832 F. Supp. 1305 (S.D. Iowa 1993); county reprimands and trial testimony quoted in the en banc opinion.

### Historical Supreme Court comparator

- **Historical Outcome and Vote:** No historical Supreme Court merits comparator. The historical anchor is the divided en banc Eighth Circuit judgment.
- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Judge Morris Sheppard Arnold wrote for the en banc majority. Judge Fagg dissented, joined by Judges Loken, Hansen, and Murphy.
- **Historical Holding:** The blanket directives violated Brown's First Amendment rights; religion was a motivating factor in his discharge, and the county failed on the existing record to prove it would have discharged him for lawful reasons alone.
- **Historical Authority:** Lower-court judgment only.
- **Historical Sources:** Brown, 61 F.3d 650.

#### Issue 1: Title VII religious accommodation

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** Beadle v. City of Tampa applies Trans World Airlines v. Hardison and Ansonia Board of Education v. Philbrook. At this date, an employer must reasonably accommodate a known sincere practice unless accommodation would impose a real, more-than-de-minimis operational burden; speculation and coworker hostility are insufficient.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Private religious practice and actual operational burden must be evaluated separately from official sponsorship and use of public resources.
- **Other Governing Law:** Title VII, 42 U.S.C. §§ 2000e(j) and 2000e-2; Hardison; Ansonia.
- **Other Authority Used:** The record distinguishes private employee expression from use of subordinates, work time, and public resources.

#### Issue 2: Free Exercise and religion-specific directives

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** Swanner, Capitol Square v. Pinette, Chabad-Lubavitch of Georgia v. Miller, and Good News/Good Sports Club preserve neutral treatment of private religious exercise while permitting source-specific restrictions on coercion and public financing.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** A religion-specific prohibition must be justified under the governing constitutional standard; neutral rules directed to resource use, harassment, and official duties remain available.
- **Other Governing Law:** Employment Division v. Smith; Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. Hialeah.

#### Issue 3: Employee speech and Establishment attribution

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** United States v. National Treasury Employees Union governs a broad prospective speech restriction. Waters v. Churchill governs public-employer reliance on disputed facts. Pinette and Chabad-Lubavitch distinguish private religious expression from government speech.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Attribution, authorship, voluntariness, official duty, resource use, and supervisory pressure determine whether expression is private or governmental.
- **Other Governing Law:** Pickering v. Board of Education; Connick v. Myers; Rankin v. McPherson; Lee v. Weisman.

### B. Stone's approved position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the en banc judgment reversing and remanding the protected-practice and broad-directive/display claims.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** On remand, the statutory and constitutional claims shall remain separate. Relief may protect genuinely private religious practice and invalidate the religion-specific blanket directives while preserving neutral restrictions on use of employees, work time, public resources, harassment, coercive supervision, and official worship. The lower court may not treat every Title VII violation as automatically establishing a First Amendment violation or assume that the Religion Clauses can never press competing concerns.

#### Issue 1: Title VII religious accommodation

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the accommodation question on the developed record.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Polk County need not permit Brown to divert a secretary or other public resources to Bible-study work, open a workspace otherwise closed to all personal use, or use supervisory authority for official worship or religious counseling that objectively burdens subordinates. It must consider accommodation for genuinely voluntary employee-initiated prayer, private religious conversation, and personal religious items not used as official directives, because speculative polarization, generalized discomfort, or offense is not undue hardship.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Title VII requires reasonable accommodation of private religious practice unless the employer proves a real, more-than-de-minimis operational burden. It does not require diversion of public resources, official worship, coercive supervision, or unequal personal use of the workplace.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The County's legitimate operational limits can be enforced through neutral rules. The record does not support a categorical conclusion that every private religious conversation, voluntary prayer, or personal item creates the burden Hardison requires. The en banc remand is therefore proper without converting statutory accommodation into an unlimited workplace privilege.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not authorize favoritism, compulsory prayer, use of subordinates for private devotion, harassment, or religious personal use where comparable secular personal use is forbidden.

#### Issue 2: Free Exercise and religion-specific directives

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the facial breadth of the directives.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** The express order to cease anything that could be considered proselytizing, witnessing, or counseling and to remove every religious item substantially burdens exercise and is neither neutral nor confined to a demonstrated operational problem. Neutral limits on work time, employees, resources, coercion, and attributed official worship remain valid.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A public employer may enforce neutral limits on resources, duties, harassment, and coercion, but a religion-specific ban on all witnessing, counseling, proselytizing, and personal religious items is unconstitutional.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The challenged language suppresses the religious character of private expression rather than isolating misuse of public authority. Narrower neutral rules address the County's lawful interests without treating religious viewpoint itself as the workplace harm.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The ruling does not protect conduct that coerces subordinates, consumes public resources, or is delivered as an official directive.

#### Issue 3: Employee speech and Establishment attribution

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the governing attribution and speech rules but leaves application to particular disputed episodes for remand.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** The County may control expression delivered through Brown's supervisory duties as its own. Genuinely private citizen speech on public concern receives Pickering balancing against actual or reasonably forecast operational disruption, and the breadth of a prospective ban requires the stronger justification recognized in NTEU. Avoiding an objectively grounded Establishment violation is a legitimate reason for tailored restrictions, especially for supervisor-led prayer in mandatory meetings. Offense or abstract fear is insufficient.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Public-employer control turns on attribution and actual function. The County may regulate official, coercive, or resource-dependent religious expression but may not suppress genuinely private religious speech through an undifferentiated prospective ban.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Authorship, voluntariness, time, location, control, resource use, and supervisory pressure separate private expression from government worship. That source-specific inquiry preserves both constitutional neutrality and ordinary public-employer authority.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The decision does not require identical treatment of official worship and private conversation, and it does not make a statutory accommodation violation automatically dispositive of a constitutional claim.

# Young v. Harper

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** Young v. Harper
- **Citation or Docket:** 520 U.S. 143 (1997); No. 95-1598
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued merits case on review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit.
- **Simulated Event Date:** March 18, 1997.
- **Question Presented:** Whether Oklahoma may return a participant in its Preparole Conditional Supervision Program to prison without the notice and hearing required for revocation of parole.
- **Question Granted:** Whether preparole created a liberty interest equivalent to parole for purposes of Morrissey v. Brewer.
- **Legal Field:** Procedural due process, conditional release, and corrections.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Tenth Circuit reversed the denial of federal habeas relief and held that the preparole program was sufficiently like parole to require Morrissey procedures before reincarceration.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** Oklahoma officials ask the Court to reverse and treat preparole as an unprotected change in degree of confinement. Harper seeks affirmance and a constitutionally adequate revocation process.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Oklahoma used preparole to relieve overcrowding and released selected prisoners into the community before ordinary parole eligibility. Harper lived at his own residence, obtained employment, reported to a parole officer, and complied with conditions similar to parole for five uneventful months. After the Governor denied formal parole, an officer telephoned Harper and directed him to report immediately to prison, without an adversarial hearing.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** Harper used habeas because he challenged the legality of his reincarceration. The case concerns process for terminating community release, not an entitlement to formal parole or a right to remain free after a valid revocation finding.
- **Relevant Dates:** Released October 1990; returned March 14, 1991; Tenth Circuit decision August 23, 1995; argued December 9, 1996; decided March 18, 1997.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None.
- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** Official U.S. Reports, 520 U.S. 143; Oklahoma program rules; state and federal habeas records.

### Historical Supreme Court comparator

- **Historical Outcome and Vote:** Affirmed, 9-0.
- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Justice Thomas delivered the Opinion of the Court for a unanimous Court.
- **Historical Holding:** The preparole program was equivalent to parole as understood in Morrissey, and its participant could not be summarily reincarcerated.
- **Historical Authority:** Opinion of the Court.
- **Historical Sources:** Young v. Harper, 520 U.S. 143 (1997).

#### Issue 1: Protected liberty in preparole

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** Morrissey v. Brewer survives as governing pre-October 4, 1993 authority. Simulated Sandin v. Conner holds that state law and the significance of the deprivation must both be examined when a claimed prison liberty interest is asserted.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** State-created liberty in prison discipline requires a genuine legal constraint and a meaningful loss of liberty, but the more specific Morrissey rule governs termination of parole-like community release.
- **Other Governing Law:** The Fourteenth Amendment; Oklahoma Preparole Conditional Supervision Program rules; Greenholtz and related conditional-release authority.
- **Other Authority Used:** The historical Young opinion is persuasive on the program's operation.

### B. Stone's approved position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Affirm the Tenth Circuit's judgment.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** Harper is entitled to the process required before revocation of conditional community liberty. Oklahoma may continue custody only through a lawful revocation determination or another independent basis; the Court does not order unconditional release.

#### Issue 1: Protected liberty in preparole

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the due-process merits. The material features of Harper's release and summary return are undisputed.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether the actual freedom conferred by preparole, rather than its label, places the termination decision within Morrissey.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies Morrissey. Harper was released from the institution, maintained a home and employment, and lived under conditions materially equivalent to parole. The State could not extinguish that conditional liberty solely because the Governor later denied formal parole. It first had to give notice, an opportunity to contest the asserted basis for return, and a neutral decision under the procedures appropriate to revocation.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A State must provide Morrissey procedures before terminating a conditional-release program that places a prisoner in the community under parole-like supervision and permits an ordinary life outside the institution. Oklahoma's summary reincarceration of Harper violated due process.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The protected interest is not freedom from all custody; it is the substantial community liberty the State had already conferred. Reporting duties, travel restrictions, and behavioral conditions did not reduce preparole to institutional confinement. Oklahoma remained free to make the Governor's parole decision relevant, but it could not use the program label to avoid the process required before returning Harper from community life to prison.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not create an entitlement to initial release, govern short furloughs or work details, or prevent immediate temporary custody when a genuine safety emergency is followed by prompt effective process.

# Lambert v. Wicklund

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** Lambert v. Wicklund
- **Citation or Docket:** 520 U.S. 292 (1997); No. 96-858
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Submitted merits case on review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit.
- **Simulated Event Date:** March 31, 1997.
- **Question Presented:** Whether Montana's parental-notification law provides a constitutionally sufficient judicial bypass when a court must waive notice upon a finding that notice is not in the minor's best interests.
- **Question Granted:** Whether the best-interests language in Montana Code § 50-20-212 satisfies the constitutional requirement for an effective bypass.
- **Legal Field:** Personal liberty, abortion regulation, minors, and procedural due process.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Ninth Circuit affirmed an injunction, reasoning that the bypass had to ask whether the abortion itself, rather than nonnotification, was in the minor's best interests.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** The county attorney asks the Court to reverse the injunction. The providers seek affirmance and continued facial relief.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Montana required notice to one parent before a minor's abortion. The statute required a confidential and expedited bypass, appointed counsel on request, a decision within forty-eight hours with automatic waiver if the court failed to act, and waiver upon clear and convincing proof of maturity, abuse, or that notification was not in the petitioner's best interests. The challenge preceded enforcement and rested on the statutory text.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** The matter was decided without argument historically but is treated as a merits case under the supplied list. Only the best-interests objection resolved below is lawfully reached. Other penalty, emergency, and application questions remain outside the judgment.
- **Relevant Dates:** Ninth Circuit decision August 13, 1996; submitted without argument; decided March 31, 1997.
- **Companion or Consolidated Matters:** None.
- **Known Participation Matter:** None identified.
- **Public Sources:** Official U.S. Reports, 520 U.S. 292; Mont. Code Ann. § 50-20-212 (1995); Wicklund v. Salvagni, 93 F.3d 567 (9th Cir. 1996).

- **Historical Outcome and Vote:** Reversed after grant of certiorari, per curiam.
- **Historical Opinion Structure:** Per curiam opinion; Justice Stevens concurred in the judgment, joined by Justices Ginsburg and Breyer.
- **Historical Holding:** The notification-not-in-best-interests formulation was materially equivalent to the best-interests bypass approved in Akron and satisfied Bellotti.

#### Issue 1: Best-interests bypass for parental notification

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** Planned Parenthood v. Miller, 63 F.3d 1452 (simulated April 15, 1996), holds that unavoidable parental notice requires an independent, confidential, and expeditious decisionmaker capable of authorizing abortion without notice under maturity and best-interests criteria.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Facial review and parental notification supplies the operative rule. Fargo Women's Health Organization v. Schafer separately governs facial denominator, cumulative operation, and usable emergency routes when those questions are presented.
- **Other Governing Law:** The Fourteenth Amendment; Bellotti v. Baird; Ohio v. Akron Center for Reproductive Health; Mont. Code Ann. § 50-20-212.
- **Other Authority Used:** The historical Lambert opinion is persuasive on the semantic equivalence of the Montana and Ohio provisions.

### B. Stone's approved position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse and remand.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** Vacate the injunction insofar as it rests on the asserted defect in the best-interests formulation. Remand for proceedings on any preserved issue not resolved by this judgment.

#### Issue 1: Best-interests bypass for parental notification

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches only whether the stated best-interests criterion is constitutionally sufficient. The statute's confidentiality, timing, and maturity routes are undisputed for this issue.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether a direction to waive notice when notification is not in the minor's best interests authorizes the same constitutionally required decision as a direction to waive when abortion without notice is in her best interests.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies simulated Planned Parenthood v. Miller. The required inquiry is practical: can the independent decisionmaker authorize the abortion without notice when involving the parent would be contrary to the minor's best interests? Montana's text says yes. Read in context, it does not authorize a court to deny waiver after finding that proceeding without notice is best for the minor.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A confidential and expeditious bypass satisfies the best-interests component of the parental-notification rule when it requires waiver upon a finding that notification is not in the minor's best interests. Montana's formulation meets that requirement.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The constitutional concern is not a verbal formula but whether the minor can obtain an independent decision that notice should not occur. Montana supplies that authority, adds a maturity route, permits an abuse finding, seals the proceeding, appoints counsel, and grants relief automatically if the court misses the deadline. The Ninth Circuit's distinction would invalidate a functionally adequate route based on wording that changes no legal outcome.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not decide whether clear-and-convincing proof is valid in every application, whether the emergency provisions are adequate, or whether implementation makes the nominally expedited route unavailable.

# Robinson v. City of Edmond

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

#### Issue 1: Findings and standard for terminating the foster-care decree

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the governing standard and adequacy of findings. He does not decide whether New Mexico has substantially complied.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether a court can dissolve a decree requiring sustained systemic performance when the adopted report states ultimate conclusions without the subsidiary facts and reasoning needed to test material compliance.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies Rule 52 and the decree's text. Substantial compliance means performance that satisfies the decree's essential purposes despite only immaterial deviations. Because the decree specifies fair, reasonable, and timely decisionmaking and durable procedures for permanent placement, the court must address each essential objective, explain the significance of deviations from enumerated criteria, state the methodology used to weigh system data and case evidence, and make enough subsidiary findings for review. Termination is not punishment or reward; it follows when the agreed legal condition is proved.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A federal court may dissolve this institutional consent decree only on reviewable findings that the Department maintained substantial and continuous compliance with the decree's essential purposes for twelve consecutive months. Conclusory findings that do not explain material deviations, methodology, or subsidiary facts do not satisfy Rule 52(a).
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The report acknowledged serious low-compliance figures and cases in which children languished, yet often declared substantial compliance without explaining why the shortfall was immaterial. It did not identify how unreliable data were treated or how the essential purposes of fairness, timeliness, and durable permanent-placement procedures were satisfied. Appellate judges may not reconstruct those missing findings from a voluminous record. Retaining the decree pending lawful findings respects both state administration and the final judgment.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not require perfect compliance, convert every numerical target into an indispensable element, authorize indefinite federal control after the termination condition is met, or permit the decree to expand beyond its terms.
