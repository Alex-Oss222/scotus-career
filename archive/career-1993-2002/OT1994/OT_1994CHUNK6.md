## City of Edmonds v. Oxford House, Inc.

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** City of Edmonds v. Oxford House, Inc.
- **Citation or Docket:** 514 U.S. 725; No. 94-23
- **October Term:** 1994
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Merits case; argued or submitted for decision on the simulated historical calendar.
- **Simulated Event Date:** May 15, 1995
- **Question Presented:** Whether Edmonds’s single-family zoning definition is a “maximum occupancy restriction” exempt from the Fair Housing Act.
- **Question Granted:** Whether Edmonds’s single-family zoning definition is a “maximum occupancy restriction” exempt from the Fair Housing Act.
- **Legal Field:** Fair housing — occupancy exemption and family-composition zoning
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** United States Court Of Appeals For The Ninth Circuit. The District Court held that the City’s zoning code rule defining “family,” ECDC § 21.30.010, is exempt from the FHA under 42 U. S. C. § 3607(b)(1) as a “reasonable . . . restrictio[n] regarding the maximum number of occupants permitted to occupy a dwelling.” The Court of Appeals reversed, holding § 3607(b)(1)’s absolute exemption inapplicable.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** Petitioner seeks reversal or vacatur of the judgment below and remand for the relief authorized by the asserted rule.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Respondent Oxford House operates a group home in Edmonds, Washington, for 10 to 12 adults recovering from alcoholism and drug addiction in a neighborhood zoned for single-family residences. Petitioner City of Edmonds (City) issued citations to the owner and a resident of the house, charging violation of the City’s zoning code. The code provides that the occupants of single-family dwelling units must compose a “family,” and defines family as “persons [without regard to number] related by genetics, adoption, or marriage, or a group of five or fewer [unrelated] persons.” Edmonds Community Development Code (ECDC) § 21.30.010.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** Stone finds no jurisdictional, preservation, or vehicle bar and reaches the question presented.

### B. Stone's approved position

#### Issue 1: Merits

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone finds no jurisdictional, preservation, or vehicle bar and reaches the question presented.

- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not invalidate single-family zoning, decide liability, or prevent genuine health-and-safety occupancy limits applied evenhandedly.

## Day v. Holahan

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** Day v. Holahan
- **Citation or Docket:** 34 F.3d 1356 (8th Cir. 1994); controlled Supreme Court grant, docket number not supplied

- **Question Presented:** Whether Minnesota may increase an opposing candidate’s spending limit and public-financing benefits when an independent speaker’s expenditures exceed a statutory threshold, without impermissibly burdening independent political speech and association.
- **Question Granted:** Whether Minnesota may increase an opposing candidate’s spending limit and public-financing benefits when an independent speaker’s expenditures exceed a statutory threshold, without impermissibly burdening independent political speech and association.
- **Legal Field:** First Amendment, campaign finance, independent expenditures, and public financing
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit affirmed in part and reversed in part in consolidated challenges to Minnesota’s campaign-finance provisions, including invalidation of the provisions that increased a candidate’s spending limit and public subsidy in response to independent expenditures.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** Minnesota officials seek reversal of the judgment invalidating the responsive spending-limit and public-financing provisions. The challengers seek affirmance of that component and preservation of any other relief obtained below.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Minnesota limited candidate campaign expenditures for candidates accepting public financing. When an independent expenditure opposed a candidate or supported that candidate’s opponent above the statutory threshold, the challenged provisions increased the affected candidate’s spending limit and made additional public funds available, including a payment tied to a portion of the independent expenditure. The independent speaker remained legally free to spend and was not fined, taxed, compelled to disclose additional information by the trigger itself, or subjected to a lower expenditure ceiling.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** The consolidated judgment contains multiple campaign-finance rulings. Stone reaches only the granted challenge to the independent-expenditure trigger and leaves unrelated judgment components undisturbed. The facial record permits resolution of the trigger’s legal operation.

### B. Stone's approved position

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** REVERSE the Eighth Circuit only insofar as it invalidated Minnesota’s independent-expenditure-triggered increase in the affected candidate’s spending limit and public-financing benefits, and REMAND. Leave all other judgment components outside the granted question undisturbed.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** The Eighth Circuit shall reinstate the challenged responsive-financing provisions against the facial First Amendment theory resolved here and conduct any remaining proceedings consistent with this holding. No relief may impose a restriction, tax, fee, liability, or additional reporting duty on the independent speaker that the enacted provisions themselves do not impose.

#### Issue 1: Independent expenditures and responsive candidate financing

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the facial First Amendment question and treats the independent speaker’s freedom to spend as distinct from the government’s treatment of the opposing candidate.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** The trigger does not cap, tax, penalize, delay, license, or attach liability to the independent expenditure. It changes only the resources and voluntary spending ceiling available to another candidate. The First Amendment protects the independent speaker against governmental suppression; it does not include a right to prevent an opponent from receiving additional lawful resources or public support in response.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A public-financing system does not burden an independent expenditure merely because that expenditure triggers additional public funds or a higher voluntary spending limit for the candidate opposed, where the independent speaker remains legally free to spend and incurs no restriction, charge, or additional legal disability.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The Eighth Circuit treated the political consequence of stimulating responsive speech as a legal penalty. That collapses a competitor’s increased capacity to answer into government suppression of the original message. Minnesota has chosen to broaden, rather than ration, the quantity of campaign expression. The speaker may dislike that consequence, but the Constitution does not guarantee that protected advocacy will remain unanswered or retain the same strategic value after others speak.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone reserves a mechanism that directly reduces the independent speaker’s rights, imposes a charge or reporting obligation because of the speech, makes public funds contingent on the speaker’s identity or viewpoint, or provides such disproportionate government support that the system becomes a device for suppressing disfavored advocacy.

#### Issue 2: Candidate spending limits within a voluntary public-financing system

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the issue only as necessary to sustain the triggered increase in the voluntarily accepted limit.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** A candidate who voluntarily accepts public financing may be subject to a spending ceiling and may receive a legislatively defined increase when specified electoral conditions occur. Increasing that ceiling enlarges the candidate’s freedom to speak and does not create a constitutional injury to the candidate or the independent spender.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Within a voluntary public-financing program, government may increase a participating candidate’s spending limit in response to qualifying independent expenditures, provided the program does not coerce participation or directly burden the independent speaker.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The challenged provision relaxes an existing contractual or statutory restraint on the publicly financed candidate. It does not impose a new restraint on anyone. The government’s use of public resources must remain viewpoint neutral, but a neutral trigger based on the amount and electoral direction of an expenditure is not a judgment that its message is unlawful.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not decide a public-financing system in which the nominally voluntary choice is practically coercive or the trigger discriminates among speakers by viewpoint, association, or protected status.

## Kelley v. Board of Trustees of the University of Illinois

#### Issue 2: Report-and-wait, bicameralism, and presentment

- **Stone's Legal Position:** A report sent to Congress is constitutional. An automatic fixed waiting period that expires without further approval ordinarily is constitutional. Congressional debate during that period is constitutional, and Congress may pass a new law through bicameralism and presentment. The line is crossed when committee approval is required before action becomes effective, committee disapproval prevents effectiveness, or a body controlled by Congress can legally determine whether the action is accelerated, suspended, or subjected to additional delay. The revised Board’s discretionary trigger crosses that line.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Congress may require reporting and an automatic, neutral waiting period, but it may not make an operational decision’s legal effectiveness depend on approval, disapproval, discretionary delay, or other control exercised outside bicameralism and presentment.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The mechanism is not neutral as to every Authority decision. The Board decides whether the action proceeds, is revised to satisfy its recommendation, or is consigned to a lengthy congressional-review period. Chadha does not permit Congress to obtain through a conditional review device the legal control that must be exercised through legislation.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone reserves a fixed report-and-wait provision under which the action becomes effective automatically at the end of the enacted period unless Congress passes a new law.

#### Issue 3: Severability and continuity of airport operations

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches remedy because the constitutional ruling does not require dismantling a functioning airport authority.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** The Board provisions are a supervisory overlay rather than the essential existence of the Authority, lease, or compact. Removing them leaves an administrable entity capable of operating the airports under valid law. Severance therefore removes the unconstitutional control while preserving lawful administration.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The unconstitutional Board-of-Review mechanism may be severed while the Metropolitan Washington Airports Authority continues to operate under the remaining valid legal framework.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Invalidating every airport action or terminating the transfer would be broader than the proven defect. The lawful remedy removes the Board’s operative control and leaves ordinary airport administration in place. Any specific past act whose validity depends on the Board can be addressed under the governing remedial law on an adequate record.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not validate an action whose only legal authorization came from the unconstitutional Board and does not decide transaction-specific reliance questions unnecessary to the facial judgment.
