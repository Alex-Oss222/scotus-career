  Equal protection does not require identical treatment of physically different ballots or different voting technologies. It requires materially equivalent legal treatment of materially comparable ballots. A lawful statewide framework may therefore recognize genuine differences among ballot technologies and ballot conditions, provided those distinctions are stated in advance, tied to objective differences, and applied consistently throughout the statewide proceeding.

  The standards must be public before their application. They must provide election officials with intelligible criteria for determining voter intent, furnish a procedure for resolving disputed ballots, permit meaningful observation and objection by the parties, create a reviewable record, and contain a statewide mechanism for correcting inconsistent applications. Standards may not be changed retrospectively because emerging results make another formulation advantageous.

  The Court does not select the ballot universe. It does not determine whether Florida must examine undervotes, overvotes, particular counties, particular machines, or particular categories of markings. Those are matters of Florida law to be resolved by the institutions authorized to make and administer that law.

  Nor does this Court determine whether Florida can complete the process before any particular federal date. December 12 gives Florida the opportunity to obtain the protection Congress attached to the safe harbor. It is not a judicial warrant to terminate a recount that Florida law otherwise permits. If Florida completes a lawful determination outside the safe harbor, the legal consequences of that timing arise through the constitutional and statutory process for appointment, transmission, and counting of electoral votes. This Court does not manufacture finality by selecting the winning slate.

* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The December 12 safe-harbor date does not itself require termination of an otherwise lawful state presidential-election contest. When a State conducts a single statewide adjudicative recount, however, equal protection requires sufficiently definite, public, prospective, and reviewable statewide standards to ensure materially equivalent treatment of materially comparable ballots. A recount conducted without those safeguards must be vacated and may resume under a constitutionally adequate framework if state law permits.

* **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Florida chose to conduct a statewide judicial recount for one statewide office. Once the State undertook that adjudicative process, the legal value assigned to a materially comparable ballot could not turn upon the county in which the voter happened to reside, the counting team that happened to inspect the ballot, or a materially different standard adopted during the recount without statewide reconciliation.

  The supplied record identifies materially varying county and counting-team standards, differing treatment of comparable ballots, and no adequate statewide mechanism for reconciling those differences. Those deficiencies violate equal protection because they permit the same statewide adjudication to assign materially different legal consequences to materially comparable evidence.

  The violation is curable. The Constitution does not require a mechanical recount, prohibit examination of voter intent, or demand one physical rule for every voting technology. It requires a legal framework adequate to make the statewide adjudication intelligible and evenhanded. Written and publicly announced standards, fixed before adjudication and accompanied by statewide review, can satisfy that obligation while leaving the substance of Florida election law to Florida.

  Section 5 does not alter that remedy. Its function is to provide a State with a condition under which its final determination receives the federal treatment Congress prescribed. The loss of that protection is a consequence of missing the statutory date; it is not an independent federal command that the State abandon its election process on that date.

  Article II likewise does not require this Court to administer the recount. The Constitution assigns the State the authority to determine the manner of appointing its electors. That allocation counsels restraint here. The State’s politically accountable lawmaking institutions must supply a sufficiently definite legal framework; its election officials and courts may administer and adjudicate within that framework. The role of this Court is limited to identifying the federal constitutional defect and requiring its correction.

* **Remedy and Remand Instruction:** Vacate the defective recount directions and remand immediately. The existing federal stay shall not operate as a final termination of the recount. If Florida law continues to authorize a recount, Florida may proceed under statewide standards established before further adjudicative counting. Those standards must be written, public, sufficiently objective, consistently applicable to materially comparable ballots, and capable of meaningful review. Florida remains responsible for determining the lawful ballot universe, treatment of different voting technologies, recount procedures, and any deadlines arising from Florida law. December 12 is not imposed as a federal termination date.

* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding creates no general federal right to a recount; does not constitutionalize every variation in decentralized election administration; does not require identical treatment of materially different ballots; establishes no federally preferred test for dimples, hanging chads, optical marks, overvotes, or undervotes; selects no ballot universe, technology, deadline, slate, or winner; does not compel the Florida Legislature to adopt any particular substantive rule; and does not decide how Congress must treat Florida electoral votes if the State ultimately fails to obtain § 5 safe-harbor protection. The broader Article II theory remains reserved except for the principle that federal courts should not themselves replace Florida’s constitutionally assigned lawmaking process with a federal ballot-counting code.
