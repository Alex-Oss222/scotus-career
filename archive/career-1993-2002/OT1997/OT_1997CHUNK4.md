# United States v. Ramirez

## Section I: Case, record, and procedural posture

- **Case Name:** United States v. Ramirez

- **October Term:** 1997 simulation term.

- **Simulated Event Date:** March 4, 1998
- **Question Presented:** Whether officers executing a warrant must satisfy a heightened no-knock standard whenever entry causes property damage.
- **Question Granted:** Whether officers executing a warrant must satisfy a heightened no-knock standard whenever entry causes property damage.
- **Legal Field:** Search and seizure, warrant execution and knock-and-announce.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Ninth Circuit affirmed suppression because officers broke a garage window during a no-knock entry without showing an exigency greater than that required for a nondestructive entry.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** The United States seeks reversal and remand for ordinary Fourth Amendment analysis.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Deputy marshals had a valid warrant to search Ramirez’s home for an escaped violent prisoner believed armed. #### Issue 1: Prior aggravated-felony conviction as an offense element

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** *United States v. Gaudin* requires the jury to determine every fact that is an element of the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *United States v. Watts* permits judicial sentencing factfinding only when the resulting punishment remains within the range legally authorized by the conviction and expressly does not permit sentencing to remove an offense element from the jury or exceed the conviction-authorized punishment. *Custis v. United States* governs collateral attack on an existing predicate conviction at sentencing; it does not decide whether a statutory-maximum-increasing recidivist fact must be charged as part of the federal offense. *Old Chief v. United States* confirms that a prior-conviction element can be proved through an admission or stipulation without exposing the jury to unnecessary prejudicial detail.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Jury determination of materiality under *Gaudin* supplies the current element-allocation rule. #### Issue 1: Prior aggravated-felony conviction as an offense element

- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone would hold that § 1326(b)(2) defines an aggravated form of unlawful reentry rather than a mere sentencing factor. A fact that changes the maximum punishment legally authorized by the charged offense from two years to twenty years performs the work of an element and must be charged in the indictment and either admitted in a plea to that charged offense or proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. The legislature's placement or label cannot transfer that maximum-defining fact entirely to the sentencing judge. Prior convictions may be established efficiently through judicial records, admissions, or stipulations, as *Old Chief* illustrates, but evidentiary convenience does not alter element status. *Custis* concerns whether an existing conviction may be collaterally attacked at sentencing and therefore does not answer the charging question. *Watts* reinforces the boundary because judicial factfinding remains permissible only within the punishment already authorized by the conviction.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** When § 1326(b)(2)'s prior aggravated-felony conviction is necessary to authorize punishment above § 1326(a)'s two-year maximum, that prior conviction is an element of the aggravated unlawful-reentry offense. Subsection (b)(2) does not merely guide selection of a sentence within that ceiling; it authorizes a tenfold increase to twenty years based on an additional historical fact. The constitutional distinction between elements and ordinary sentencing facts turns on legal effect, not drafting placement. *Gaudin* protects the jury's authority over elements, while *Watts* preserves judicial sentencing factfinding only inside the conviction-authorized range. Treating the aggravated-felony fact as a sentencing factor would allow a plea to the two-year offense to support punishment Congress reserved for a materially aggravated offense that the indictment never charged. Almendarez-Torres admitted the prior convictions, so their existence is not disputed, but he did not plead guilty to an indictment containing that maximum-defining element. The eighty-five-month sentence therefore exceeds the punishment authorized by the conviction before the Court.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** This holding does not prevent courts from considering prior convictions when selecting a sentence within an independently authorized range, permit collateral attack beyond *Custis*, require prejudicial details of a prior conviction to be placed before a jury when an admission or stipulation establishes the element, or decide sentencing facts that do not increase the statutory maximum.
