#### Controlling simulated holdings

**TrafFix Devices, Inc. v. Marketing Displays, Inc., 532 U.S. 23 (2001); Functionality shown by an expired utility patent**

> A utility patent that claims and explains a product feature is strong evidence that the feature is functional. When the patent materials establish that the feature is essential to the product’s use or purpose, the feature cannot acquire trade-dress protection merely because alternative designs exist or consumers associate it with one producer.

**Recorded force:** This proposition remains controlling as stated through the synchronized cutoff.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Qualitex Co. v. Jacobson Products Co.*: clarified; alternative-design evidence does not defeat functionality when the asserted feature is essential to the article’s use or purpose.
*Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Samara Brothers, Inc.*: followed; product-design protection remains dependent on nonfunctionality as well as any required showing of source significance.

**Effective:** March 20, 2001. **Source:** `HOLDINGS(20260905-075915).md`, lines 17827–17846, “TrafFix Devices, Inc. v. Marketing Displays, Inc., 532 U.S. 23 (2001)”.

**Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Samara Brothers, Inc., 529 U.S. 205 (2000); Whether product design can be inherently distinctive trade dress protectable under § 43(a) of the Lanham Act without proof of secondary meaning**

> Product design is protectable under § 43(a) only upon proof that its primary significance to consumers is source identification. The claimant must separately establish nonfunctionality, likely confusion, and the remaining statutory elements.

**Recorded force:** This proposition remains controlling as stated through the synchronized cutoff.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc.*: clarified; its recognition of inherently distinctive trade dress does not dispense with secondary meaning for product design.

*Inwood Laboratories, Inc. v. Ives Laboratories, Inc.*: applied; secondary meaning asks whether the feature’s primary significance identifies source rather than the product itself.

**Effective:** March 22, 2000. **Source:** `HOLDINGS(20260905-075915).md`, lines 17847–17869, “Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Samara Brothers, Inc., 529 U.S. 205 (2000)”.

**Qualitex Co. v. Jacobson Products Co., 514 U.S. 159; Color as a protectable trademark**

#### Current reusable doctrine

**Color as a protectable trademark**

**Current rule:** The Lanham Act permits a color, standing alone, to serve as a trademark when the color has acquired source-identifying significance and is not functional. A claimant seeking protection for color alone must establish that consumers understand the color to identify the source of the goods. Protection is unavailable when the color is essential to the product’s use or purpose, affects cost or quality, or is competitively necessary. Ordinary likelihood-of-confusion requirements remain applicable.

**Exceptions and limits:** The decision does not protect color in the abstract, functional or competitively necessary colors, or colors lacking secondary meaning. It does not eliminate ordinary proof of likely confusion.

**Same-decision proof and retrospective relief for a discrete governmental decision**

**Current rule:** Conclusive proof that the government would have made the same discrete decision through a lawful process defeats retrospective § 1983 liability for that decision, including compensatory and nominal damages.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule does not validate the challenged policy, establish that an impermissible classification was used, decide remaining statutory claims, or bar a separately live claim that ongoing unequal competition causes a distinct injury supporting prospective relief.

**Race-determinative governmental benefits**

**Current rule:** Strict scrutiny governs when race determines or effectively determines who receives a particular governmental contract, payment, school seat, transfer opportunity, or comparable discrete benefit. Government must establish a compelling interest and narrow tailoring. The inquiry includes the identified remedial predicate, necessity, actual operation and duration, workable race-neutral or less restrictive alternatives, and whether the racial rule reaches materially beyond the demonstrated injury. A K–12 school system not remedying its own adjudicated constitutional violation may not make an identified student’s race determinative of access to a specific school, program, or transfer unless that classification satisfies strict scrutiny.

**Exceptions and limits:** A genuinely inclusionary structural measure that does not allocate an identified benefit by race is governed by the separate nonexclusive-access rule. Meaningful integration and prevention of serious racial isolation are distinct from racial balance, but proportional racial numbers cannot serve as an independent end or as the decisional mechanism for an identified individual benefit.

**Source:** `STANDARDS_AND_TESTS(20260905-075915).md`, lines 1532–1550, “Race-determinative governmental benefits”.
