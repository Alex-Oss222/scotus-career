# Justice Stone: Second Amendment build, OT1994 through OT2015

**Simulation handoff. Proposed positions, not predetermined Court holdings.**

## Scope and how to use this build

This build runs from *United States v. Lopez* in OT1994 through OT2015 and now deliberately develops a fuller mature doctrine rather than stopping at the home-defense core. It remains a stable individual-right doctrine, not a maximal all-weapons presumption: ordinary arms and lawful self-defense receive strong protection, while demonstrated dangerous persons, dangerous and unusual weapons, specially regulated weapon categories, objective registration, classification-changing modifications, sensitive places, and genuine safety administration remain regulable. The planning instruction still rejects adding a case merely because an October Term otherwise lacks a gun decision; the added vehicles below perform specific doctrinal work that the project now affirmatively wants.

**Existing record note.** The formal simulation record presently reaches the early-2000s portion of this project; the later vehicles in this file are roadmap positions, not silently imported holdings. *Heller I*, *Heller II*, *Ezell*, *Moore*, *Jackson*, *Bonidy*, and *Heller III* therefore state the disposition and reasoning Stone should take if and when those vehicles are selected. There is one inherited record issue worth preserving rather than silently repairing: the controlling handoff instruction treats the Second Amendment as an individual and incorporated right by the end of OT1999, while the earlier *Love* and *Hale* writings did not themselves produce a clean controlling incorporation holding. The roadmap treats incorporation as an operational simulation premise rather than taking *McDonald* solely to fix recordkeeping.

**Status tags.** "Essential" means the case is load-bearing for the architecture now requested. "Recommended" means it materially improves the doctrine and should ordinarily be taken if the vehicle remains clean. "Optional" remains a genuine refinement rather than filler. "Deliberate silence" means Stone should not manufacture a gun case merely to occupy an October Term.

## The standards Stone is building

**Status note.** These standards describe Stone's mature target architecture. A proposition is not controlling simulation law merely because it appears here; before the case that supplies the relevant vehicle, it is a planned position. The case sections below identify what has actually been established, what remains proposed, and what should remain open.

### S1. The sequence for every Second Amendment case

Stone does not adopt a universal scrutiny tier. For every case he asks, in order: Who is claiming the right? What acquisition, possession, carriage, use, transfer, component, or modification is regulated? What arm or device is involved? What lawful purpose is asserted? Where does the conduct occur? Does an established dangerous-person, weapon-category, sensitive-place, commercial, or safety branch apply? What process supports any disqualification? What does the rule do in practical operation? Does it administer the right or destroy the protected application? If federal law is involved, what enumerated power independently supports it? What remedy is necessary? What questions can honestly be left undecided?

History supplies regulatory principles and boundaries, not a demand for an exact historical twin and not a license to reason at unlimited generality. A court must identify the concrete principle that made the earlier regulation permissible and compare the modern rule's reason and burden.

### S2. The ordinary right-holder

The right belongs to an individual natural person. The presumptive claimant is an adult not subject to a constitutionally sufficient disqualification who seeks an ordinary arm for a lawful purpose. "Law-abiding," "responsible," and "dangerous" are descriptive terms, not blank checks. Government must identify the legal predicate that removes a claimant from the ordinary category.

Arrest, accusation, unpopular association, administrative suspicion, an untested report, diagnosis alone, or a generalized risk score does not itself extinguish the right.

### S3. Protected purposes and the home-defense core

Immediate lawful self-defense is central, especially in the home, but the constitutional field also includes peaceable possession, lawful acquisition, ordinary maintenance, transportation, training, hunting, sporting use, and other traditionally lawful uses.

Government may not prohibit every ordinary handgun, force every lawful home firearm to remain continuously unusable, make lawful acquisition practically impossible, use licensing as a discretionary veto, or eliminate ordinary protected arms one category at a time while pointing to substitutes.

The Amendment protects possession, readiness, and carriage; it does not create a substantive license to assault, threaten, fire recklessly, trespass while armed after lawful exclusion, resist lawful arrest, or use force when ordinary self-defense law forbids it.

### S4. Ordinary arms

Ordinary handguns are protected. Ordinary semiautomatic rifles and shotguns do not become constitutionally equivalent to machineguns because of appearance, military ancestry, detachable magazines, pistol grips, adjustable stocks, or a legislative label.

Classification turns on operation and function: whether the arm fires one round per trigger activation or continuously; whether its destructive effect materially differs from ordinary individual firearms; whether it is commonly possessed or ordinarily suited to lawful civilian use; whether the challenged features materially alter operation rather than appearance; and whether government itself suppressed possession and then relies on that suppression to prove uncommonness.

Government may not prohibit an ordinary protected arm merely because another protected arm remains available.

### S5. Dangerous and unusual weapons; specially regulated categories

The right does not protect every object that can linguistically be called an arm. Under *Hale* and *Rybar*, post-1986 civilian fully automatic machineguns may be prohibited. Weapons whose ordinary function is explosive or area-destructive rather than ordinary individual defense may likewise fall outside the core or be subjected to exceptional regulation.

Between ordinary arms and prohibitable weapons is a specially regulated tier. Government may require enhanced registration, transfer controls, identifying information, secure transport, and other objective conditions for categories defined by material functional characteristics. Suppressors or silencers, short-barreled rifle or shotgun configurations, destructive-device categories, and comparable historically distinct classes may be regulated in this manner without automatically treating every item in the tier as constitutionally unprotected.

"Dangerous" alone is insufficient because every firearm is dangerous. "Unusual" alone is insufficient because government cannot manufacture constitutional authority by first suppressing an arm and then citing the suppression as proof of unusualness.

### S6. Modifications, accessories, and regulated components

Ordinary maintenance, repair, replacement of ordinary components, sights, lights, grips, slings, cosmetic changes, and comparable accessories do not become separate constitutional events merely because they alter a firearm.

A different rule applies when a modification materially changes the legal or functional category of the weapon. Government may require advance approval or registration when a modification creates a specially regulated short-barreled configuration, adds a separately regulated suppressor, converts an ordinary firearm into a prohibited automatic category, incorporates a separately regulated destructive device, or otherwise changes the weapon into a category that could lawfully have been specially regulated if manufactured that way originally.

The registration may attach to the regulated component, the resulting weapon configuration, or both when that is reasonably necessary to identify the regulated item. The owner may be required to update the host firearm's record after a material classification change. Government may not require separate registration of every ordinary replacement part or accessory merely because it can attach to a firearm.

Any modification regime must be objective, reasonably prompt, accessible, and reviewable. Administrators may not possess open-ended authority to deny a modification that satisfies the governing legal conditions.

### S7. Person-based disqualification: governing principle

The Second Amendment permits defined disqualification of persons whose adjudicated conduct or constitutionally sufficient proceedings establish a materially heightened danger of firearm misuse. The strength and duration of the disability must track the predicate that justifies it.

Government may regulate demonstrated dangerousness; it may not convert every criminal label, diagnosis, status, or accusation into permanent civil death for the right.

### S8. Violent convictions and restoration

A constitutionally adequate criminal conviction may support durable disarmament when the offense itself establishes intentional violence, attempted violence, a credible threat of serious physical injury, armed criminal conduct, stalking accompanied by qualifying threats, misuse of a deadly weapon, or comparable demonstrated danger.

The criminal adjudication supplies process and proof beyond a reasonable doubt, so government need not relitigate present dangerousness at periodic intervals merely to enforce the disability while the conviction and its legally prescribed consequences remain operative.

"Durable" does not necessarily mean constitutionally irreversible for life. A pardon, expungement, set-aside, successful collateral attack, restoration statute, or other legal mechanism that removes the predicate's force matters. A future as-applied challenge involving a remote conviction, completed sentence, long period of lawful conduct, and no other danger evidence remains open. A legislature may not make the word "felony" alone do all the constitutional work for a nonviolent regulatory offense.

### S9. Civil protective orders and emergency risk orders

A civil protective order may support temporary disarmament when the restrained person received actual notice, had a meaningful opportunity to participate, and a neutral judicial officer entered a current danger-linked order after constitutionally adequate process. The disability ends when the qualifying order ends unless another sufficient predicate exists.

Emergency ex parte authority may exist for a genuine immediate threat, but it must remain temporary. A sworn particularized showing to a neutral judicial officer, prompt adversarial review, a heightened burden for any continuing order, fixed duration, meaningful renewal standards, and prompt return or lawful transfer of firearms when the predicate ends are central safeguards.

A generic divorce injunction, routine mutual civility order, accusation alone, expired order, or administrative notation does not automatically satisfy this branch.

### S10. Mental-health and substance-use predicates

A psychiatric diagnosis, voluntary treatment, medication, disability status, or counseling does not by itself extinguish the right. Government may impose a temporary or durable disability following a constitutionally adequate adjudication or qualifying commitment that establishes serious danger to self or others. The duration of the restriction and access to restoration must track the dangerousness predicate.

Government may prohibit carrying or using firearms while presently intoxicated or impaired to a degree that materially compromises safe handling. Addiction or substance use may support a disability when adjudicated conduct or reliable individualized evidence establishes concrete danger. Mere status or regular use, standing alone, should not automatically create permanent disarmament.

### S11. Licensing and registration

Objective, timely, reviewable, and nonprohibitory licensing and registration may coexist with the right. Reasonable administration may include identity verification, background checks, one-time fingerprints or photographs when genuinely useful, accessible training, registration of ordinary firearms, and more detailed registration of specially regulated arms or classification-changing modifications.

For ordinary firearms, registration should ordinarily be simple, inexpensive, ministerial, and stable. Government may require updates for change of address, transfer, loss, theft, or a material classification-changing modification rather than forcing repeated re-registration of the same unchanged person and firearm without demonstrated need.

Licensing fails when its actual operation produces arbitrary denial, open-ended discretion, extraordinary-need requirements, indefinite delay, cumulative unaffordable cost, impossible conditions, repeated unsupported demands, or practical prohibition.

### S12. Serial numbers and traceability

Government may require commercially manufactured firearms and separately regulated weapons or components to bear durable identifying information reasonably connected to lawful tracing, transfer administration, and enforcement of valid ownership restrictions. There is no constitutional right to deliberately obliterate or falsify a required identifying mark.

Personal manufacture for lawful personal use is not automatically outside the Second Amendment. Government may nevertheless require objective serialization or registration of a completed personally manufactured firearm when it enters commerce or when it falls within a specially regulated category, provided lawful compliance is practically available.

A technical paperwork error is not automatically equivalent to violent misuse. Knowledge, notice, opportunity to cure, and the seriousness of the violation matter when criminal penalties attach to administrative requirements.

### S13. Acquisition, dealers, transfers, and waiting periods

A meaningful right to keep arms includes practical lawful acquisition. Government may license dealers, require commercial recordkeeping and background checks, prohibit sales to legally disqualified persons, punish straw purchasing and trafficking, and require accessible screening for private transfers.

A short, objective waiting period may be permissible when genuinely connected to screening or a defined safety function. Indefinite delay, repetitive waiting periods without a current function, or administration designed to defeat otherwise lawful acquisition is not.

Commercial regulation becomes unconstitutional when government nominally permits possession while eliminating every practical lawful channel through which an ordinary qualified person can acquire a protected arm or ordinary ammunition.

### S14. Safe storage and unauthorized access

Government may require reasonable precautions against unauthorized access by children, prohibited persons, or others when a firearm is unattended.

The rule must preserve meaningful defensive access by the lawful possessor. Government may not require the only firearm available for home defense to remain continuously disabled while the lawful possessor is present and reasonably relying on it for immediate defense.

Negligent or reckless storage can be regulated without converting every unauthorized access into strict criminal liability regardless of reasonable precautions.

### S15. Training and ranges

Government may require basic, genuinely accessible firearms-safety training as an objective condition of public carriage or another regulated activity. It may regulate instructor qualifications, range location, construction, ventilation, lead containment, noise, hours, storage, security, and ordinary business operation.

Government may not require live-fire training while simultaneously prohibiting every practical means of obtaining it. The Amendment does not require government-financed instruction, a preferred range, advanced tactical training, or recreational shooting on demand.

### S16. Ammunition and feeding devices

The right to possess an operable firearm necessarily includes practical access to ordinary ammunition suitable for that firearm. Government may regulate particularly hazardous ammunition, commercial sale, storage, and unlawful use, but it may not eliminate every practical source of ordinary ammunition for protected arms.

Magazines integral to the ordinary operation of protected semiautomatic firearms fall within the constitutional field. The Constitution contains no numerical magazine-capacity limit. Classification must use the record: ordinary factory configurations, prevalence in lawful possession, ordinary lawful use, effect on operation, the characteristics of unusually large drums or other materially distinct feeding devices, and the safety mechanism asserted by government.

Ordinary magazines cannot be banned merely because materially different unusually large devices may be specially regulated.

### S17. Public carriage

"Bear arms" protects peaceable carriage of an ordinary firearm for lawful self-defense outside the home. A State may regulate manner and may require an objective permit, but ordinary qualified adults must retain a practical lawful means of public carriage.

A State need not constitutionalize every manner of carry simultaneously. It may choose reasonable open- or concealed-carry rules, training requirements, identification rules, and manner restrictions so long as the system does not amount to a general prohibition or discretionary veto.

Extraordinary-need, political approval, personal-connection, or open-ended "good character" requirements are incompatible with an ordinary right when they let officials deny qualified applicants for reasons not tied to an established disqualification.

### S18. Sensitive places and private property

Government may prohibit firearms in a genuinely sensitive place when the location's function, custody, vulnerability, or security arrangements materially distinguish it from ordinary public space. Courthouses, secure portions of government buildings, jails and prisons, polling places while voting is occurring, and schools involving minors are strong candidates.

The label "government property" is not itself enough. An ordinary unsecured parking lot, public sidewalk, park, transportation system, or commercial district does not become sensitive merely because the government owns, licenses, or regulates it.

Private owners remain free to exclude firearms from their property, and government may enforce that choice. Government should not circumvent the public-carry right by declaring all private property open to the public gun-free unless each owner affirmatively authorizes carriage.

### S19. Minors and age

Government retains substantial authority to regulate unsupervised firearm purchase, possession, carriage, and training by minors. Parental supervision requirements and age-appropriate safety rules are generally available.

Restrictions on children do not automatically determine the constitutional status of legal adults. A State cannot defend a categorical adult restriction merely by pointing to historical rules governing minors.

### S20. Seizure, forfeiture, and return

The Second Amendment does not immunize firearms from ordinary warrants, evidence rules, forfeiture following qualifying criminal misuse, or seizure under a valid dangerous-person order. But lawful seizure is not automatically permanent forfeiture.

When the legal predicate ends and the firearm is not contraband or independently forfeitable, government must provide a practical method for return or lawful transfer. Administrative retention cannot become permanent confiscation merely because return is inconvenient.

### S21. Fees and taxes

Ordinary generally applicable taxation does not become unconstitutional merely because firearms are sold. A fee imposed specifically as a condition of exercising the right must remain connected to reasonable administration and may not price ordinary people out of the right.

Registration, licensing, background-check, training, and modification fees must be evaluated cumulatively. Government cannot evade the prohibition on a practical financial barrier by dividing it among numerous individually modest charges.

### S22. Federalism and the Commerce Clause remain separate

Second Amendment validity and federal legislative power are independent inquiries. A firearm regulation may be substantively permissible yet beyond Congress's enumerated powers; a State may possess ordinary police power over the subject yet violate the incorporated right.

For federal firearm laws, Stone identifies the protected-right issue, identifies Congress's enumerated-power theory, tests any anti-evasion or market relationship, requires a genuine offense-specific commerce connection when the statute depends on one, and separately determines whether the law regulates individuals or commandeers States.

A firearm's remote historical crossing of a state line, standing alone and performing no present constitutional work, is not a perpetual federal jurisdictional passport. Manufacturing, interstate commercial transfer, importation, exportation, interstate trafficking, and genuine anti-evasion components of those market systems stand differently from punishment of purely local possession based solely on old interstate movement.

### S23. Remedies and cumulative burdens

Second Amendment adjudication should be provision-specific. Registration, arm prohibitions, magazine rules, training, inspection, fees, renewal, storage, carry, and sensitive-place restrictions do not become one undifferentiated "firearms scheme."

A court should invalidate or narrow the offending provision while preserving severable lawful regulation. Denials and licensing delays require prompt review. Administrative burdens must also be evaluated cumulatively because numerous individually modest requirements can become a practical prohibition when stacked together.

### S24. What the architecture does not do

This doctrine does not produce a right for every person to possess every weapon in every place. It protects ordinary qualified people, ordinary arms, meaningful acquisition, functional home possession, and peaceable carriage while preserving substantial authority over adjudicated dangerous persons, violent misuse, machineguns and other materially distinct weapons, specially regulated categories, sensitive places, commercial transfer, identification, genuine safety rules, and material classification-changing modifications.

It does not use a nonviolent felony label, drug status, diagnosis, arrest, accusation, political label, or firearm aesthetic as a substitute for constitutional analysis. It does not constitutionalize a ten-round number, declare all government property sensitive, make every accessory separately registrable, or treat survival of one administrative requirement as validating all others.

## OT1994: the enumerated-powers foundation

### United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549 (1995)

**Status.** Essential, structural — not itself a Second Amendment case.

**Historical questions.** Did federal prosecution of a student for possessing a handgun in a school zone rest on a valid exercise of the commerce power, where the statute regulated neither commerce nor an economic transaction and contained no case-specific interstate-commerce element?

**Stone's disposition.** Reject the chain of inferences running from school gun possession to violent crime, educational impairment, productivity, consumption, and the national economy as a basis for the statute.

**Reasoning.** The simulation uses *Lopez* for a broader but disciplined commerce framework: Congress may regulate interstate transactions, channels, instrumentalities, economic classes substantially affecting an actual interstate market, intrastate conduct necessary to prevent evasion or impairment of a valid interstate regime, and materially established interstate externalities. It may not rely on generalized social consequences, insurance costs, fear, lost productivity, or national importance alone; a jurisdictional phrase must perform genuine constitutional work in the individual case. Stone's purpose is to prevent the Second Amendment inquiry from swallowing the enumerated-powers inquiry — a federal firearm restriction can be consistent with the Second Amendment and still exceed Congress's power, and conversely a State may possess ordinary police power to enact a regulation but still violate the incorporated right. This separation becomes essential later: *Hale* and *Rybar* uphold machinegun restrictions because possession is functionally tied to a closed national transfer market; *Fraternal Order of Police* requires a genuine offense-specific commerce connection; *Gillespie* distinguishes direct federal regulation of individuals from federal commands to state government; *Emerson*, if selected, must satisfy the same commerce rule independently of its Second Amendment validity.

**What this establishes.** No Second Amendment rule and no classification of any firearm as protected or unprotected — only a limit preventing Congress from converting every local firearm event into federal law through downstream economic consequences.

## OT1995: the initial individual-right position and the machinegun branch

### Love v. Pepersack, 47 F.3d 120 (1996)

**Status.** Essential; Stone's underlying individual-right reasoning is a noncontrolling concurrence.

**Historical questions.** April Love truthfully applied to purchase a handgun in Maryland in 1990; state police denied the application based on an arrest record whose dispositions were unclear, though arrest alone was not a statutory disqualification. She won administrative and state-court review, then sued the responsible officers under § 1983. Did the mistaken denial state a substantive-due-process claim, and were the officers entitled to qualified immunity?

**Stone's disposition.** Join the unanimous result: the mistaken application of state licensing law does not become a federal substantive-due-process violation merely because it was unauthorized or arbitrary under state law, given that the state-review system completely corrected the error and Love pleaded no procedural-due-process claim; the officers receive qualified immunity because no sufficiently specific body of law in 1990 clearly established that the Second and Fourteenth Amendments protected acquisition of a handgun against denial based solely on an unresolved arrest record.

**Reasoning.** Stone's separate writing, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas, reaches the underlying arms question without commanding a majority. The right belongs to an individual claimant and is not merely a derivative entitlement of state governments or organized militia units; current militia enrollment is not required to invoke it. Acquisition is connected to keeping — a nominal right to possess an arm would be hollow if government could categorically prevent every lawful means of acquiring one, though this does not make every commercial condition unconstitutional. The arm matters: Love sought an ordinary handgun, not a fully automatic machinegun or an unusually destructive device, beginning the distinction between ordinary personal arms and materially different weapon categories. The licensing ground matters: an unresolved arrest record is not an adjudicated violent predicate, and government cannot transform an accusation into the equivalent of a conviction merely by calling the applicant risky. The remedy remains separate from the right — even where Stone believes the constitutional right exists, the officers cannot be held personally liable under law that was not clearly established in 1990.

**What this establishes.** Four ideas planted for later use without prematurely creating a complete doctrine: the right is individual; States are operationally bound by it; ordinary handgun acquisition is within the constitutional field; and recognition of the right does not erase qualified immunity or ordinary licensing administration. *Love* does not establish a controlling majority for the individual-right proposition, a home-defense core, an operability requirement, a public-carry right, a universal licensing standard, a scrutiny tier, a restoration rule, or a damages entitlement, and it does not hold that police must approve an applicant whose record contains an actual legal disqualification.

### United States v. Hale, 978 F.2d 1016 (1996)

**Status.** Essential, narrow controlling holding.

**Historical questions.** Wilbur Hale was convicted on thirteen counts of possessing machineguns under § 922(o) and three counts involving unregistered firearms, after federal agents recovered fully automatic weapons, conversion equipment, receivers, and a silenced pistol. Does § 922(o) violate the Second Amendment as applied to his post-1986 possession, and does the statute exceed the commerce power?

**Stone's disposition.** Join the unanimous as-applied result that § 922(o) does not violate the Second Amendment for post-1986 possession of fully automatic machineguns, and join the Commerce Clause holding that Congress may close the post-1986 civilian machinegun transfer market and include possession within that closure when possession and transfer are functionally inseparable.

**Reasoning.** Stone, joined again by Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas, states an individual-right rationale that still lacks a majority. Begin with the arm, not a universal scrutiny tier — even under an individual-right understanding, the right does not necessarily cover every weapon falling within the broadest linguistic definition of "arms." Fully automatic operation is constitutionally material: these weapons fire repeatedly through a single trigger activation, with sustained-fire characteristics materially different from an ordinary handgun or semiautomatic firearm. Resolve only the category presented — post-1986 civilian machineguns may remain outside the ordinary-arms category without announcing a universal test for every firearm, accessory, or firing mechanism. The Commerce Clause reasoning stays independent: the law survives not because machineguns are frightening or nationally important, but because possession is a necessary anti-evasion component of a direct national transfer-market prohibition; excluding possession would permit concealment, retention, acquisition, and other means of defeating the market prohibition, and generalized effects of gun violence, insurance costs, fear, or lost productivity remain insufficient. *Love*'s common-arms-for-home-defense view is repeated but remains noncontrolling.

**What this establishes.** One narrow category placed outside the eventual protected core, before that core is defined, so that later recognition of an individual right cannot be misconstrued as a right to possess every weapon. Ordinary handguns, semiautomatic rifles, ordinary shotguns, home possession, operability, public carry, licensing, training, ranges, every other automatic-weapon restriction, and whether legislatively labeled "military-style" weapons fall outside the right all remain undecided; the machinegun category must not later be extended to semiautomatic firearms merely through rhetoric.

## OT1996: structural support

### Printz v. United States, 521 U.S. 898 (1997)

**Status.** Structural foundation, not a Second Amendment merits case.

**Historical questions.** Did the federal command requiring state and local chief law-enforcement officers to conduct background-check work under the Brady Act violate the anti-commandeering principle?

**Stone's disposition.** Join the holding invalidating the federal command.

**Reasoning.** *Printz*'s significance here is anti-commandeering, not the substantive right to possess a firearm. Stone's later distinction, applied repeatedly in *Fraternal Order of Police* and *Gillespie*, is exact: Congress may directly regulate an individual who possesses a firearm, provided it has an enumerated-power basis; Congress may not require a State or its officers to administer a federal program merely because the subject involves firearms; the practical effect of federal law on state employment qualifications does not itself amount to commandeering; and the federal government cannot evade *Printz* by describing compelled state administration as ordinary regulation.

## OT1997: stabilizing the machinegun rule

### United States v. Rybar, 103 F.3d 273 (1997)

**Status.** Essential, unanimous at its narrow scope.

**Historical questions.** Raymond Rybar, a federally licensed firearms dealer, entered conditional guilty pleas to two § 922(o) machinegun-possession counts arising from gun-show transactions and challenged the law under the Commerce Clause and Second Amendment. Does § 922(o) survive both challenges as applied to his possession?

**Stone's disposition.** Join the unanimous holding that § 922(o) does not violate the Second Amendment as applied to Rybar's possession, and that possession may be included in the closed post-1986 civilian machinegun market because it is reasonably necessary to prevent evasion or material impairment of that market, following *Hale*. His two possession convictions stand; the dismissed National Firearms Act transfer counts and the constitutional status of ordinary firearms remain outside the judgment.

**Reasoning.** *Rybar* is not meant to enlarge *Hale* — its job is to stabilize it. The Court now has a unanimous narrow rule: fully automatic machineguns involved in post-1986 civilian market transactions may be prohibited. The transactional setting strengthens anti-evasion, since gun-show possession and transfer conduct directly implicate the market Congress closed, unlike a theory resting on remote downstream consequences. Unanimity is purchased through narrowness — the Court need not agree on every historical or theoretical proposition about the Second Amendment, only that this arm, in this regulatory setting, may be prohibited. Ordinary handguns, ordinary semiautomatic firearms, home possession, operability, carry, and licensing are all affirmatively preserved as open.

**What this establishes.** By the end of OT1997, a stable machinegun rule exists, but the simulation still lacks a controlling ordinary-arms core. The right is being developed category by category, not from an all-weapons presumption.

## OT1999: the first person-based disqualification branch

### Fraternal Order of Police v. United States, 173 F.3d 898 (2000)

**Status.** Essential.

**Historical questions.** The Fraternal Order of Police challenged § 922(g)(9), which prohibits firearm possession by a person convicted of a qualifying misdemeanor crime of domestic violence, on the absence of an official-use exemption, equal protection, the Second Amendment, congressional commerce authority, and its effect on state and local law-enforcement officers. Does the statute survive?

**Stone's disposition.** Uphold the disqualification as applied to a constitutionally adequate domestic-violence conviction involving actual or attempted physical force or threatened use of a deadly weapon. Hold that the violent conviction itself supplies an adjudicated danger predicate sufficient to bar possession, including possession of a government-issued duty firearm, while the conviction and its legally prescribed firearms consequences remain operative. The Constitution does not require a second present-danger trial every few years merely to enforce that current disability. Do not hold that the disability is constitutionally irreversible for life regardless of pardon, expungement, set-aside, restoration law, successful collateral attack, completion of all legal consequences, remoteness, or a materially different future as-applied record. Join the equal-protection holding rejecting a mandatory official-use exemption and the anti-commandeering holding that direct regulation of individual possession is not commandeering. On the Commerce Clause, require a genuine offense-specific connection to commerce rather than remote historical movement alone.

**Reasoning.** The predicate is adjudicated rather than resting on arrest, allegation, bureaucratic suspicion, or a generalized risk score. It is also substantively violent: actual or attempted force or threatened use of a deadly weapon places the case within a demonstrated-danger branch rather than a generic misdemeanor or felony rule. A criminal conviction supplies proof beyond a reasonable doubt and ordinary criminal process, so the Constitution does not require government to relitigate present dangerousness whenever it enforces the current disability.

The domestic relationship matters because the statute addresses violence already adjudicated in a context of repeated personal access and potential escalation, but Stone should not convert that empirical premise into a permanent constitutional conclusion about every person forever. "Durable" means government may attach serious continuing firearms consequences to a qualifying violent conviction without constant rehearing. It does not mean the Court must decide in this case that no future restoration or as-applied challenge can ever exist after the predicate's legal force changes or decades of lawful conduct produce a materially different record.

Government employment does not erase the predicate; issuance of a firearm by a police department does not transform the person into a constitutionally different claimant. On equal protection, the cleaner rationale is institutional rather than moral ranking: domestic violence was often prosecuted through misdemeanor provisions that did not trigger older firearm disabilities, and Congress could address that gap. On commerce, this remains a deliberate simulation departure from the broadest real-world minimal-nexus approach: an object's decades-old crossing of a state line cannot become a permanent federal jurisdictional passport.

**What this establishes.** The first person-based disqualification branch: a defined violent conviction can justify serious and durable disarmament without creating a general power to disarm anyone government calls dangerous. The rule does not establish a disability based on a nonviolent offense, arrest, accusation, civil order, diagnosis, unrelated status, or a conviction that has ceased to have legal force. It also does not decide the constitutional treatment of a remote completed conviction presented with a genuine restoration record.

### Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis, 185 F.3d 693 (2000)

**Status.** Essential, narrow.

**Historical questions.** Jerald Gillespie, a police officer of more than twenty-five years, lost his ability to carry a firearm under § 922(g)(9) after a misdemeanor battery conviction involving his former wife, threatening his position because the department required officers to be armed. He challenged the law under the Commerce Clause, Tenth Amendment, equal protection, and Second Amendment.

**Stone's disposition.** Hold that direct federal regulation of Gillespie's possession is not commandeering merely because he is a municipal police officer, reiterate that federal authority requires a genuine offense-specific commerce connection, and grant no reinstatement or other relief on those theories. Do not resolve an independent Second Amendment claim.

**Reasoning.** *Fraternal Order of Police* already established the controlling violent-conviction branch; a second broad merits opinion would add little and risk confusing employment consequences with the constitutional rule. Stone's goals here are narrower: confirm that direct regulation of an individual is not the same as commandeering a State; confirm that a state employer's decision to require armed service remains the State's own decision; preserve the requirement of a real commerce nexus; avoid creating a constitutional entitlement to a police position or official-use exception; and demonstrate that the Court does not repeat a constitutional holding merely because another litigant presents the same substantive concern.

**What this establishes.** No expansion of *Fraternal Order of Police* to nonviolent convictions, no decision on restoration, and no holding that employment consequences are irrelevant in every constitutional case. It simply recognizes that a generally applicable federal possession rule does not become commandeering because state employment makes firearm possession occupationally necessary.

## OT2001: the temporary protective-order branch

### United States v. Emerson, 270 F.3d 203 (2002)

**Status.** Recommended. Historical certiorari disposition: cert. denied, 536 U.S. 907 (2002).

**Historical questions.** Emerson was indicted under § 922(g)(8) for possessing a firearm while subject to a domestic-violence restraining order entered after a state-court evidentiary hearing at which his wife testified about threats; the order prohibited threats and bodily injury. The district court dismissed the indictment on Second and Fifth Amendment grounds and the Fifth Circuit reversed and remanded, so the case reaches the certiorari stage before any final conviction. The statute applied only when the order followed a hearing with actual notice and an opportunity to participate, restrained harassment, stalking, threats, or comparable conduct involving an intimate partner or child, and contained either a credible-threat finding or an explicit prohibition on force expected to cause bodily injury.

**Stone's disposition.** Reject the facial and as-applied Second Amendment objection to the qualifying temporary disability; reject the due-process challenge on the established hearing record; reverse the dismissal of the indictment and remand, permitting the prosecution to proceed on the condition that the Government must still prove all statutory elements, any knowledge required by governing law, and — departing from the real case's reliance on the firearm's prior interstate movement — a genuine offense-specific commerce connection.

**Reasoning.** *Fraternal Order of Police* rests on a criminal conviction; a civil protective order is materially different, since the proceeding may use different burdens of proof, the order may be temporary, the restrained person may not have been convicted of anything, some orders follow contested hearings while others are entered ex parte, some contain actual danger findings while others are routine or consent-based, and duration varies — the conviction rule cannot simply be copied. Stone should uphold the temporary disability as applied only when actual notice was given; the person had a meaningful opportunity to appear, present evidence, use counsel when governing law permits, and contest the allegations; the order contains an individualized judicial finding of a credible threat or expressly prohibits actual, attempted, or threatened physical force expected to cause bodily injury; the order remains currently valid and operative; and the federal disability's duration is coextensive with the order's, ending when it ends absent a different, independently sufficient predicate. No single element should suffice alone: notice without a danger-linked order is insufficient, a protective label without meaningful process is insufficient, a danger finding from an expired order is insufficient, a generic divorce injunction should not become a firearm disability merely because it directs both parties to behave lawfully, and later misconduct cannot retroactively cure a constitutionally inadequate order. On due process, the hearing requirement and opportunity to participate ordinarily supply the necessary process, but Stone should expressly reserve an ex parte order lacking prompt and meaningful review, an order entered without actual notice, a nominal hearing at which participation was not genuinely available, an order extended indefinitely without review, and an order whose qualifying findings are absent, stale, or legally vacated. On commerce, this is the most important risk in a simple *Emerson* recommendation: the real case treated prior interstate movement of the firearm as sufficient, but the simulation's *Fraternal Order of Police* rule rejects a remote historical movement performing no present constitutional work, so Stone cannot casually affirm federal power merely because the pistol crossed a state line at some past date. Because the case arrives before final conviction, this disposition is procedurally workable and does not need to adjudge Emerson guilty.

**What this establishes.** One carefully bounded category — a temporary firearm disability may rest on a currently operative, danger-linked protective order entered after actual notice and meaningful opportunity to participate — without creating a general authority to disarm anyone government considers dangerous. Left open: ex parte orders, emergency orders before a hearing, delayed post-order review, orders lacking a threat finding or force prohibition, routine mutual restraining orders, permanent disability after expiration, non-domestic civil orders, general dangerousness statutes, red-flag procedures with materially different safeguards, the treatment of firearms acquired before entry of the order, and property-transfer mechanics during the temporary period.

## OT2007: the essential home-defense core

### District of Columbia v. Heller, 554 U.S. 570 (2008) — "Heller I"

**Status.** Absolutely essential.

**Historical questions.** The District prohibited registration of handguns and required lawful firearms in the home to remain unloaded and disassembled or bound by a trigger-lock mechanism; Heller sought to keep an operable handgun in his home. Does the Second Amendment protect an individual right to possess a firearm for traditionally lawful purposes, particularly self-defense in the home, against both the prohibition and the disabling requirement?

**Stone's disposition.** A law-abiding natural person has a constitutional right to possess an ordinary handgun in the home for immediate lawful self-defense. Government may not categorically prohibit possession of that arm in the home, and may not require every lawful home firearm to remain continuously unloaded, disassembled, locked, or otherwise unavailable for immediate lawful defensive use. Objective licensing and registration may remain available when they are genuine administrative measures and do not operate as discretionary or practical prohibitions. Invalidate the categorical handgun prohibition as applied to ordinary home possession; invalidate the continuous inoperability requirement insofar as it prevents immediate lawful self-defense; direct the District to permit Heller to register the handgun and issue the necessary license if he is otherwise qualified under valid objective standards; leave severable and unchallenged provisions intact.

**Reasoning.** Stone should not re-litigate whether the right is individual — the simulation's operational premise already answers that, and a long historical contest over militia-only versus individual-right theories would waste the vehicle and destabilize earlier development. He should focus on what is still missing, working through the same sequence used throughout this build: identify the claimant (Heller is not subject to a violent conviction, active protective order, or another established disqualification; "law-abiding" describes the absence of an independently established disqualifying predicate, not an unlimited delegation to legislatures); identify the arm (a handgun is an ordinary personal firearm commonly selected for lawful home protection, materially different from the fully automatic machineguns adjudicated in *Hale* and *Rybar* — protection should not be defined solely through current ownership numbers, since government could suppress an arm and then rely on the suppression to prove uncommonness); identify the purpose (immediate lawful self-defense is central, without creating an unrestricted right to use force — the law of necessity, imminence, proportionality, defense of others, and unlawful aggression remains intact, since the Amendment protects possession and readiness of the arm, not substantive self-defense law); identify the place (the home is the strongest application, being where lawful defense of the person, family, and dwelling is most acute and where public-order concerns associated with carriage are lowest, without deciding whether the same protection extends identically to streets, vehicles, parks, businesses, schools, or courthouses); measure the burden (the District's law does not merely regulate manner, storage, registration, or training — it removes the ordinary handgun entirely and renders remaining lawful firearms unavailable for the immediate purpose that gives the home application practical value, and the right cannot be reduced to theoretical ownership of an unusable object); and avoid a universal scrutiny tier (a total prohibition on the central application fails because it destroys the protected right; later regulations should be classified according to claimant, arm, purpose, place, predicate, and practical burden, not a single strict- or intermediate-scrutiny label). On licensing, the District may retain an objective system provided the criteria are clear and lawful, officials lack open-ended discretion to deny qualified applicants, applications are processed within a reasonable time, fees do not price ordinary people out of the right, the license is not conditioned on surrender of the protected core, and Heller receives the license if he satisfies valid objective conditions — every registration requirement need not be decided in this one case.

**What this establishes.** The hard center of the doctrine. Everything before this case defines exclusions or disqualification branches; everything after it asks whether regulation leaves this core meaningfully intact. Preserved without enlargement: *Hale* and *Rybar* for fully automatic machineguns, *Fraternal Order of Police* for legally operative violent domestic-abuse convictions, *Emerson* (if adopted) for qualifying temporary protective orders, and genuine licensing and safety administration. Left open: public carry, open versus concealed carry, sensitive places, semiautomatic rifles, magazines, commercial sale conditions, training requirements, range access, detailed registration mechanics, storage rules that preserve meaningful immediate access, minors, nonviolent criminal predicates, mental-health predicates, corporate claimants, and the full treatment of ammunition.

## OT2009: incorporation without a redundant case

### McDonald v. City of Chicago, 561 U.S. 742 (2010)

**Status.** Recommended: do not take.

**Historical questions.** The real case's principal function was to hold that the right recognized in *Heller* applies against state and local governments through the Fourteenth Amendment.

**Stone's disposition.** Decline the case.

**Reasoning.** The simulation already operates on the premise that States are bound. *Love* introduced Stone's incorporation position, and the controlling handoff expressly directs that the incorporated right be treated as part of the simulation's architecture. Taking *McDonald* would either duplicate an answered issue or invite an unnecessary dispute over the Due Process Clause and the Privileges or Immunities Clause. The formal Holdings Register's failure to identify a majority case that clearly accomplished incorporation is a documentation gap, not a reason to manufacture litigation. The correct action is to preserve the handoff premise, add a transparent standards note identifying incorporation as an operational simulation premise, avoid misdescribing the *Love* concurrence as a controlling holding, and not take *McDonald* merely to clean up recordkeeping.

## OT2011: the first post-core regulatory case, and an optional access branch

### Heller v. District of Columbia, 670 F.3d 1244 (2011) — "Heller II"

**Status.** Essential.

**Historical questions.** After *Heller I*, the District enacted a scheme requiring registration, prohibiting firearms classified as "assault weapons," and prohibiting magazines holding more than ten rounds, along with numerous registration conditions including fingerprinting, photographs, background checks, training, and purchase-frequency restrictions. When does a firearm regulation administer the right, and when does it destroy a protected application?

**Stone's disposition.** Affirm basic objective handgun registration; reject automatic reliance on the machinegun cases for semiautomatic firearms; vacate or reverse the semiautomatic-rifle prohibition if common ordinary status is established on the record, otherwise remand it; remand the magazine limit unless the actual record supports its classification and fit; remand novel registration conditions for separate, provision-specific analysis; preserve severable valid provisions.

**Reasoning.** The roadmap directs Stone not to copy a universal scrutiny tier, not to equate semiautomatic firearms with machineguns merely because lawmakers use the phrase "assault weapon," and not to invent a constitutional ten-round limit. The Court should not speak of a single undifferentiated "firearms scheme" — registration, arm prohibition, magazine prohibition, fingerprints, fees, training, inspection, and purchase-frequency limits impose different burdens and serve different claimed functions, and each must receive its own classification, factual record, and remedy. On whether an arm is protected, the label "assault weapon" is not constitutional analysis; Stone should examine whether the firearm fires one round per trigger activation or fires continuously, whether the prohibited models are commonly possessed for lawful purposes, whether their ordinary operating characteristics differ materially from protected handguns and other semiautomatic firearms, whether the challenged features are functional, cosmetic, safety-related, or associated with a materially different destructive capacity, and whether the law prohibits an actual category of ordinary arms or only an unusual and distinct weapon — the *Hale* and *Rybar* machinegun rule cannot be extended simply by describing a semiautomatic rifle as "military-style." On practical operation, for registration and licensing Stone should ask whether the criteria are objective, whether approval is available to a qualified applicant, whether the process is timely, whether fees are limited to reasonable administration, whether the process is duplicative or recurrent without demonstrated need, whether it creates a practical barrier comparable to prohibition, whether noncompliance carries criminal consequences disproportionate to an administrative lapse, and whether there is review of a denial. Government must connect each particular provision to its stated administrative or safety function with actual support, not labels — this matters especially for categorically banned semiautomatic firearms, magazine limits, repeated registration, physical inspection requirements, training mandates, and purchase-frequency restrictions. A regulation cannot survive merely because some other firearm remains available, since that reasoning would allow government to eliminate one ordinary protected arm at a time; at the same time, not every inconvenience, recordkeeping requirement, or cost destroys the right, and the focus should be on whether the protected application remains meaningfully and practically available. On the magazine limit specifically, the Constitution contains no ten-round number; Stone should require evidence of ordinary magazine capacities supplied with protected firearms, prevalence in lawful possession, relationship to ordinary defensive use, whether the limit materially impairs the firearm's ordinary operation, the practical availability and consequences of compliant alternatives, the stated safety mechanism, and why ten rounds rather than another number is constitutionally and factually fitted — if the record does not answer those questions, remand is more honest than inventing a constitutional number. For novel registration conditions, the best disposition is mixed: uphold genuinely basic objective registration, but remand novel and materially burdensome conditions for provision-specific justification.

**What this establishes.** The line between regulation and destruction without a general license for judicial policy balancing: government may administer the exercise of the right through objective, supported, and nonprohibitory measures, but may not use labels, cumulative burdens, or categorical prohibitions to eliminate an ordinary protected arm or the meaningful exercise of the home-defense core.

### Ezell v. City of Chicago, 651 F.3d 684 (2011)

**Status.** Optional.

**Historical questions.** Chicago required firearm-permit applicants to complete live-fire range training while simultaneously prohibiting every firing range within city limits; the Seventh Circuit held the plaintiffs had a strong likelihood of success and were entitled to preliminary relief. May government make training a legal condition of possession while prohibiting every practical local means of completing it?

**Stone's disposition.** Enjoin enforcement of the total range ban, holding that the plaintiffs are likely to succeed on the merits, that the constitutional injury is irreparable, and that the total ban cannot remain in force pending final adjudication; permit the city to adopt and enforce supported safety, zoning, and operational rules; do not order the city to build, finance, or subsidize a range.

**Reasoning.** *Heller I* establishes possession and operability, but not necessarily a freestanding right to recreational shooting, commercial range operation, or every form of training. *Ezell* presents a narrower contradiction: government itself makes live-fire training a legal condition of firearm possession and then prohibits every practical local means of satisfying that condition. Government may regulate range location, building construction, ventilation, lead containment, noise, hours, instructor qualifications, storage, security, age-appropriate supervision, and ordinary business operation — but it may not require firearm training as a condition of exercising the right while prohibiting every practical means of completing that training. The right to effective home possession also protects the practical opportunity to acquire and maintain the basic proficiency reasonably necessary for safe lawful use, a derivative protection limited to what makes the recognized core effective. Avoid a broad recreational-right theory — the constitutional defect is not that every resident is entitled to a nearby shooting club, but the government-created contradiction between mandatory training and universal prohibition. A city cannot point to the theoretical legality of training while making the required activity impossible within the jurisdiction that imposes the condition, and cannot impose a prerequisite and categorically shift every means of compliance outside its boundaries, particularly where it is regulating the exercise of a constitutional right by its own residents. Because the real decision concerns a preliminary injunction, Stone should not use it to enter a final, exhaustive constitutional code governing every firing-range regulation; if the simulation later needs a final merits rule, a later final-judgment vehicle would be cleaner than treating this preliminary record as conclusive.

**What this establishes.** A practical-access branch, added only if the simulation affirmatively wants it: government may not nullify the right indirectly through contradictory prerequisites. Left open: purely recreational shooting, public subsidies, a right to a range in a preferred neighborhood, regulation of outdoor versus indoor ranges, particular buffer distances, age restrictions, commercial insurance conditions, advanced tactical training, and training involving weapons outside the protected category.

## OT2013: public carriage

### Moore v. Madigan, 702 F.3d 933 (7th Cir. 2012)

**Status.** Essential lower-court simulation vehicle.

**Historical questions.** Illinois broadly prohibited ordinary people from carrying ready-to-use firearms in public, subject to limited exceptions. Does the Second Amendment stop at the threshold of the home, or must a State leave ordinary qualified persons a practical lawful means of carrying an ordinary firearm for self-defense?

**Stone's disposition.** Invalidate the general public-carry prohibition. Hold that the right to "bear" arms includes peaceable public carriage of an ordinary firearm for lawful self-defense by an adult not subject to a valid disqualification. Stay the mandate briefly to allow Illinois to enact an objective licensing and manner-of-carry system. Do not require the State to permit every method of carriage simultaneously and do not decide every sensitive-place question.

**Reasoning.** *Heller I* makes the home the strongest application, not the only place where "bear" has constitutional work to do. A State may respond to the greater public-order concerns outside the home with training, objective licensing, identification, manner rules, and genuine sensitive-place restrictions, but it cannot answer those concerns by eliminating ordinary carriage for the ordinary qualified population. The relevant distinction is regulation versus destruction: a shall-issue system with clear eligibility rules administers public carriage; an extraordinary-need system or flat prohibition converts a general right into official dispensation.

Stone should avoid forcing the open-carry versus concealed-carry issue unnecessarily. The Constitution requires a practical lawful avenue for ordinary defensive carriage. A State may choose the manner so long as its choice is real, generally available to qualified applicants, and not defeated by open-ended character or special-need discretion.

**What this establishes.** A public-carry branch coupled to objective licensing. Left open are detailed sensitive-place boundaries, vehicle rules, demonstrations, transit, private-property default rules, and the full treatment of particular training requirements.

## OT2014: safe storage and ammunition access

### Jackson v. City and County of San Francisco, 746 F.3d 953 (9th Cir. 2014)

**Status.** Essential lower-court simulation vehicle.

**Historical questions.** San Francisco required a handgun in the home to be stored in a locked container or disabled with a trigger lock unless carried on the person, and separately restricted local commercial sale of certain ammunition while leaving possession and use lawful. How far may safe-storage rules go after *Heller I*, and does practical ammunition access fall within the constitutional right?

**Stone's disposition.** Draw a narrower line than the historical Ninth Circuit. Uphold an unattended-storage requirement directed to access by children, prohibited persons, theft, or other unauthorized users. Invalidate the ordinance insofar as it requires a lawful possessor who is present in the home and reasonably relying on the handgun for immediate defense to keep it continuously locked merely because it is not literally being carried on the body. Recognize that practical access to ordinary ammunition is derivative of the protected firearm right. Treat the local ammunition-sales restriction provision by provision and uphold it only to the extent ordinary lawful ammunition remains practically obtainable without turning acquisition into a jurisdiction-wide prohibition.

**Reasoning.** *Heller I* invalidated a rule that made home firearms unavailable for immediate defense, but it did not constitutionalize careless unattended storage. The sensible boundary is control and availability. When the lawful possessor is absent or the firearm is otherwise unattended, a rule requiring reasonable precautions against children, prohibited persons, and theft regulates risk without destroying defensive use. When the lawful possessor is present and reasonably relying on the firearm for immediate defense, a continuous-lock command recreates the operability defect in a different form.

A right to an operable firearm would be hollow if government could eliminate every practical source of ordinary ammunition. That does not make every ammunition product or every retail rule immune from regulation. The constitutional question is whether the rule leaves practical access to ordinary ammunition for protected firearms while pursuing a real safety or commercial function.

**What this establishes.** Safe storage is valid when directed to unauthorized access and unattended firearms, but cannot function as continuous home disarmament. Ordinary ammunition access is within the constitutional field. Particular ammunition categories, ammunition taxes, purchase quantities, and unusually hazardous rounds remain open.

## OT2015: sensitive places and detailed registration

### Bonidy v. United States Postal Service, 790 F.3d 1121 (10th Cir. 2015)

**Status.** Recommended lower-court simulation vehicle.

**Historical questions.** A licensed carrier sought to carry a firearm into a post-office building or at least store it in his vehicle in the adjacent customer parking lot. A federal regulation prohibited firearms throughout postal property. Is every piece of government-owned property a sensitive place?

**Stone's disposition.** Uphold the prohibition inside the post-office building. Do not automatically extend the sensitive-place rule to the ordinary unsecured customer parking lot merely because the Postal Service owns it. Reverse or remand as to the parking area unless the Government establishes that the lot is functionally integrated with secure operations, controlled access, screening, or another feature that materially distinguishes it from ordinary public parking.

**Reasoning.** Sensitive-place doctrine must be functional. The government building itself houses public employees, official operations, controlled work areas, and governmental functions that reasonably support a location-specific weapons rule. Ownership of adjacent asphalt does not perform the same constitutional work. If "government property" alone were sufficient, legislatures could turn streets, parks, transit systems, parking areas, and vast public lands into right-free zones by changing title rather than function.

The government remains free to establish secure perimeters, employee-only areas, screening zones, and site-specific restrictions where actual function justifies them. The Court should decide the building and parking area separately rather than treating the property parcel as a single constitutional object.

**What this establishes.** Courthouses, secure government facilities, and comparable genuinely sensitive places may prohibit firearms, but public ownership is not a categorical exception from the Second Amendment. The decision preserves later case-by-case treatment of schools, universities, transit, polling places, legislatures, demonstrations, parks, and private property open to the public.

### Heller v. District of Columbia, 801 F.3d 264 (2015) — "Heller III"

**Status.** Essential. The project now affirmatively wants a detailed registration and administration branch.

**Historical questions.** The actual scheme at issue included long-gun registration, fingerprints, photographs, in-person appearance, physical presentation of the firearm, periodic re-registration, fees, safety training, a test on local firearm law, and a one-pistol-per-thirty-days limit. When do cumulative administrative conditions remain regulation, and when do they become a practical denial of the right?

**Stone's disposition.** Uphold basic objective registration of ordinary firearms when it is ministerial, timely, inexpensive, and connected to a genuine administrative function. Uphold one-time fingerprinting, photographs, and in-person identity verification when they materially improve accurate identification and are not repeatedly demanded without need. Permit reasonable cost-recovery fees and accessible safety training.

Invalidate physical presentation of the firearm absent evidence that inspection is necessary to accomplish a valid function that cannot reasonably be achieved through less burdensome identification. Invalidate periodic re-registration of the same unchanged owner and firearm absent a demonstrated need that cannot be served through address, transfer, loss, theft, or disqualification updates. Invalidate the one-pistol-per-thirty-days restriction absent an actual trafficking or anti-circumvention justification tied to the regulated transaction. Permit a basic legal-knowledge requirement only if the materials are accessible, the test is objective, retesting is reasonably available, and failure cannot become a discretionary veto.

Require the District and later governments to evaluate cumulative burden. A fingerprint fee, registration fee, training charge, travel requirement, appearance requirement, renewal requirement, and delay that might each be modest in isolation can together become a practical barrier.

**Reasoning.** *Heller I* allowed genuine licensing and *Heller II* rejected the idea that every administrative condition rises or falls together. This case should now complete that branch. The governing distinction is whether the requirement identifies the person or firearm, verifies a lawful predicate, supplies genuine safety training, or administers a specially regulated category without making exercise of the right depend on official grace.

Registration itself is not confiscation and is not unconstitutional merely because government records ownership. But the administrative system must remain ministerial: clear criteria, predictable processing, reasonable cost, review of denial, and no repeated burden without current function. Government cannot rely on a generic invocation of "public safety"; it must identify what each materially burdensome condition actually accomplishes.

This case also supplies the administrative principle for later modification regulation without pretending the present record decides suppressors, short-barreled configurations, automatic-conversion devices, serialization, or homemade firearms. If a later law requires registration because a modification changes the firearm into a separately regulated category, the same rules govern: the classification must be functionally legitimate, compliance must be practically available, the particular regulated component or configuration may be identified, and ordinary non-classification-changing parts cannot be swept into the registry merely for convenience.

**What this establishes.** A detailed licensing and registration doctrine, including cumulative-burden review, objective identity verification, cost limits, accessible training, skepticism toward unnecessary repeated processing, and the general administrative framework for later registration of materially classification-changing modifications. It does not itself adjudicate every future component or modification category.

## Questions that should remain unresolved through OT2015

Even with the fuller architecture, do not pretend the simulation has adjudicated every branch merely because Stone now has a target rule. Unless a genuine later vehicle is deliberately selected, the following remain unresolved as holdings: categorical treatment of nonviolent felonies and very old convictions; the circumstances in which a completed violent conviction constitutionally requires restoration; particular mental-health restoration procedures; emergency ex parte risk-order timing and burdens of proof; substance-use disabilities outside present intoxication or independently dangerous conduct; age restrictions for eighteen-to-twenty-year-olds; exact magazine classifications beyond the *Heller II* record; particular suppressor, short-barreled, destructive-device, and classification-changing modification statutes; serial-number rules and personally manufactured firearms; dealer-zoning rules; waiting periods beyond the general principle; ammunition taxes and unusually hazardous ammunition categories; transportation across jurisdictions; vehicle-specific sensitive-place rules; universities, transit systems, demonstrations, parks, polling-place perimeters, and private-property default rules; corporate Second Amendment rights; advanced or recreational training; and a universal test for every imaginable arm, component, claimant, or location.

The project should carry forward a sharp distinction between **mature target architecture** and **controlling simulated holdings**. Future cases may apply these planned principles, alter them when the record exposes a defect, or leave them undeveloped. The roadmap is not a substitute for an actual vehicle.
