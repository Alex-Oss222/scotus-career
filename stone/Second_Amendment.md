# Justice Stone: Second Amendment build, OT1994 through OT2015

**Simulation handoff. Proposed positions, not predetermined Court holdings.**

## Scope and how to use this build

This build runs from *United States v. Lopez* in OT1994 through the optional *Heller III* vehicle in OT2015. It is not a maximally comprehensive gun-rights code. It is a stable individual-right doctrine with a firm home-defense core and carefully bounded regulatory branches, built category by category rather than from a single all-weapons presumption. The planning instruction expressly rejects adding a case merely because an October Term otherwise lacks a gun decision — several terms below are deliberately silent, and that silence is as much a doctrinal choice as any decided case.

**Existing record note.** The formal Holdings and Standards files are synchronized through the June 27, 2002 decision group; everything through *Emerson* can be evaluated against an existing record. *Heller I*, *Heller II*, *Ezell*, and *Heller III* remain proposed future developments, not existing simulation law. There is one formal record issue worth preserving rather than silently repairing: the controlling handoff instruction says that, by the end of OT1999, the Second Amendment is an individual and incorporated right, but the Holdings Register describes Stone's individual-right and incorporation reasoning in *Love* as a noncontrolling four-Justice concurrence, and *Hale* repeats that position without a majority. *Fraternal Order of Police* presupposes an individual right and establishes a controlling disqualification rule, but it is a federal case and does not itself incorporate the right against the States. The clean solution is to record individual-right status and incorporation as a simulation premise until a source-authorized formal entry is added — not to take *McDonald* merely to repair this clerical gap, which would conflict with the intended path.

**Status tags.** Each entry below is marked essential, optional, or default-skip, following the source planning document's own distinctions — some cases are load-bearing for the architecture, others are elective refinements the simulation need not take.

## The standards Stone is building

### S1. Nine questions, asked in order for every case

Stone does not adopt a universal scrutiny tier. For every case he proceeds through the same sequence: Who is claiming the right? What arm is involved? What lawful purpose is asserted? Where is the right being exercised? Does an established disqualification or regulatory branch apply? What does the law do in practical operation? Does it regulate the right or destroy the protected application? For federal law, what enumerated power supports it? What is the narrowest remedy that resolves the actual violation?

### S2. The right-holder

The right belongs to an individual natural person, not merely a state militia institution. The strongest claimant is a person not subject to an independently established disqualification who seeks an ordinary arm for a lawful purpose. "Law-abiding" is descriptive, not a blank check — government must still identify the legal predicate that removes a claimant from the ordinary category.

### S3. The protected core

The central application is possession of an ordinary operable firearm, especially a handgun, in the home for immediate lawful self-defense. Government may not prohibit every handgun, force every lawful home firearm to remain unusable, use licensing as a discretionary veto, make a prerequisite impossible to satisfy, or eliminate ordinary arms one category at a time while pointing to substitutes.

### S4. Arm categories

Ordinary handguns are protected. Semiautomatic rifles and ordinary magazines require actual classification based on operation, ordinary lawful use, and common possession — not political labels. Post-1986 fully automatic machineguns may be prohibited under *Hale* and *Rybar*; those cases do not automatically decide semiautomatic firearms, magazines, or any arm merely because of a military-style origin or appearance.

### S5. Person-based disabilities

An adjudicated conviction for domestic violence involving actual or attempted physical force, or threatened use of a deadly weapon, supplies a durable disqualification while it remains legally operative — no second danger hearing, constitutional sunset, or automatic periodic reconsideration is required. If *Emerson* is adopted, a qualifying civil protective order supplies a separate, temporary disability requiring actual notice, a meaningful opportunity to participate, a danger-linked finding or force prohibition, and current legal force; it ends when the order ends. The simulation has not established a general dangerousness category: arrests, accusations, nonviolent misdemeanors, old unadjudicated conduct, and unrelated status do not inherit either branch's rules.

### S6. Licensing and safety regulation

Objective, timely, reviewable, and nonprohibitory licensing may exist. Reasonable administration may include registration, identity verification, genuine safety measures, and cost-recovery fees. Licensing fails when its actual operation produces arbitrary denial, indefinite delay, unaffordable cost, impossible conditions, repeated unsupported demands, or cumulative prohibition. If *Ezell* is adopted, government may regulate safe range operation but may not require live-fire training while prohibiting every practical means of completing it.

### S7. Federalism and the Commerce Clause stay separate from the right

Second Amendment validity and federal legislative power are independent inquiries; a law can pass one and fail the other. For federal firearm laws, identify the protected-right issue, identify Congress's enumerated-power theory, test any anti-evasion or market relationship, require a genuine offense-specific commerce connection when the statute depends on one, and separately determine whether the law directly regulates individuals or commandeers States. A remote historical fact — for example, a firearm's decades-old crossing of a state line — performing no present constitutional work cannot become a permanent federal jurisdictional passport.

### S8. What the architecture does not do

It does not use a random felon-possession, drug-user, hunting-regulation, or public-carry case to define the home core indirectly. It does not extend the machinegun rule to semiautomatic firearms through rhetoric alone. It does not treat survival of one administrative requirement as validating all others. It leaves public carry, sensitive places, magazine limits beyond any actual record, commercial-sale conditions, and several other categories listed in the final section below open unless a genuine later vehicle is deliberately selected.

## OT1994: the enumerated-powers foundation

### United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549 (1995)

**Status.** Essential, structural — not itself a Second Amendment case.

**Historical questions.** Did federal prosecution of a student for possessing a handgun in a school zone rest on a valid exercise of the commerce power, where the statute regulated neither commerce nor an economic transaction and contained no case-specific interstate-commerce element?

**Stone's disposition.** Reject the chain of inferences running from school gun possession to violent crime, educational impairment, productivity, consumption, and the national economy as a basis for the statute.

**Reasoning.** The simulation uses *Lopez* for a broader but disciplined commerce framework: Congress may regulate interstate transactions, channels, instrumentalities, economic classes substantially affecting an actual interstate market, intrastate conduct necessary to prevent evasion or impairment of a valid interstate regime, and materially established interstate externalities. It may not rely on generalized social consequences, insurance costs, fear, lost productivity, or national importance alone; a jurisdictional phrase must perform genuine constitutional work in the individual case. Stone's purpose is to prevent the Second Amendment inquiry from swallowing the enumerated-powers inquiry — a federal firearm restriction can be consistent with the Second Amendment and still exceed Congress's power, and conversely a State may possess ordinary police power to enact a regulation but still violate the incorporated right. This separation becomes essential later: *Hale* and *Rybar* uphold machinegun restrictions because possession is functionally tied to a closed national transfer market; *Fraternal Order of Police* requires a genuine offense-specific commerce connection; *Gillespie* distinguishes direct federal regulation of individuals from federal commands to state government; *Emerson*, if selected, must satisfy the same commerce rule independently of its Second Amendment validity.

**What this establishes.** No Second Amendment rule and no classification of any firearm as protected or unprotected — only a limit preventing Congress from converting every local firearm event into federal law through downstream economic consequences.

## OT1995: the initial individual-right position and the machinegun branch

### Love v. Pepersack, 47 F.3d 120 (1996)

**Status.** Essential; Stone's underlying individual-right reasoning is a noncontrolling concurrence.

**Historical questions.** April Love truthfully applied to purchase a handgun in Maryland in 1990; state police denied the application based on an arrest record whose dispositions were unclear, though arrest alone was not a statutory disqualification. She won administrative and state-court review, then sued the responsible officers under § 1983. Did the mistaken denial state a substantive-due-process claim, and were the officers entitled to qualified immunity?

**Stone's disposition.** Join the unanimous result: the mistaken application of state licensing law does not become a federal substantive-due-process violation merely because it was unauthorized or arbitrary under state law, given that the state-review system completely corrected the error and Love pleaded no procedural-due-process claim; the officers receive qualified immunity because no sufficiently specific body of law in 1990 clearly established that the Second and Fourteenth Amendments protected acquisition of a handgun against denial based solely on an unresolved arrest record.

**Reasoning.** Stone's separate writing, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas, reaches the underlying arms question without commanding a majority. The right belongs to an individual claimant and is not merely a derivative entitlement of state governments or organized militia units; current militia enrollment is not required to invoke it. Acquisition is connected to keeping — a nominal right to possess an arm would be hollow if government could categorically prevent every lawful means of acquiring one, though this does not make every commercial condition unconstitutional. The arm matters: Love sought an ordinary handgun, not a fully automatic machinegun or an unusually destructive device, beginning the distinction between ordinary personal arms and materially different weapon categories. The licensing ground matters: an unresolved arrest record is not an adjudicated violent predicate, and government cannot transform an accusation into the equivalent of a conviction merely by calling the applicant risky. The remedy remains separate from the right — even where Stone believes the constitutional right exists, the officers cannot be held personally liable under law that was not clearly established in 1990.

**What this establishes.** Four ideas planted for later use without prematurely creating a complete doctrine: the right is individual; States are operationally bound by it; ordinary handgun acquisition is within the constitutional field; and recognition of the right does not erase qualified immunity or ordinary licensing administration. *Love* does not establish a controlling majority for the individual-right proposition, a home-defense core, an operability requirement, a public-carry right, a universal licensing standard, a scrutiny tier, a restoration rule, or a damages entitlement, and it does not hold that police must approve an applicant whose record contains an actual legal disqualification.

### United States v. Hale, 978 F.2d 1016 (1996)

**Status.** Essential, narrow controlling holding.

**Historical questions.** Wilbur Hale was convicted on thirteen counts of possessing machineguns under § 922(o) and three counts involving unregistered firearms, after federal agents recovered fully automatic weapons, conversion equipment, receivers, and a silenced pistol. Does § 922(o) violate the Second Amendment as applied to his post-1986 possession, and does the statute exceed the commerce power?

**Stone's disposition.** Join the unanimous as-applied result that § 922(o) does not violate the Second Amendment for post-1986 possession of fully automatic machineguns, and join the Commerce Clause holding that Congress may close the post-1986 civilian machinegun transfer market and include possession within that closure when possession and transfer are functionally inseparable.

**Reasoning.** Stone, joined again by Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas, states an individual-right rationale that still lacks a majority. Begin with the arm, not a universal scrutiny tier — even under an individual-right understanding, the right does not necessarily cover every weapon falling within the broadest linguistic definition of "arms." Fully automatic operation is constitutionally material: these weapons fire repeatedly through a single trigger activation, with sustained-fire characteristics materially different from an ordinary handgun or semiautomatic firearm. Resolve only the category presented — post-1986 civilian machineguns may remain outside the ordinary-arms category without announcing a universal test for every firearm, accessory, or firing mechanism. The Commerce Clause reasoning stays independent: the law survives not because machineguns are frightening or nationally important, but because possession is a necessary anti-evasion component of a direct national transfer-market prohibition; excluding possession would permit concealment, retention, acquisition, and other means of defeating the market prohibition, and generalized effects of gun violence, insurance costs, fear, or lost productivity remain insufficient. *Love*'s common-arms-for-home-defense view is repeated but remains noncontrolling.

**What this establishes.** One narrow category placed outside the eventual protected core, before that core is defined, so that later recognition of an individual right cannot be misconstrued as a right to possess every weapon. Ordinary handguns, semiautomatic rifles, ordinary shotguns, home possession, operability, public carry, licensing, training, ranges, every other automatic-weapon restriction, and whether legislatively labeled "military-style" weapons fall outside the right all remain undecided; the machinegun category must not later be extended to semiautomatic firearms merely through rhetoric.

## OT1996: structural support

### Printz v. United States, 521 U.S. 898 (1997)

**Status.** Structural foundation, not a Second Amendment merits case.

**Historical questions.** Did the federal command requiring state and local chief law-enforcement officers to conduct background-check work under the Brady Act violate the anti-commandeering principle?

**Stone's disposition.** Join the holding invalidating the federal command.

**Reasoning.** *Printz*'s significance here is anti-commandeering, not the substantive right to possess a firearm. Stone's later distinction, applied repeatedly in *Fraternal Order of Police* and *Gillespie*, is exact: Congress may directly regulate an individual who possesses a firearm, provided it has an enumerated-power basis; Congress may not require a State or its officers to administer a federal program merely because the subject involves firearms; the practical effect of federal law on state employment qualifications does not itself amount to commandeering; and the federal government cannot evade *Printz* by describing compelled state administration as ordinary regulation.

## OT1997: stabilizing the machinegun rule

### United States v. Rybar, 103 F.3d 273 (1997)

**Status.** Essential, unanimous at its narrow scope.

**Historical questions.** Raymond Rybar, a federally licensed firearms dealer, entered conditional guilty pleas to two § 922(o) machinegun-possession counts arising from gun-show transactions and challenged the law under the Commerce Clause and Second Amendment. Does § 922(o) survive both challenges as applied to his possession?

**Stone's disposition.** Join the unanimous holding that § 922(o) does not violate the Second Amendment as applied to Rybar's possession, and that possession may be included in the closed post-1986 civilian machinegun market because it is reasonably necessary to prevent evasion or material impairment of that market, following *Hale*. His two possession convictions stand; the dismissed National Firearms Act transfer counts and the constitutional status of ordinary firearms remain outside the judgment.

**Reasoning.** *Rybar* is not meant to enlarge *Hale* — its job is to stabilize it. The Court now has a unanimous narrow rule: fully automatic machineguns involved in post-1986 civilian market transactions may be prohibited. The transactional setting strengthens anti-evasion, since gun-show possession and transfer conduct directly implicate the market Congress closed, unlike a theory resting on remote downstream consequences. Unanimity is purchased through narrowness — the Court need not agree on every historical or theoretical proposition about the Second Amendment, only that this arm, in this regulatory setting, may be prohibited. Ordinary handguns, ordinary semiautomatic firearms, home possession, operability, carry, and licensing are all affirmatively preserved as open.

**What this establishes.** By the end of OT1997, a stable machinegun rule exists, but the simulation still lacks a controlling ordinary-arms core. The right is being developed category by category, not from an all-weapons presumption.

## OT1999: the first person-based disqualification branch

### Fraternal Order of Police v. United States, 173 F.3d 898 (2000)

**Status.** Essential.

**Historical questions.** The Fraternal Order of Police challenged § 922(g)(9), which prohibits firearm possession by a person convicted of a qualifying misdemeanor crime of domestic violence, on the absence of an official-use exemption, equal protection, the Second Amendment, congressional commerce authority, and its effect on state and local law-enforcement officers. Does the statute survive?

**Stone's disposition.** Join the unanimous rule that a constitutionally adequate conviction for domestic violence involving actual or attempted physical force, or threatened use of a deadly weapon, supplies an adjudicated danger predicate sufficient to prohibit firearm possession — including government-issued duty firearms — while the conviction remains legally operative; the Constitution requires no second present-danger hearing, no five- or ten-year constitutional expiration date, no automatic periodic reconsideration, and no general constitutional right to restoration. Join the equal-protection holding that Congress does not violate equal protection by withholding the official-use exemption from domestic-violence misdemeanants while making it available under other firearm disabilities, and the anti-commandeering holding that direct regulation of individual possession is not commandeering. On the Commerce Clause, join the eight-Justice requirement (Thomas not joining) that the government genuinely establish the offense-specific connection to commerce on which federal power depends.

**Reasoning.** The predicate is adjudicated, not resting on an arrest, allegation, untested report, bureaucratic suspicion, or general risk score — a court of competent jurisdiction has entered a legally operative conviction through constitutionally adequate procedures. The predicate is violent, tied to actual or attempted physical force or threatened use of a deadly weapon, not a generic misdemeanor rule. The relationship is specific: domestic abuse presents a defined context in which access to a firearm may materially intensify an already adjudicated pattern of violence or threat. The legal effect supplies the duration boundary — the disability continues while the conviction remains legally operative, and an applicable pardon, expungement, set-aside, restoration law, or successful attack removing the predicate's legal force may remove this justification. Government employment does not erase the predicate; issuance of a firearm by a police department does not transform the person into a constitutionally different claimant. On equal protection, the cleaner rationale is institutional rather than a judgment that domestic-violence misdemeanants are less worthy than felons: domestic violence had often been prosecuted through misdemeanor provisions that did not trigger older firearm disabilities, and Congress could close that specific gap. On commerce, this is a deliberate simulation departure from the broadest real-world "minimal nexus" approach — an object's decades-old crossing of a state line cannot become a permanent federal jurisdictional passport.

**What this establishes.** The first person-based branch, not a general dangerousness exception: an individual constitutional right can coexist with a carefully defined disqualification, but the right is not so absolute that an adjudicated violent domestic-abuse conviction becomes constitutionally irrelevant, and government may not disarm anyone it labels dangerous without a defined category and constitutionally adequate predicate. The rule does not establish a disability based on a nonviolent offense, a mere arrest, an accusation, a civil restraining order, unrelated status, or a conviction that has ceased to be legally operative, and it preserves applicable pardon, expungement, restoration, set-aside, and collateral-attack routes.

### Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis, 185 F.3d 693 (2000)

**Status.** Essential, narrow.

**Historical questions.** Jerald Gillespie, a police officer of more than twenty-five years, lost his ability to carry a firearm under § 922(g)(9) after a misdemeanor battery conviction involving his former wife, threatening his position because the department required officers to be armed. He challenged the law under the Commerce Clause, Tenth Amendment, equal protection, and Second Amendment.

**Stone's disposition.** Hold that direct federal regulation of Gillespie's possession is not commandeering merely because he is a municipal police officer, reiterate that federal authority requires a genuine offense-specific commerce connection, and grant no reinstatement or other relief on those theories. Do not resolve an independent Second Amendment claim.

**Reasoning.** *Fraternal Order of Police* already established the controlling violent-conviction branch; a second broad merits opinion would add little and risk confusing employment consequences with the constitutional rule. Stone's goals here are narrower: confirm that direct regulation of an individual is not the same as commandeering a State; confirm that a state employer's decision to require armed service remains the State's own decision; preserve the requirement of a real commerce nexus; avoid creating a constitutional entitlement to a police position or official-use exception; and demonstrate that the Court does not repeat a constitutional holding merely because another litigant presents the same substantive concern.

**What this establishes.** No expansion of *Fraternal Order of Police* to nonviolent convictions, no decision on restoration, and no holding that employment consequences are irrelevant in every constitutional case. It simply recognizes that a generally applicable federal possession rule does not become commandeering because state employment makes firearm possession occupationally necessary.

## OT2001: the optional temporary protective-order branch

### United States v. Emerson, 270 F.3d 203 (2002)

**Status.** Proposed and optional. Historical certiorari disposition: cert. denied, 536 U.S. 907 (2002).

**Historical questions.** Emerson was indicted under § 922(g)(8) for possessing a firearm while subject to a domestic-violence restraining order entered after a state-court evidentiary hearing at which his wife testified about threats; the order prohibited threats and bodily injury. The district court dismissed the indictment on Second and Fifth Amendment grounds and the Fifth Circuit reversed and remanded, so the case reaches the certiorari stage before any final conviction. The statute applied only when the order followed a hearing with actual notice and an opportunity to participate, restrained harassment, stalking, threats, or comparable conduct involving an intimate partner or child, and contained either a credible-threat finding or an explicit prohibition on force expected to cause bodily injury.

**Stone's disposition.** Reject the facial and as-applied Second Amendment objection to the qualifying temporary disability; reject the due-process challenge on the established hearing record; reverse the dismissal of the indictment and remand, permitting the prosecution to proceed on the condition that the Government must still prove all statutory elements, any knowledge required by governing law, and — departing from the real case's reliance on the firearm's prior interstate movement — a genuine offense-specific commerce connection.

**Reasoning.** *Fraternal Order of Police* rests on a criminal conviction; a civil protective order is materially different, since the proceeding may use different burdens of proof, the order may be temporary, the restrained person may not have been convicted of anything, some orders follow contested hearings while others are entered ex parte, some contain actual danger findings while others are routine or consent-based, and duration varies — the conviction rule cannot simply be copied. Stone should uphold the temporary disability as applied only when actual notice was given; the person had a meaningful opportunity to appear, present evidence, use counsel when governing law permits, and contest the allegations; the order contains an individualized judicial finding of a credible threat or expressly prohibits actual, attempted, or threatened physical force expected to cause bodily injury; the order remains currently valid and operative; and the federal disability's duration is coextensive with the order's, ending when it ends absent a different, independently sufficient predicate. No single element should suffice alone: notice without a danger-linked order is insufficient, a protective label without meaningful process is insufficient, a danger finding from an expired order is insufficient, a generic divorce injunction should not become a firearm disability merely because it directs both parties to behave lawfully, and later misconduct cannot retroactively cure a constitutionally inadequate order. On due process, the hearing requirement and opportunity to participate ordinarily supply the necessary process, but Stone should expressly reserve an ex parte order lacking prompt and meaningful review, an order entered without actual notice, a nominal hearing at which participation was not genuinely available, an order extended indefinitely without review, and an order whose qualifying findings are absent, stale, or legally vacated. On commerce, this is the most important risk in a simple *Emerson* recommendation: the real case treated prior interstate movement of the firearm as sufficient, but the simulation's *Fraternal Order of Police* rule rejects a remote historical movement performing no present constitutional work, so Stone cannot casually affirm federal power merely because the pistol crossed a state line at some past date. Because the case arrives before final conviction, this disposition is procedurally workable and does not need to adjudge Emerson guilty.

**What this establishes.** One carefully bounded category — a temporary firearm disability may rest on a currently operative, danger-linked protective order entered after actual notice and meaningful opportunity to participate — without creating a general authority to disarm anyone government considers dangerous. Left open: ex parte orders, emergency orders before a hearing, delayed post-order review, orders lacking a threat finding or force prohibition, routine mutual restraining orders, permanent disability after expiration, non-domestic civil orders, general dangerousness statutes, red-flag procedures with materially different safeguards, the treatment of firearms acquired before entry of the order, and property-transfer mechanics during the temporary period.

## OT2007: the essential home-defense core

### District of Columbia v. Heller, 554 U.S. 570 (2008) — "Heller I"

**Status.** Absolutely essential.

**Historical questions.** The District prohibited registration of handguns and required lawful firearms in the home to remain unloaded and disassembled or bound by a trigger-lock mechanism; Heller sought to keep an operable handgun in his home. Does the Second Amendment protect an individual right to possess a firearm for traditionally lawful purposes, particularly self-defense in the home, against both the prohibition and the disabling requirement?

**Stone's disposition.** A law-abiding natural person has a constitutional right to possess an ordinary handgun in the home for immediate lawful self-defense. Government may not categorically prohibit possession of that arm in the home, and may not require every lawful home firearm to remain continuously unloaded, disassembled, locked, or otherwise unavailable for immediate lawful defensive use. Objective licensing and registration may remain available when they are genuine administrative measures and do not operate as discretionary or practical prohibitions. Invalidate the categorical handgun prohibition as applied to ordinary home possession; invalidate the continuous inoperability requirement insofar as it prevents immediate lawful self-defense; direct the District to permit Heller to register the handgun and issue the necessary license if he is otherwise qualified under valid objective standards; leave severable and unchallenged provisions intact.

**Reasoning.** Stone should not re-litigate whether the right is individual — the simulation's operational premise already answers that, and a long historical contest over militia-only versus individual-right theories would waste the vehicle and destabilize earlier development. He should focus on what is still missing, working through the same sequence used throughout this build: identify the claimant (Heller is not subject to a violent conviction, active protective order, or another established disqualification; "law-abiding" describes the absence of an independently established disqualifying predicate, not an unlimited delegation to legislatures); identify the arm (a handgun is an ordinary personal firearm commonly selected for lawful home protection, materially different from the fully automatic machineguns adjudicated in *Hale* and *Rybar* — protection should not be defined solely through current ownership numbers, since government could suppress an arm and then rely on the suppression to prove uncommonness); identify the purpose (immediate lawful self-defense is central, without creating an unrestricted right to use force — the law of necessity, imminence, proportionality, defense of others, and unlawful aggression remains intact, since the Amendment protects possession and readiness of the arm, not substantive self-defense law); identify the place (the home is the strongest application, being where lawful defense of the person, family, and dwelling is most acute and where public-order concerns associated with carriage are lowest, without deciding whether the same protection extends identically to streets, vehicles, parks, businesses, schools, or courthouses); measure the burden (the District's law does not merely regulate manner, storage, registration, or training — it removes the ordinary handgun entirely and renders remaining lawful firearms unavailable for the immediate purpose that gives the home application practical value, and the right cannot be reduced to theoretical ownership of an unusable object); and avoid a universal scrutiny tier (a total prohibition on the central application fails because it destroys the protected right; later regulations should be classified according to claimant, arm, purpose, place, predicate, and practical burden, not a single strict- or intermediate-scrutiny label). On licensing, the District may retain an objective system provided the criteria are clear and lawful, officials lack open-ended discretion to deny qualified applicants, applications are processed within a reasonable time, fees do not price ordinary people out of the right, the license is not conditioned on surrender of the protected core, and Heller receives the license if he satisfies valid objective conditions — every registration requirement need not be decided in this one case.

**What this establishes.** The hard center of the doctrine. Everything before this case defines exclusions or disqualification branches; everything after it asks whether regulation leaves this core meaningfully intact. Preserved without enlargement: *Hale* and *Rybar* for fully automatic machineguns, *Fraternal Order of Police* for legally operative violent domestic-abuse convictions, *Emerson* (if adopted) for qualifying temporary protective orders, and genuine licensing and safety administration. Left open: public carry, open versus concealed carry, sensitive places, semiautomatic rifles, magazines, commercial sale conditions, training requirements, range access, detailed registration mechanics, storage rules that preserve meaningful immediate access, minors, nonviolent criminal predicates, mental-health predicates, corporate claimants, and the full treatment of ammunition.

## OT2009: incorporation without a redundant case

### McDonald v. City of Chicago, 561 U.S. 742 (2010)

**Status.** Recommended: do not take.

**Historical questions.** The real case's principal function was to hold that the right recognized in *Heller* applies against state and local governments through the Fourteenth Amendment.

**Stone's disposition.** Decline the case.

**Reasoning.** The simulation already operates on the premise that States are bound. *Love* introduced Stone's incorporation position, and the controlling handoff expressly directs that the incorporated right be treated as part of the simulation's architecture. Taking *McDonald* would either duplicate an answered issue or invite an unnecessary dispute over the Due Process Clause and the Privileges or Immunities Clause. The formal Holdings Register's failure to identify a majority case that clearly accomplished incorporation is a documentation gap, not a reason to manufacture litigation. The correct action is to preserve the handoff premise, add a transparent standards note identifying incorporation as an operational simulation premise, avoid misdescribing the *Love* concurrence as a controlling holding, and not take *McDonald* merely to clean up recordkeeping.

## OT2011: the first post-core regulatory case, and an optional access branch

### Heller v. District of Columbia, 670 F.3d 1244 (2011) — "Heller II"

**Status.** Essential.

**Historical questions.** After *Heller I*, the District enacted a scheme requiring registration, prohibiting firearms classified as "assault weapons," and prohibiting magazines holding more than ten rounds, along with numerous registration conditions including fingerprinting, photographs, background checks, training, and purchase-frequency restrictions. When does a firearm regulation administer the right, and when does it destroy a protected application?

**Stone's disposition.** Affirm basic objective handgun registration; reject automatic reliance on the machinegun cases for semiautomatic firearms; vacate or reverse the semiautomatic-rifle prohibition if common ordinary status is established on the record, otherwise remand it; remand the magazine limit unless the actual record supports its classification and fit; remand novel registration conditions for separate, provision-specific analysis; preserve severable valid provisions.

**Reasoning.** The roadmap directs Stone not to copy a universal scrutiny tier, not to equate semiautomatic firearms with machineguns merely because lawmakers use the phrase "assault weapon," and not to invent a constitutional ten-round limit. The Court should not speak of a single undifferentiated "firearms scheme" — registration, arm prohibition, magazine prohibition, fingerprints, fees, training, inspection, and purchase-frequency limits impose different burdens and serve different claimed functions, and each must receive its own classification, factual record, and remedy. On whether an arm is protected, the label "assault weapon" is not constitutional analysis; Stone should examine whether the firearm fires one round per trigger activation or fires continuously, whether the prohibited models are commonly possessed for lawful purposes, whether their ordinary operating characteristics differ materially from protected handguns and other semiautomatic firearms, whether the challenged features are functional, cosmetic, safety-related, or associated with a materially different destructive capacity, and whether the law prohibits an actual category of ordinary arms or only an unusual and distinct weapon — the *Hale* and *Rybar* machinegun rule cannot be extended simply by describing a semiautomatic rifle as "military-style." On practical operation, for registration and licensing Stone should ask whether the criteria are objective, whether approval is available to a qualified applicant, whether the process is timely, whether fees are limited to reasonable administration, whether the process is duplicative or recurrent without demonstrated need, whether it creates a practical barrier comparable to prohibition, whether noncompliance carries criminal consequences disproportionate to an administrative lapse, and whether there is review of a denial. Government must connect each particular provision to its stated administrative or safety function with actual support, not labels — this matters especially for categorically banned semiautomatic firearms, magazine limits, repeated registration, physical inspection requirements, training mandates, and purchase-frequency restrictions. A regulation cannot survive merely because some other firearm remains available, since that reasoning would allow government to eliminate one ordinary protected arm at a time; at the same time, not every inconvenience, recordkeeping requirement, or cost destroys the right, and the focus should be on whether the protected application remains meaningfully and practically available. On the magazine limit specifically, the Constitution contains no ten-round number; Stone should require evidence of ordinary magazine capacities supplied with protected firearms, prevalence in lawful possession, relationship to ordinary defensive use, whether the limit materially impairs the firearm's ordinary operation, the practical availability and consequences of compliant alternatives, the stated safety mechanism, and why ten rounds rather than another number is constitutionally and factually fitted — if the record does not answer those questions, remand is more honest than inventing a constitutional number. For novel registration conditions, the best disposition is mixed: uphold genuinely basic objective registration, but remand novel and materially burdensome conditions for provision-specific justification.

**What this establishes.** The line between regulation and destruction without a general license for judicial policy balancing: government may administer the exercise of the right through objective, supported, and nonprohibitory measures, but may not use labels, cumulative burdens, or categorical prohibitions to eliminate an ordinary protected arm or the meaningful exercise of the home-defense core.

### Ezell v. City of Chicago, 651 F.3d 684 (2011)

**Status.** Optional.

**Historical questions.** Chicago required firearm-permit applicants to complete live-fire range training while simultaneously prohibiting every firing range within city limits; the Seventh Circuit held the plaintiffs had a strong likelihood of success and were entitled to preliminary relief. May government make training a legal condition of possession while prohibiting every practical local means of completing it?

**Stone's disposition.** Enjoin enforcement of the total range ban, holding that the plaintiffs are likely to succeed on the merits, that the constitutional injury is irreparable, and that the total ban cannot remain in force pending final adjudication; permit the city to adopt and enforce supported safety, zoning, and operational rules; do not order the city to build, finance, or subsidize a range.

**Reasoning.** *Heller I* establishes possession and operability, but not necessarily a freestanding right to recreational shooting, commercial range operation, or every form of training. *Ezell* presents a narrower contradiction: government itself makes live-fire training a legal condition of firearm possession and then prohibits every practical local means of satisfying that condition. Government may regulate range location, building construction, ventilation, lead containment, noise, hours, instructor qualifications, storage, security, age-appropriate supervision, and ordinary business operation — but it may not require firearm training as a condition of exercising the right while prohibiting every practical means of completing that training. The right to effective home possession also protects the practical opportunity to acquire and maintain the basic proficiency reasonably necessary for safe lawful use, a derivative protection limited to what makes the recognized core effective. Avoid a broad recreational-right theory — the constitutional defect is not that every resident is entitled to a nearby shooting club, but the government-created contradiction between mandatory training and universal prohibition. A city cannot point to the theoretical legality of training while making the required activity impossible within the jurisdiction that imposes the condition, and cannot impose a prerequisite and categorically shift every means of compliance outside its boundaries, particularly where it is regulating the exercise of a constitutional right by its own residents. Because the real decision concerns a preliminary injunction, Stone should not use it to enter a final, exhaustive constitutional code governing every firing-range regulation; if the simulation later needs a final merits rule, a later final-judgment vehicle would be cleaner than treating this preliminary record as conclusive.

**What this establishes.** A practical-access branch, added only if the simulation affirmatively wants it: government may not nullify the right indirectly through contradictory prerequisites. Left open: purely recreational shooting, public subsidies, a right to a range in a preferred neighborhood, regulation of outdoor versus indoor ranges, particular buffer distances, age restrictions, commercial insurance conditions, advanced tactical training, and training involving weapons outside the protected category.

## OT2015: optional detailed licensing doctrine

### Heller v. District of Columbia, 801 F.3d 264 (2015) — "Heller III"

**Status.** Default recommendation: skip.

**Historical questions.** The actual scheme at issue included long-gun registration, fingerprints, photographs, in-person appearance, physical presentation of the firearm, periodic re-registration, fees, safety training, a test on local firearm law, and a one-pistol-per-thirty-days limit; the D.C. Circuit upheld some conditions and invalidated others based on the evidentiary record.

**Stone's disposition, if the case is taken.** Uphold basic objective registration if it is objective, timely, inexpensive, and supported by a real administrative function (the government must still identify why the requirement is useful and nonprohibitory, not simply invoke *Heller I*). Uphold one-time identity-verification measures that genuinely improve accurate identification and prevent fraudulent registration without repeated unnecessary processing. Uphold in-person appearance only when genuinely necessary to complete valid identity verification, not as a recurring burden. Permit reasonable cost-recovery fees, invalid when they materially exceed cost recovery, accumulate across repeated requirements, make ordinary possession unaffordable, finance unrelated government activity, or function as a tax on the right. Permit accessible safety training if practical means of satisfying it remain available. Invalidate physical presentation of the firearm absent evidence that inspection is necessary and materially advances the identified function. Invalidate periodic re-registration unless government explains why less burdensome tools — reporting address changes, reporting loss or transfer, lawful background checks — are inadequate. Uphold a legal-knowledge test only if government identifies the safety or compliance function and provides accessible materials, fair retesting, and review; it cannot become a discretionary civics examination. Invalidate the one-pistol-per-thirty-days limit absent an actual connection to trafficking or circumvention, since the plural right is not exhausted by a single firearm.

**Reasoning.** By default the case is unnecessary: *Heller I* establishes the core, and *Heller II* can establish that objective registration may coexist with the right and that categorical arm prohibitions require genuine classification, which is enough for a stable doctrine. *Heller III* goes much further into administrative detail, and taking it risks turning the simulation into a licensing manual — the roadmap says to use it only if detailed registration law is affirmatively desired. If it is taken, licensing cannot become disguised prohibition through cumulative administration; every burden must be evaluated in actual operation, not merely by reading each requirement in isolation.

**What this establishes, if taken.** That licensing cannot become disguised prohibition through cumulative administration, while preserving the possibility that a differently designed and better-supported provision could still be valid. The stronger project choice remains to stop with *Heller II* unless this level of detail is genuinely needed.

## Questions that should remain unresolved through OT2015

Unless a genuine later vehicle is deliberately selected: public carry; whether government must allow open carry, concealed carry, or one of the two; sensitive-place boundaries, including vehicles and parking lots, schools, universities, courthouses, legislatures, polling places, transit systems, and public demonstrations; nonviolent felony disabilities; old misdemeanors unrelated to violence; drug-user restrictions; mental-health restrictions; emergency ex parte risk orders; minors and age restrictions; ammunition acquisition and taxes; magazine limits beyond the record in *Heller II*; commercial-sale conditions; dealer licensing; waiting periods; serial-number rules; homemade firearms; transportation requirements; safe-storage rules that preserve meaningful access; corporate Second Amendment rights; advanced or recreational training; a general right to restoration; and a universal doctrinal test for every arm or claimant.
