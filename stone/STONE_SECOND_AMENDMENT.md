# Stone tracker: Second Amendment

Scope: the Second Amendment right to keep and bear arms and who holds it; its application to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment; federal and state firearm laws as they bear on the right, including the possession bans of 18 U.S.C. §922(g) and their restoration rules, licensing, dealers and background checks, storage, training, carry, transport and sensitive places, categories of arms (machineguns, destructive devices and other National Firearms Act weapons, semiautomatic arms, magazines), accessories, parts kits and ATF rules; and the line between the right and Congress's commerce power. The commerce power itself, including Lopez, the machinegun and §922(g) commerce holdings and Printz's commandeering rule, appears here only as it bears on the right. Cases construing the federal statutes that punish using or carrying a gun during another crime or that enhance sentences for prior convictions (18 U.S.C. §924(c) and §924(e): Smith, Deal, Custis, Bailey, Muscarello, Castillo), and evidence rules in §922(g) trials (Old Chief), are not covered here. This document records only what the Court has decided and what it must still answer.

## 0. Stone's intended doctrine (the character's goal, not the Court's law)

This is the framework Stone is working toward. It is not law. The Court adopted its core in United States v. Wright (1998): Stage 1, the three tiers of review and the home-possession floor, with five votes for the framework. Section 3.3 states exactly what Wright adopted and what it reserved, including public-carry licensing, training and recertification (part 10 below). The distance between this section and section 3.3 is the work left to do.

**Core-Preservation Review: Stone-Zsela’s Second Amendment Framework**

The Second Amendment protects an individual right whose exercise may be regulated but whose protected function must remain meaningfully available.

The governing principle is a **sliding scale of constitutional burden, governmental justification, and regulatory fit**:

> The more seriously a regulation interferes with protected keeping, bearing, or lawful use of arms, the stronger the government’s justification must be and the closer the relationship between the restriction and the harm it addresses. Government cannot preserve the right in name while making its exercise practically unavailable.

The framework has two stages: **constitutional coverage** and **constitutional justification**. Within the second stage, the actual burden determines the applicable level of review.

**1. Stage One: Identify the protected activity**

The initial question is whether the challenged law interferes with conduct protected by the Second Amendment.

The right belongs to individuals and does not depend on enrollment in an organized militia. Its central protection includes possession of an ordinary, functional defensive firearm in the home. Its protection also extends to activities necessary to make keeping and bearing arms meaningful.

The court identifies precisely what the person seeks to do:

- Keep an arm for lawful defense.
- Acquire an arm through a lawful transaction.
- Obtain ammunition necessary for its lawful operation.
- Maintain, repair, or train with an arm.
- Transport an arm for acquisition, repair, training, lawful use, or relocation.
- Carry an arm for lawful defense outside the home.
- Possess or use a particular type of arm, feature, or accessory.

These activities are related, but their constitutional treatment depends on their function and the restriction imposed.

Necessary acquisition cannot be separated from possession so completely that government permits ownership while preventing people from obtaining usable arms. Likewise, permission to possess a firearm means little if necessary ammunition is effectively unavailable.

Transportation of an unloaded, secured firearm is distinguishable from carrying a loaded firearm for immediate defensive use. A rule justified for one activity does not automatically justify the same restriction on the other.

Coverage is determined through constitutional text, structure, history, and applicable precedent. The challenged prohibition cannot establish its own constitutionality by defining the protected conduct as unlawful. Nor does a regulatory label, such as “military,” “dangerous,” or “assault,” resolve whether an arm or activity receives protection.

If the conduct falls outside constitutional coverage, the Second Amendment claim ends. Other constitutional objections remain independently available.

**2. Identify the actual burden**

If protected activity is implicated, the court determines what the restriction does to that activity in practice.

Relevant considerations include:

- Whether the law regulates the manner of exercise or prevents exercise altogether.
- Whether it affects ordinary defensive arms or a specified characteristic associated with a particular danger.
- The restriction’s duration and geographic reach.
- Its costs, delays, administrative demands, and conditions.
- Whether exceptions are realistically available.
- Whether remaining lawful options preserve the protected activity.
- Whether requirements challenged together create a substantially greater burden than each would impose separately.

These are considerations for judgment, not mandatory elements that every litigant must separately prove.

The inquiry concerns the law’s text and reasonably established operation. A nominal licensing system that rarely issues licenses may function as a prohibition. A formally available training course may provide no meaningful access if its cost, location, or scheduling makes completion practically impossible for ordinary qualified applicants.

The existence of some alternative does not automatically answer the constitutional objection. The alternative must preserve the relevant protected function. At the same time, the right does not guarantee freedom from every inconvenience or entitlement to every preferred method of exercise.

**3. Stage Two: Select the level of review**

The framework uses three defined levels of review. The sliding scale governs how the court classifies the burden and evaluates justification and fit within the applicable level.

| Actual constitutional burden | Governing standard |
|---|---|
| **Core deprivation or severe interference:** the restriction eliminates, nearly eliminates, or seriously defeats a central protected activity. | **Strict scrutiny** |
| **Substantial regulation:** the restriction materially conditions or limits protected activity while leaving meaningful exercise available. | **Intermediate scrutiny** |
| **Incidental regulation:** the restriction imposes a minor burden that does not materially obstruct protected activity. | **Deferential reasonableness review** |

The government’s description of the law does not control its classification. An effective prohibition receives demanding review even when described as an administrative requirement or safety measure.

Location informs the analysis but does not settle it. A restriction outside the home does not automatically receive deferential review.

Likewise, invoking the Second Amendment does not automatically trigger strict scrutiny. The court must identify the actual protected activity and the seriousness of the interference.

**4. Strict scrutiny: Core deprivation and severe interference**

The government must establish:

1. A compelling governmental interest.
2. Narrow tailoring between the restriction and that interest.
3. The absence of a comparably effective, materially less restrictive alternative.

The government must explain why the severe burden is necessary. A general assertion of public safety does not establish that a particular restriction satisfies these requirements.

The court examines the specific danger, the restriction’s operation, the people and conduct it reaches, and the practical availability of measures that would address the danger while preserving substantially more protected activity.

A restriction effectively eliminating meaningful lawful public carry receives strict scrutiny. A restriction seriously obstructing ordinary defensive possession in the home receives the same demanding review.

There is also an **irreducible core of protection**: government cannot abolish meaningful possession of ordinary, functional defensive arms in the home by ordinary qualified individuals. A universal requirement that renders those arms continuously unavailable for immediate lawful defense defeats that protection.

This constitutional floor does not make every weapon, possession circumstance, or individual immune from regulation. Restrictions directed at particular dangers must receive their own analysis.

**5. Intermediate scrutiny: Substantial regulation**

The government must establish:

1. An important governmental objective.
2. A substantial, supportable relationship between the restriction and that objective.
3. A burden that does not unnecessarily or effectively eliminate the protected activity.

The justification must address the regulation actually imposed. The court evaluates whether the rule materially advances its objective and whether its burdens extend substantially beyond what that explanation supports.

Intermediate scrutiny permits reasonable legislative judgments and supported predictions. It does not require proof that the legislature selected the single least restrictive conceivable policy.

Nevertheless, practical alternatives may expose an unjustified mismatch between the restriction and its purpose. The government cannot defend substantial interference through speculation alone when the decisive factual premises are genuinely disputed.

A law that effectively destroys access cannot remain in this category merely because it takes the form of a licensing condition.

**6. Deferential reasonableness: Incidental regulation**

Minor regulations that leave protected activity realistically available receive substantial legislative latitude.

The requirement must serve a legitimate governmental objective and bear a reasonable relationship to that objective. Ordinary experience, the regulation’s design, and reasonable predictions may provide sufficient support.

This level can accommodate routine administration, modest commercial requirements, and equipment or handling rules that impose no meaningful obstruction.

Deference does not protect arbitrary restrictions, discriminatory administration, or requirements whose cumulative operation materially burdens the right.

If the established operation reveals substantial interference, the court must reconsider the classification and apply the appropriate heightened standard.

**7. Identify the harm and explain the regulatory fit**

At every level, the analysis must connect the restriction to an identifiable governmental objective.

Public safety can supply an important or compelling interest. Its invocation does not independently prove the validity of every firearms regulation.

The court identifies the particular concern, such as:

- Armed violence by persons with qualifying violent histories.
- Accidental discharge or unsafe handling.
- Diversion into unlawful transactions.
- A weapon’s particular operating characteristics.
- Armed coercion in institutions requiring secure proceedings.
- Unauthorized access by children or other prohibited users.

The court then examines how the restriction addresses that concern:

- Does the regulated characteristic bear the asserted relationship to the danger?
- Does the rule reach the relevant conduct, equipment, or category of persons?
- Do exceptions support or materially undermine the explanation?
- Does the restriction burden protected activity beyond what its justification supports?
- Under the applicable scrutiny, do practical alternatives undermine the claimed need for the burden?

Neither overinclusion nor underinclusion automatically invalidates a law. Government may address problems incrementally. But as the burden increases, unexplained mismatches become less acceptable.

**8. Allocate evidentiary responsibility according to the standard**

The claimant must identify the protected activity and establish the interference necessary to support the challenge. A burden apparent from the law’s text or necessary operation does not require additional proof of facts already evident.

Under intermediate and strict scrutiny, the government bears the burden of establishing the required justification and fit.

Relevant support may include:

- Established or stipulated facts.
- Legislative and administrative materials.
- Historical experience.
- Reliable empirical research.
- Institutional knowledge.
- Reasonable inferences from the nature of the regulated conduct.

Scientific certainty is unnecessary. A formal legislative record, individualized hearing, or new evidentiary proceeding is not universally required.

The court must distinguish general legislative judgments from facts about a particular person. A sufficiently justified category based on qualifying violent convictions need not require a new personal dangerousness trial in every application. Conversely, an accusation cannot be treated as an adjudicated conviction, and a court cannot invent an individual’s history.

Unresolved material facts are handled through the governing procedural rules and burdens of proof. They are not filled with assumptions favoring either side.

**9. Apply the framework to the particular regulatory setting**

| Regulatory setting | Governing considerations |
|---|---|
| **Home possession and defensive availability** | Preserve meaningful access to ordinary, functional defensive arms. Evaluate whether restrictions defeat immediate lawful defense. |
| **Weapons, features, and accessories** | Identify the actual characteristic, its protected function, and its relationship to harm. Neither military utility nor a military label determines the result. |
| **Ammunition** | Recognize its necessary relationship to usable arms. Examine restrictions on particular characteristics separately from measures that effectively prevent lawful operation. |
| **Disqualification of persons** | Require a defensible relationship between the predicate and the danger addressed. Examine the reliability of the predicate, procedural protections, scope, and duration. |
| **Public carry and licensing** | Preserve a meaningful route for qualified individuals to carry, while permitting justified competence, safety, and eligibility requirements. |
| **Sensitive places** | Examine the place’s function and the particular need for security or freedom from armed coercion. A label cannot substitute for justification. |
| **Storage and handling** | Consider the danger addressed and whether the requirement preserves practical access for lawful defense. |
| **Acquisition and transportation** | Preserve the activities necessary to obtain, maintain, and lawfully use arms. Distinguish secured transportation from immediate defensive carriage. |
| **Manufacture, sale, transfers, records, and registration** | Evaluate actual costs, delays, availability, and administrative burdens. Registration systems also require adequate protection against disclosure and misuse. |

Danger-related disqualifications may rest on serious violent convictions, qualifying domestic-violence predicates, or judicial danger findings supported by adequate process.

An unrelated nonviolent offense, unresolved accusation, or administrative designation does not supply sufficient justification merely because the legislature calls it disqualifying.

Where restoration procedures exist, they must be genuinely available. The framework does not automatically create a new restoration procedure for every restriction.

Sensitive-place restrictions require attention to institutional function. Courts, legislatures, polling places, schools, and comparable settings can present particular security concerns. Treating virtually every publicly accessible location as sensitive must be evaluated as the resulting broad restriction on public carry.

Courts evaluate legislative lines; they do not invent magazine capacities, ammunition quantities, training hours, waiting periods, or storage specifications without a legal basis for doing so.

**10. Public-carry licensing, training, and recertification**

A system imposing substantial qualifications on public carry ordinarily receives intermediate scrutiny, provided it preserves meaningful access.

Permissible requirements can include:

- Background checks.
- Firearm-safety instruction.
- Written examinations.
- Demonstrated safe handling and practical proficiency.
- Periodic refresher training.
- Recertification before renewal.
- Compliance with otherwise valid weapon and modification restrictions.

Each requirement must have a genuine relationship to the relevant safety or eligibility objective.

A qualified applicant who satisfies lawful, objective requirements must receive the license. Officials cannot deny permission through standardless discretion, personal opposition to civilian carry, or an additional unwritten demand.

The system must operate within reasonable timeframes. Fees, training access, examination procedures, and renewal requirements must be assessed together when their combined effect is challenged.

Legislatures may choose reasonable training and renewal arrangements. Courts determine whether those arrangements satisfy the constitutional standard; they do not prescribe a preferred curriculum simply because another policy appears better.

If the system effectively prevents ordinary qualified individuals from carrying, its actual burden requires more demanding review.

**11. Keep history, precedent, governmental authority, and other rights distinct**

History informs constitutional meaning, protected purposes, and the character of permissible regulation. An identical historical restriction is not the exclusive condition of constitutional validity.

Historical practices must be examined in context. Past exclusion from arms ownership does not automatically justify continuing that exclusion.

Applicable precedent remains binding according to its actual holding, limitations, and effective date. A judicial preference or proposed doctrinal refinement does not itself change governing law.

When a departure is legally available and expressly proposed, the analysis must identify:

- The existing rule.
- The legal reason for changing it.
- The replacement rule and its boundaries.
- What remains intact.
- The relevant reliance interests.

Other constitutional questions require independent analysis. A legislature must possess authority to enact the regulation, and a valid exercise of legislative power must still respect the individual right. Equal protection, due process, and restrictions on searches and seizures retain their own standards.

Private property rights also require separate treatment. Recognition of a right against government does not automatically establish permission to carry on another person’s property.

**12. Reach the judgment and select the authorized remedy**

The framework supports a substantive decision whenever the legal questions, procedural posture, and established record permit one. It creates no automatic preference for invalidation, affirmance, avoidance, or remand.

The final analysis should answer:

1. **What precise provision or governmental action is challenged?**
2. **What protected activity does it affect?**
3. **What burden does it impose in its established operation?**
4. **Which level of review follows from that burden, and why?**
5. **What governmental objective justifies the restriction?**
6. **What evidence and reasoning establish or defeat the required fit?**
7. **Does the restriction satisfy the applicable standard and preserve the protected constitutional function?**
8. **What judgment and legally authorized relief follow?**

Relief must correspond to the constitutional defect and the claims properly before the court. Whether relief applies to a particular person, a provision, or a broader regulatory scheme depends on the governing rules for the challenge, severability, and remedies.

Further proceedings are appropriate when unresolved material issues require them. When the record and governing law establish the result, the court should decide it.

## 1. Completed cases

OT1991
- United States v. Thompson/Center Arms Co.
  > Held (no opinion of the Court, 5-4): A pistol and conversion kit with genuine lawful pistol and rifle uses, never assembled as a short-barreled rifle, is not clearly the making of that regulated firearm, so lenity resolves the doubt for the maker even in a tax refund case. Seven Justices agreed that making need not wait for final assembly.

OT1993
- Beecham v. United States; Jones v. United States
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 9-0): Whether expungement, pardon or restored civil rights erases a conviction for §922(g) purposes is decided under the law of the convicting jurisdiction, so a State's restoration of civil rights does not remove a federal conviction. Both judgments were affirmed.
- Staples v. United States
  > Held (Thomas for the Court, 7-2): To convict for possessing an unregistered machinegun under 26 U.S.C. §5861(d), the Government must prove the defendant knew the characteristics that made the weapon fire automatically, though not that it was unregistered or illegal. The conviction was reversed and remanded.

OT1994
- United States v. Lopez
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 5-4): The ban on possessing a gun in a school zone exceeds the commerce power, because the Court must find an interstate connection for the regulated class before aggregating effects, and chains through crime and education costs cannot supply it. The decision created no Second Amendment rule.

OT1995
- Love v. Pepersack
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court; six votes for the arms holdings, remand 5-4): The Second Amendment protects individuals without any militia tie, applies to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause, and covers acquisition necessary to protected keeping. The judgment affirming dismissal of Love's arms claim against the officers was vacated and remanded, with infringement and immunity undecided.
- United States v. Hale
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 8-1): Protection depends on the actual weapon, conduct and restriction, and military usefulness neither removes an arm from the right nor exempts it from regulation, so the appellate affirmance of thirteen §922(o) machinegun convictions, which rested on a militia-tie premise, was vacated for a real arms analysis. The registration convictions were affirmed.

OT1996
- Printz v. United States; Mack v. United States
  > Held (Souter for 5 on Part I; 5-4 judgment): Congress is not categorically barred from requiring a state officer's appropriate, temporary, records-based help, such as the Brady Act's interim background checks, when federal law supplies the rule and federal authorities keep coercive enforcement. The judgments were vacated and remanded on the officers' fiscal objections, with no majority rationale for that remand.

OT1997
- United States v. Rybar
  > Held (Kennedy for the Court; 9-0 on commerce, 8-1 on the arms remand): A seller of machineguns at gun shows may invoke the personal arms right, and §922(o) validly reaches his possession under the commerce power. Because the court below required a militia tie, the affirmance of the possession convictions was vacated and remanded for a real arms analysis.
- United States v. Kirk
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 9-0): Section 922(o) may reach possession of a locally converted post-1986 machinegun without proof of an interstate transaction, because the possession ban is reasonably necessary to prevent evasion of the national restriction on that stock. No Second Amendment claim was decided.
- Bryan v. United States
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 7-2): Willful dealing in firearms without a federal license requires proof that the defendant knew his dealing was unlawful, not that he knew of the licensing requirement. The conviction was affirmed.
- Caron v. United States
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 6-3): When a State restores civil rights but still bars the person from carrying handguns outside home or business, the restoration does not remove the conviction under §921(a)(20). The federal firearm consequences then reach even the rifles and shotguns the State allows.

OT1998
- United States v. Wright
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 9-0; framework by five votes): Restrictions on the arms right get Core-Preservation Review: coverage first, then justification scaled to the practical burden (strict, intermediate or deferential review), and government may never abolish meaningful home possession of ordinary functional defensive arms. The affirmance of the machinegun count was vacated for that analysis, and the pipe-bomb registration conviction was affirmed.
- Fraternal Order of Police v. United States
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 9-0): Section 922(g)(9)'s ban on possession after a qualifying domestic-violence misdemeanor is a substantial regulation of particular persons that satisfies intermediate scrutiny, and neither the Second Amendment nor equal protection requires an exception for officers' service weapons. The judgment extending that exception was reversed.
- Hiley v. Barrett
  > Held (O'Connor for 8, 9-0 judgment): Section 922(g)(9) survives the preserved equal protection, ex post facto, bill of attainder, Tenth Amendment and commerce challenges. Rejecting the lower court's collective-right premise does not revive Hiley's abandoned Second Amendment claim, so the dismissal was affirmed.
- Peoples Rights Organization, Inc. v. City of Columbus
  > Held (Thomas for the Court; 9-0 to 7-2 by component): Columbus's assault-weapon definitions keyed to actual compatible magazines, shotgun capacity and original handgun design are not facially vague, but its two handgun-modification branches are invalid because they never say what relationship makes a handgun prohibited. No Second Amendment judgment was made.
- United States v. Franklyn; United States v. Gonzalez
  > Held (Ginsburg for 8, 9-0 judgment): Section 922(o) reaches Franklyn's retained machinegun as an anti-evasion part of the post-1986 market ban, and §922(g)(1) reaches a convicted person's ammunition as a fitted counterpart to the ban on supplying convicted persons. Past interstate movement satisfies the enacted nexus but is not the constitutional basis.
- United States v. Griffin; United States v. Langley
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 9-0): Congress may bar qualifying convicted persons from possessing firearms under §922(g)(1) as a fitted counterpart to §922(d)(1)'s ban on supplying them. A gun's past interstate movement can satisfy the statute's nexus element but is not by itself the constitutional basis for regulating possession.

OT1999
- Fraternal Order of Police v. United States (return, 2000)
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 9-0): Section 922(g)(9) is within the commerce power as a fitted anti-evasion part of the ban on supplying firearms to persons with qualifying domestic-violence convictions, and applying it to state and local officers does not commandeer them. The Government must still prove the enacted commerce element in any prosecution.
- Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 9-0): The Second Amendment protects individuals, including keeping and bearing for work, but §922(g)(9)'s domestic-violence disability satisfies intermediate scrutiny on this official-use record and requires no exemption for police. It is also valid commerce legislation and does not commandeer the city.

### 2.3 Weapons, accessories and ATF rules

### OT2015: Caetano v. Massachusetts, 577 U.S. 411 (No. 14-10078)

Area: Arms invented after the founding; nonlethal arms; dangerous and unusual weapons.
Status: Framed. Hale, Rybar and Wright hold that military usefulness neither removes an arm from the guarantee nor exempts it from regulation, and Wright decides coverage from text, history and precedent without a closed catalogue of weapons. No decision addresses arms that did not exist in 1791, nonlethal arms or a "dangerous and unusual" category. Whether a lack of military usefulness can exclude an arm is open, since Love kept Miller's weapon-and-record inquiry and militia-related reasoning while dropping any enrollment requirement.

## 3. The doctrine as it stands

### 3.1 The arms right in plain terms

The Second Amendment protects "the right of the people to keep and bear Arms." This Court has settled who holds that right and how restrictions on it are judged, but it has applied that method to only one kind of law. Since 1995 the right has belonged to individuals, with no tie to a militia required, and has bound the States as well as the federal government (Love). Since 1998 a restriction is judged in two stages: first, whether the law touches protected keeping, bearing, acquisition, necessary ammunition or transport of arms; second, if it does, whether it is justified, with the required justification rising with the law's practical burden and with an absolute bar on abolishing meaningful home possession of ordinary functional defensive arms (Wright). The Court has upheld one disability under that method, the federal ban on possession by persons convicted of a qualifying domestic-violence misdemeanor (Fraternal Order of Police, Gillespie). Each machinegun case that raised the right has gone back to the lower courts to apply the method in the first instance. No case has yet decided whether any ban on a type of arm, licensing scheme, carry rule, sensitive-place rule, storage rule, training requirement or magazine limit is valid. Most of the Court's other firearms work concerns the statutes themselves (what counts as a machinegun or a regulated kit, when a conviction stops counting, what a defendant must know) and Congress's power to enact them. The doctrine is therefore thin beyond its foundations.

### 3.2 Who holds the right, and against whom

Doctrine now: the Second Amendment protects an individual's keeping and bearing of arms within its scope; formal militia enrollment, current service or preparation for organized military activity is not required (Love Part III-A, O'Connor, six votes; Hale, Rybar and Wright, unanimous on this point). The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause applies the guarantee to the States under the same standards that govern federal action. Cruikshank and Presser are overruled only insofar as they barred that application; the Court adopted neither total incorporation nor a Privileges or Immunities holding (Love Part III-B, six votes). Acquisition necessary to protected keeping is itself protected, so a State cannot defeat a claim because it blocked a purchase rather than seized a gun; but the right does not guarantee purchase from every seller or forbid licensing and investigation, and a violation of state licensing law is not by itself an infringement (Love Part III-C, six votes). Protection depends on the actual weapon, conduct and restriction. Military usefulness neither removes an arm from the guarantee nor exempts it from controls on manufacture, transfer, possession, taxation or registration (Hale Part III-A, unanimous). A commercial setting does not by itself erase the claim (Rybar), and keeping and bearing do not lose protection because they happen at work, though there is no independent right to an armed public job (FOP, Gillespie). The Ninth Amendment supplies no separate immunity (Wright). Rejecting a lower court's collective-right premise does not revive an arms claim the litigant abandoned (Hiley). Love left the officers' qualified immunity undecided.

Love v. Pepersack in plain terms. Maryland police denied April Love's application to buy a handgun because of an arrest record, allegedly under a practice of denying applicants on arrests alone, although an arrest was not a legal disqualification; she sued the officers for damages. The Fourth Circuit affirmed dismissal of her suit, and the real Supreme Court never decided the case; the real Court first recognized an individual right in Heller (2008) and applied it to the States in McDonald (2010). Here, in 1995, the Court (O'Connor, joined on the arms holdings by Stone, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas) held the right individual, applied it to the States and protected necessary acquisition. By 5-4 it vacated the judgment affirming dismissal of the arms claim and remanded; Stone alone would have held the arrest-only denial unconstitutional, and Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer would have affirmed.

### 3.3 How restrictions are judged: Core-Preservation Review

Doctrine now (Wright Part II-B, Stone, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, who adopt the whole framework):
- Stage 1 asks whether the law interferes with protected keeping, bearing, acquisition, necessary ammunition or transportation of arms. Coverage comes from text, history and the Court's precedents; there is no closed catalogue of arms.
- Stage 2 requires justification matching the severity of the law's practical operation. A core deprivation receives strict scrutiny: a compelling interest, narrow tailoring and no comparably effective, materially less restrictive alternative. A substantial regulation receives intermediate scrutiny: an important objective, a substantial relationship to it, and no unnecessary or effective elimination of the protected activity. An incidental regulation imposing no meaningful interference receives deferential reasonableness review, but arbitrary administration, discrimination and cumulative destruction of the right remain unconstitutional, and equipment rules, administrative procedures and minor requirements qualify only if they do not materially obstruct the right.
- Independently, government may not abolish meaningful possession of ordinary functional defensive arms in the home.
- Actual operation controls: calling a prohibition a safety measure or an administrative rule does not lower the review. Ammunition and transport are protected as incidents of keeping or bearing, not item by item. A ban on ordinary functional handguns in the home and the effective elimination of all lawful public carry illustrate core burdens.
- Reserved: public-carry licensing, safety instruction, examinations, demonstrated proficiency, refresher training and recertification. No numerical rule, militia exclusion, immunity for military weapons or list of prohibited arms is adopted.

Applications so far: the §922(g)(9) disability is a substantial regulation of particular persons that satisfies intermediate scrutiny on the developed official-use challenge. Preventing armed recurrence is important; the adjudicated force, attempted-force or threatened-deadly-weapon predicate in defined domestic relationships, counsel and jury safeguards, and statutory relief provisions supply the substantial connection; and the right does not compel an official-use exception (FOP, Souter, 9-0; applied to an individual officer in Gillespie, Stone, 9-0). FOP does not authorize disarmament for any misdemeanor, a mere allegation or an unrelated offense, and reserves an improperly classified conviction, an unavailable statutory safeguard and a materially different private-possession record. Registration of explosive destructive devices is an incidental regulation that passes reasonableness review (Wright Part III, the same five); eight Justices independently sustain it on the National Firearms Act's tax-administration basis. The tier and validity of the machinegun ban remain undecided.

United States v. Wright in plain terms. Wright pleaded guilty, reserving his constitutional objections, to possessing a machinegun and to possessing three unregistered pipe bombs. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed on the premise that the right required a tie to a state militia, and the real Supreme Court never decided the case. The real Court set no standard of review until Heller (2008), which declined to choose one while rejecting interest balancing, and Bruen (2022), which replaced means-end scrutiny with a test of text, history and tradition. Here, in 1998, the Court (Stone, joined in the framework by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg) adopted tiered Core-Preservation Review, unanimously vacated the affirmance of the machinegun count so the lower courts could apply both stages, and affirmed the pipe-bomb registration conviction.

### 3.4 Weapons: machineguns, registration, definitions and kits

Doctrine now: no decision says whether any category of arm lies outside the right or may be banned. Hale (O'Connor, 8-1), Rybar (Kennedy, 8-1) and Wright (9-0) each vacated an appellate affirmance of §922(o) machinegun convictions because the court of appeals had relied on a militia-affiliation requirement, and sent the arms question back; Rybar reserved receivers, conversion parts, accessories and ordinary semiautomatic arms, and Wright reserved the tier and validity of the ban. National Firearms Act registration stands on its own footing: personal protection and military usefulness do not exempt a possessor from registration requirements administering lawful making and transfer (Hale Part IV, eight joins), and registration of pipe bombs is incidental regulation (Wright). Whether a weapon that cannot lawfully be registered may be punished for nonregistration is reserved (Hale).

On the statutory definitions: a §5861(d) machinegun conviction requires proof that the defendant knew the characteristics that make the weapon shoot automatically more than one shot by a single function of the trigger, but not knowledge of registration or illegality (Staples, Thomas, 7-2); Staples supplies no constitutional classification (Rybar). Making a firearm under the National Firearms Act is not invariably postponed until final assembly (Thompson/Center, seven votes), but a pistol and conversion kit with genuine lawful pistol and rifle uses, never assembled as a short-barreled rifle, was not clearly the making of a regulated firearm, and lenity applied even in a civil tax case because the definition carries criminal consequences (five votes, no opinion of the Court); pure-parts kits and sham configurations are reserved. Columbus's assault-weapon definitions, construed with Ohio's default recklessness requirement, are not facially vague where they turn on a rifle's acceptance of an existing detachable magazine of twenty or more rounds, a semiautomatic shotgun's capacity for more than six rounds of actual compatible ammunition, or a handgun's original design for a magazine of more than twenty rounds; the restoration and parts branches stand only when tied to a valid definition; and the two handgun-modification branches are invalid because they do not say whether conversion, design descent or resemblance is the test (Peoples Rights, Thomas). The grandfather exceptions for weapons registered under the city's earlier ordinance survive equality review (Part IX, seven votes). Peoples Rights made no Second Amendment judgment.

United States v. Hale and United States v. Rybar in plain terms. Federal agents found machineguns, conversion equipment and other weapons in Wilbur Hale's possession; he was convicted on thirteen counts of possessing machineguns under §922(o) and three counts of possessing unregistered firearms under §5861(d). The Eighth Circuit affirmed on the premise that the right protects only militia-related possession, and the real Supreme Court never decided the case. Here the Court (O'Connor) held unanimously that the right is personal and that military usefulness gives no immunity, vacated the affirmance of the thirteen machinegun convictions 8-1 for a real arms analysis (Stone would have affirmed on the functional ground that sustained automatic fire differs from ordinary defensive arms), affirmed the registration convictions, and unanimously upheld §922(o) under the commerce power. Rybar, who sold two machineguns at gun shows, received the same treatment (Kennedy; 9-0 on commerce, 8-1 on the arms remand, Stone dissenting), as did Wright.

Peoples Rights Organization v. City of Columbus in plain terms. Columbus, Ohio banned "assault weapons" and large magazines, defined by features and capacity, with exceptions for weapons registered under an earlier ordinance that a court had struck down. The Sixth Circuit invalidated the definitions as vague and the registration exceptions under equal protection, and the real Supreme Court never decided the case. Here the Court (Thomas) kept only the two modification branches invalid (9-0), reversed the other vagueness rulings (8-1, Stone dissenting), sustained the registration exceptions (7-2, Stone and Stevens dissenting) and returned the case for Ohio severability.

### 3.5 Disqualified persons and restoration

Doctrine now: §922(g)(9), the ban on possession by persons convicted of a misdemeanor crime of domestic violence, is the only disability tested under the Second Amendment, and it is valid on the official-use records presented (FOP, Gillespie). The qualifying misdemeanor must have as an element the use or attempted use of physical force or the threatened use of a deadly weapon, committed by a current or former spouse, parent or guardian, a person sharing a child with the victim, a current or former cohabitant as spouse, parent or guardian, or a similarly situated person; the defendant must have had counsel or knowingly waived it, and a jury trial or a knowing waiver where the jurisdiction grants a jury. A conviction does not count if expunged, set aside or pardoned, or if civil rights were restored where the jurisdiction provides for their loss, unless the relief expressly bars firearms. The disability also survives equal protection (no official-use exception is required, FOP; Congress may single out domestic-violence misdemeanors and accept different state restoration systems, Hiley), the Ex Post Facto Clause (it governs later possession, not the earlier offense, Hiley) and the Bill of Attainder Clause (Hiley).

For §921(a)(20)'s conviction rule, the law of the convicting jurisdiction decides whether expungement, pardon or restoration removes the conviction, so a State's restoration does not erase a federal conviction (Beecham, 9-0); and a restoration that keeps a substantial state firearm restriction, such as a ban on carrying handguns outside home or business, does not remove the conviction, so the federal ban reaches even the long guns the State allows (Caron, Stone, 6-3). Whether rights never lost count as restored is reserved (Hiley). The felon ban in §922(g)(1) has been sustained only under the commerce power (Franklyn/Gonzalez, Griffin/Langley); no decision addresses it under the Second Amendment. No decision addresses protective orders (§922(g)(8)), drug users (§922(g)(3)), mental-health adjudications or commitments (§922(g)(4)), noncitizens (§922(g)(5)) or any other category. Love held that a violation of state licensing law is not itself an infringement and did not decide whether denial of a permit on an arrest alone violates the right; Stone alone would have so held.

Fraternal Order of Police and Gillespie in plain terms. In 1996 Congress barred anyone convicted of a misdemeanor crime of domestic violence from possessing a gun and, unlike the older felon ban, gave no exception for government-issued service weapons. A police union challenged the missing exception; a D.C. Circuit panel held it failed equal protection and extended the exception, and the same court reversed itself on rehearing (173 F.3d 898). Officer Gillespie, who lost his job after a battery conviction involving his former wife, lost in the Seventh Circuit on a militia-only view of the right. The real Supreme Court decided neither case. Here the Court reversed the panel (Souter, 9-0), holding the disability a substantial regulation that survives intermediate scrutiny, applied that holding to Gillespie on the individual-right premise (Stone, 9-0), and upheld the commerce basis of §922(g)(9) when FOP returned (O'Connor, 9-0).

### 3.6 Acquisition, dealers and background checks

Doctrine now: acquisition necessary to protected keeping is protected, but licensing and investigation are not categorically barred and the right does not guarantee purchase from every seller (Love Part III-C). Willful dealing in firearms without a license requires proof that the defendant knew his dealing was unlawful, not that he knew of the licensing requirement (Bryan, O'Connor, 7-2). In implementing a valid direct regulation of private transactions, Congress is not categorically barred from requiring appropriate, temporary, records-based help from a state executive officer, such as the Brady Act's interim background checks, while federal law supplies the rule and federal authorities keep coercive enforcement (Printz Part I, Souter, joined by Stone, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer). The officers' fiscal and unauthorized-expenditure objections went back for decision, with no majority rationale for that remand. No decision addresses waiting periods, private-sale screening, purchase limits, straw purchases, dealer licensing burdens, serial numbers or industry liability.

Printz v. United States in plain terms. The Brady Act's interim provisions required local chief law enforcement officers to make reasonable efforts to check whether handgun buyers were eligible. The real Court (Scalia, 5-4) held that Congress may not command state officers to administer a federal program and struck the duty down. Here five Justices (Souter, Stone, Stevens, Ginsburg, Breyer) held there is no categorical bar on that limited records assistance, while a different five (O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas) vacated the judgments sustaining the duty and remanded for the officers' budget objections.

### 3.7 The right and the commerce power

Doctrine now: whether a firearm law violates the right and whether Congress had power to enact it are separate questions. For conduct that is not itself commerce or its channels, instrumentalities, persons or things, the Court first identifies the regulated class and the interstate mechanism connecting it to commerce, and only then lets aggregation measure size; the school-zone possession ban fails, and the Court will not insert a missing commerce element (Lopez, Stone, 5-4). Congress may include intrastate possession when that is reasonably necessary to prevent evasion or material impairment of a valid interstate regime: possession of post-May 19, 1986 machineguns within §922(o)'s ban on additions to the civilian stock (Hale, unanimous; Rybar, Kirk, Wright, Franklyn), and possession by persons whom §922(d) bars suppliers from arming, under §922(g)(1) (Franklyn/Gonzalez, Griffin/Langley) and §922(g)(9) (Hiley, FOP on return, Gillespie). A firearm's past interstate movement can satisfy §922(g)'s enacted nexus element, which the Government must prove in a prosecution, but it is not by itself the constitutional basis, and Congress cannot evade Lopez merely by adding a jurisdictional element (Griffin/Langley). A valid federal disability does not commandeer a State or city merely because its own job rules make an affected officer ineligible for armed work (Hiley, FOP on return, Gillespie). National Association of Home Builders v. Babbitt adds a separate route for supported interstate externalities, and Morrison rejects findings of economic cost as a substitute for the connection.

United States v. Lopez in plain terms. A high school student was convicted under the federal ban on possessing a gun in a school zone. The real Court (Rehnquist, 5-4) held the ban beyond the commerce power because possession is not economic activity and the statute had no jurisdictional element. Here the Court (Stone, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas) reached the same result on a different rule, "connection before magnitude," and expressly created no Second Amendment rule.

### 3.8 Where this Court departed from history

Results that differ: Printz (the background-check duty was not struck down; the judgments were vacated and remanded on the officers' fiscal objections). In lower-court cases the real Supreme Court never decided: Love (the judgment affirming dismissal of the arms claim was vacated); Hale, Rybar and Wright (affirmances of machinegun convictions vacated for an arms analysis); Fraternal Order of Police (1998) (the panel's extension of the exception reversed, the result the D.C. Circuit itself reached on rehearing in history); Peoples Rights (most vagueness rulings reversed and the grandfather exceptions sustained).

Doctrine that arrived early or in a different form: the individual right without a militia tie and its application to the States came in Love (1995), not Heller (2008) and McDonald (2010), by the same due process route McDonald's plurality later used; tiered Core-Preservation Review came in Wright (1998), where history had no standard until Heller declined to set one and Bruen (2022) rejected means-end scrutiny; and a disarmament category was upheld under intermediate scrutiny in Fraternal Order of Police and Gillespie, where history's first such holding was Rahimi (2024), under Bruen's historical test and for a different subsection.

Same results, different reasoning: Lopez (connection before magnitude); Hiley (affirmed while rejecting the lower court's collective-right premise); Gillespie (affirmed on the individual-right framework rather than a militia-only premise); Kirk (a unanimous affirmance where the en banc Fifth Circuit divided equally); Franklyn/Gonzalez and Griffin/Langley (affirmed on the anti-evasion supply-regime ground, with past interstate movement satisfying only the enacted element).

Same results: Thompson/Center, Staples, Beecham, Bryan and Caron, the statutory cases, came out as in history.

Stone's distinctive positions, none controlling: Love (would reverse outright and hold the arrest-only denial unconstitutional, under his own rule that disqualification requires adjudicated conduct or incapacity genuinely connected to danger); Hale and Rybar (would affirm the machinegun possession convictions on the functional ground of sustained automatic fire rather than remand); Peoples Rights (would affirm the invalidation of all five challenged definition categories and of both earlier-registration exceptions); Thompson/Center (concurred only in the judgment, reserving pure-parts kits and sham configurations); Hiley and Franklyn/Gonzalez (concurred only in the judgments, on his own grounds).

### 4.2 Who holds the right

5. Who are "the people" who hold the right: every adult, noncitizens lawfully or unlawfully present, persons with nonviolent convictions? Is "law-abiding" a limit on who holds the right or only a description of who has not been validly disarmed? Love and Hale settle that the holder is an individual without a militia tie but say nothing about these groups.
    > Answer before: OT2007, District of Columbia v. Heller; OT2018, Rehaif v. United States; OT2023, United States v. Rahimi.
6. Should application to the States also rest on the Privileges or Immunities Clause, which Love did not reach, and does every federal rule carry over to the States unchanged?
    > Answer before: OT2009, McDonald v. City of Chicago.
7. When does an official who wrongly denies a permit or seizes a gun owe damages: when is the arms right clearly established for qualified immunity, and does later correction through state review matter? Love left the officers' immunity undecided and held that state-law illegality alone is not a federal wrong.
    > Answer before: no listed landmark.

### 4.3 The home and ordinary arms

8. What does Wright's categorical home rule cover: only handguns, or every ordinary functional defensive arm? Does it forbid requiring home guns to be kept unloaded, disassembled or trigger-locked? Wright named a ban on ordinary functional handguns in the home as a core burden but decided no disabling requirement.
    > Answer before: OT2007, District of Columbia v. Heller; OT2009, McDonald v. City of Chicago.
9. Beyond the home core, how are protected arms identified: by common possession, lawful use, function or something else, and may a government rely on its own ban to show that an arm is uncommon? Hale, Rybar and Wright hold that military usefulness alone neither removes an arm from the guarantee nor exempts it from regulation, and they adopt no catalogue.
    > Answer before: OT2015, Caetano v. Massachusetts; OT2026, Viramontes v. Cook County.
10. Are arms invented after 1791, including nonlethal arms such as stun guns, covered at Stage 1?
    > Answer before: OT2015, Caetano v. Massachusetts.
11. May a government ban semiautomatic rifles defined by features, magazine compatibility or a label such as "assault weapon," and is such a ban a core deprivation or a substantial regulation? Rybar and Kirk left ordinary semiautomatic arms open, and Peoples Rights construed Columbus's definitions only for vagueness.
    > Answer before: OT2026, Viramontes v. Cook County.
12. May a government ban one ordinary arm because other protected arms remain available?
    > Answer before: OT2007, District of Columbia v. Heller; OT2026, Viramontes v. Cook County.

### 4.4 Machineguns, specially regulated weapons and accessories

13. Is the post-1986 machinegun ban in §922(o) valid under the Second Amendment, and at which tier? Hale, Rybar and Wright sent the question back without deciding the tier or the result; Stone's functional ground drew no other vote.
    > Answer before: no listed landmark.
14. Is there a category of "dangerous and unusual" weapons outside the right, and does it belong at Stage 1 (coverage) or Stage 2 (justification)? What role do danger and rarity each play?
    > Answer before: OT2015, Caetano v. Massachusetts; OT2026, Viramontes v. Cook County.
15. How are suppressors, short-barreled rifles and shotguns, and destructive devices treated: outside the right, covered but open to registration, or something else? Hale and Wright upheld National Firearms Act registration against the objections presented and called registration of pipe bombs incidental; whether a weapon that cannot lawfully be registered may be punished for nonregistration is reserved.
    > Answer before: no listed landmark.
16. When does an accessory or conversion device make a weapon a "machinegun": what is a "single function of the trigger," and what work do lenity and an agency's interpretation do? Staples applied the definition to a complete weapon, Kirk and Rybar reserved conversion parts and accessories, and Thompson/Center applied lenity to a definition with criminal consequences.
    > Answer before: OT2023, Garland v. Cargill.
17. When does a parts kit, an unfinished frame or receiver, or a weapon that "may readily be converted" become a "firearm" under the Gun Control Act, and when is making complete? Thompson/Center rejected a universal final-assembly rule but reserved pure-parts kits and sham configurations.
    > Answer before: OT2024, Bondi v. VanDerStok.
18. Does the right reach ordinary accessories, repairs and modifications, and may government require approval or registration when a modification changes a weapon's legal category? Peoples Rights struck two modification definitions for vagueness without reaching the right.
    > Answer before: OT2023, Garland v. Cargill; OT2024, Bondi v. VanDerStok.

### 4.5 Disqualified persons

19. What predicate must support disarming a class of persons: an adjudicated violent act with criminal safeguards, as in Fraternal Order of Police, or may a legislature disarm by status or by a general finding of danger? FOP declined to authorize disarmament for any misdemeanor, a mere allegation or an unrelated offense.
    > Answer before: OT2023, United States v. Rahimi; OT2025, United States v. Hemani.
20. Is the §922(g)(1) felony ban valid as applied to nonviolent or old convictions, and do pardon, expungement, restoration or long lawful conduct matter constitutionally? Franklyn/Gonzalez and Griffin/Langley decided only the commerce question for §922(g)(1).
    > Answer before: OT2023, United States v. Rahimi.
21. Does a foreign conviction count under §922(g)(1), and would it supply the safeguards FOP relied on?
    > Answer before: OT2004, Small v. United States.
22. Under §921(a)(20), can civil rights that were never lost count as "restored"? Hiley reserved it; Beecham chose the convicting jurisdiction's law, and Caron held that a retained state firearm restriction keeps the conviction in force.
    > Answer before: OT2007, Logan v. United States.
23. What is a "misdemeanor crime of domestic violence": must the domestic relationship be an element of the offense, what does "physical force" mean, and does a reckless offense qualify? FOP and Gillespie upheld §922(g)(9) on an adjudicated force predicate and reserved improperly classified convictions.
    > Answer before: OT2008, United States v. Hayes; OT2013, United States v. Castleman; OT2015, Voisine v. United States.
24. Is §922(g)(9) valid as applied to an ordinary person's private possession in the home, to a remote conviction, or where restoration is denied? FOP reserved a materially different private-possession record, and Gillespie reserved home possession and restoration denial.
    > Answer before: OT2015, Voisine v. United States.
25. Is §922(g)(8) valid on its face and as applied? Must the protective order follow notice, a hearing and a finding of danger; how are ex parte and emergency orders treated; and must the disability end when the order ends? FOP's reasoning rested on a criminal conviction, which a civil order lacks.
    > Answer before: OT2000, United States v. Napier (on the OT2000 list); OT2023, United States v. Rahimi.
26. What must the Government prove a defendant knew under §922(g): his disqualifying status, the existence of the order or conviction, or the federal prohibition itself? Staples requires knowledge of a weapon's defining characteristics, and Bryan requires knowledge of unlawfulness for willful dealing.
    > Answer before: OT2000, United States v. Napier (on the OT2000 list); OT2018, Rehaif v. United States.
27. When may a mental-health adjudication or commitment support disarmament, for how long, and with what route to restoration? No decision addresses it.
    > Answer before: no listed landmark.
28. May Congress disarm unlawful drug users or addicts without an adjudication, and is present intoxication different from habitual use?
    > Answer before: OT2025, United States v. Hemani.

### 4.6 Licensing, registration, sales and fees

29. Which licensing and registration conditions are incidental, substantial or core: background checks, fingerprints, photographs, appearance in person, presenting the firearm, renewal, purchase limits, waiting periods and official discretion? Wright reserved licensing details, and Love held that licensing and investigation are not categorically barred.
    > Answer before: OT2007, District of Columbia v. Heller; OT2021, New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen.
30. May government require serial numbers on commercially made and personally made firearms, and is making a gun for one's own use protected?
    > Answer before: OT2024, Bondi v. VanDerStok.
31. How far does Love's acquisition holding limit the regulation of sales: dealer licensing, background checks, private-sale screening, straw-purchase rules and waiting periods? Printz Part I permits limited state records assistance with background checks, and Bryan construed willful unlicensed dealing.
    > Answer before: OT2013, Abramski v. United States.
32. May firearm makers and sellers be held liable for others' criminal misuse of lawfully sold guns, and does the acquisition right bear on that liability?
    > Answer before: OT2024, Smith & Wesson Brands, Inc. v. Estados Unidos Mexicanos.
33. When does a fee or tax on exercising the right become an unconstitutional barrier, and are fees judged together? Wright upheld National Firearms Act registration, with eight Justices relying on its tax-administration basis.
    > Answer before: OT2021, New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen.

### 4.7 Storage, training, ammunition and magazines

34. When may a law require locks or locked storage, and how does Wright's home rule apply when the owner is present and relying on the gun for defense?
    > Answer before: OT2007, District of Columbia v. Heller.
35. May a government require training for possession or carry, and may it at the same time ban or restrict ranges? Wright expressly reserved safety instruction, examinations, proficiency, refresher training and recertification.
    > Answer before: OT2021, New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen.
36. What does "necessary ammunition" in Wright's Stage 1 cover, and may government restrict ammunition sales or types?
    > Answer before: no listed landmark.
37. Are magazines arms or protected incidents of arms, and may government limit their capacity? Peoples Rights construed definitions keyed to twenty-round magazines only for vagueness.
    > Answer before: no listed landmark.

### 4.8 Carry, transport and sensitive places

38. Does "bear" protect carrying outside the home, and is a proper-cause or discretionary license a core deprivation? Wright named effective elimination of all lawful public carry as a core burden but reserved public-carry licensing.
    > Answer before: OT2021, New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen.
39. May a State choose between open and concealed carry, or require a permit for either?
    > Answer before: OT2021, New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen.
40. How far does protected transport reach: to a second home, a range, or another jurisdiction? Wright covers transportation only as an incident of keeping or bearing.
    > Answer before: OT2019, New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. City of New York.
41. What makes a place sensitive (courthouses, schools, polling places, government buildings and their parking lots, transit, parks), and may a State make private property open to the public off-limits unless the owner consents? No decision addresses sensitive places.
    > Answer before: OT2021, New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen; OT2025, Wolford v. Lopez.

### 4.9 Minors, seizure and return

42. What authority does government have over minors' acquisition, possession and carry of arms, and do rules for minors justify restrictions on adults under twenty-one?
    > Answer before: no listed landmark.
43. After a lawful seizure or a disqualifying conviction, must government return firearms or allow their transfer or sale? The Hale, Rybar and Wright remands ordered no return of arms and decided nothing about it.
    > Answer before: OT2014, Henderson v. United States.

### 4.10 Remedies and the commerce line

44. How are remedies shaped: provision by provision with severability, as Peoples Rights required under Ohio law, and what becomes of a challenge when the government repeals or amends the law?
    > Answer before: OT2019, New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. City of New York.
45. Does a firearm's past interstate movement ever supply Congress's constitutional power by itself, or only the statute's enacted element? Griffin/Langley and FOP on return say the element must be proved but is not the constitutional basis.
    > Answer before: OT2000, United States v. Napier (on the OT2000 list).
46. Does the supply-regime route that sustains §922(g)(1) and §922(g)(9) also sustain the protective-order and drug-user bans in §922(g)(8) and §922(g)(3), which have matching supply bans in §922(d)(8) and §922(d)(3)? What power supports a federal possession ban with no matching supply rule?
    > Answer before: OT2000, United States v. Napier (on the OT2000 list); OT2025, United States v. Hemani.
