# Stone doctrine workbook: equality, voting and state immunity

Planning material under `stone/README.md`, written for the user, who is not a lawyer. It lists the questions Stone (the character, not the Court) still has to answer, explains each in plain terms, shows how a candidate answer would be applied, and sorts every question by whether it should be closed now or kept open for a historical landmark case. Candidate answers are analysis for the user to accept, change or reject. Nothing here is an approved Section II, and nothing binds another Justice. Controlling law is as of June 29, 2000; see `STONE_EQUALITY_VOTING_ENFORCEMENT_DIGEST.md` for the case-by-case record.

## 0. Reading guide

### 0.1 Terms used below

- Elements: the list of things a person suing must prove to win. A doctrine without elements tells courts what is not a claim but not what is.
- Standing: whether this particular person may bring the case at all. It is decided before the merits.
- Purpose and effect: purpose asks why government acted; effect asks what the action did. Constitutional equality claims generally need purpose (Washington v. Davis); §2 of the VRA is satisfied by effect.
- Scrutiny tiers: how hard government must work to justify a classification. Rational basis is easy; intermediate needs an important objective and a substantial fit; Stone's common standard governs race.
- Immunity: a State cannot ordinarily be sued for money without its consent. Abrogation: Congress removing that immunity by statute.
- Article I: Congress's ordinary powers, such as commerce, patents and bankruptcy. Section 5: Congress's power to enforce the Fourteenth Amendment (not VRA §5, the preclearance provision).
- Officer suit: a suit against a state official for an order to stop violating federal law (Ex parte Young), which immunity does not bar.
- Landmark: a historical Supreme Court case the user wants to arrive with its central question still open.
- Vehicle: a lower-court case given a simulated Supreme Court hearing.

### 0.3 Branch policy

The user's rule: the landmark historical cases keep their central question open so the simulation can decide them in their own cases. Lower-court vehicles are used to close the side questions that Stone's own doctrinal changes opened, so the landmarks arrive on a clean, coherent record. Cases should not decide more than their question requires; Vera decided more than it had to, which created branches that now need tracking word by word.

Every question below carries one of four tags:

| Tag | Meaning | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Cleanup | A side question the simulation's own changes opened, which no landmark will answer in the form this timeline needs. | Close it with a vehicle before the related landmark arrives. |
| Reserved | The central question of a landmark case. | Leave it open. A vehicle that touches it decides on a narrower ground and names the question in its "Limits and questions not reached." |
| Collision | A scheduled vehicle whose question presented is a landmark's central question. | Decide whether to narrow the vehicle, accept that it pre-decides the landmark, or drop it. |
| Pre-decided | A landmark whose central question an earlier simulated decision has already answered. | Record it, so the case list treats the landmark as changed, reconstructed or deleted under the build's causal rule. |

The practical tracking mechanism: when a cleanup decision stops at the edge of a reserved question, its Record should name that question in "Limits and questions not reached." The Holdings register then carries the reservation forward, and a search of the register for the landmark's issue shows whether anything has closed it. That replaces tracking every word.

The "free play" areas. The user's working assumption is that the Second Amendment, campaign finance, parts of tort law, standing and digital privacy are areas the historical Court did not really touch, so it does not matter what the simulation does there. That holds for much of state tort law. It does not hold for the others: the historical Court decided landmark cases in each of them, mostly after 2000, and the simulation has already reached several of their central questions early.

None of this means those areas cannot be played with. It means play there replaces the historical line, and the causal rule then deletes or reconstructs the landmarks that depended on it. Free play is genuinely cost-free only where no historical Supreme Court decision through OT2026 answers the question. Order still has to be kept, just with the simulation's own earlier holdings instead of history. That choice belongs to the user, area by area; this workbook flags the collisions so it is made knowingly.

## Part A. The four districting injuries

Tag for the whole part: Cleanup. The Shaw change created these injuries as the remaining routes, and no historical case defines them, because historically the Shaw claim made them unnecessary. They need elements before the landmarks below arrive. The stop lines in A.6 keep the reserved landmark questions open.

### A.0 The problem

Every districting decision since 1993 says predominant racial design is not an injury, but "racial exclusion, intentional dilution, materially unequal participation, legally inferior civic membership or other independently cognizable constitutional deprivation" remain actionable and "retain their own elements." Miller adds that a status or participation injury "can be complete when government itself imposes unequal racial treatment" without electoral loss, and that "a discriminatory participation or constituent-service practice requires its own examination." The Court cites Gomillion, Anderson v. Martin, White v. Regester and McLaurin as the precedents that keep these injuries alive. It has never said what the elements are, and Hunt left them open.

Without elements, lower courts have a rejection rule and no replacement. Plaintiffs who lost the Shaw claim will relabel it, and there is no principled way to sort a real claim from a relabeled one. And the historical racial-gerrymandering cases (Alabama Legislative Black Caucus, OT2014; Bethune-Hill and Cooper v. Harris, OT2016; Alexander v. South Carolina NAACP, OT2023) can return in this timeline only as one of these four claims.

Four design choices run through all four injuries:

1. Purpose. Each constitutional claim requires that race caused the treatment, through an express racial rule, ancestry used as race, or proven purpose. Effect alone belongs to VRA §2. Otherwise these become a judge-made results test that duplicates §2 without its statutory limits.
2. Comparator. Each injury needs a baseline that is not a racial target: exclusion compares eligibility; dilution compares a lawful alternative plan (Holder); participation compares other voters in the same election; status compares equal official treatment.
3. Remedy follows the injury. Only dilution can justify redrawing lines, and then only to a lawful plan, not a racial percentage.
4. Standing follows the injury. Whoever personally bears the specific injury claimed may sue.

### A.1 Racial exclusion

Plain terms: government shuts people out of voting, or out of the political community, because of race.

Anchors: Gomillion v. Lightfoot (1960), where Alabama redrew Tuskegee's limits into a 28-sided figure that removed nearly every Black resident from the city and its elections; the white primary cases (Smith v. Allwright, 1944; Terry v. Adams, 1953); Hunter v. Underwood (1985), a disenfranchisement law adopted to exclude Black voters; Rice v. Cayetano (2000), which Stone joined.

Candidate elements: (1) a governmental act that removes or withholds eligibility to vote, to have a vote counted, or to belong to the political unit; (2) on account of race, by express rule, ancestry used as race, or proven purpose; (3) the claimant is among those excluded; (4) remedy: restore eligibility or membership.

Test question: is anyone made ineligible? Moving a voter from one district to another inside the same unit is not exclusion; changing who belongs to the unit can be (Gomillion).

### A.2 Intentional vote dilution

Plain terms: everyone can vote, but the system is built on purpose so one racial group's votes count for less. The methods are cracking (spreading the group thinly so it is outvoted everywhere) and packing (concentrating it far beyond what it needs to win, wasting its surplus and weakening it elsewhere).

Anchors: White v. Regester (1973) and Rogers v. Lodge (1982) found unconstitutional dilution; Mobile v. Bolden (1980, plurality) required purpose, which is why Congress changed §2 to a results test in 1982. In this timeline Voinovich held a dilution-intent finding clearly erroneous, and Holder left the constitutional claim undecided.

Candidate elements: (1) a districting plan or election structure; (2) actual dilutive effect: the group's ability to elect is materially reduced compared with a lawful, workable alternative rooted in the State's own criteria, proved with the same kind of evidence §2 uses (cohesion, bloc voting, an alternative plan); (3) purpose: the structure was adopted or kept in material part because it would produce that effect; (4) remedy: a lawful plan removing the dilution, with no racial floor or proportional result.

The application to think about now is packing defended as VRA compliance. Suppose a State draws a district at 60% Black voting-age population when the evidence shows 45% would reliably elect the community's candidates, and the surplus came from a neighboring district where those voters had real influence. If the purpose was to drain that neighboring influence, that is intentional dilution, and the compliance label does not save it. Master Section 9 already says statutory implementation cannot authorize "intentional destruction of a protected electoral opportunity." This is how the Bethune-Hill and Cooper fact patterns could return: not as "race predominated" but as "race was used to pack us so we would count for less." Those cases' historical central question (strict scrutiny of predominance) no longer exists here, so closing this point is cleanup, not pre-decision.

Choice for the user: whether unnecessary packing alone supports an inference of purpose, or is one factor among the ordinary purpose evidence. The candidate answer treats it as strong circumstantial evidence, not an automatic finding (master Section 8: impact is evidence, not conversion).

### A.3 Materially unequal participation

Plain terms: you can vote and your vote counts at full weight, but government makes taking part harder or worse for your racial group: fewer polling places, shorter hours, stricter registration windows, ballots handled under different rules, your district's election skipped, or your representative's services closed to your neighborhood.

Anchors: Lane v. Wilson (1939), where Oklahoma gave Black citizens it had excluded through its grandfather clause a 12-day window to register or lose the vote permanently; the Court struck it down because the Constitution "nullifies sophisticated as well as simple-minded modes of discrimination." McLaurin v. Oklahoma State Regents (1950), where a Black graduate student was admitted but made to sit apart. White v. Regester, preserved in Miller for participation beyond formal eligibility. Miller's own reservation of "a discriminatory participation or constituent-service practice."

Candidate elements: (1) a governmental rule or practice governing how citizens register, vote, have ballots counted, receive election notice, or reach their elected officials; (2) unequal terms: the group participates on materially worse terms than similarly situated voters in the same jurisdiction and election, where "materially" means the difference changes practical ability to participate (time, distance, risk of rejection, loss of a scheduled election, denial of a service); (3) on account of race, by express rule, assignment by race to the worse terms, or proven purpose, with disparity counting as evidence but not proof; (4) remedy: equalize the terms, not redraw the district.

Test question: remove the racial composition of the map from the story. Is this voter still treated worse than other voters in the act of participating? If the only complaint left is who else lives in the district, it is the rejected Shaw claim.

What it looks like in districting:
- A plan renumbers districts so predominantly Black precincts land where the senate seat is not up for four years, delaying their vote, and the record shows the renumbering was chosen for that reason. Neutral delays from staggered terms are ordinarily lawful (Pate v. El Paso County, 1970, summary affirmance); a racially targeted delay is unequal participation.
- A county uses a new line to send Black-majority precincts to one distant consolidated polling place while other precincts keep neighborhood polls.
- An officeholder limits constituent offices, meetings or casework to parts of a district chosen by race. This is Miller's reserved "constituent-service practice."

Outside districting: targeted precinct closures (master Section 8's example), a registration window open only when one group can use it (Lane), or ballots counted under different rules in different racial areas.

The holistic link: Bush v. Gore (OT2000) asks whether materially equivalent ballots must get substantially equal treatment. That is a landmark in its own right and is decided in its own case. If Stone's rule there is the build's (equivalent ballots, consistent nonarbitrary method), unequal participation can be defined as the racial branch of the same principle: the same comparison, plus racial causation, plus the racial-equality consequences. One theory of equal participation, not two inventions.

Choice for the user: whether element 3 accepts racial assignment to worse terms without separate proof of purpose. The candidate answer says yes: sorting people by race into a worse procedure is itself racial treatment. That is consistent with Miller, because the shared district there imposed worse terms on no one.

### A.4 Legally inferior civic status

Plain terms: government officially marks a racial group as second-class members of the political community, even if no one loses a vote.

Anchors: Anderson v. Martin (1964), where Louisiana printed each candidate's race on the ballot; the Court struck it down although the label applied to every race and no one was stopped from voting. Hamm v. Virginia State Board of Elections (E.D. Va. 1964), summarily affirmed as Tancil v. Woolls (1964), striking down voter and tax lists kept separately by race. Strauder v. West Virginia (1880), calling exclusion of Black men from juries "a brand upon them." Romer v. Evans (1996, Stone joined) is the nonracial cousin.

Candidate elements: (1) an official act with legal force or official designation (statute, ballot, voter roll, official record, eligibility rule, office qualification); (2) it classifies or marks citizens by race in their civic capacity (voter, candidate, juror, officeholder, member of the polity), or assigns them lesser legal standing in that capacity; (3) no electoral or financial loss is needed, and applying the label to every race does not cure it (Anderson); (4) remedy: remove the designation.

The line Stone drew in Miller: the injury must come from what government officially does, not from a message someone reads into a map's demographics. A district that happens to be 55% Black is not a designation. A statute that names it "the Black district," reserves its seat for a Black candidate, or tells its representative to serve only Black constituents is.

The gray zone is official statements, such as a committee report calling a seat "the African-American district." Candidate answer: only designations with legal operation count as the status injury; statements are evidence of purpose for the other three claims. Counting statements as status injuries would revive much of the Shaw claim through legislative history.

Related docket item: Cook v. Gralike (OT2000) concerns Missouri's ballot labels for candidates who would not support term limits. It is not racial, but it uses Anderson's mechanism, so how Stone treats official ballot labels there shapes this claim.

### A.5 Summary

| Injury | Plain question | Racial causation shown by | Comparator | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exclusion | Is anyone made ineligible because of race? | Express rule or proven purpose | Eligibility of others | Restore eligibility |
| Intentional dilution | Was the plan built to make the group's votes count for less? | Proven purpose | Lawful alternative plan | Lawful plan |
| Unequal participation | Is the group made to participate on worse terms? | Express rule, racial assignment, or proven purpose | Other voters in the same election | Equalize the terms |
| Inferior civic status | Does government officially mark the group as lesser? | The official classification itself | Equal official treatment | Remove the designation |

### A.6 Stop lines: what these elements must not decide

These keep the reserved landmark questions open:

- LULAC v. Perry (OT2005). Reserved: whether mid-decade redistricting or partisan motive changes the analysis; whether a State that dismantles an opportunity district can offset it by creating one elsewhere; the partisan-gerrymandering test. The dilution elements should be stated for a plan considered on its own, without deciding offsets or partisan overlap.
- Georgia v. Ashcroft (OT2002). Reserved: whether replacing majority-minority districts with influence or coalition districts is backsliding under VRA §5. Valdespino's ability-to-elect focus frames it but does not decide it.
- Abbott v. Perez (OT2017). Reserved: whether legislatures get a presumption of good faith when purpose is contested. The dilution element should rely on the ordinary purpose evidence without choosing a presumption.
- Vieth (OT2003) and Rucho (OT2018). Reserved: partisan-gerrymandering justiciability and test. Master Elections has a position; no vehicle should adopt it.
- Easley and the race-versus-party problem (OT2000; Alexander, OT2023). Hunt Pt I already sends a genuine race-versus-politics dispute to trial when motive is material. How a court separates the two at trial is reserved.

### A.7 Applying the structure to OT2000

Sinkfield v. Kelley (November 27, 2000). This is itself a historical case (per curiam, 2000), so it decides its own question: whether voters next to the challenged districts have standing. Under "standing follows the injury," they do if they claim their own votes were diluted (for example, that the neighboring district was packed to drain them), and not if they only object to the neighbor district's composition. That resolves Stone's own tension: keep his Miller personal-exposure rule, require exposure to the specific injury claimed, and drop the Vera narrowing. Master Elections already says the territorial rule "is a position on the electoral claim, not a general restriction on standing." Decide only that; do not use Sinkfield to state the merits elements.

Easley v. Cromartie (April 18, 2001). The historical plaintiffs pleaded only racial predominance. If no independent claim was preserved, Hunt's mandate requires judgment for the State and no elements are needed. If the record is reconstructed with a preserved claim, it is most plausibly packing-based dilution. Either way the case needs a reconstruction premise first.

Chen v. City of Houston (May 21, 2001). Collision; see Part D.

## Part B. State immunity after Florida Prepaid, Alden and Kimel

### B.0 What Stone has built

| Route | Rule | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Article I abrogation | Congress may authorize private suits against States when the duty the State broke and the suit enforcing it both fall within an Article I power, and Congress says so unmistakably. | Florida Prepaid Pt III (5-4), Kimel (5-4) |
| Article I in state court | Same rule; a State's own competent courts must hear the authorized suit. | Alden (5-4) |
| Section 5 abrogation | Congress may authorize suits as a congruent and proportional remedy for constitutional violations by States, without proving a violation in every case. | Boerne (6-3), Florida Prepaid Pt II (5-4), College Savings Pt II (8-1), Morrison (5-4) |
| Consent | Express waiver, voluntary invocation of federal court, or accepting federal money under a clear condition. Doing business is not consent. | Bradley (9-0), College Savings Pt III |
| Officer suits | Orders against state officials to stop ongoing violations, without retroactive money. | Seminole Tribe, Coeur d'Alene |

### B.1 What this branch already pre-decided

The Article I rule is general: any Article I power, unmistakable authorization, valid duty. That already answers the central question of several later landmarks before they arrive, much as Vera did for districting:

| Landmark | Historical central question | Status here |
|---|---|---|
| Katz (OT2005) | Bankruptcy Clause abrogation | Pre-decided by Florida Prepaid Pt III |
| Allen v. Cooper (OT2019) | Copyright abrogation | Pre-decided |
| Torres v. Texas DPS (OT2021) | War-powers abrogation (USERRA) | Pre-decided |
| Hibbs (OT2002), Coleman (OT2011) | FMLA damages against States under §5 | Answerable through Article I (FMLA is commerce legislation), so §5 need not be reached |
| Garrett (OT2000) | ADA Title I damages under §5 | Same |

These should be recorded in the case lists as changed landmarks under the causal rule, not left to arrive as if their questions were open.

### B.2 What still has to be cleaned up

These are side questions the Article I rule opened that no landmark will answer in the form this timeline needs. Each is Cleanup.

1. The valid-duty limit. Congress can authorize suits only to enforce an obligation it could lawfully impose on the State. That excludes commandeering (Congress cannot order a legislature to legislate, so it cannot authorize damages for refusing) and duties outside the enumerated power. The rule implies this; no decision says it.
2. Unmistakable authorization. Must the statute name States as defendants, or can a general "any employer" definition suffice? Kimel Pt I found the ADEA clear, statute-specific. Candidate: the text must refer to States or their immunity expressly.
3. Sovereign functions. Does Article I reach a State's legislative, judicial, taxing or policing acts, or only its conduct as employer, competitor and market participant (Garcia)? Candidate: the abrogation follows the duty; where the commerce power does not reach a sovereign function, Article I supplies neither the duty nor the suit, and §5 is the route. This is the line that keeps Lane and Georgia meaningful.
4. Remedy limits. "Award-specific limits" were left open. Candidate: compensatory damages follow from valid authorization; punitive damages against a State require their own clear statement and face a presumption against them, since punishing a sovereign falls on its taxpayers.
5. State-court forum beyond the FLSA. Alden's rule is FLSA-specific in its holding.
6. The Seminole leftover. Direct-State amenability under the gaming statute is still fractured, though Florida Prepaid Pt III's logic covers the Indian Commerce power.

These have to be closed by vehicles, since no historical Supreme Court case will raise them in this form. Candidate lower-court vehicles from 1999 to 2002, to be verified before any is added to a case list (court and year from memory; citations not yet checked): Alsbrook v. City of Maumelle (Eighth Circuit en banc, 1999), ADA Title II abrogation, for item 3; Hason v. Medical Board of California (Ninth Circuit, 2002), Title II and professional licensing, for item 3; Jim C. v. United States (Eighth Circuit en banc, 2000), the Arkadelphia school case, for §504 consent by accepting funds, which Bradley left open, decided without reaching Sossamon's damages-scope question. No vehicle yet identified for items 1, 2 or 4. None of these is in any inventory now.

### B.4 Garrett under the branch policy

1. Did Congress clearly authorize suit? The ADA says a State "shall not be immune under the eleventh amendment" (42 U.S.C. §12202). Yes.
2. Is Title I's duty within Article I as applied to States? Title I regulates employment, and Garcia and EEOC v. Wyoming let Congress regulate state employment under the commerce power. Kimel already says so. Yes.
3. The damages claims therefore proceed on Article I alone.
4. Under the branch policy, Stone should not also reach §5. My earlier suggestion to bank a §5 holding as insurance is withdrawn: it would decide disability's §5 status in a case that does not need it and pre-decide part of Lane. The cost is losing a second foundation if the five-vote Article I rule is later narrowed; Lane remains the place to build §5.
5. The Title II employment portion: the build would dismiss it as improvidently granted because statutory coverage was not briefed. That also keeps Lane's ground clear.
