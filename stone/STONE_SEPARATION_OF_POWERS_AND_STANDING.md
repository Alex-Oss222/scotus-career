## 3. The doctrine as it stands

Rules as of June 29, 2000.

### 3.1 Where this Court departed from history

Standing and most separation-of-powers law track the real Court. The departures:

1. Congress and final judgments (the major one). Historically, Morgan Stanley (1994) ended 4-4 with no precedent, and Plaut (1995) held 7-2 that Congress can never require courts to reopen a final judgment. Here, Stone wrote both: Morgan Stanley (five votes: Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg; O'Connor not participating) and Plaut (5-4: Stone, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer; Scalia, O'Connor, Kennedy and Thomas dissenting, stating the historical rule). Congress may, by a general and objective law, require reinstatement of cases already finally dismissed as time-barred. See 3.4.
2. Akins v. FEC. Standing matches history, but O'Connor joins the majority and the Court decides the "major purpose" question the real Court left open.
3. Commerce v. House. The Census Act allows sampling here (historically it did not), so the House's institutional standing had to be decided, and it lost, 7-2.
4. Franklin v. Massachusetts and Dalton v. Specter. Here the Secretary's census decision is reviewable final agency action, which was the real Court's four-Justice dissent. Whether the President is an "agency" is left open. Dalton rests on narrower statutory preclusion.
5. Miller v. French. Same result, but it does not rely on Plaut's line between final judgments and prospective relief, and it expressly reserves an Article III challenge to a particular application.
6. Districting. Miller, Vera and Lawyer come out differently on the merits because of Shaw; their standing rules match history.
7. Metropolitan Washington Airports Authority v. Hechinger and FEC v. Legi-Tech were decided here; historically only the D.C. Circuit decided them.

### 3.2 Standing

Doctrine now: a plaintiff must show a concrete and imminent personal injury, traceable to the defendant and redressable by the court, proved with evidence suited to the stage of the case (Lujan; five votes control the rationale here, six historically). A vague intention to return to a place someday is not enough. A citizen-suit clause cannot replace a concrete interest; procedural rights relax only the immediacy and certainty of redress. Congress may waive prudential limits (United Food and Commercial Workers, Bennett) but cannot create Article III injury by statute alone (Raines). Cognizable injuries include denial of information Congress requires to be disclosed, even when widely shared (Akins); denial of equal opportunity to compete, without proof the plaintiff would have won (Northeastern Florida, Adarand); reasonably curtailed use of a resource (Laidlaw); a relator's assigned share of the government's claim (Vermont Agency); and a State's loss of its own tax revenue (Wyoming v. Oklahoma). Individual legislators lack standing to claim abstract dilution of their votes (Raines). A taxpayer cannot assert absent third parties' rights (Nordlinger). Competitors arguably within a statute's zone of interests may sue (National Credit Union Administration).

How it operates:
1. Injury: concrete, particularized and actual or imminent; for procedural claims, a concrete interest the procedure protects.
2. Traceability to the challenged conduct.
3. Redressability by the relief sought; penalties can redress ongoing violations by deterring them (Laidlaw), but not violations completed before suit (Steel Co.).
4. Proof matched to the stage: allegations at pleading, evidence at summary judgment.

### 3.3 Mootness, ripeness, order of decision and political questions

Doctrine now: courts must establish their jurisdiction before reaching the merits; there is no "hypothetical jurisdiction" (Steel Co.), but a court may choose among threshold grounds, such as deciding personal jurisdiction first (Ruhrgas). A defendant claiming voluntary cessation carries a heavy burden to show the conduct cannot reasonably recur (Laidlaw; Adarand v. Slater). A case mooted on appeal is ordinarily vacated (Arizonans), but settlement does not by itself justify vacatur (U.S. Bancorp). A challenge to a programmatic plan, or a request to exempt future acts, is unripe until concrete (Ohio Forestry, Texas v. United States). A freestanding declaration about a future procedural question is not a case or controversy (Calderon v. Ashmus). Political questions are decided issue by issue: the Senate's use of a committee to take impeachment evidence is unreviewable (Nixon), while the method of apportioning House seats is reviewable (Montana).

### 3.4 Congress and the courts

Doctrine now: Congress may change the law that governs pending cases, even with an eye to particular litigation, without directing results (Robertson). Congress may change the conditions for continuing prospective relief, such as prison decrees, because judges still find the facts (Plyler, Rouse, Hadix, Benjamin, Gavin); a mandatory automatic stay of that relief is not categorically invalid (Miller v. French). And, departing from history, Congress may by a general and objective law require reinstatement of cases already finally dismissed as untimely (Morgan Stanley, Plaut).

What Plaut means in plain terms. In 1991 the Court shortened the time limit for private securities-fraud suits and applied it to pending cases, so many suits were thrown out as late. Congress responded with §27A(b): any such suit dismissed as late after June 19, 1991, that would have been timely under the old rule, "shall be reinstated" if the plaintiff asked within 60 days. Plaut's case had already been dismissed and not appealed, so the dismissal was final. The real Court said Article III makes a final judgment the judiciary's last word, and Congress can never order it reopened. Your Court said that §27A(b) does not decide anyone's case. It creates a new legal right to have the case heard, defined by objective criteria that judges apply. Judges still decide eligibility, fraud, liability and damages.

The limits your Court stated: Congress may not order courts to make findings contrary to the record, declare named defendants liable, or disguise an individual award as a reopening law. Left undecided: whether Congress may reopen final judgments on the merits (as opposed to timeliness dismissals), and individualized legislative commands aimed at a single case. Later cases describe the result as "neither absolute finality nor unrestricted congressional reopening power."

How it operates:
1. Is Congress changing the law for pending cases (permitted) or directing a result under existing law (forbidden)?
2. If the statute reaches a final judgment: is it a general, objective rule that courts apply case by case, leaving them the merits? If yes, Morgan Stanley and Plaut permit it for timeliness dismissals. A merits judgment, or a rule aimed at a single case, is undecided.
3. For continuing prospective relief: Congress may change the governing conditions; an application that denies a meaningful chance to adjudicate remains open to challenge (Miller v. French).

### 3.5 Appointments and officers

Doctrine now: inferior officers are those whose work is directed and supervised by a principal officer, and a department head may appoint them (Edmond, Stone's opinion). Officers already appointed may take on germane duties without a second appointment (Weiss). A timely challenger to an officer's appointment gets a fresh hearing, and the de facto officer doctrine does not save the judgment (Ryder). Actual reconsideration by a properly constituted agency cures an earlier structural defect (Legi-Tech). An independent agency cannot seek Supreme Court review on its own authority, and a late authorization cannot ratify it (FEC v. NRA Political Victory Fund). A corporation the government creates by special law and controls by permanently appointing a majority of its directors is part of the government (Lebron).

### 3.6 Delegation and legislative process

Doctrine now: Congress validly delegated the definition of military capital aggravating factors to the President (Loving). The line-item cancellations violated bicameralism and presentment (Clinton v. City of New York); nondelegation was not reached there. Congress's own agent may not hold legally operative control over an executive body's decisions (Hechinger). No removal case has been decided.

### 3.7 The President and legislators

Doctrine now: a sitting President has no temporary immunity from a civil suit over unofficial conduct before taking office; official-act immunity turns on the function performed, not on the office; and a stay lasting the whole term was an abuse of discretion (Clinton v. Jones). The Secretary's census decision is reviewable final agency action; whether the President is an "agency" is open (Franklin). Local legislators have absolute immunity for legislative acts (Bogan). The Westfall Act's scope certification is judicially reviewable (Gutierrez de Martinez).

## 4. Questions to answer before the landmarks

Questions only. Each names the landmarks it should arrive before.

### 4.1 Standing

1. How far may Congress define new injuries? Is the bare violation of a statutory right, with no further harm, a concrete injury (Stone's Steel Co. view says invasion of a statutory information right is), or does Raines's rule that a statute cannot itself supply injury control? Before: Spokeo (OT2015), TransUnion (OT2020).
2. Do States receive special standing for sovereign or quasi-sovereign interests beyond their own revenue? Before: Massachusetts v. EPA (OT2006), United States v. Texas (OT2022).
3. Does taxpayer standing exist for Establishment Clause challenges (federal, state and municipal), and does it reach tax credits and executive spending? Before: Hein (OT2006), Arizona Christian v. Winn (OT2010). Coordinate with the religion document.
4. How imminent must a threatened injury be, and does a substantial risk of future harm count? Before: Clapper (OT2012), TransUnion (OT2020).
5. When may an organization sue for its members, and must it identify a specific injured member? Before: Summers (OT2008).
6. Who may defend a law in court when the government will not (initiative sponsors, legislative bodies)? Before: Hollingsworth (OT2012), Windsor (OT2012).
7. When does a legislature, or a house of Congress, have institutional standing? Raines left this open. Before: no listed landmark.
8. Does a government's change of policy after suit moot a claim for nominal damages for the past violation? Before: Uzuegbunam (OT2020).
9. Does the political question doctrine bar enforcement of a statute that touches foreign affairs? Before: Zivotofsky v. Clinton (OT2011).

### 4.2 Congress and the courts

10. May Congress require courts to reopen final judgments on the merits, not only timeliness dismissals? Before: none listed; it shapes Bank Markazi (OT2015).
11. May Congress pass a statute aimed at a single pending case, or direct dismissal of one named suit, without amending the general law? Before: Bank Markazi (OT2015), Patchak v. Zinke (OT2017).
12. What is the full reach of United States v. Klein's rule against Congress prescribing rules of decision? Robertson reserved it. Before: Bank Markazi.
13. When may an agency or a bankruptcy court, rather than an Article III court, finally decide a claim involving private rights, and does consent cure the problem? Before: Stern (OT2010), Wellness (OT2014), Oil States (OT2017), Jarkesy (OT2023).
14. Does the Seventh Amendment require a jury when an agency seeks civil penalties? Before: Jarkesy (OT2023).

### 4.3 Executive structure

15. May Congress restrict the President's power to remove officers, including the head of a single-director agency or officers protected by two layers of for-cause removal? Before: Free Enterprise Fund (OT2009), Seila Law (OT2019), Collins (OT2020).
16. Who is an "officer" rather than an employee, and is an officer whose decisions no principal officer can review a principal officer? Edmond reserved the second question. Before: Lucia (OT2017), Arthrex (OT2020).
17. When may the President make recess appointments? Before: Noel Canning (OT2013).
18. Is there an enforceable limit on how much policymaking Congress may delegate, and should courts require clear authorization for major agency actions? Loving reserved enhanced specificity for criminal delegations. Before: Whitman (OT2000, on the list), Gundy (OT2018), West Virginia v. EPA (OT2021).
19. What remedy follows a removal or appointment defect: setting aside the action, a fresh hearing, or severing the restriction? Before: Collins (OT2020), Arthrex (OT2020).

### 4.4 The President

20. Does a former President have immunity from criminal prosecution for official acts, and how does Clinton v. Jones's function test apply? Clinton v. Jones reserved criminal proceedings. Before: Trump v. United States (OT2023).
21. Is the President an "agency" whose actions are reviewable under the APA? Franklin and Dalton left it open. Before: no listed landmark.
22. Does the President hold exclusive power to recognize foreign governments against a contrary statute? Before: Zivotofsky v. Kerry (OT2014).
23. May the President direct state courts to comply with an international judgment, and are treaties enforceable in state courts without implementing legislation? Before: Medellín (OT2007).
