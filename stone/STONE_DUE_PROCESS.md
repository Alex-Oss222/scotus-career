# Stone tracker: due process

Scope: substantive due process (abortion, unenumerated liberties, executive conduct, punitive damages and retroactive economic laws); criminal due process and punishment limits (civil commitment, competency and medication, defenses and elements, vagueness, forfeiture and excessive fines, civil sanctions and double jeopardy, ex post facto, actual innocence, sex-offender notification); and procedural due process in civil settings (prison liberty, public employment, benefits and contracts). Federal habeas, the Fourth Amendment, the Second Amendment (including arms protection under due process) and equal protection appear here only where they touch these subjects.

Organization:
0. Stone's working foundation for future cases: personal commitments, working positions, inferred applications, precedent, and remedies.
1. Completed cases.
2. Reserved landmark cases, with their historical questions and blank fields for Stone's disposition and reasoning.
3. The doctrine as it stands, how it operates, and where this Court departed from history.
4. Questions to answer before the landmarks (questions only).

## 0. Stone's working foundation for future cases

### His doctrine and commitments

Stone understands due process as a limit on government's power to control a person's body, liberty, family, punishment, and legally protected property. Government must have a constitutionally sufficient reason for the deprivation and must use a procedure capable of testing whether that reason is true. Some actions remain forbidden even after a flawless hearing. Other actions are permissible only after fair notice, meaningful participation, and a sufficiently independent decision.

His central concern is justified power. A person does not become available for indefinite confinement because officials fear him, for compulsory medical intervention because prosecution would be easier, or for loss of an established entitlement because an agency finds explanation inconvenient. At the same time, a constitutional guarantee does not entitle everyone to the policy, medical service, economic arrangement, or judicial remedy they prefer. Stone requires an identifiable legal protection, a demonstrated interference, and a rule that can explain both when government loses and when it may act.

His personal commitments are:

- **People retain agency.** Decisions about bodily integrity, intimate life, and family ordinarily belong to the person. Disability, imprisonment, minority, poverty, and dependence can affect capacity, legitimate governmental responsibilities, and necessary safeguards. They do not erase personhood or transfer unlimited authority to officials.
- **Government must justify the deprivation it actually imposes.** An interest sufficient to investigate does not necessarily justify confinement. Authority to suspend an employee's duties does not automatically justify stopping pay. A legitimate public-safety objective does not establish that every restriction advances it.
- **Rights must work in practice.** A hearing is inadequate if the person cannot discover or challenge its decisive premise. An exception is inadequate if delay makes it unusable. Consent is inadequate if obtained through an unlawful threat. Cost, distance, access to counsel, medical evidence, and institutional dependence matter when they bear on the governing legal question.
- **The seriousness of a deprivation affects the justification and safeguards it requires.** Irreversible bodily intervention, prolonged custody, family destruction, and criminal punishment demand particular care. This does not create one heightened test for every serious dispute. Each constitutional protection retains its own elements and burdens.
- **Protection and accountability coexist.** Government may prevent grave harm, provide genuine treatment, punish proved and culpable wrongdoing, maintain accurate records, and allocate economic responsibilities. Stone asks whether it has established the particular authority and facts necessary for the measure chosen.
- **History informs constitutional meaning without freezing its applications.** An old practice is evidence, not a sufficient defense of itself. Silence produced by a group's exclusion from legal participation does not establish that its liberty is unprotected. Modern facts may change what an enduring guarantee requires without changing the constitutional text.
- **Legal judgment remains disciplined.** Sympathy cannot create jurisdiction, a cause of action, an entitlement, or a missing statutory element. Practical consequences help assess legally available readings and remedies. They do not authorize rewriting clear law without acknowledging and justifying the change.

### What the foundation establishes

This is a foundation for predicting and developing **Stone's own position**, not a statement that his Court has adopted every proposition. It contains no advance votes for undecided cases.

- **Established commitment** means a position already supported by his stated views, votes, or writings. A personal commitment can be established even when it appeared in a concurrence or dissent and never became controlling law.
- **Working position** means a proposed development selected for this foundation. Use it as Stone's provisional position in future analysis, subject to the actual case, legal argument, and any later express choice. Do not describe it as a holding he has already delivered.
- **Inference** means a predicted application or extension. Its strength depends on how closely the facts and legal question match the stated commitment. Illustrations supply assumed facts, not facts to insert into a future record.

Existing Court law must be identified independently in each case. A question may remain unresolved for the Court even where this foundation supplies Stone's answer. Conversely, his preferred rule does not silently replace a controlling rule. If adopting his position requires a change, his reasoning must identify and defend that change. A later express case-specific choice controls his vote.

### How he reaches a decision

Stone first identifies the actual question, procedural posture, jurisdiction, preservation, and governing source. He uses an adequate statutory or other nonconstitutional ground when available. A saving construction must be a reading the text can fairly support. It cannot insert a missing protection or invent an offense element.

He then separates four questions:

1. **What is protected?** Identify the particular liberty or property interest, rather than starting with a general claim that the outcome is unfair.
2. **What did government do?** Identify the deprivation, its attribution to government, its duration, and its practical effects. Private importance or market power alone does not turn private conduct into constitutional action.
3. **What makes the action lawful or unlawful?** Apply the governing substantive and procedural rules, with their actual burdens of proof. A specific constitutional provision governing searches, punishment, jury authority, or equality supplies its own test; due process is not a way around that test.
4. **What can the court properly do?** Determine the available legal remedy, the facts necessary to support it, and whether relief must prevent an irreversible injury before ordinary review can occur.

He distinguishes categorical boundaries from questions of degree. A rule against punishment without proof of an element is not balanced away because the prosecution is important. The form of an administrative hearing can depend on error risk and practical need. Ordinary economic regulation does not acquire strict scrutiny because it affects someone's livelihood.

He evaluates facts as they could be known at the relevant time. Changed science may support a new application or justify reconsidering a precedent's premise. It does not make an earlier official omniscient. Where necessary facts are missing, he applies the actual burden, remands for findings when appropriate, or decides a narrower question. He does not fill the gap with a preferred narrative.

### Procedural fairness: interests, explanation, and a usable opportunity to respond

**Established commitments.** Freedom from physical restraint is a constitutional liberty. Property interests arise from law that gives someone a genuine entitlement, including qualifying statutes, regulations, contracts, tenure arrangements, licenses, or legally recognized understandings. An expectation is insufficient where governing law reserves discretion. A contractual dispute does not automatically become a constitutional case merely because government is a party. Reputation alone does not ordinarily suffice; stigma coupled with a change in legal status presents a different question.

Once an interest exists, government cannot make its own chosen procedure the constitutional maximum. Stone weighs the interest at stake, the risk of mistaken deprivation, the likely value of an added safeguard, and the government's lawful needs, including urgency and cost. He asks what would actually let this person contest this decision. Sometimes disclosed documents and a written response suffice. Sometimes disputed facts, credibility, disability, technical evidence, or the consequence of error require questioning, assistance, or a fuller hearing. There is no universal constitutional right to a trial or appointed lawyer for every administrative dispute.

Notice must identify the material grounds. Access to evidence must permit a meaningful response. A sufficiently neutral decisionmaker must be able to correct the decision and explain it enough for available review. Confidentiality can justify protective arrangements, but cannot make the decisive accusation impossible to contest. Combining investigative and adjudicative functions does not alone establish bias; a direct financial stake or an intolerable structural incentive can do so without an admission of personal prejudice.

Advance process is ordinarily required when practicable. A genuine emergency can justify prompt temporary action followed by effective review. Government cannot create urgency through its own delay. An adequate later remedy may suffice for an unpredictable unauthorized deprivation that officials could not practicably prevent. It does not excuse a defective established procedure or foreseeable misuse of delegated power merely because a rule nominally forbade the conduct. Negligent loss alone is not a constitutional tort.

These principles apply to imprisonment and public employment without making every adverse decision actionable. Meaningful legal limits on prison officials' discretion can matter to a liberty claim; Stone does not make an unusually harsh comparison with ordinary prison life the exclusive doorway to protection. Discretionary release and liberty already granted on conditions are different interests. An arrest may justify temporarily removing an employee from sensitive duties while leaving the need to stop pay or delay a hearing unproved.

Access to courts protects a real opportunity to present a nonfrivolous claim. A claimant must identify an actual or sufficiently imminent obstacle to that opportunity, not merely an imperfect library or preferred form of assistance. The claimant need not prove the underlying case would ultimately win. Where a required filing, transcript, or technical presentation makes an important protected proceeding inaccessible, Stone examines the actual legal entitlement and the assistance necessary to make that proceeding usable.

**Inference.** An automated benefits decision that conceals its material inputs and cannot be corrected by an accountable reviewer is highly vulnerable. A transparent calculation with an effective correction procedure can survive. The defect is inability to challenge the actual ground, not the use of a computer. Similarly, a general legislative rule ordinarily needs no individualized hearing, while a targeted factual adjudication cannot escape safeguards by being called legislation.

### Personal liberty: the boundary of substantive protection

**Established commitments.** Stone protects bodily integrity, consensual adult intimacy, family integrity, and movement. He defines the claimed liberty with enough precision to identify its constitutional source and a workable boundary, while avoiding a description so narrow that every unfamiliar application appears to be an entirely new right. He considers text, structure, historical evidence, precedent, the function of the protection, and sound analogies to recognized rights. Neither the word autonomy nor the absence of an exact historical practice decides the question.

Government may not criminalize consensual adult intimacy merely from moral disapproval. Where it monopolizes a civil family status, access must satisfy the applicable constitutional requirements of liberty and equality. Religious institutions retain authority over their own sacraments. This does not require government to create every desired status or benefit.

Direct bodily invasion, sterilization, unwanted medication, and compelled exposure to serious physical danger receive demanding examination. Ordinary public-health regulation does not automatically receive the same treatment. Stone examines the intervention, its authority, actual risks, affected rights, and the doctrine governing it.

**Inference.** He is strongly disposed against exclusion from civil marriage that rests on condemning a couple's protected adult relationship. The precise division of work between due process and equal protection requires its own reasoning. He would not infer a general constitutional entitlement to any relationship arrangement from that conclusion.

### Reproductive liberty: choice, regulation, methods, and funding

**Established commitments.** Before viability, the pregnant person makes the decision without having to supply a government-approved reason. Government may support potential life, truthful informed choice, competent care, and genuine safety. It may not prohibit the protected decision, transfer it to another person's veto, or impose a substantial obstacle. Stone assesses the people actually burdened and the law's actual operation, including cost, delay, distance, provider availability, confidentiality, and cumulative practical effects. He does not require proof that every possible application fails or add a numerical population threshold to defeat an otherwise established claim.

His analysis has a defined order:

1. Identify any prohibited interference, including a previability ban, third-party veto, substantial obstacle, or denial of constitutionally necessary timely care.
2. Identify the government's actual purpose. Accurate information, informed consent, safety, and lawful persuasion differ from obstruction, punishment of the choice, or misleading medical instruction.
3. Determine the real burden caused by the law on the affected population, using reliable evidence rather than formal availability alone.
4. Apply the consequence warranted by that burden. A prohibited interference is invalid. Other severe interference requires compelling justification and narrow tailoring. Meaningful lesser interference requires substantiated reasons, a defensible fit, and examination of materially less burdensome alternatives. Ordinary regulation below those thresholds retains appropriate latitude.
5. Protect truthful medical communication and reasonable clinical judgment. Government cannot turn a consent process into compelled misinformation or make a clinical exception unusable.
6. Choose relief that removes the proved violation and preserves valid, separable regulation where possible.

After viability, the balance changes, but prompt care must remain possible when good-faith medical judgment identifies a material risk of death, serious physical injury, substantial irreversible impairment, or grave medically established mental-health danger. The patient need not approach death. Genuine uncertainty supported by substantial medical authority is not proof that waiting is safe. Minors retain rights; parental-involvement rules require a confidential, timely, criteria-bound alternative and an effective emergency exception. Provider-conscience claims depend on their own legal sources, while government remains responsible for arranging lawful access where it controls access.

**Working position on method restrictions.** Protecting the decision does not constitutionally entrench every technique. A precisely defined method restriction can survive if it serves an independently legitimate medical or ethical interest, actually advances that interest, preserves realistically available alternatives, and satisfies the standard warranted by its burdens. Ethical language cannot conceal condemnation of abortion itself. A claimed medical difference requires evidence. Alternatives must be assessed for individual safety, availability, cost, delay, and enforcement effects, rather than deemed equivalent because both terminate pregnancy. A usable exception must allow timely reasonable medical judgment where substitution creates material health risk. Medical disagreement is neither automatic permission nor automatic invalidity.

**Working position on funding.** Stone distinguishes a general decision not to create insurance from selective denial within an existing medical benefit. When government finances a category of pregnancy care, it cannot exclude otherwise eligible treatment solely because it implements the protected decision, where the exclusion materially interferes with that decision and fails the corresponding justification requirement. A categorical exclusion of medically necessary lawful abortion from otherwise comprehensive pregnancy coverage is strongly inconsistent with this position. Neutral program-wide clinical and financial eligibility rules remain permissible. An abortion-specific demand to justify the choice is suspect where comparable pregnancy care carries no such demand.

Custody or effective governmental control over medical access supplies an additional reason to require necessary care. This position does not establish universal public financing of every treatment. It can require equal administration of an established benefit, but a court must separately address appropriations, severability, lawful funding authority, and the remedy. It cannot invent an appropriation.

**Inferences.** A method restriction with a genuine independent purpose, usable substitutes, no meaningful additional burden, and an effective medical exception is likely to survive. The same text may fail where providers disappear or the exception exposes reasonable urgent care to prosecution. Funding ordinary support for childbirth is generally permissible; using an existing comprehensive medical entitlement to impose a substantial obstacle to the protected alternative is not.

### Family authority and children's independent interests

**Established commitments.** A fit parent's decisions receive a strong presumption. A judge's preference for another upbringing or a more beneficial visitation schedule is insufficient. Children possess their own interests in safety, liberty, and established family relationships. Government may protect against demonstrated serious harm, but poverty, disability, unconventional family structure, or lawful disagreement with officials does not itself establish danger or parental unfitness.

State intervention is stage-specific. A report may justify investigation without justifying removal. Emergency removal requires specific facts showing imminent serious harm and insufficient time for judicial authorization. Continued separation requires prompt neutral review and clear and convincing proof of present serious danger, a material relationship between separation and protection, and the absence of a workable less disruptive arrangement. Government cannot turn an inaccessible service plan or its own delay into proof that the family should be permanently separated.

Stone supports appointed counsel for an indigent parent no later than the first hearing seeking judicially authorized continued removal, and in termination proceedings. Children require independent representation where removal, prolonged custody, or termination is sought, with their own position and any divergence from the adults' interests taken seriously. Necessary expert assistance must make material specialized evidence contestable, and indigent access must include a transcript where meaningful appellate review requires it. These are personal procedural commitments, not an assumption that all civil litigation requires appointed counsel.

**Working position on third-party contact.** Serious harm is sufficient but not the only permissible reason to override a fit parent's decision. A second, bounded ground is necessary protection of a child's independently established legal interest in the continuity of an exceptional caregiving relationship. The claimant must establish by clear and convincing evidence sustained parental functions, settled dependence, a legitimate basis for the arrangement, and a child-centered need for intervention. The arrangement must not have been obtained through abuse, coercion, or exploitation of economic vulnerability.

The interest needs a constitutionally defensible legal source. A statute cannot defeat parental liberty merely by calling every affectionate relationship protected. Nor can a judge manufacture an independent constitutional right from a preference for continued contact. The parent must have meaningfully supported an actual parental role, the caregiver must hold an independent legal responsibility, or exceptional circumstances such as abandonment must supply a sound legal basis. Routine childcare assistance is insufficient.

Even a qualifying relationship does not automatically entitle the adult to visitation. The claimant must show that the parent's decision materially impairs the protected relationship and that the requested intervention is necessary. The parent retains substantial deference and need not prove the caregiver dangerous. Relief should preserve the established interest with the least intrusion justified by the record, without automatically transferring medical, educational, or residential authority. Procedures should prevent repeated litigation from becoming coercion.

**Inference.** A grandparent's loving after-school relationship ordinarily leaves the parent's choice controlling. A legally recognized caregiver who actually raised the child and became a parental figure may support limited continuity of contact even without proof of impending clinical injury. The decisive difference is the established legal relationship and necessity of relief, not which adult the judge prefers.

### Medical refusal, forced medication, and competence

**Established commitments.** A competent adult may refuse unwanted treatment, including life-sustaining treatment. Reliable capacity safeguards, valid advance directives, and legally adequate substituted judgment protect rather than extinguish that choice. Good-faith palliative care is distinguishable from an asserted right to assistance in dying. Stone recognizes no freestanding constitutional right to physician-assisted death and leaves its authorization or prohibition to states within other constitutional limits.

Trial competence, medical decision-making capacity, criminal responsibility at the time of an act, and present dangerousness are separate questions. One finding does not prove the others. Once a sufficiently grounded doubt about trial competence requires adjudication, Stone's established personal preference places the burden on government to establish competence by a preponderance of the evidence. He does not treat a defendant's difficulty proving incompetence as evidence of competence.

Compulsory medication independently requires lawful authority, individualized medical appropriateness and necessity, meaningful consideration of less intrusive alternatives, and adequate review. The effect on communication with counsel, testimony, and participation matters to trial fairness. An established medication error is not automatically structural; the appropriate harmless-error rule and its burden must be applied where that doctrine governs.

**Working position on prosecution-only medication.** The desire to make prosecution possible does not authorize forcible psychiatric medication of a nondangerous defendant who refuses it and for whom no independent lawful treatment ground exists. This is a substantive boundary, including for exceptionally grave accusations. The strength of the prosecutorial interest does not itself establish guilt, danger, or medical incapacity.

Independent emergency, protective, or patient-welfare authority remains possible, but must actually be proved under the governing standards. Trial incompetence alone does not establish incapacity to refuse medication. A nominally therapeutic order cannot disguise a prosecution-only intervention. Voluntary treatment, appropriate accommodations, and other lawful restoration measures remain available. Consent cannot be manufactured through unlawful detention or unrelated threats.

**Inference and remedy.** On the stipulated absence of independent treatment authority, Stone would reverse an order authorizing medication solely to restore competence and seek effective review before treatment occurs. That does not acquit the defendant or automatically dismiss charges. Continuing custody requires its own review; futile restoration efforts cannot justify indefinite confinement. If existing precedent permits the intervention, adopting this working position requires a candid doctrinal change rather than pretending the precedent concerned only procedure.

### Criminal responsibility, evidence, and fair notice

**Established commitments.** Government must prove every enacted offense element beyond a reasonable doubt. It cannot retain a mental-state element while arbitrarily excluding material evidence that bears on whether the defendant possessed that state. Evidence can still be regulated for genuine reliability and trial-management reasons. Competence to stand trial addresses participation now; responsibility addresses the person's condition during the alleged act.

Criminal law must give fair notice and constrain enforcement discretion. A prosecutor's or officer's instruction cannot supply an intelligible legal boundary absent from the prohibition. Stone does not turn every instance of imprecision into invalidity; he examines the actual conduct regulated, the governing vagueness standard, and the opportunities for arbitrary enforcement. Courts cannot retroactively enlarge criminal liability through an unforeseeable interpretation.

**Working position on profound incapacity.** Intentional conduct and criminal responsibility are not interchangeable. Due process should protect a minimum defense where a severe mental disorder or comparably profound impairment caused an inability, at the time of the act, meaningfully to appreciate its serious wrongfulness in the circumstances as the defendant understood them. A diagnosis, diminished judgment, lack of remorse, ideological justification, or disagreement with the law does not establish that incapacity. Being able to recite a prohibition does not necessarily establish a meaningful ability to appreciate and apply it.

The defendant must identify an evidence-supported claim. Government retains its burden on offense elements. A defendant's preponderance burden on the separate affirmative defense is a provisional acceptable arrangement, not a settled constitutional requirement for every jurisdiction. The defense must remain realistically provable, with necessary psychiatric assistance for an indigent defendant and access to material contemporaneous evidence. The legal factfinder decides responsibility; an expert supplies evidence rather than the legal judgment.

**Inference.** If profound incapacity is established but the law treats proof of physical intent as the complete answer, Stone is likely to reject that law's sufficiency. If the defendant understood the serious wrongfulness but approved the act for personal ideological reasons, the constitutional defense fails. An irresistible impulse without inability to appreciate wrongfulness presents a separate unresolved boundary. A finding of past nonresponsibility does not establish present grounds for confinement, and is not automatically equivalent to factual innocence for every postconviction rule.

### Sentencing facts and the jury's authority

**Established commitment.** Labels cannot transfer to a sentencing judge factual authority constitutionally assigned to the jury. Judges may find facts to select a sentence within the range already authorized by a valid verdict or admission. That differs from finding a fact necessary to make a greater punishment legally available or mandatory.

**Working position.** The same effect-based rule should govern facts increasing either the statutory maximum or a mandatory minimum, including prior-conviction enhancements. Stone would recognize no categorical prior-conviction exception. Disputed historical facts necessary for the increase, including identity and facts about the earlier conviction, require jury determination beyond a reasonable doubt unless validly admitted or resolved through a valid jury waiver. Judges retain legal interpretation, admissibility decisions, and sentencing discretion within the independently authorized range. The legal classification of a proved conviction differs from finding new disputed facts about earlier conduct.

A valid jury waiver changes the factfinder, not the government's obligation to prove disputed facts necessary for the greater authorized punishment beyond a reasonable doubt.

Adequate timely notice must permit a defense. Federal felony indictment requirements arise from their own constitutional source; the notice rule does not itself incorporate the federal grand-jury requirement against states. A separate enhancement phase, stipulations, and carefully limited records can protect the guilt decision from unfair prejudice. Reliability of the earlier judgment is relevant evidence, but does not decide which institution has authority to find the facts necessary for the new punishment. Whether an earlier judgment can be collaterally attacked is a separate question.

**Inference and remedy.** If a disputed prior increases a mandatory floor, Stone would require the constitutionally assigned factfinding even when the judge regards the record as reliable. If it only influences a sentence within the authorized range, judicial consideration can remain valid. A violation ordinarily calls for correction of the enhancement or resentencing through lawful procedures; it does not automatically invalidate the underlying conviction, eliminate preservation rules, or establish collateral relief.

### Civil commitment and other continuing custody

**Established commitments.** Government must identify a present lawful basis for confinement. A completed sentence, a frightening past act, or a civil label does not provide indefinite authority. Conversely, the end of a criminal sentence does not prevent a genuinely independent civil commitment when its substantive conditions are satisfied. Conditions, purpose, treatment, and a real route to release matter to whether confinement operates as civil care or punishment.

**Working position on preventive commitment after punishment.** Secure confinement requires clear and convincing government proof of a serious present functional impairment in behavioral control or relevant appreciation of reality, a substantial connection between that impairment and a demonstrated likelihood of grave harm to others, and the inadequacy of reasonably workable less restrictive alternatives. A diagnostic label plus statistical danger is insufficient. The impairment need not eliminate all control, but it must distinguish serious incapacity from ordinary unwillingness to obey the law. The anticipated harm must be tied to a defensible time horizon; it need not be an attack expected within hours.

The inquiry is individualized. Past conduct can supply evidence without becoming a permanent presumption. Age, present functioning, changed circumstances, reliable risk evidence, and feasible supervision matter. Government cannot dismiss a workable alternative solely by preferring institutional convenience. Financial constraints remain relevant to what arrangements are realistically available, but do not automatically justify indefinite maximum restraint.

Confinement carries duties of individualized clinically appropriate treatment or supportive care, humane nonpunitive conditions, and a genuine path to reduced restrictions. A cure need not exist, and therapeutic failure does not automatically require release. Symptom management, prevention of deterioration, and risk-management care may be legitimate treatment. Custody, meals, and guards alone are not treatment. Release cannot depend exclusively on an unavailable program, an unattainable therapeutic goal, or compulsory confession. Authority to confine does not automatically authorize medication.

Independent judicial review should occur automatically, ordinarily at least annually, with earlier review after a material change. Government retains the burden of showing that the substantive grounds and need for the degree of restraint continue. Indigent people require counsel at significant adversarial proceedings initiating or continuing secure commitment, necessary independent expert assistance, access to material clinical evidence, and reasoned decisions. A nominal right to file an unaided petition does not replace a usable review system.

**Inferences and remedies.** A seriously impaired person who remains gravely dangerous may remain lawfully confined even if the condition is incurable, provided necessity, care, and review remain real. If supervised placement can adequately address the danger, secure confinement should give way to the justified alternative. If impairment or qualifying danger ends, the commitment must end, subject to any independent lawful custody. An isolated remediable care failure ordinarily warrants correction. Persistent warehousing that defeats the civil justification can require a bounded opportunity to cure followed by transfer or termination of the unlawful confinement if the defect remains.

Other custody requires its own analysis. Pretrial restraint cannot be allocated by wealth where workable conditions address the lawful risk. Detention awaiting restoration or removal cannot continue indefinitely merely because the government's objective remains unachieved. When removal is not reasonably foreseeable, that failure cannot itself become a new preventive-detention authority; any independent protective confinement needs its own lawful basis and findings. A person mistakenly released from a valid criminal sentence does not automatically acquire immunity from serving its lawful remainder; process, credit, government misconduct, and extraordinary reliance require separate consideration.

### Actual innocence and access to evidence

**Established commitments.** Stone rejects execution in the face of clear and convincing affirmative proof of factual innocence. More broadly, reliable proof of innocence cannot simply be ignored to preserve finality while criminal punishment continues. The strength required for ultimate relief, the available forum, and the applicable procedural rules remain distinct questions. An asserted constitutional protection is not an automatic grant of federal habeas jurisdiction or an unrestricted right to relitigate a conviction.

**Working position on evidence access.** A person facing continuing punishment should not have to prove innocence before obtaining government-controlled evidence that could materially establish it. Stone would recognize an independent procedural due-process minimum implemented through two stages:

1. A specific, nonfrivolous factual-innocence claim triggers reasonable access to basic information needed to identify relevant evidence, including appropriate inventory, preservation, and testing records. The prisoner need not supply catalog information known only to government.
2. Reliable, feasible examination should ordinarily be authorized where there is a reasonable possibility of producing new, materially probative innocence evidence. The applicant must identify a concrete factual issue, sufficiently identifiable material, a scientifically defensible method, and reasonable scope. The applicant need not predict a favorable result or show that one favorable test would independently require release.

Government must explain facts exclusively within its control, including an adequately supported search and disposition account if it says evidence no longer exists. A guilty plea, delay, or earlier strategic choice is relevant but not a categorical bar. Materially improved science can justify retesting; identical requests without meaningful new circumstances can be denied. Legitimate privacy, sample preservation, scientific feasibility, and verification concerns can shape access. General confidence in the verdict is not an independent answer to an otherwise qualifying request.

Indigence cannot defeat an established testing entitlement. Public payment for authorized testing, counsel when substantial legal or scientific development is necessary, and limited independent expert assistance where needed make this particular protection usable. This is not a general right to publicly financed postconviction investigation. States may administer adequate procedures; absence or practical failure of those procedures cannot erase the constitutional minimum.

**Inference and remedy.** Stone would likely order an inventory and qualifying identity testing where officials demand advance proof of innocence that only the withheld sample could supply. He would deny a speculative request for every investigative file with no material innocence issue. Relief ordinarily provides preservation, disclosure, testing, or reconsideration. Favorable results require a separate judgment about the conviction or punishment. Imminent execution or threatened destruction can require timely protective relief through an authorized judicial channel.

### Medical information and continuing public registration

**Established commitments on information.** Stone recognizes a protected interest against unjustified governmental disclosure of highly personal medical information. The purpose, sensitivity, scope, recipients, safeguards, and actual use matter. Acquisition and internal use present related questions; his personal approach requires a proportionate justification for identified medical information rather than accepting fiscal oversight as a complete answer. Consent is relevant without being either universally necessary or a license for every later use. He does not impose the least intrusive imaginable method on every legitimate administrative inquiry.

Private holders of information acquire constitutional duties only through a valid basis for attribution to government. Other privacy duties depend on applicable statutes, contracts, professional obligations, or other law. Neither privacy's importance nor the permanence of a record creates a universal right to erase truthful history.

**Working position on conviction-based registration.** Stone separates an accurate historical conviction record, a confidential police database, continuing public disclosure of current identifying information, compulsory reporting, and restrictions on residence, movement, work, or family life. These impose different burdens and require different justifications. Private stigma alone is not automatically a constitutional deprivation, but government cannot ignore severe predictable consequences of its own continuing publication when assessing a combined regime of legal restraints.

A conviction can initially justify appropriately connected regulatory measures. As substantial restrictions become more severe and prolonged, government needs a defensible continuing connection between the burden and its actual protective purpose. A credible showing of severe continuing burdens and materially weakened original risk assumptions should require government to justify continued application with relevant evidence. The original offense, later conduct, age, time without offending, reliable assessments, effectiveness of disclosure, and workable alternatives matter. No fixed number of offense-free years automatically proves safety or creates a universal expiration date.

The issue is substantive before it is procedural. A hearing about present dangerousness does no legal work if a valid law makes dangerousness irrelevant. Stone must first justify why this particular continuing restraint constitutionally requires reconsideration. Once that requirement is established, an accessible review procedure must make it effective. Ordinary archival accuracy does not become unlawful merely because the person has rehabilitated.

A separate inquiry asks whether the regime actually imposes punishment. The governing legal framework for that question must be used, rather than treating every burden or deterrent effect as conclusive. A punitive finding has issue-specific consequences, including for retroactive additions; a civil finding does not itself settle substantive liberty objections.

**Inference and remedy.** A modest confidential registry with a demonstrated investigative function is likely to receive substantial deference. Decades of irreversible public listing combined with serious movement and residence restrictions, despite credible changed circumstances, would face a strong challenge. Stone would target the unjustified restrictions or provide effective reconsideration, rather than automatically erase the historical conviction. Vacatur of the sole predicate requires a fresh legal basis for continued restrictions.

### Executive abuse, custody, and affirmative danger

**Established personal commitments.** Due process does not make government an insurer against private harm or unsafe voluntary employment. Custody changes the analysis because government has restrained the person's ability to obtain safety, basic necessities, or care. The applicable custodial standard governs, including respect for genuine professional judgment where appropriate.

Outside custody, Stone supports a claim when an affirmative use of authority creates or materially increases a specific foreseeable danger, or knowingly blocks available protection or rescue, and the official consciously disregards a known serious risk despite time and a practical opportunity to act. The claimant must establish the government's contribution, required culpability, and causation. Mere knowledge of private wrongdoing, general regulatory responsibility, and negligence are insufficient. Where existing law does not recognize that rule, his support is a proposal to change the law.

A genuine fast-moving emergency affects what an official could perceive and do. Conduct during an opportunity for deliberation must be judged in that setting. Deliberately injuring someone for an unauthorized purpose cannot be justified merely by invoking official responsibility. Where a more specific constitutional protection governs, its elements control.

**Inference.** Failing to prevent every known private threat is not enough. Using official authority to strand someone in a known serious danger or prevent an available rescue can qualify when the required knowledge, opportunity, and causation are established. Even then, damages require a valid cause of action, a proper defendant, and resolution of any applicable immunity.

### Retroactive punishment, civil sanctions, and forfeiture

**Established commitments.** The government cannot evade punishment-related protections simply by naming a sanction civil. Stone examines statutory purpose, design, and actual operation under the legal standard governing the specific claim. A civil label is meaningful evidence, not conclusive authority. Where the governing punitive-effects rule demands the clearest proof to overcome civil intent, he does not silently replace that burden with ordinary balancing.

Different constitutional provisions ask different questions. A financial sanction's punitive component for excessive-fines analysis does not automatically make it criminal punishment for double jeopardy. A severe civil consequence is not automatically an additional criminal prosecution. The same distinction applies to genuinely therapeutic confinement and confinement functioning as retrospective punishment.

Retroactive criminal changes cannot increase punishment or reduce the legally required proof of guilt. Ordinary procedural or evidentiary alterations require careful classification rather than an assumption that every disadvantage is prohibited. For release and sentence-credit changes, Stone examines the concrete entitlement, the actual risk of greater punishment, and whether a claimed substitute is genuinely available in time. The challenger must establish the constitutional injury; government must substantiate factual claims about substitutes within its knowledge. A paper possibility of leniency is not equivalent to a usable legal protection.

Forfeiture requires attention to ownership, participation, notice, proportionality, and a meaningful opportunity to contest the taking. Stone supports protection for a genuinely independent innocent co-owner who did not participate, lacked awareness, and took reasonable preventive steps where available. Mere assertion of ignorance is not an unlimited defense. Ordinary real-property seizure requires advance process unless government proves genuine exigency and the inadequacy of less drastic measures. Punitive forfeiture also receives its own excessive-fines inquiry.

**Inference and remedy.** Stone would likely reject an automatic deprivation of an uninvolved co-owner's interest where no meaningful innocent-ownership protection exists. He would preserve lawful forfeiture of a responsible person's interest where the statute, facts, procedure, and proportionality support it. A defect in one share or one use of the sanction need not invalidate the entire enforcement system.

### Ordinary economic regulation, retroactive liability, and punitive damages

**Established commitments.** Ordinary licensing, price regulation, and economic legislation receive rational-basis review. Stone does not constitutionalize his preferred economic policy. Legitimate protection of property and genuine reliance does not entail a fundamental right to freedom from regulation. An unlawful purpose or a sham justification must still be assessed under the governing rule, rather than presumed from poor policy design.

**Working position on exceptional retroactive liability.** Government may allocate the consequences of past activities where a reasonable relationship connects the assessment to responsibility, an undertaking, risk creation, or legally relevant participation. Prior legality does not guarantee permanent immunity from later civil liability. A company can bear responsibility for harm without having been negligent or having anticipated every legal consequence.

The challenger must first identify a genuinely retrospective new burden and facts suggesting serious disconnection, severe disproportion, or exceptional impairment of legitimate settled reliance. Government must then provide a reasoned basis for that retrospective allocation, not merely a good reason for addressing the social problem prospectively. Stone examines the actual historical connection, passage of time, completed transactions, ability to plan or insure, magnitude and allocation of liability, availability of evidence, and meaningful review of disputed responsibility and calculations. These considerations guide judgment; they are not a requirement that every economic statute satisfy a new checklist or strict scrutiny.

Courts should defer to reasonable methods of distributing genuine collective responsibilities. General taxation does not require a causal connection between every taxpayer and each funded program. A special historical assessment demands a more specific account than the payer's wealth. Reliance on concealing harm or continuing to shift its costs to others is weaker than reliance on a genuinely settled transaction.

**Inferences.** Requiring a producer to contribute reasonably to cleanup or compensation for harm linked to its own activity is likely permissible, even where the risk was originally unknown. Charging a long-departed firm for unrelated later promises by others is much more vulnerable. An extreme allocation to one minor contributor may justify as-applied relief without invalidating the entire compensation program. A procedural defect calls for a meaningful contest; a constitutionally disconnected obligation calls for relief from the obligation itself.

**Established position on punitive damages.** Stone requires fair procedures, a record adequate to explain and review the award, and meaningful judicial review of the amount. He does not favor an independent federal substantive ceiling merely because a reviewing court considers a properly reviewed award excessive, and he does not adopt a fixed constitutional ratio. The distinction is between review that guards against arbitrary decision and federal substitution of its own preferred number. Punishing lawful out-of-state conduct without a sufficient in-state connection presents a separate territorial limit. His position can therefore demand meaningful amount review while rejecting a general federal power to recalculate every punitive award.

### Treatment of precedent

**Established commitments.** Stone starts with the actual holding, its legal source, its factual premises, later treatment, workability, and concrete reliance. His disagreement with the result is insufficient to overrule it. Neither age nor a change in Court membership supplies an independent reason to retain or discard a rule.

He distinguishes a precedent when a legally material difference means its rule does not decide the case. He narrows it when its proper rationale does not support its broader language or reach. He will overrule it when a sufficient legal reason warrants replacement, including conflict with superior text or structure, internal contradiction, demonstrated unworkability, a failed factual premise, or systematic frustration of the constitutional protection the rule claims to implement. He does not multiply artificial distinctions until a supposedly retained rule has no content.

Concrete reliance matters. Settled property and contractual arrangements deserve serious attention. So do lives and institutions organized around established access, equal status, and procedural protection. An expectation that other people will remain excluded does not carry the same weight. Administrative preference is not equivalent to a completed reliance interest. Statutory precedent ordinarily receives additional respect because the legislature can amend the law, without becoming unchangeable in every circumstance.

When change is necessary, his opinion should explain the prior rule, the legal defect, the replacement boundary, the applications that remain valid, and the treatment of reliance. The correction must respond to the demonstrated defect. It may be categorical, contextual, procedural, or a changed burden; he has no general preference for one form. A new burden of proof is a legal change, not simply a stricter assessment of the same evidence.

**Inference.** He is most likely to depart when a doctrine permits the very deprivation its constitutional source should constrain, such as an evidence-access rule demanding proof obtainable only from withheld evidence. He may still apply an unfavorable precedent where its validity is not properly presented or the claimed defect has not been established. Calling a rule harsh does not substitute for the required legal argument.

### Disposition and remedy

**Established commitments.** Stone separates finding a violation from authority to redress it. Jurisdiction, standing, the proper defendant, a recognized cause of action, immunity, and the available remedy must each be satisfied. A constitutional conclusion does not automatically authorize damages, an appropriation, suppression, release, or relief from a final judgment.

Where lawful relief is available, it must actually correct the proved injury. He ordinarily prefers an adequate as-applied judgment or severance to unnecessary facial invalidation. That preference is not a reason to preserve an inseparable unconstitutional scheme or require repetitive individual litigation where a lawful broader remedy is necessary. Structural relief requires proof of institutional failure and a legal vehicle capable of addressing it.

The violation determines the remedial task:

- Inadequate notice, inaccessible evidence, or a biased decision ordinarily calls for a valid procedure, disclosure, or a neutral redetermination. Interim protection depends on the injury from waiting and the applicable remedial standard.
- An impermissible substantive interference requires ending that interference. Another hearing cannot authorize an action government has no constitutional power to take.
- Unjustified custody requires release from that custody or transition to an independently lawful, less restrictive arrangement. It does not extinguish unrelated lawful grounds for detention.
- A trial error receives its governing prejudice or harmless-error analysis. Stone examines the actual proceeding and what the error could have affected, rather than treating enough untainted evidence to convict as the complete answer. A genuinely structural error retains its distinct rule; seriousness alone does not make an error structural.
- A missing necessary factual finding may call for remand. Remand is not a device to invent proof, evade an assigned burden, or give unlimited chances to justify a completed violation.
- Threatened forced treatment, execution, destruction of material evidence, or another irreversible injury can require timely interim protection through an authorized channel. Relief after the event may fail to protect the right at all.

He does not presume that a newly recognized rule applies retroactively in every procedural posture. Direct review, final judgments, collateral proceedings, prospective relief, and damages can present different legal questions. Those questions must be answered when they are necessary to the requested disposition.

### What can be inferred, and what still needs a case

The foundation is sufficient to make educated predictions across the principal due-process issues, including difficult cases. Confidence is strongest about the interests Stone protects, the defects he regards as constitutionally serious, and the reasoning he would demand. Confidence about a final disposition depends on the record and controlling law. Confidence about a remedy additionally depends on posture, jurisdiction, and the available enforcement mechanism.

A future analysis should state the likely position, identify whether it rests on an established commitment or a working position, explain the decisive facts and burden of proof, identify any necessary treatment of precedent, and give the lawful disposition and remedy. It should state the strongest contrary argument and which factual or legal change could alter the result. Predictions must be labeled as inferences rather than converted into completed votes.

The remaining choices most likely to alter a prediction at this level are:

- **Responsibility beyond inability to appreciate wrongfulness.** Whether a proved inability to control conduct independently requires a constitutional defense remains unsettled. So does the maximum permissible burden of persuasion for the separate incapacity defense. These can change whether an intentional act supports criminal punishment; they do not reopen the chosen minimum protection for profound inability to appreciate wrongfulness.
- **Administration of continuing-registration review.** The substantive need for renewed justification is supplied. The precise trigger, proof standard, and review interval for a particular combination of restrictions require evidence about its burdens and protective function. A modest confidential database and pervasive lifetime public restrictions cannot be assigned one arbitrary deadline.
- **The outer reach of protected caregiving relationships.** The exceptional, legally grounded continuity category is supplied. Whether a particular informal arrangement has a defensible legal source, rather than merely substantial affection or useful assistance, requires the governing family law and actual responsibilities. Recognition of a new constitutional child interest requires an express legal argument.
- **Exceptional reliance after erroneous release and finality after new proof.** Ordinary mistaken release does not erase a valid sentence, and qualifying evidence access does not itself vacate a conviction. Extraordinary official misconduct, exceptionally settled reliance, the ultimate showing for noncapital innocence relief, and the legal route to reopen a final judgment remain posture-specific questions. Their answers can change relief even where Stone's substantive concern is clear.

These remaining boundaries are reasons to identify the decisive issue in the next case, not reasons to withhold a supported prediction on the questions already answered.

## 1. Completed cases

OT1991
- Collins v. City of Harker Heights
  > Held (Stevens for the Court, 9-0): The Due Process Clause does not require a city to give its voluntary employees a workplace free of unreasonable risks. Taking a public job is not the kind of custody that creates a constitutional duty of care.
- Foucha v. Louisiana
  > Held (White for the Court, 6-3): Once Louisiana conceded that an insanity acquittee was no longer mentally ill, it could not keep him in psychiatric confinement because he failed to prove he was not dangerous. Five Justices added that a new commitment requires proof of current mental illness and dangerousness by clear and convincing evidence.
- Riggins v. Nevada
  > Held (O'Connor for 6, judgment 7-2): Once a pretrial detainee objects to antipsychotic medication, the State must show it is medically appropriate and needed enough to override his refusal, considering less intrusive means. Forced medication through trial without that showing required vacating the convictions for a new trial.
- Medina v. California
  > Held (no opinion of the Court, 6-3): Six Justices agreed that California may require a defendant claiming incompetence to prove it by a preponderance of the evidence at an adequate hearing. No single opinion drew five joins, so no general method controls.
- Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey
  > Held (Stone for 6): Before viability a woman keeps the right to decide whether to continue a pregnancy, and the State may not forbid that choice, give anyone else control over it, or block timely care needed for her life or health. In a part joined by five, the Court replaced the trimester timetable with a six-step standard for previability regulation.

OT1992
- Herrera v. Collins
  > Held (no opinion of the Court, 5-4): Herrera's late, inconsistent affidavits do not entitle him to federal habeas relief, even assuming a truly persuasive showing of innocence could bar an execution. Whether such a freestanding innocence claim exists was left undecided.
- Reno v. Flores
  > Held (Scalia for 6, judgment 7-2): A juvenile held pending deportation who has no available parent, guardian or close relative has no fundamental right to release to an unrelated willing adult instead of temporary, nonpunitive government care. Review on request, rather than an automatic hearing, is not facially inadequate.
- Sullivan v. Louisiana
  > Held (Scalia for the Court, 9-0): A reasonable-doubt instruction that vitiates the standard for all of the jury's findings leaves no valid guilty verdict. The error is structural and requires reversal without harmless-error review.
- Heller v. Doe
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 5-4): Kentucky's lower proof burden for committing adults with intellectual disability (clear and convincing evidence, against proof beyond a reasonable doubt for mental illness) violates equal protection. In a six-Justice part by Kennedy, letting relatives take part as parties does not by itself deny the adult due process where he has counsel and a fair chance to contest.
- TXO Production Corp. v. Alliance Resources Corp.
  > Held (no opinion of the Court, 5-4): The $10 million punitive award was affirmed. Five Justices agreed only that the jury process and judicial review given here satisfied due process; no rule about a permissible award size commands a majority.
- Austin v. United States
  > Held (Blackmun for the Court, 9-0): Civil forfeitures under 21 U.S.C. 881(a)(4) and (a)(7) are at least partly punishment and so are subject to the Excessive Fines Clause. The case was remanded to decide whether this forfeiture is excessive.
- Alexander v. United States
  > Held (Souter for 6): A criminal RICO forfeiture taken as punishment is subject to the Excessive Fines Clause regardless of the validity of the convictions or the prison term, so the lower court must weigh the forfeiture against the offenses. All nine accepted that remand.

OT1993
- Cavanaugh v. Roller
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 6-3): Applying to an earlier offender a later law that stretches parole reconsideration from every year to every two years violates the Ex Post Facto Clause when it removes a meaningful release opportunity with no substitute. The prisoner need not prove the lost hearing would have freed him.
- United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property
  > Held (Kennedy for the Court, 6-3): Before seizing real property for civil forfeiture, the Government must ordinarily give the owner notice and a hearing. Skipping that step requires showing that less drastic measures, such as a recorded notice, a restraining order or a bond, would not prevent sale, destruction or continued illegal use.
- Albright v. Oliver
  > Held (no opinion of the Court, 7-2): Dismissal of Albright's claimed substantive due process right against prosecution without probable cause was affirmed. No rationale drew a majority, so no general rule follows.
- Victor v. Nebraska / Sandoval v. California
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 9-0): A reasonable-doubt instruction violates due process if, read as a whole, there is a reasonable likelihood the jury understood it to allow conviction on less than proof beyond a reasonable doubt; no set wording is required. Both charges passed that test (Victor 6-3, Sandoval 9-0).
- Department of Revenue of Montana v. Kurth Ranch
  > Held (Blackmun for the Court, 6-3): Montana's drug tax, collected in a separate proceeding after criminal punishment for the same offense, is a second punishment barred by the Double Jeopardy Clause. That conclusion rests on the tax's combined features, not any single one.
- Honda Motor Co. v. Oberg
  > Held (Blackmun for the Court, 8-1): A State may not abolish judicial review of the amount of a punitive damages award without an adequate substitute. Oregon's safeguards did not replace that review, so the $5 million award was sent back for proper review.
- Shannon v. United States
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 6-3): Federal law does not require telling the jury what happens to a defendant found not guilty only by reason of insanity. A corrective instruction may be needed to fix a specific misstatement at trial, such as a claim that the defendant would go free, but none is reported here.

OT1994
- Fargo Women's Health Organization v. Schafer
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 6-3): Casey's complete six-step standard governs challenges to previability access rules, without Salerno's no-valid-application barrier, and finding no substantial obstacle does not end review. The access ruling was vacated and remanded; the vagueness challenges to the law's definitions failed, 8-1.
- California Department of Corrections v. Morales
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 5-4): Applying California's 1981 hearing-deferral law to a 1980 offense violates the Ex Post Facto Clause because it takes away required annual parole consideration without a proven substitute. The prisoner must show a meaningful lost opportunity but need not show the hearing would have freed him; the State must substantiate any substitute it invokes.
- Sandin v. Conner
  > Held (Breyer for the Court, 5-4): A State creates protected prison liberty when its substantive rules genuinely limit officials' power to impose a significant deprivation; mandatory wording alone is not enough. The case was remanded to decide the constraint, the deprivation and the process due.
- United States v. Gaudin
  > Held (Scalia for the Court, 9-0): When materiality is an element of a criminal false-statement offense, the Fifth and Sixth Amendments require the jury, not the judge, to decide it beyond a reasonable doubt.

OT1995
- A. St. P. C. v. B. C.
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 5-4): Louisiana must prove a parent's sexual abuse of a child by clear and convincing evidence before applying its statute cutting off all visitation and contact. A bare preponderance cannot trigger that severe restriction.
- Bennis v. Michigan
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 5-4): Due process bars forfeiting a co-owner's share of a car because of another's misuse when she proves she neither took part in nor knew of the misuse and did all she reasonably could to prevent it. The case was remanded so Tina Bennis could prove that defense.
- Planned Parenthood, Sioux Falls Clinic v. Miller
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 6-3): Under Casey's six-step standard, South Dakota's compulsory parental notice for minors' previability abortions is invalid where its operation gives a parent practical control or substantially obstructs timely care. Independently, the State failed to justify it against a confidential maturity or best-interests alternative.
- Cooper v. Oklahoma
  > Held (Kennedy for the Court, 9-0): Due process forbids making a defendant prove incompetence to stand trial by clear and convincing evidence, though a preponderance burden remains permissible. The conviction may stand only if a reliable look back finds he was competent at trial.
- Sheets v. Salt Lake County
  > Held (Stevens for the Court, 7-2): The Fourteenth Amendment protects intimate marital and family information given to officials for a limited criminal investigation against intentional official disclosure that causes its publication and lacks sufficient justification.
- BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore
  > Held (Stevens for the Court, 5-4): A grossly excessive punitive award violates due process, judged by reprehensibility, its relation to the harm, and comparable sanctions, and this $2 million award was grossly excessive. Six Justices, including Stone, held that a State may not punish conduct that was lawful where it occurred and had no effect in the State.
- Montana v. Egelhoff
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 5-4): Because Montana's courts read deliberate homicide as still requiring purpose or knowledge, due process forbids barring otherwise admissible intoxication evidence on that element without sufficient justification. The new-trial order stands.
- Leavitt v. Jane L.
  > Held (per curiam, 5-4): Utah's severability clause lets its separate ban after twenty weeks (section 302(3)) operate even though the separate prohibition in section 302(2) is invalid, so the appeals court could not strike it on inseverability alone. Its federal validity was left for the lower courts.
- Baby Richard v. Kirchner
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 5-4): When the State abruptly ends an established caregiving relationship to carry out a lawful custody transfer, the child has a liberty interest against serious, avoidable harm from how the transfer is done and must get a prompt chance to show it. That interest gives no right to defeat the father's custody.
- Lewis v. Casey
  > Held (Kennedy for 5): A prisoner claiming denial of court access must show actual or imminent hindrance of a nonfrivolous legal claim, though not that he would have won it. In a unanimous part, the Court held that books alone do not satisfy the access right for prisoners who cannot use them without help.
- United States v. Ursery
  > Held (O'Connor for 5): These civil forfeitures are not criminal punishment for double jeopardy purposes, because a civil label is overcome only by the clearest proof of punitive purpose or effect. Coverage by the Excessive Fines Clause does not settle that question.

OT1996
- United States v. Sahhar
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 9-0): Federal civil commitment under 18 U.S.C. 4246 does not end merely because the charges were dismissed or custody has outlasted the longest possible sentence. Continued custody must rest on current mental illness causing danger, with review, release and state-placement safeguards.
- Curtis v. School Committee of Falmouth
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 9-0): An optional school condom-availability program does not violate parental liberty or family privacy merely because parents cannot make the school exclude their children or notify them. Participation is voluntary and the program does not displace parents' own teaching.
- Lynce v. Mathis
  > Held (Thomas for 8, judgment 9-0): Before granting habeas relief on Lynce's Ex Post Facto claim, the lower courts must first decide exhaustion and the effect of the State's limited futility concession. The merits were not decided.
- Brown v. Hot, Sexy & Safer Productions
  > Held (Ginsburg for the Court, 9-0): Parental liberty protects choosing among lawful schooling options and teaching one's own children, but gives no parent a veto over each public-school lesson reasonably related to a genuine teaching goal. The complaint about a required school presentation stated no federal claim.
- Wright v. MetroHealth Medical Center
  > Held (Breyer for the Court, 9-0): A marriage-triggered employment rule gets heightened scrutiny only if it directly and substantially interferes with marrying. This hospital's limit on spouses working together in its flight unit was an incidental assignment burden that passes rational-basis review.
- Young v. Harper
  > Held (Breyer for the Court, 9-0): A prisoner released to live at home and work under a preparole program has conditional liberty protected by Morrissey v. Brewer. Revoking it requires Morrissey's preliminary and final hearing protections.
- Lambert v. Wicklund
  > Held (per curiam, 9-0): Montana's judicial bypass is not defective because it waives parental notice when notice is not in the minor's best interests, rather than asking whether the abortion itself is. A finding on notice alone requires authorization without notice.
- Doe v. SEPTA
  > Held (Kennedy for the Court, 5-4): Assuming without deciding a constitutional interest against unjustified government acquisition and use of intimate medical information, the transit agency's limited, benefits-related inquiry into the medical information it received did not violate it.
- Edwards v. Balisok
  > Held (Scalia for the Court, 9-0): A prisoner's damages claim that a disciplinary officer hid evidence or was biased cannot proceed under section 1983 while his loss of good-time credits stands, if success would necessarily show that loss invalid. Claims that do not imply invalidity are analyzed on their own.
- Gilbert v. Homar
  > Held (Breyer for the Court, 8-1): Assuming a property interest, due process allows a brief unpaid suspension of a police officer without a prior hearing after an independent felony arrest and formal charges, if prompt later process follows. All nine held that continued nonpayment requires a prompt, meaningful chance to contest it.
- Mazurek v. Armstrong
  > Held (per curiam, Part I for 6): A physician-only abortion rule must be reviewed under Casey's complete six-step standard, including its purpose inquiry and its protection against interference short of a substantial obstacle. Five Justices vacated the appeals court's judgment and remanded.
- Kansas v. Hendricks
  > Held (Breyer for the Court, 5-4): A law labeled civil is punishment only on the clearest proof of punitive purpose or effect, and Hendricks's commitment, added after his prison term, prisonlike and with inadequate treatment, met that test. Applied to his earlier conduct it violates the Ex Post Facto Clause; no due process holding was made.
- Vacco v. Quill
  > Held (Thomas for 5, judgment 9-0): New York does not deny equal protection by letting competent patients refuse treatment while banning assisted suicide. The line rests on consent, causation and intent and serves legitimate protective interests.
- Washington v. Glucksberg
  > Held (Kennedy for 5, judgment 9-0): The Due Process Clause gives competent, terminally ill adults no fundamental right to a physician's help in suicide through a lethal prescription, so Washington's ban needs only a rational basis, which its protective interests supply. A narrower, more particular claim is not foreclosed.

OT1997
- Hudson v. United States
  > Held (Breyer for 7, judgment 9-0): A civil label on sanctions is overcome only by the clearest proof that their purpose or effect is criminal; these banking penalties and the occupational bar fall short, so the later prosecution is not double jeopardy. Six Justices held that Halper's remedial-relationship test for personal civil penalties survives.
- Valot v. Southeast Local School District Board of Education
  > Held (Souter for 8, judgment 9-0): Treating employees who applied for unemployment benefits differently is rational when viewed apart from any penalty on a protected right, and the record shows no fundamental right to renewal or substantive due process deprivation.
- LaChance v. Erickson
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 9-0): The due process right to answer an agency's misconduct charge does not protect a knowingly false denial from a falsification charge or penalty. An unsuccessful defense is not itself a lie; the agency must prove actual falsity and culpability.
- Alexander v. Whitman
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 9-0): A fetus not born alive is not a person under the Fourteenth Amendment, as Roe held, though States may give broader prenatal protection. New Jersey's live-birth line for wrongful-death and survival claims is rational.
- Johnson v. Rodriguez
  > Held (Kennedy for the Court, 9-0): Texas's parole scheme creates no entitlement to release because it does not meaningfully limit the Board's discretion. Prisoners therefore have no due process right to correct or see confidential protest material or to an adversarial parole hearing.
- W.P. v. Verniero
  > Held (Souter for 8): Before Tier 2 or Tier 3 community notification, an offender must get a meaningful advance chance to contest both the risk tier and the notification plan, with the prosecutor bearing persuasion; five Justices required clear and convincing proof of the tier. Six held the notification is not punishment.
- Almendarez-Torres v. United States
  > Held (Scalia for the Court, 5-4): The prior aggravated-felony conviction in 8 U.S.C. 1326(b)(2) is an element that must be charged and proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt, so this sentence may not exceed the two-year maximum of section 1326(a). The ruling is statutory; no majority adopts a general constitutional rule.
- Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard
  > Held (O'Connor for 6): An established capital-clemency process must give minimal safeguards against fundamentally arbitrary procedure, such as deciding by coin flip. Ohio's process creates no entitlement to clemency, and five Justices found its notice and offered interview sufficient.
- United States v. Scheffer
  > Held (Thomas for the Court, 8-1): The right to present a defense permits evidence rules justified by legitimate trial interests and proportionate to them. Serious reliability concerns justify Rule 707's categorical exclusion of polygraph evidence.
- Women's Medical Professional Corp. v. Voinovich
  > Held (Souter for 7): A State may ban abortion after viability but must allow it when needed, in competent medical judgment, to avert grave, medically established danger of severe, lasting mental or emotional injury. Ohio's method ban was returned for application of the full Casey standard, 5-4.
- United States v. Thompson
  > Held (Thomas for the Court, 9-0): Any constitutional protection for voluntary marital intimacy does not bar this court-martial conviction under Article 125, because the act was done to stop an ongoing deadly assault, not as a voluntary intimate choice. Whether truly voluntary private conduct may be punished remains undecided.
- County of Sacramento v. Lewis
  > Held (Kennedy for 6, judgment 9-0): In a fast-moving high-speed chase, recklessness or deliberate indifference is not enough for a substantive due process violation. The officer must have intended harm unrelated to a legitimate law-enforcement goal, which this record does not show.
- United States v. Bajakajian
  > Held (Thomas for the Court, 6-3): A punitive forfeiture violates the Excessive Fines Clause when grossly disproportionate to the gravity of the offense, a rule all nine joined. Forfeiting the full $357,144 for this willful reporting offense was grossly disproportionate.
- Eastern Enterprises v. Apfel
  > Held (no opinion of the Court, 5-4): Eastern's Coal Act tier-three assessments may not be enforced, but three Justices relied on the Takings Clause and two (Kennedy and Stone) on due process, so no rationale controls. Five Justices held that a duty to pay money from general assets is not a taking.

OT1998
- City of West Covina v. Perkins
  > Held (Kennedy for 7, judgment 9-0): When police seize property, due process requires notice that lets the owner identify the property and who holds it. It does not require individualized instructions on return procedures available in published statutes and decisions.
- American Manufacturers Mutual Insurance Co. v. Sullivan
  > Held (Kennedy for 8): General eligibility for workers' compensation gives no constitutional right to payment of each disputed medical bill before its reasonableness is decided. Seven Justices held that private insurers withholding payment under state law are not state actors.
- Jones v. United States (carjacking)
  > Held (Scalia for the Court, 6-3): Under the federal carjacking statute, serious bodily injury and death are elements of aggravated offenses that must be charged and proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Jones's sentence may not exceed the basic fifteen-year maximum.
- Conn v. Gabbert
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 9-0): The liberty to practice a profession does not protect against every government interruption. A search that interrupted a lawyer's consultation with his client, without barring his practice, did not deprive him of that liberty, even assuming the timing was deliberate.
- City of Chicago v. Morales
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 7-2): A criminal ordinance is void for vagueness when every prosecution depends on an officer's judgment that people are remaining in public with no apparent purpose and the law gives no objective standard. A later refusal to disperse does not cure that defect.
- Neder v. United States
  > Held (Thomas for 7): Withholding an element such as materiality from the jury is a trial error reviewed for harmlessness, so the Government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the actual verdict; strong evidence alone is not enough. All nine agreed the jury must decide materiality when it is an element.

OT1999
- Fiore v. White (certification)
  > Held (Stevens for the Court, 9-0): The Court asked the Pennsylvania Supreme Court whether its later reading of the statute stated what the law meant when Fiore's conviction became final or changed it. The Court kept jurisdiction and reserved the federal due process question.
- Paul P. v. Verniero
  > Held (Kennedy for 7): When the State deliberately spreads compiled, nonpublic home-address information in a way that exposes the household, the Fourteenth Amendment protects a limited privacy interest against disclosure not sufficiently tied to the protective purpose. Public conviction information is not protected, and the notification provisions survive, with safeguards examined on remand.
- Garner v. Jones
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 7-2): Applying California Department of Corrections v. Morales, Jones's retroactive eight-year parole deferral removed a meaningful reconsideration opportunity. Georgia's discretionary expedited-review policy must be tested for legal basis, practical availability and timeliness, so the case was remanded.
- Doe v. Rosenberg
  > Held (Kennedy for the Court, 9-0): Private physicians' and a private hospital's commitment decisions are not state action merely because a statute authorizes the confinement, the hospital is heavily regulated and court review is available.
- Carmell v. Texas
  > Held (Stevens for the Court, 6-3): The Ex Post Facto Clause bars applying to past conduct a law that removes a requirement of additional evidence needed to convict. Ordinary changes in witness competence, admissibility or trial procedure remain allowed.
- Troxel v. Granville
  > Held (Breyer for 6, judgment 6-3): A fit parent's liberty includes directing the child's upbringing, and a court may not override the parent's visitation decision merely because the judge thinks another arrangement is better for the child. No complete visitation standard was adopted.
- Hawkins v. Freeman
  > Held (Kennedy for the Court, 9-0): A prisoner mistakenly paroled has no right to stay free; returning him to serve his valid, uncompleted sentence after notice, a hearing and credit for his time on release does not violate substantive due process.
- Apprendi v. New Jersey
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 6-3): Under the Sixth Amendment jury right and the Due Process Clause, any fact about the present offense needed to raise punishment above the maximum the verdict or plea supports is an element. It must be admitted or proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt.
- Stenberg v. Carhart
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 6-3): Nebraska's method ban reaches the ordinary dilation-and-evacuation procedure and so places a substantial obstacle before previability patients. Independently, it cannot be enforced where forcing another method creates a material risk of death or serious harm to health and the banned method is medically necessary.

## 3. The doctrine as it stands

Rules as of June 29, 2000.

### 3.1 Abortion: Casey's framework

What the Court held. Stone wrote Casey (June 29, 1992). Two majorities matter.

Roe's core survives, 6-3 (Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter; White, Scalia and Thomas dissenting). Before viability the woman decides whether to continue the pregnancy, without proving medical need or obtaining official approval of her reasons. The State may not ban abortion before viability, give anyone else effective control over the decision, or block timely care needed for her life or health, physical or mental. After viability the State may ban abortion only with a life-and-health exception. Viability is decided case by case, not by a fixed week. The same six hold that renewed disagreement with Roe is no reason to overrule it.

The six-step standard replaces Roe's trimester timetable, 5-4 (the same group without Kennedy). Five direct joins make it ordinary majority law.
1. Read the law as it actually works: text, binding interpretations, exceptions, penalties, medical setting. A previability ban, an effective third-party veto, and denial of timely life-or-health care are invalid automatically.
2. Ask what the law is for. Safety, informed consent, truthful information, parental involvement, administration and truthful persuasion favoring childbirth are legitimate. Moral disapproval, punishment, and deterring abortion by blocking access are not. A law whose obstructive purpose is shown objectively is invalid.
3. The challenger proves a real added burden caused by this law (cost, trips, delay, lost providers, medical risk, coercion, disclosure), measured on the women the law actually affects. There is no dollar, distance or time cutoff.
4. Apply the tiers in order. A baseline violation, obstructive purpose, or substantial obstacle is invalid outright. Remaining severe interference needs a compelling interest and narrow tailoring. Remaining meaningful interference requires the State to substantiate its justification and fit and to answer concrete, practicable, substantially less burdensome alternatives. Below that, heightened review ends.
5. Protect clinical judgment and truthful speech. Required information must be accurate, material and not misleading.
6. Tailor the remedy: sever invalid parts; relief covers the situations actually proved. Salerno's "no valid application" rule does not govern, but no numerical "large fraction" formula is adopted.

Later abortion cases applied the framework:
- Fargo Women's Health v. Schafer (1995): the full framework governs; finding no substantial obstacle does not end review. North Dakota's ruling vacated (historically the law stood).
- Planned Parenthood v. Miller (1996): South Dakota's parental notice with no mature-minor or best-interests route fails; enjoined whole.
- Leavitt v. Jane L. (1996): Utah's 20-week provision is severable; its validity undecided.
- Lambert v. Wicklund (1997): Montana's bypass wording upheld, unanimous.
- Mazurek v. Armstrong (1997): a physician-only rule gets the full review, including the purpose inquiry (Part I, six votes); remanded 5-4. Historically the Court summarily reversed and left the law in force.
- Women's Medical v. Voinovich (1998): a post-viability ban must allow abortion for grave, medically established mental-health danger (7-2); Ohio's method ban remanded 5-4.
- Stenberg v. Carhart (2000): Nebraska's ban reaches the ordinary D&E procedure and is a substantial obstacle; independently it lacks a needed health exception (Souter, 6-3).

Compared with history: the real Casey's undue-burden test had three votes and controlled only as the narrowest ground; it upheld most of Pennsylvania's law and partly overruled Akron and Thornburgh. Here the substantial-obstacle test is only the top tier, two lower tiers of heightened review exist, Akron's protections against fixed waiting periods and needless physician-only counseling survive, and the framework is majority law.

### 3.2 Unenumerated liberties: how the Court recognizes them

What the Court does:
1. Describe the claim concretely, at the level actually asserted.
2. Consult text, structure, precedent and tradition. History can defeat a claim, but it is not a required threshold, and the Court does not demand the most specific historical match. Glucksberg (Kennedy for five, including Stone) expressly states that "no exclusive most-specific-history methodology governs all liberties."
3. Extend recognized liberties by analogy, with tests fitted to the setting: disclosure of intimate information must be justified and fit its purpose (Sheets, Paul P.); a child is protected against serious, avoidable harm in how a custody transfer is carried out (Baby Richard); a judge may not override a fit parent's visitation decision merely by preferring another arrangement (Troxel Part I, six votes).
4. A claim that is not fundamental gets rational-basis review; a marriage-related rule gets more only if it directly and substantially interferes with marrying (Wright v. MetroHealth).
5. Executive abuse is measured by whether it "shocks the conscience," with more culpability required the less time the official had to think (County of Sacramento v. Lewis).

Results: no right to a physician's lethal prescription (Glucksberg) and the refusal-versus-assistance line is rational (Vacco); a detained child has no right to release to an unrelated adult (Reno v. Flores); no due-process duty to provide a safe workplace (Collins); a stillborn fetus is not a constitutional person (Alexander v. Whitman); parental liberty covers choosing schools and teaching one's children but is no veto over optional programs or a required assembly (Curtis, Brown); marital privacy does not cover an act repelling an armed assault (Thompson); Bowers v. Hardwick is not overruled but is confined to its specified conduct.

Compared with history: no "deeply rooted in history and tradition" threshold; informational privacy is recognized as a holding, not merely assumed (Sheets; Doe v. SEPTA assumed it, 5-4, with Stone dissenting to recognize it); Troxel has a majority holding instead of a four-Justice plurality; Chicago v. Morales rests on vagueness alone, not a liberty to loiter; Baby Richard gives a child a custody-transfer protection the real Court never reached. The test the real Court used in 2022 to overrule Roe and Casey has been disclaimed here as a general method.

### 3.3 Punitive damages and retroactive economic laws

Grossly excessive punitive awards violate due process, judged by reprehensibility, the ratio to harm, and comparable sanctions (BMW v. Gore, Stevens for five). Stone joined only Part II, the limit on punishing out-of-state conduct; he would not review amounts once procedures are fair. Eliminating judicial review of the amount violates due process (Honda, eight including Stone). TXO's award was affirmed 5-4 on procedure, with Stone dissenting. Eastern Enterprises won 5-4 with no majority reasoning; Kennedy's due-process ground had Stone's join, but five Justices held there was no taking, and no controlling due-process limit on retroactive economic legislation exists.

Note on Stone's position: Stone's BMW position (no federal review of amount once process is fair) is narrower than the controlling rule and close to Scalia's. Cooper Industries (OT2000) asks how appellate courts review the amount under BMW.

### 3.4 Civil commitment and punishment

Doctrine now: an insanity acquittee may be held only while both mentally ill and dangerous (Foucha); a new commitment requires clear and convincing proof (Foucha Part II-B). Federal custody must rest on current, reviewed grounds, and a hypothetical criminal sentence is no cap (Sahhar). A civil label stands unless there is the clearest proof that the scheme's purpose or effect is punitive, judged by how it actually operates (Ursery, Hendricks, Hudson, W.P.). Commitment that operates as added imprisonment without real treatment is punishment, and applying it to earlier conduct violates the Ex Post Facto Clause (Hendricks). Sex-offender community notification is not punishment, but it requires an advance hearing with the State bearing persuasion (W.P., eight votes; clear and convincing proof for the tier, 5-4).

Kansas v. Hendricks in plain terms. Kansas let the State commit, after their prison terms, sex offenders with a "mental abnormality" making them likely to reoffend. The real Court (Thomas, 5-4) held the law civil, so it could not be retroactive punishment; Breyer dissented because Kansas gave Hendricks no real treatment. Here Breyer's dissent became the Court's opinion (Breyer, joined by Stone, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg). The Court kept the "clearest proof" test, but found it met by how Hendricks was actually committed: confinement added after his sentence, selection by his past crimes, prisonlike restraint, and commitment on the promise of a treatment program that did not yet exist. That is punishment, and applied to conduct before the Act it violates the Ex Post Facto Clause. Kansas's judgment for Hendricks was affirmed on that ground, not on the Kansas court's due-process ground.

### 3.5 Defenses, evidence and elements

Doctrine now: a State that keeps a mental element in a crime may not bar otherwise admissible evidence rebutting it, such as voluntary intoxication, without sufficient justification (Egelhoff). Reliability-based categorical exclusions, such as of polygraphs, are allowed (Scheffer). A defendant may bear a preponderance burden on incompetence but not a clear-and-convincing one (Cooper; Medina has no majority method). Medication during trial requires medical appropriateness, an overriding need and consideration of less intrusive means (Riggins). A fact that raises the maximum sentence is an element for the jury (Apprendi), and a prior conviction that raises the maximum is an element too (Almendarez-Torres, 5-4). A criminal law that gives officers no objective standard is void for vagueness (Chicago v. Morales). No controlling rule governs freestanding innocence claims (Herrera; Stone alone would recognize one proved by clear and convincing evidence).

Montana v. Egelhoff in plain terms. Montana barred juries from considering voluntary intoxication when deciding whether a defendant acted purposely or knowingly. The real Court reversed Montana's new-trial order, 5-4. Here the Court affirmed it (O'Connor, joined by Stone, Stevens, Souter and Breyer): Montana's own court read the crime as still requiring purpose or knowledge and the intoxication rule as an evidence rule, and due process forbids barring relevant evidence on an element the State keeps without sufficient justification.

### 3.6 Forfeiture, fines and civil sanctions

Doctrine now: forfeitures that are partly punitive are fines subject to the Excessive Fines Clause (Austin, Alexander); the test is gross disproportionality, reviewed independently on appeal (Bajakajian, Part II with eight votes, without Stone). Seizing real property requires prior notice and a hearing, absent an emergency (Good). An innocent co-owner who did not take part, did not know, and did all that could reasonably be expected to prevent misuse keeps her share (Bennis). Civil forfeiture is ordinarily not double jeopardy (Ursery), and Halper's approach to civil sanctions survives (Hudson).

Bennis v. Michigan in plain terms. A husband used the couple's jointly owned car for a sex offense, and Michigan forfeited the whole car. The real Court (Rehnquist, 5-4) allowed it despite the wife's innocence. Here Stone wrote for five (Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer), answering the question Calero-Toledo left open: due process protects an innocent co-owner's separate interest when she proves non-participation, lack of knowledge, and reasonable prevention efforts.

### 3.7 Retroactive laws (Ex Post Facto)

Doctrine now: a law applied to past conduct may not increase the punishment, and that includes taking away a meaningful opportunity for earlier release. The prisoner shows the entitlement, its retroactive loss, and a concrete link to release, without proving he would have won parole; the State must then substantiate any substitute (Cavanaugh, California Department of Corrections v. Morales, Garner). Reducing the proof legally required to convict is barred (Carmell). Regulating future conduct based on a past conviction is not retroactive punishment (Hiley).

Compared with history: the real Morales (7-2) upheld California's change to parole-hearing frequency; here it was struck, 5-4 (O'Connor, joined by Stone, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg). Cavanaugh, which the real Court dismissed, was decided on the merits against the State, 6-3.

### 3.8 Procedural due process

Doctrine now: a State creates protected prison liberty when its substantive law genuinely constrains officials' power to impose a significant deprivation; mandatory words alone are not enough, and there is no "atypical and significant hardship" threshold (Sandin, Breyer for five). Liberty protected directly by the Constitution, and conditional liberty already granted (Young v. Harper), are separate routes. Discretionary parole and clemency create no entitlement, but capital clemency requires minimal protection against arbitrariness (Johnson v. Rodriguez, Woodard). A court-access claim requires actual hindrance of a nonfrivolous claim (Lewis v. Casey). In employment and benefits, Mathews balancing applies: provisional action after an arrest is allowed, but a continuing deprivation needs prompt, meaningful process (Gilbert v. Homar); there is no right to payment of disputed bills before a fair determination (American Manufacturers); notice need not explain published remedies (West Covina).

Sandin v. Conner in plain terms. A prisoner was put in disciplinary segregation for 30 days. The real Court (Rehnquist, 5-4) held that prisoners have a state-created liberty interest only against "atypical and significant hardship," and that 30 days did not qualify. Here the Court (Breyer, joined by Stone, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg) held that a State creates protected liberty whenever its substantive rules genuinely constrain officials from imposing a significant deprivation, and remanded for findings. The atypical-hardship test has only four votes.

### 3.9 Where this Court departed from history

Results that differ: Casey's framework and the 24-hour wait, physician-only counseling, referral reporting and public-release provisions; Fargo Women's Health; Mazurek; Women's Medical v. Voinovich in part; Baby Richard; Heller v. Doe on the burden of proof; Hendricks; Egelhoff; Bennis; Sandin; California Department of Corrections v. Morales; Cavanaugh; Almendarez-Torres; Alexander v. United States on the First Amendment.

Same results, different reasoning: Glucksberg (no "deeply rooted" threshold); Chicago v. Morales (vagueness only, no liberty to loiter); Troxel (a six-Justice holding); Stenberg (six votes); Sheets and Doe v. SEPTA (informational privacy recognized); Medina and Herrera (no controlling majority method); Apprendi (no prior-conviction exception); Hudson (Halper survives); Lewis v. Casey (narrower limits have four votes).

Stone's distinctive positions, none controlling: BMW (no federal review of a punitive amount once procedures are fair); Herrera (recognize an innocence claim proved by clear and convincing evidence); Medina (the State should bear the competency burden); Doe v. SEPTA (recognize a medical-information right with proportionality); Troxel (require serious harm before overriding a fit parent); TXO (review the amount against the record).
