# Justice Stone's Commerce Clause and federalism jurisprudence through October Term 1993

## Research judgment

The strongest development of the simulated Justice Stone preserves a substantial national commercial sphere and substantial state governing capacity by distinguishing the legal relationships at issue. It does not make either government presumptively victorious. Stone should police geographic commercial preferences, permit fairly structured taxation and actual public purchasing, enforce the particular federal rules Congress has adopted, and preserve state control over governmental machinery against conscription. Each proposition needs its own source, comparator, and remedy.

The main revisions concern reasoning more than outcomes. Stone should keep the proposed invalidations in *Oregon Waste*, *Carbone*, and *West Lynn Creamery*, but improve their foundations. *Carbone* cannot rest on a simplistic private/public distinction: Clarkstown already bore financial risk for a facility destined for municipal ownership. *West Lynn* cannot rest on a fictional literal refund to farmers of a tax legally paid by dealers or an unproved prediction about outsiders' prices. *Oregon Waste* requires more than a genuine environmental purpose to justify a differential origin charge. The integrated system and the applicable legal comparison matter in each case.

Stone should also uphold the taxes in *Barclays* and *Colgate* while separating permissible income attribution from automatic international uniformity and separating congressional silence from authorization. State autonomy should lose when it becomes a reason to erase a federal statutory standard: the strongest contrary positions are a limited remand in *ACF Industries*, preserving a possible statutory discrimination claim, and a dissent in *BFP*, preserving a meaningful inquiry into reasonably equivalent value. Neither recommendation dictates that the taxpayer or bankruptcy estate ultimately wins.

The term does not supply a legitimate occasion to announce the attachment's mature *Lopez*, *Printz*, spending-coercion, or sovereign-immunity doctrines. Those remain future questions. An honest reconstruction can become more precise without becoming artificially complete.

## Method, evidence, and navigation

The inquiry proceeds in three separate evidentiary registers. **Simulation evidence** establishes Stone's attributed position. **Contemporaneous law and record** establish what the disputes and legal authorities actually were. **Retrospective evidence** tests assumptions and future stability. Later decisions are not treated as either historical OT1993 authority or an answer key.

Primary opinions, including separate opinions, supply the legal analysis; selected lower-court decisions clarify actual disputes and remedies. Statutes and regulations are read in their historical setting. Current codifications are used for later amendments only where identified. Economic models, original studies, historical scholarship, and government reports inform mechanisms and institutional costs. A theory is not an empirical result; a later regulatory response is not proof of a decision's net social benefit; chronology alone does not establish causation. Abstract-only or otherwise limited access is identified where it affects a claim. Books whose arguments were verified only through judicial discussion are not represented as having been read in full.

The research includes 15 prior entries and 18 OT1993 merits cases. The universe is deliberately broader than conventional Commerce Clause coding but tied to Stone's existing jurisprudential problems. The exclusion audit explains why adjacent rights, habeas, jurisdiction, and tribal-land cases do not automatically belong. No statistical voting claim is made, and no historical database could supply this fictional Justice's missing votes. The report's core is individual analysis of Stone, with historical coalitions included only to explain the alternatives he faces.

## I. Stone before OT1993

### Evidentiary limits and reading protocol

The attached document is a jurisprudential build and planning file, not a set of judicial opinions. Its opening describes proposed positions, and its status legend says a historical merits case is a real vehicle with Stone's recommended disposition, not the historical Court's holding. The file supplies eight inherited cases and seven OT1992 cases. Treat their entries as the stipulated pre-OT1993 Stone baseline for this analysis, while acknowledging that the record cannot establish the existence or wording of actual Stone opinions.

**Authorship limitation applying to all fifteen cases:** no entry identifies whether Stone wrote the Court opinion, joined somebody else's opinion, concurred, concurred only in judgment, or dissented. None identifies a simulated vote coalition. Do not invent these details or transfer historical opinion assignments to Stone. A prescribed result can be stated as his recorded position; an opinion posture cannot.

**Precedent limitation applying to all fifteen cases:** the entries generally give no discussion of identified precedents, overruling, reliance, or stare decisis. Case chains describe the author's intended development and frequently point forward. They do not establish that Stone himself expressly cited a particular precedent. Contemporary authorities below establish the legal materials available to Stone, not unrecorded reasons he demonstrably adopted.

**Chronology limitation:** the introduction's mature Commerce formulation, F1-F6, and concluding one-sentence project description synthesize development through OT2014. They are evidence of the planner's ambition, not established holdings in OT1992. In particular, Stone does not yet possess the later anti-evasion/material-impairment/substantial-externality test, a Printz holding, a practical-choice Spending doctrine, an Article I immunity rule, a public-facility dormant-Commerce exception, or a completed internal-consistency tax doctrine. Even entries labeled “Historical Supreme Court merits case” after OT1993 remain later for the present reconstruction. The file expressly cautions that OT1992 must not prematurely create Lopez.

**Labels used below:** “Express” means stated in the relevant baseline entry. “Inference” means a reasoned reconstruction from that entry or multiple entries. “Historical law” identifies the real legal framework. Unless identified as express, constitutional provisions and precedent references are background law, not citations preserved in a Stone opinion.

### Eight inherited entries

#### 1. New York v. United States, 505 U.S. 144 (1992)

- **Question and operation:** Congress sought disposal arrangements for low-level radioactive waste. Its take-title device required noncompliant States, on request, to assume ownership and liability; monetary and access incentives formed separate parts of the legislation. The actual problem was insufficient disposal capacity and interstate coordination, not a general federal power to direct state legislation.
- **Stone's result and posture:** expressly invalidate take-title while preserving lawful incentives, preemption, and cooperation. The precise mandate language is absent. Writing/joining/dissent status is unknown.
- **Rule and constitutional source:** expressly distinguish direct regulation of commerce from conscripting a State into a federal program. Political accountability is expressly the deeper reason. Commerce and Spending powers, structural federalism, and the Tenth Amendment are the relevant historical constitutional framework, though the entry does not choose a text-versus-structure derivation.
- **Precedent:** no backward precedent treatment is supplied. Historical New York drew on Hodel, FERC, and Dole; these are available sources, not proven Stone commitments. Its forward Printz/Condon/Kansas/NFIB chain cannot settle those issues now.
- **Institutional allocation:** Congress can regulate private radioactive-waste activity itself and offer constitutional incentives. It cannot assign unwilling state governments the responsibility for an otherwise federal regulatory choice.
- **Factual/economic assumptions:** federal responsibility would become obscured when state officials implement the federal program. This is an express explanatory assertion, not empirical evidence furnished by the file. A national interstate waste market is presupposed.
- **Values and rejection:** accountability and the constitutional allocation of governmental responsibility are express. He rejects the forced choice between taking title and implementing Congress's program; he does not reject national waste regulation.
- **Open questions:** scope of lawful incentives, coercive funding, executive assistance, generally applicable federal duties on States, congressional authorization of discrimination, and the extent of permissible federal insistence on state information are not worked out. The file does not establish Stone's position on state consent to an otherwise impermissible structural arrangement.
- **Tension and classification:** chiefly a categorical institutional boundary with lawful alternatives preserved, not free-form balancing of the federal problem's importance. Its relation to substantive obligations on state enterprises remains open. The apparent tension between congressional market integration and geographically discriminatory access incentives is resolvable by congressional authorization but not expressly elaborated in Stone's entry.

Historical-law verification: the real Court invalidated take-title and sustained the other incentives; it treated regulation of the interstate waste-disposal market as within Congress's commerce authority. [Opinion, 505 U.S. 144, 155-183](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/505/144/).

#### 2. Quill Corp. v. North Dakota, 504 U.S. 298 (1992)

- **Question:** whether North Dakota could require an out-of-state mail-order seller to collect use tax without the physical presence then demanded under dormant-Commerce doctrine.
- **Stone's result and posture:** **no disposition is supplied**. The entry cannot establish that he upheld physical presence, rejected it, concurred, or dissented. It supplies only a doctrinal orientation.
- **Rule and source:** expressly reject general immunity for interstate businesses; insist on nexus, allocation, nondiscrimination, and protection against multiple or extraterritorial taxation. Dormant Commerce Clause limitations are explicit in context. The entry does not separate Due Process nexus from Commerce nexus.
- **Precedent:** expressly says later tax cases should pursue structural principles rather than preserve every Quill formality forever. This signals revisability, not a recorded decision to overrule physical presence. There is no Stone treatment of Bellas Hess reliance or congressional competence.
- **Institutional allocation:** States may tax interstate activity with a legitimate connection. The relationship between courts and Congress over remote-seller collection remains unspecified.
- **Factual/economic assumptions:** interstate sellers benefit from or participate in state markets, and poorly coordinated taxation can burden interstate transactions. These are inferences. The file provides no compliance-cost evidence or judgment on the then-important mail-order industry's reliance.
- **Values and rejection:** structural equality and territorial discipline are express; a deregulatory tax immunity is rejected. No explicit rejection of bright-line administrability appears.
- **Open questions:** physical presence itself, retroactivity, required credits, internal consistency, the definition of substantial nexus, and choice among feasible apportionment rules.
- **Tension and classification:** principle-based orientation rather than an operational test. It leaves an administrability-versus-substance tension that Itel does not resolve. Do not characterize later rejection of physical presence as reversal of a proved Stone vote.

Historical-law verification: Quill rejected physical presence as a Due Process prerequisite while retaining Bellas Hess's dormant-Commerce collection rule, emphasizing reliance, a workable bright line, and congressional authority to alter it. Those are the legal constraints available to Stone even though his own vote is missing. [Opinion, 504 U.S. 298, 305-318](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/504/298/).

#### 3. Chemical Waste Management, Inc. v. Hunt, 504 U.S. 334 (1992)

- **Question:** Alabama's extra hazardous-waste disposal charge depended on the waste's out-of-state origin.
- **Stone's result and posture:** expressly invalidate the surcharge; authorship and formal appellate disposition unspecified.
- **Rule and source:** origin cannot impose a burden that materially equivalent local commerce escapes. He expressly permits charges for actual costs, risks, inspections, disposal, environmental burdens, or services. Dormant Commerce Clause nondiscrimination supplies the historical constitutional basis.
- **Precedent:** no express Stone analysis of Philadelphia v. New Jersey, Maine v. Taylor, or compensatory-tax precedents. The future Oregon Waste chain is a plan, not additional present authority.
- **Institutional allocation:** States retain power to fund and regulate hazardous-waste disposal; courts police the geographic criterion used to allocate the burden.
- **Factual/economic assumptions:** geography does not itself establish additional environmental risk or service cost. “Identical local commerce” is an express premise of the objection, not a finding that all waste has identical risks. Economic effects of additional transport or enforcement could differ if demonstrated.
- **Values and rejection:** equal competitive access, anti-cost-exporting discipline, and legitimate environmental cost recovery. The first two are inferences from the operative rule. Origin used as an unsupported risk or expense proxy is expressly rejected.
- **Open questions:** proof required for additional costs, whether residual risk can be charged rather than measured expense, which parties and activities form the comparator, and how to assess broad prior taxpayer contributions. No general Pike view is established.
- **Tension and classification:** strongly anti-discriminatory rule with fact-based cost differentiation preserved. It is not an absolute equal-fee rule for materially different transactions and not a general warrant to balance any environmental fee against market efficiency.

Historical-law verification: the Court invalidated Alabama's differential charge because the State had not shown an adequate non-discriminatory justification unavailable through alternatives. A uniform charge, a genuine cost-linked distinction, and other evenhanded regulation were analytically different possibilities. [Opinion, 504 U.S. 334, 342-346](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/504/334/).

#### 4. Fort Gratiot Sanitary Landfill, Inc. v. Michigan Department of Natural Resources, 504 U.S. 353 (1992)

- **Question:** Michigan's county-based planning law restricted privately operated landfills from taking waste generated outside the host county, including interstate waste.
- **Stone's result and posture:** expressly reject the geographic exclusion; no opinion posture or precise mandate is recorded.
- **Rule and source:** a State cannot evade dormant-Commerce limits by using county borders; excluding both other counties and other States does not cure the impediment to interstate commerce.
- **Precedent:** the entry provides no express reliance discussion. Chemical Waste supplies an already recorded anti-origin principle, but the case order within the file does not prove an actual citation.
- **Institutional allocation:** state delegation to local governments cannot authorize a commercial barrier the State itself could not erect. Local planning authority is bounded, not extinguished.
- **Factual/economic assumptions:** the excluded waste is “otherwise eligible,” an express limitation. The rule therefore does not decide rules responding to genuine differences in waste composition, capacity, hazard, or processing requirements.
- **Values and rejection:** practical market access is express; opposition to subdividing the national market is an inference. The defense that intrastate outsiders also suffer is expressly rejected.
- **Open questions:** government-owned facilities, bona fide municipal procurement, capacity allocation by neutral criteria, and a nondiscriminatory requirement to use a specified site. Nothing here decides Carbone's public/private question.
- **Tension and classification:** categorical anti-evasion principle applied to geographic protectionism. It foreshadows willingness to examine practical effects but does not establish that every local monopoly is discrimination. It must coexist with Boston Harbor's protection of the State's own purchasing choices.

Historical-law verification: the Court held that county-level discrimination against other counties' and other States' waste remained an interstate-commerce problem; local waste planning did not justify the challenged exclusion. [Opinion, 504 U.S. 353, 359-367](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/504/353/).

#### 5. Morales v. Trans World Airlines, Inc., 504 U.S. 374 (1992)

- **Question:** whether state consumer-protection enforcement of airline fare-advertising guidelines was displaced by the Airline Deregulation Act's preemption clause.
- **Stone's result and posture:** expressly accept meaningful preemption of state prescriptions governing airline price-related economic conduct. The exact injunction, treatment of all challenged guidelines, and appellate disposition are not supplied; authorship is unknown.
- **Rule and source:** identify the state duty, the federal subject actually protected, and Congress's actual displacement. He expressly rejects an unlimited reading of “related to.” Historical source: the ADA's express clause, then 49 U.S.C. App. §1305(a)(1), operating through the Supremacy Clause.
- **Precedent:** no Stone discussion of Shaw's ERISA analogy, the saving clause, or traditional state police-power presumptions. Later Wolens and other cases cannot furnish current holdings.
- **Institutional allocation:** Congress can choose competition over state economic prescription. Judges must enforce that choice without expanding it into general immunity from state law.
- **Assumptions:** advertising requirements can effectively regulate fares. This is express at the level of “price-related economic conduct”; the entry supplies no empirical account of competitive harm or consumer deception.
- **Values and rejection:** enacted deregulation receives legal effect; state duty specificity restrains judicial displacement. Inference: the aim is fidelity to Congress, not deregulation as a constitutional value.
- **Open questions:** voluntary contracts, general fraud, remoteness thresholds, the role of express language versus obstacle preemption, and particular remedies.
- **Tension and classification:** statutory interpretation with a limiting method, not a categorical ban on airline consumer protection. How much connection suffices remains undertheorized, especially beside Greater Washington.

Historical-law verification: the real decision found the fare-advertising standards preempted but disclaimed extension to state actions too remotely connected with airline rates, routes, or services. It also limited the injunction as to matters not adequately threatened. [Opinion, 504 U.S. 374, 383-391](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/504/374/).

#### 6. Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Association, 505 U.S. 88 (1992)

- **Question:** whether federal occupational-safety standards displaced Illinois hazardous-waste-worker licensing requirements overlapping the same occupational subject.
- **Stone's result and posture:** the entry conditionally accepts displacement where Congress has occupied the subject. It does not expressly state a complete case disposition or select the historical plurality's, concurrence's, or dissent's posture.
- **Rule and source:** statutory structure, subject, and actual overlap must demonstrate displacement; the presence of federal regulations by itself is insufficient. Historical source: OSH Act §18 and its federal/state-plan structure, implemented through Supremacy.
- **Precedent:** no express precedent treatment. The file's “occupy” formulation should not be mistaken for an adoption of a particular historical preemption category: the actual plurality used implied preemption while Kennedy used express preemption.
- **Institutions:** a valid enacted federal occupational-safety arrangement can control the operative subject. States retain other regulatory subjects and whatever concurrent or approved-plan authority Congress preserves.
- **Assumptions:** overlapping licensing can impose operational occupational-safety obligations despite a second public-health purpose. The file does not supply findings on actual cost, duplicative training, or risks.
- **Values and rejection:** legal source and functional overlap matter; labels and bare federal regulatory presence do not. Protecting ordinary state police power is a plausible inference, not an explicit general presumption.
- **Open questions:** breadth of the occupied subject, saving clauses, general public-safety regulations, actual compliance impossibility versus federal program design, and what text is sufficient.
- **Tension and classification:** source-grounded statutory inquiry. A broader occupied subject might preempt without exact rule-by-rule conflict, potentially in tension with an overread Easterwood. It is not inconsistent if Congress actually chose the broader subject; the file does not yet articulate that limiting explanation completely.

Historical-law verification: the Court preempted unapproved occupational standards on federally covered issues, including dual-purpose provisions; generally applicable laws regulating workers as members of the public were distinguished. Kennedy's express-preemption analysis differed from the plurality's implied-preemption account. [Opinion, 505 U.S. 88, 96-113](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/505/88/).

#### 7. Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc., 505 U.S. 504 (1992)

- **Question:** which state common-law cigarette claims were displaced by federal labeling and advertising legislation.
- **Stone's result and posture:** no claim-by-claim outcomes or complete disposition are recorded. What is express is the legitimacy and necessity of different preemption answers for different claims. No writing/joining status is given.
- **Rule and source:** identify the precise state duty and compare it to the federal clause, rather than preempt an entire lawsuit by subject label. Historical source: 1965/1969 cigarette statutes, particularly §5, and the Supremacy Clause.
- **Precedent:** no recorded Stone treatment of the 1965/1969 difference, presumptions, or competing historical opinions. Do not assign him the entire Stevens plurality because he endorses its method.
- **Institutions:** Congress determines the displacement; state courts administer duties that remain outside it. That allocation is inferred from the prescribed comparison.
- **Assumptions:** a lawsuit can contain legally distinct duties even when the evidence overlaps. The file assumes legal disaggregation is workable; it provides no assessment of pleading manipulation or jury administration.
- **Values and rejection:** duty-level precision, preserving state law where not displaced, rejecting all-or-nothing lawsuit classification.
- **Open questions:** individual tort claims, express warranty, fraud, conspiracy, express versus implied preemption, and preemption of parallel duties. Later Wolens/Medtronic answers remain future.
- **Tension and classification:** methodological and statutory, not a categorical substantive result. The method may demand distinctions between claims sharing facts; Stone has not addressed administrability objections.

Historical-law verification: the historical decision treated the 1965 and 1969 texts differently and differentiated failure-to-warn, warranty, fraud, and conspiracy theories, with fractured opinions. Those actual outcomes cannot fill the gaps in Stone's record. [Opinion, 505 U.S. 504, 518-531](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/505/504/).

#### 8. Kraft General Foods, Inc. v. Iowa Department of Revenue and Finance, 505 U.S. 71 (1992)

- **Question:** Iowa deducted dividends from domestic subsidiaries while including dividends from foreign subsidiaries in the corporate tax base.
- **Stone's result and posture:** expressly reject the discriminatory tax structure; formal mandate and opinion posture absent.
- **Rule and source:** foreign commerce may be ordinarily taxed but cannot itself trigger unfavorable treatment. The Foreign Commerce Clause is the contextual source; the file does not explain its distinctive one-voice component.
- **Precedent:** no express Stone treatment of Container Corp., Japan Line, or federal tax conformity. The Itel/Jefferson Lines chain is partly prospective.
- **Institutions:** state corporate taxation remains legitimate; courts prevent foreign-commerce disadvantage. No recorded allocation of responsibility between the Executive and Congress over foreign-policy objections.
- **Assumptions:** subsidiary classification produces unequal treatment of comparable income. The entry contains no accounting or evidence of offsetting taxes sufficient to establish a compensatory justification.
- **Values and rejection:** structural nondiscrimination, not ordinary tax immunity. Inference: a tax can fail without identifying a favored firm inside Iowa, because national-versus-foreign differentiation can matter.
- **Open questions:** integrated versus separate accounting, adequate tax offsets, regulatory foreign-policy uniformity, multiple taxation, and how closely the constitutional comparator must match.
- **Tension and classification:** strong anti-discrimination principle. It sits consistently with Itel if discrimination and taxing a local transaction are kept distinct; it does not decide worldwide combined reporting.

Historical-law verification: Iowa followed federal income definitions but did not provide the corresponding foreign-tax credit. The Court invalidated the facial discrimination and did not require proof that Iowa firms, rather than domestic firms generally, enjoyed the preference. [Opinion, 505 U.S. 71, 75-82](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/505/71/).

### Seven OT1992 entries

#### 9. Boston Harbor, 507 U.S. 218 (1993)

- **Question and mechanism:** the Massachusetts Water Resources Authority specified a project labor agreement for contractors on its own construction project; the question was federal labor preemption.
- **Stone's result and posture:** expressly reverse, with Massachusetts and the authority prevailing. No authorship or joining information.
- **Rule and source:** functional proprietor/regulator distinction; project ownership, purchasing, management, and transaction-specific requirements can fall on the proprietary side. A contractual form does not immunize an outside regulatory command. Historical source: statutory NLRA preemption under Garmon and Machinists, not a constitutional permission to ignore enacted federal law.
- **Precedent:** no express Stone precedent treatment. Historical Boston Harbor distinguished Gould's regulatory debarment; that distinction helps specify available law but is not an independently recorded Stone citation.
- **Institutions:** the State may manage its own purchase much like an ordinary purchaser. Valid federal statutes can still reach state market activity, expressly noted by the file.
- **Assumptions:** the challenged labor terms are confined to performance of the government's project. Inference: procurement flexibility can facilitate delivery without dictating labor policy for independent transactions.
- **Values and rejection:** government operational capacity and a practical limit on regulatory evasion. Expressly reject “contract” as a magic word and constitutional immunity for every act called market participation.
- **Open questions:** how far a transaction extends; downstream contract conditions; publicly owned facilities operated through private parties; subsidies; and application of this statutory distinction to the dormant Commerce Clause.
- **Tension and classification:** functional, transaction-bounded distinction. It provides an analogy for Carbone, not a binding dormant-Commerce holding. A public-purpose contract and sovereign routing of all private waste are different acts even if they fund the same facility.

Historical-law verification: the Court upheld the project-specific labor agreement under federal labor-preemption doctrine, distinguishing regulatory procurement designed to punish violations elsewhere. [Opinion, 507 U.S. 218, 226-233](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/507/218/).

#### 10. Itel Containers International Corp. v. Huddleston, 507 U.S. 60 (1993)

- **Question:** Tennessee imposed sales tax on local container leases; the containers traveled in international commerce. Treaty, statutory, Foreign Commerce, and Import-Export objections were historically presented.
- **Stone's result and posture:** expressly affirm Tennessee. Authorship/joining unspecified.
- **Rule and source:** a local taxable transaction does not become immune because goods later move internationally. Ask about discrimination, serious multiple taxation, territorial overreach, and conflicting operative federal law or treaty. Foreign Commerce is the principal constitutional source identifiable from the entry; Import-Export is historical background not separately analyzed by Stone.
- **Precedent:** no Stone statement on Complete Auto, Japan Line, or Container Corp. Historical doctrine includes all three. Importantly, the file does not record a decision to abolish Japan Line's one-voice inquiry.
- **Institutions:** States retain normal revenues connected to local activity; international use alone does not transfer every tax decision to national institutions.
- **Assumptions:** a discrete local lease is a legitimate tax event; no demonstrated relevant defect warrants immunity. The wording “serious multiple-taxation problems” is open-ended, not a finding that any overseas tax duplication is forbidden.
- **Values and rejection:** ordinary fiscal capacity, territorial connection, and equality; express rejection of international-commerce tax immunity.
- **Open questions:** what double taxation is constitutionally excessive, foreign-parent reporting burdens, weight of Executive objections absent enacted law, and necessary one-voice uniformity.
- **Tension and classification:** a structured defects inquiry rather than a general balancing test. Consistent with Kraft's discrimination rule; unresolved interaction between source discipline and a freestanding foreign-policy dormant limitation is especially relevant to Barclays.

Historical-law verification: the historical Court sustained the tax against treaty, preemption, Foreign Commerce, and Import-Export challenges; Complete Auto compliance was conceded. Japan Line's additional concerns remained operative. [Opinion, 507 U.S. 60, 64-77](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/507/60/).

#### 11. CSX Transportation, Inc. v. Easterwood, 507 U.S. 658 (1993)

- **Question:** federal railroad-safety regulation versus excessive-speed and inadequate-crossing-warning negligence claims.
- **Stone's result and posture:** expressly preserve the split: speed theory preempted, warning-device theory not preempted merely because railroads are federally regulated. Historical formal result was affirmance; no Stone opinion posture given.
- **Rule and source:** compare the exact state duty with the federal rule and subject. An operative federal speed standard can displace a contrary negligence speed duty. General regulatory presence does not decide a crossing-specific warning obligation. Historical source: FRSA's “covering” clause and federal regulations under Supremacy.
- **Precedent:** the file calls this a first strong statement while already describing Cipollone as methodological foundation. That is refinement in degree of articulation, not an actual documented reversal. No express external precedent analysis is recorded.
- **Institutions:** federal regulators control the subjects Congress authorizes them to cover; ordinary state tort law operates outside the actual displacement.
- **Assumptions:** federal speed regulations substantially resolve the same safety subject; the federal warning regime did not govern the crossing on the actual record. A federal ceiling's treatment as sufficient safety remains a contestable interpretive assumption, not mathematical necessity.
- **Values and rejection:** precise scope, administrable allocation, and preserving state remedies where law has not displaced them. Reject “federal regulation of railroads” as a sufficient reason.
- **Open questions:** specific hazards requiring slowing/stopping, later federal funding of warning devices, parallel state duties, and broader field or obstacle preemption. The historical opinion reserved specific individual hazards; the simulation does not separately record Stone adopting that reservation.
- **Tension and classification:** duty-specific, statutory and record-dependent. It cannot be turned into a rule that preemption always requires impossibility of dual compliance, given Gade and Morales.

Historical-law verification: FRSA regulations covered train speed, but the cited crossing regulations did not preempt the warning claim on this record. The Court reserved the distinct duty to slow or stop for a specific individual hazard. [Opinion, 507 U.S. 658, 664-676 & n.15](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/507/658/).

#### 12. District of Columbia v. Greater Washington Board of Trade, 506 U.S. 125 (1992)

- **Question:** D.C. required employer health-insurance continuation for injured employees, measuring the required benefits by existing ERISA-plan coverage.
- **Stone's result and posture:** expressly affirm preemption narrowly; no joining/authorship information.
- **Rule and source:** using the ERISA plan's benefit structure as the operative compulsory benefit measure creates a prohibited connection. Mere reference, cost effect, or incidental interaction is insufficient as a universal rule. Historical source: ERISA §514(a), 29 U.S.C. §1144(a), with its exemptions and saving provisions.
- **Precedent:** no express Stone treatment of Shaw, Alessi, or Metropolitan Life. The planner's statement that later broad doctrine needs retreat is retrospective editorial judgment, not evidence available before later developments.
- **Institutions:** Congress can protect ERISA benefit structures; states retain ordinary regulation of tax, tort, contracts, wages, property, and health despite effects on employers with plans.
- **Assumptions:** making one plan's benefits the mandatory measure regulates it more directly than imposing an unrelated cost. That distinction is express, but the file gives no proof of administrative burden or why an independently administered workers' compensation plan is inadequate.
- **Values and rejection:** administrable legal connection instead of unlimited relational language; expressly reject broad economic-effect preemption.
- **Open questions:** boundaries of “operative measurement,” independently administered exempt plans, plans used merely as evidence, and survival of statutes prescribing a fixed benefit independent of ERISA coverage.
- **Tension and classification:** narrow category within statutory preemption. The real dispute is not resolved simply by chanting “duty-specific”: Stone must justify why this use of plan terms warrants displacement while other employer-cost rules do not. D.C. is not a sovereign State, so this is an application of local regulatory autonomy/statutory scope, not direct proof of a Tenth Amendment holding.

Historical-law verification: the Court preempted the requirement even though it could be administered through an ERISA-exempt workers' compensation arrangement; Stevens dissented. The decision illustrates the force of “relates to” language against a state-law structure that borrows ERISA benefits as a measure. [Opinion, 506 U.S. 125, 129-138](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/506/125/).

#### 13. United States Department of Treasury v. Fabe, 508 U.S. 491 (1993)

- **Question and correction:** whether McCarran-Ferguson preserved Ohio's insurance-liquidation priorities against the federal priority statute. Ohio put federal claims fifth, below administration, certain wages, policyholders, and general creditors. The attachment's description of priorities “including federal tax claims” ahead of others obscures the actual conflict.
- **Stone's result and posture:** expressly affirm in part, reverse in part, remand; authorship/joining unknown.
- **Rule and source:** classify priorities by function. Costs necessary to administer liquidation and deliver promised policyholder benefits have the required relation to insurance; ordinary general-creditor preferences do not become insurance regulation from their statutory location. Historical sources: McCarran-Ferguson §2(b), 15 U.S.C. §1012(b), versus 31 U.S.C. §3713. The Supremacy Clause executes Congress's chosen allocation; no freestanding constitutional insurance reservation is established.
- **Precedent:** no express Stone treatment of Royal Drug/Pireno or the difference between clauses of McCarran-Ferguson. Its broader cross-doctrinal usefulness is an inference from method.
- **Institutions:** Congress may preserve state insurance policy against general federal statutes. Courts identify the covered function without granting the entire state statutory scheme immunity.
- **Assumptions:** administration enables payment of policyholders; generic creditor preferences have a weaker relationship. This causal linkage, not the insurer's mere identity, controls.
- **Values and rejection:** functional specificity, Congress's intended priority of state regulation, and opposition to attaching protected labels to unrelated preferences.
- **Open questions:** borderline expenses, indirect protection, employee priorities, savings and reverse-preemption beyond this statute. The entry omits an explicit result for wage claims although the historic holding did not preserve their priority over the United States.
- **Tension and classification:** functional statutory classification, partly severable. It supports careful contextual assessment but does not establish that legal form is always irrelevant. Context determines when a legal category matters.

Historical-law verification: the Court preserved state priorities for administration and policyholder obligations, not the priorities of employees and general creditors over the United States. It interpreted the first clause's insurance-protection scope distinctly from the narrower antitrust framework. [Opinion, 508 U.S. 491, 493-509](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/508/491/).

#### 14. Puerto Rico Aqueduct & Sewer Authority v. Metcalf & Eddy, Inc., 506 U.S. 139 (1993)

- **Question:** immediate appeal from denial of a claimed Eleventh Amendment immunity before final judgment in a contract dispute involving a public authority.
- **Stone's result and posture:** expressly reverse and remand on appealability only. No authorship or joining information.
- **Rule and source:** immunity from being subjected to litigation cannot be restored after litigation; allow immediate review but separate this procedural ruling from immunity merits. Historical sources: §1291 collateral-order doctrine and asserted Eleventh Amendment immunity. The file does not resolve Puerto Rico's constitutional equivalence to a State.
- **Precedent:** no express Cohen/Mitchell treatment. A forward immunity architecture is described, but its eventual substantive components are not already holdings.
- **Institutions:** appellate courts protect an arguable immunity's procedural value; they must not decide every entity-status, duty, or remedy issue by allowing an appeal.
- **Assumptions:** litigation itself can destroy the claimed benefit of immunity, even if final judgment is later reversed. This is an express legal-functional premise; no empirical litigation-cost analysis is provided.
- **Values and rejection:** differentiated legal analysis, restraint on premature merits decisions, and a rejection of “dignity” as a substitute for legal status, structure, historical immunity, and the judicial act resisted.
- **Open questions expressly preserved:** actual arm-of-State status, immunity for this claim, forbidden remedies, and automatic appealability of all immunity-related rulings. Federal duty, cause of action, waiver, forum, and remedy remain separate rather than answered.
- **Tension and classification:** an administrability-based procedural rule founded on the nature of the claimed immunity. It supplies no conclusion that States may violate federal law with impunity, that commerce power cannot abrogate immunity, or that commercial authorities always lack immunity.

Historical-law verification: the real Court allowed interlocutory review without deciding PRASA's immunity status. The historical opinion's dignity rationale was more expansive than the restrained reasoning recorded for Stone. [Opinion, 506 U.S. 139, 143-147](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/506/139/).

#### 15. United States v. California, 507 U.S. 746 (1993)

- **Question and correction:** the United States sought recovery of taxes allegedly misapplied under California law to its reimbursed contractor. The opinion expressly says a constitutional tax-immunity challenge was not presented. The attachment misframes the merits question as whether the contractor's tax was a tax on the Federal Government.
- **Stone's result and posture:** expressly affirm California. No authorship/joining information.
- **Rule and source:** distinguish legal from economic incidence; voluntary federal reimbursement does not itself create federal law, constitutional immunity, or an independent federal restitution action. Ordinary state-law remedies govern without an independent federal source. Historical source: federal-common-law limits, subrogation, state refund procedure; underlying intergovernmental-tax-immunity doctrine explains the background, not the actual constitutional issue decided.
- **Precedent:** no express Stone citations. Historical New Mexico supplies the contractor-immunity premise; California addresses recovery and subrogation. Do not credit Stone with newly deciding New Mexico's constitutional holding here.
- **Institutions:** States administer their tax law; federal fiscal participation does not give federal courts authority to recreate lost state claims automatically.
- **Assumptions:** the contractor is legally distinct, reimbursement is voluntary under contract, and the state tax dispute can use state procedures. A federal payer need not receive a superior forum or cause of action because it ultimately bears the expense.
- **Values and rejection:** legal-source discipline and respect for ordinary remedial structures. Expressly reject creation of federal common law from federal money alone.
- **Open questions:** direct taxes on the United States, discriminatory taxes on federal functions, federal statutory remedies, and cases with a genuine conflict between state law and federal operations.
- **Tension and classification:** source- and remedy-specific. Legal incidence matters here while functional economic effects matter in anti-discrimination cases; that is not automatically inconsistent because the constitutional and remedial questions differ. The file does not establish a universal “substance defeats form” philosophy.

Historical-law verification: WBEC settled part of its refund claim and dismissed its state actions. The United States could not obtain superior rights through federal restitution or untimely subrogation. The Court explicitly distinguished this state-law recovery dispute from a constitutional tax-immunity case. [Opinion, 507 U.S. 746, 748-759](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/507/746/).

### End-of-OT1992 doctrinal map

| Branch | Established express resource | Reasonable inference | Not established |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dormant interstate Commerce | Reject unequal origin charges and county exclusion; allow genuine cost recovery | Protect equal market access and prevent local cost exporting | A general test for Pike, discriminatory effects, private monopolies, public facilities, or subsidies |
| Foreign Commerce/state tax | Reject foreign-source discrimination; permit ordinary tax on local transactions | Fiscal autonomy and market equality can coexist | Executive veto, global tax uniformity, worldwide reporting, one-voice revision |
| Tax structure/nexus | Nexus, allocation, nondiscrimination, territorial discipline matter | Structural rather than interstate-business immunity | A Quill vote, physical-presence rejection, a completed credit or internal-consistency rule |
| Affirmative congressional power | Congress can regulate interstate radioactive-waste commerce | Enumeration is a distinct threshold from permissible regulatory means | Lopez/Morrison boundaries, economic/non-economic categories, anti-evasion test, production-input nexus |
| Anti-commandeering | Invalidate forced state take-title/administration choice; allow lawful incentives and preemption | Accountability helps explain actor-specific limits | Executive-command rule, spending-coercion test, categorical invalidity of direct duties on state enterprises |
| Preemption | Duty- and subject-specific enacted displacement; protect proprietary transactions where statutory scheme permits | Resist judicial creation of federal exclusivity | A blanket presumption against preemption, rejection of all obstacle/field preemption, complete ERISA revision |
| Sovereign immunity | Immediate appeal can preserve an immunity claim; separate procedural and merits issues | Legal status and remedial specificity constrain sovereignty rhetoric | Article I abrogation, state-court immunity, commercial-entity exceptions, comprehensive officer-relief doctrine |
| Federal common law/remedies | Federal expenditure is not itself a cause of action or superior state refund right | Federal interests require legal sources and proper remedial channels | Abolition of federal common law or ordinary equitable remedies |

### Actual institutional problems the baseline appears to address

1. **Discriminatory allocation of market access and regulatory costs:** directly supported by Chemical Waste, Fort Gratiot, Kraft. The remedy aims at a burden tied to outsider status, not every economic inefficiency. National market integration is a persuasive inference; antipathy to regulation is not.
2. **Usable state fiscal capacity:** directly supported by Quill's orientation, Itel, and California. The record accepts state taxation even when it affects national or international actors or federal spending. Territorial connection and nondiscrimination, not a broad free-trade slogan, do the work.
3. **Uncontrolled judicial enlargement of federal statutory displacement:** supported by the limiting language in Morales, Gade, Cipollone, Easterwood, and Greater Washington. The problem is overextension beyond enacted choices, not federal preemption itself.
4. **Government ability to procure and run its own projects:** supported by Boston Harbor. This does not prove a preference for public enterprises over private ones. It identifies the scope of the government's own transaction.
5. **Accountability for government-imposed programs:** explicitly central to New York. The file asserts, but does not empirically demonstrate, the voter-confusion mechanism. Stone's later anti-coercion architecture cannot be treated as already developed.
6. **Misclassification of legal status and remedies:** supported by Fabe, PRASA, California. But functional classification coexists with legal categories; the record does not support universal economic-substance balancing.

**Unsupported as settled pre-OT1993 aims:** regulatory capture, subsidiarity, maximizing state experimentation, market deregulation, minimizing national power, national uniformity as an independent preference, or skepticism about all nondiscriminatory burdens. Each might illuminate a later dispute, but none should be put into Stone's mouth on this record.

### Tensions requiring honest preservation

- **Text/source discipline versus inferred dormant restraints:** Stone accepts dormant-Commerce enforcement without the record supplying its historical or structural justification. He has not yet explained why judicial market policing without enacted federal law fits his preemption and common-law source discipline.
- **No universal choice between formal and functional analysis:** Chemical Waste/Fort Gratiot attack formal geography tricks, Boston Harbor/Fabe inspect function, while California respects legal incidence. These are reconcilable by asking which legal relationship the doctrine governs, but that explanation is an analytical reconstruction.
- **Costs and discrimination:** the permission to charge for actual environmental burdens leaves unresolved how to price diffuse risks and past public contributions without returning to origin as an unproved proxy.
- **Preemption granularity:** Gade's occupied subject and Morales's protection of deregulation cannot be reduced to Easterwood's exact-rule language. Conversely, Gade cannot support field preemption whenever a federal agency has acted.
- **ERISA functionalism:** Greater Washington gives dispositive weight to borrowing plan benefits as a measure without fully explaining why independent administration of an exempt benefit does not remove the problem. This is a genuine pressure point, not a settled general rule.
- **Procurement versus market foreclosure:** Boston Harbor furnishes a boundary, not an answer to whether a designated facility is a proprietary transaction, government enterprise, private franchise, or regulation of an entire local market.
- **Accountability versus institutional cooperation:** New York preserves cooperation but rejects one intensely negotiated arrangement. The file provides no developed treatment of interstate bargaining, consent, or federal fallback costs.
- **Tax administration versus structural purity:** the missing Quill disposition prevents assigning Stone a settled view on when bright-line predictability justifies doctrinal formalism. Itel's rejection of immunity does not decide this.

### Constraints on the OT1993 analysis

The strongest analysis should present pre-OT1993 positions as an incomplete but usable architecture. It can legitimately refine a comparator, define proof requirements, distinguish government procurement from outside coercion, or specify the relation between state fiscal authority and congressional control. It cannot describe a move from a future roadmap outcome as a reversal of an existing decision. It also cannot say Stone “joined” or “dissented” in prior cases or fabricate a prior Quill vote. Where OT1993 entries supply Oregon Waste, Carbone, and West Lynn recommendations, those are candidate positions to reassess under the user's instruction, not settled premises of the reconstructed baseline.

## II. Governing law, theory, and evidence

### The legal world Stone enters in OT1993

Stone's own recorded jurisprudence is incomplete, but the governing legal world is not empty. The distinction is essential. An unrecorded Stone vote in an earlier historical case does not remove that precedent. A proposed departure from that law needs an explanation even if it harmonizes with his simulated commitments.

#### Distinct constitutional questions

| Question | Source and legal task | What does not answer it |
|---|---|---|
| Does Congress possess the power? | Article I's enumerated powers and applicable means-end doctrine; identify the regulated activity and the relevant grant | National importance, state tradition, or a dormant-Commerce violation alone |
| Is the federal means permissible? | Constitutional structure, the Tenth Amendment, and governing anti-commandeering precedent | Proof that the national objective is desirable or within the commerce power |
| Does federal law displace state law? | The scope and effect of an identified valid federal source under Article VI, including statutes, regulations, treaties, and appropriate federal common law | The mere presence of federal regulation or an official preference for uniformity |
| Does state action discriminate against or unduly burden commerce? | The negative Commerce principle, its precedents, and any valid congressional authorization | A general assumption that interstate firms should face no state costs |
| Can the plaintiff obtain this relief here? | Cause of action, defendant, immunity, forum, waiver, and remedy | The existence of a substantive federal duty by itself |

This separation explains why invalidating a municipal market barrier does not imply weak congressional commerce power, and why preserving state law does not imply immunity from a valid federal obligation. It also prevents a sophisticated-sounding reference to federalism from substituting for the legal question actually presented.

#### Affirmative power and governmental means

The governing affirmative-Commerce tradition included regulation of economic classes with substantial interstate effects and deferential review of Congress's factual judgments. *Hodel v. Virginia Surface Mining*, 452 U.S. 264 (1981), upheld regulation of surface mining and a cooperative structure allowing a State to implement a program or leave implementation to federal authorities. Rehnquist's separate opinion insisted on substantial effects rather than merely any effect, while accepting the judgments under precedent. The case supplies an actual pre-1993 source for demanding a connection and for respecting genuine federal fallbacks; it does not establish the attachment's later detailed mechanism test. [Hodel, 276-283, 288-290, 307-313](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/452/264/).

*Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority*, 469 U.S. 528 (1985), rejected the traditional-governmental-function test and sustained federal wage-and-hour duties on a public transit operator. Its political-safeguards theory was strongly contested in dissent. Stone's *New York* commitment need not overturn *Garcia*: regulating an employer's own activity differs from requiring state legislation to govern third parties. But that reconciliation is an explanation Stone should supply, not a claim that the simulation contains a *Garcia* opinion. A public-function exception in dormant-Commerce doctrine would require its own rationale; it could not be borrowed as a general exemption from valid federal regulation. [Garcia, complete opinions](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/469/528/).

The boundary is harder than the formula “any federal instruction to a State is commandeering.” *FERC v. Mississippi*, 456 U.S. 742 (1982), sustained specified consideration and procedural obligations in a preemptible regulatory field. State courts also occupy a distinct position under Article VI. *New York* itself preserved lawful incentives, preemption, and cooperation. OT1993 should therefore refine the distinction between appropriating another government's regulatory machinery and enforcing valid federal constraints on that government's conduct. It should not silently invalidate existing cooperation or assume *Printz* has already answered the executive question. [FERC, 759-771 and separate opinions](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/456/742/); [New York, 161-169, 175-188](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/505/144/).

Sovereign immunity was also governed by actual precedent. *Pennsylvania v. Union Gas*, 491 U.S. 1 (1989), accepted Commerce Clause abrogation with five votes on that issue, although White rejected the plurality's reasoning. It was not yet the legal world of *Seminole Tribe*. Stone's *PRASA* position on immediate appeal does not resolve abrogation, much less authorize pretending the later rule already governs. No included OT1993 case requires Stone to settle the entire matter. [Union Gas, opinions and alignments](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/491/1/).

#### Dormant-Commerce review and the identity of the comparison

The accepted framework distinguished discrimination from incidental interstate burdens. Geographic discrimination ordinarily demanded a genuine legitimate purpose that available nondiscriminatory means could not adequately achieve. That was stringent, but not an irrebuttable ban on every origin distinction: *Maine v. Taylor*, 477 U.S. 131 (1986), sustained a baitfish restriction on a record of ecological risks and unavailable adequate inspection alternatives. Its dissent disputed the sufficiency of that showing. Stone should preserve that evidence-sensitive exception rather than describe all origin distinctions as automatically invalid or accept any asserted environmental concern as enough. [Maine v. Taylor, 138-152 and dissent](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/477/131/).

*Pike v. Bruce Church*, 397 U.S. 137 (1970), involved an Arizona packing requirement and stated the familiar standard for evenhanded regulation imposing incidental burdens. The doctrine cannot be reduced to judicial optimization of every local rule. *Exxon Corp. v. Governor of Maryland*, 437 U.S. 117 (1978), upheld a restriction affecting out-of-state refiners without treating injury to particular interstate firms or a particular market organization as injury to commerce itself. These authorities leave space for demonstrable incompatible requirements, exclusion, and duplicative local facilities while rejecting the proposition that every business compliance cost requires judicial balancing. The theory discussion below explains the recommended restrained approach and its unresolved boundaries. [Pike](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/397/137/); [Exxon, 125-129](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/437/117/).

Classification precedes application. The court must identify the regulated market, the actual favored and burdened transactions, and whether purported counterparts are genuinely comparable. A monopoly that excludes both local and outside competitors can still reserve a local processing market; conversely, an industry concentrated outside the State is not necessarily discriminated against whenever it bears a neutral regulation. Legal form, practical effects, and purpose provide evidence; none supplies a universal shortcut. Stone's choice is strong antidiscrimination with careful comparison, not an automatic win for either formal neutrality or unequal economic impact.

Congressional authorization and state market participation are separate doctrines. Congress can authorize otherwise forbidden burdens when its decision is sufficiently clear. General federal encouragement of waste planning is not enough. In *South-Central Timber Development v. Wunnicke*, 467 U.S. 82 (1984), the Court rejected claimed authorization; the market-participant analysis restricting downstream conditions was a plurality. *Reeves* and *White* also supplied preterm proprietary precedents. Their proper use is to distinguish the government's actual transaction from sovereign control of other transactions, not to declare that every government financial interest immunizes coercion. Stone's *Boston Harbor* method is useful by analogy, while its historical holding remains one about labor preemption. [South-Central Timber, full opinions](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/467/82/); [Reeves v. Stake, 447 U.S. 429](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/447/429/); [White v. Massachusetts Council, 460 U.S. 204](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/460/204/).

#### Taxes, fees, and uniformity

*Complete Auto Transit v. Brady*, 430 U.S. 274 (1977), supplied the four central requirements: substantial nexus, fair apportionment, nondiscrimination, and fair relation to state services. It rejected a formal immunity for the privilege of doing interstate business. Stone's *Quill* and *Itel* entries clearly draw on that structural orientation, but do not resolve every implementing rule. Taxes can legitimately support general government; the fair-relation requirement is not an invoice for precisely measured individual benefits. A service fee and an origin-based compensatory tax present different questions. [Complete Auto, 279-289](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/430/274/); [Commonwealth Edison v. Montana, 453 U.S. 609, 622-629](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/453/609/).

Compensation is a constrained justification for an otherwise different interstate burden, requiring a real corresponding local tax on a substantially equivalent event and appropriate matching. It is not permission to assemble all taxes residents ever paid. Conversely, lawful sales and use taxes need not employ identical legal events if they impose genuine equivalent burdens. That distinction makes *Lohman* as important as *Oregon Waste*: economic integration of a fiscal scheme must sometimes validate the interstate charge, not invariably invalidate it.

Foreign commerce adds concerns about international multiple taxation and national uniformity, classically associated with *Japan Line*, 441 U.S. 434 (1979). *Container Corp.*, 463 U.S. 159 (1983), had already sustained worldwide unitary income apportionment in its setting. Neither precedent makes all overlapping international claims constitutionally equivalent, and neither establishes that the most common accounting method is constitutionally mandatory. *Barclays* requires Stone to distinguish a discriminatory or distorted claim from the difficult coordination of otherwise permissible tax systems. [Japan Line](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/441/434/); [Container Corp.](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/463/159/).

#### Preemption and federal common law

Stone's most defensible general rule is that displacement requires an **identified, valid source of controlling federal law**, followed by an explanation of its scope and application to the disputed state duty. In statutory cases, Congress's enacted choices do the work. But the Constitution, valid regulations, treaties, and recognized federal common law are also legal sources. A slogan that only express statutory language can displace state law would conflict with both dormant-Commerce review and legitimate federal maritime law.

Likewise, duty-specific inquiry does not require an identical affirmative federal duty. *Morales* recognizes legally protected deregulation; *Gade* recognizes an occupied subject; *Easterwood* examines whether regulations cover the relevant subject matter. Stone should retain these distinctions while rejecting an inference of total exclusivity from mere federal involvement. *American Dredging* and *O'Melveny* are particularly useful because they expose the difference between recognized federal common-law authority and a judicially invented rule justified only by a federal litigant's financial interest. Their individual studies supply the controlling details.

### Competing theories of the dormant Commerce Clause

#### Protectionism, representation, and economic integration are different objectives

Donald H. Regan's *The Supreme Court and State Protectionism: Making Sense of the Dormant Commerce Clause*, 84 Michigan Law Review 1091-1287 (1986), argues in its verified author abstract that the movement-of-goods cases should focus exclusively on purposeful protectionism rather than open-ended balancing. Crucially, he separately recognizes limited additional purposes in state-tax and railroad/highway cases, including specific balancing in transportation. Thus “Regan supports no balancing anywhere” would misstate even the article's abstract. The full download could not be obtained reliably in this research run; the attribution here is limited to the author's publicly accessible abstract. [Regan, institutional author page](https://repository.law.umich.edu/facarticles/344/).

This limited claim illuminates Stone without defining him. *Chemical Waste* and *Fort Gratiot* address barriers constructed around an origin line. Their strongest rationale does not depend on proving that the affected industry would be more efficient under judicially preferred regulation. Nevertheless, Stone's existing tax concerns include territorial reach and multiple taxation; an exclusively motive-based test would leave those concerns unexplained. Moreover, Stone's functional method implies that an origin preference does not become constitutional because its drafter had several sincere purposes. A fiscal measure can finance a worthy service while employing an impermissible discriminatory instrument.

Julian N. Eule's *Laying the Dormant Commerce Clause to Rest*, 91 Yale Law Journal 425-485 (1982), proposes reallocating the national-economic-policy inquiry to Congress while using Article IV privileges and immunities to address exclusion from representative processes. His objection to balancing is substantially democratic and institutional, not simply the assertion that judges never balance. He expressly recognizes that process protection can sometimes invalidate regulations a goods-flow theory would sustain, discussing *Exxon*. His alternative therefore is not judicial indifference to outsiders. [Eule, full original article reprint](https://scispace.com/pdf/laying-the-dormant-commerce-clause-to-rest-21pzsczobk.pdf).

For Stone, Eule exposes a real choice: Is the protected interest access by goods, equal treatment of persons, the integrity of representative processes, or some combination? These cannot automatically be equated. Corporations' position under Article IV, the Clause's treatment of fundamental activities, and the different consequences of congressional authorization make wholesale substitution a considerable doctrinal reconstruction. Stone has no demonstrated reason in OT1993 to abandon the dormant Clause and rebuild the same outcomes under a different provision. He can adopt Eule's warning against constitutionalizing policy preferences without accepting Eule's entire reconstruction.

Martin H. Redish and Shane V. Nugent, *The Dormant Commerce Clause and the Constitutional Balance of Federalism*, 1987 Duke Law Journal 569-617, argue in their verified abstract that the doctrine lacks textual foundation and disturbs the constitutional allocation of authority. They reject longevity as self-validating and point to other express constitutional constraints. This is a serious challenge to the **source** of judicial authority, not merely to particular applications of *Pike*. Only the abstract was accessible here; no detailed argument from an unread section is attributed to them. [Redish and Nugent, publisher page](https://scholarship.law.duke.edu/dlj/vol36/iss4/1/).

The most useful contemporaneous judicial expression of the restrained alternative is Scalia's concurrence in *CTS Corp. v. Dynamics Corp. of America*, 481 U.S. 69, 94-96 (1987): absence of discrimination and absence of inconsistent interstate regulation should suffice, with strong skepticism about judicial weighing of burdens against benefits. His separate opinion in *Tyler Pipe Industries v. Washington Department of Revenue*, 483 U.S. 232, 254-265 (1987), raises the deeper textual and institutional challenge. Those are distinguishable positions: narrowing judicial review to established categories versus questioning the entire doctrinal source. [*CTS*, full opinions](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/481/69/); [*Tyler Pipe*, full opinions](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/483/232/).

The strongest defense of continuing judicial review is also more than a policy assertion that national free trade is good. Barry Friedman and Daniel T. Deacon, *A Course Unbroken: The Constitutional Legitimacy of the Dormant Commerce Clause*, 97 Virginia Law Review 1877-1938 (2011), defend the doctrine through text, history, and institutional development. They analyze the dormant doctrine as allocating the burden of seeking congressional correction. Congress's ability to authorize otherwise prohibited burdens distinguishes this allocation from a judicially entrenched laissez-faire code. Their historical argument contests the skeptics' interpretation; it does not demonstrate that every modern balancing decision is legitimate. [Friedman and Deacon, full article](https://virginialawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/1877.pdf).

That later scholarship is useful to the retrospective assessment, not an OT1993 citation Stone could historically have made. Its institutional question is timeless: should affected outsiders need to assemble a national legislative coalition against each local barrier, or should a State seeking a discriminatory exception obtain national authorization? Either default allocates legislative inertia. Calling one of them “leaving the matter to Congress” does not eliminate the choice. Equally, congressional failure to act cannot automatically establish approval, disapproval, institutional incapacity, or democratic consensus. Those are separate inferences requiring evidence.

Norman R. Williams, *The Foundations of the American Common Market*, 84 Notre Dame Law Review 409-470 (2008), distinguishes theories aimed at protectionism from theories concerned with unrepresented interests and develops a deliberative-equality approach. This helps identify a tension: represented residents may share compliance burdens while outsiders still suffer exclusion, and a nondiscriminatory law may impose significant uncompensated costs outside the electorate. Equal consideration is not identical to maximizing total output. [Williams, full article](https://ndlawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/07/Williams.pdf).

Stone should not turn that deliberative theory into an inquiry into whether a legislature gave every outside interest equal subjective attention. That would be difficult to prove and administer. Observable legal distinctions, market access, actual costs, and operational incidence supply more disciplined proxies. The underlying political-process concern can justify the proxy without becoming an unbounded independent cause of action.

#### Recommended theoretical choice for Stone

The defensible development is a strong, rebuttable anti-discrimination principle with a distinct and restrained avenue for serious nondiscriminatory interstate burdens. This preserves the baseline while identifying the constitutional work the rule performs: a State ordinarily may not make outside origin itself the reason for a commercial disadvantage. It does not entitle firms to exemption from general regulation or to the cheapest available production method.

“Protectionism” should describe the operative preference, not demand proof that officials were insincere about every public objective. Conversely, disproportionate costs borne by outsiders are evidence to investigate, not automatic discrimination. Where an entire industry happens to be outside the State, equal regulation of sales does not necessarily favor a local competitor. The proper comparator and market matter. *Carbone*, which reserves a processing function, and *West Lynn*, whose linked instruments alter relative burdens, expose why a facial-label test is insufficient. They do not establish that all public financing or selective expenditure is discriminatory.

The strongest objection is that this approach still depends on judicially chosen categories not expressly stated in the constitutional text. The response should be candid: it is a narrow structural inference implemented through a long line of precedent, with congressional power to authorize exceptions, rather than an express prohibition equivalent to Article I, Section 10. That justification creates a reason for doctrinal restraint. Stone should not claim an uncontested originalist command for his entire architecture.

### Pike, judicial competence, and the limits of comparisons

Stone's record before OT1993 does not decide whether all *Pike* balancing should disappear. That absence matters. Recommending a disciplined standard would be an articulation or refinement, not a reversal of an invented anti-balancing holding.

Three alternatives deserve separate treatment. First, ordinary balancing can ask whether local benefits justify all aggregate economic costs. Its strength is sensitivity to regulations that obstruct commerce without an explicit origin preference. Its danger is constitutional cost-benefit review of ordinary police power. Second, an anti-discrimination-only rule is comparatively administrable but risks leaving genuinely incompatible requirements or barriers to transportation unaddressed. Third, restrained *Pike* review can demand an identifiable burden on interstate commerce and keep the inquiry tied to the actual evidentiary dispute, institutional competence, and substantial disproportionality. The third option is most compatible with Stone's source discipline and structural-tax concerns.

A court should identify what makes the burden interstate: practical exclusion from a market, forced duplicative facilities, incompatible requirements on the same interstate operation, or comparable obstruction. A firm's compliance expense alone does not answer that question. Nor should “interstate” become a magic word whenever a regulated firm sells nationally. The challenger must establish the claimed mechanism and substantial burden; the court must then examine the State's actual interests and evidence. This formulation leaves difficult applications, but it prevents the inquiry from beginning with an assumed judicial mandate to optimize regulation.

A later working draft by Ruth Mason and Michael S. Knoll, *Unbundling Undue Burdens* (November 9, 2021), usefully separates burdens attributable to a single State's treatment of interstate activity from burdens created by the interaction of different States' laws. Resolving the second category may implicitly select a national rule and redistribute regulatory authority. The accessible document is expressly a draft with unfinished citations, not a verified final publication. Its conceptual distinction is helpful; it is not authority for a new constitutional test. [Mason and Knoll, NYU workshop draft](https://www.law.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/mason-knoll%20Unbundling%20Undue%20Burdens.pdf).

For example, inconsistent trucking equipment requirements can impair one vehicle's passage through several States in a way that different rules for products offered for sale do not always do. On the other hand, integrated production systems sometimes make segregation unusually expensive. The law should require evidence about that difference rather than assuming every national supply chain must operate under its least demanding State's rule. Where factual uncertainty concerns feasibility, judicial fact-finding or remand is preferable to invented economic predicates.

This approach is principled across political valence. It can protect neutral environmental, labor, animal-welfare, consumer, or morally motivated sales regulations from automatic invalidation while also examining neutral-looking protectionist devices. None of those purposes gets a categorical exemption. The question is how the challenged law works, what constitutional burden it creates, and whether the asserted justification meets the applicable standard.

### Fiscal federalism: what economics explains, and what it cannot decide

Charles M. Tiebout's *A Pure Theory of Local Expenditures*, 64 Journal of Political Economy 416-424 (1956), supplies a model of households sorting among local tax-service packages. Its assumptions include substantial mobility and information, many communities, and no intercommunity externalities; its stylized treatment removes employment constraints. It is a theoretical mechanism, not a finding that actual households freely relocate or that every State market is efficient. [Tiebout, publisher article reprint](https://courses.cit.cornell.edu/econ4260/out%20combined/Tiebout-PureTheoryLocal-1956.pdf).

Wallace E. Oates's fiscal-federalism framework asks which level of government can match public services to heterogeneous preferences while accounting for scale and spillovers. His 1999 survey is retrospective; the decentralization framework itself precedes OT1993. The author abstract confirms that the field compares allocation of functions and fiscal instruments, rather than supplying an unconditional pro-local rule. [Oates, *An Essay on Fiscal Federalism*, 37 Journal of Economic Literature 1120-1149 (1999), publisher page](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jel.37.3.1120).

Applied to Stone, the conditional logic is more useful than a slogan. Local waste-management choices may reflect different land availability, disposal risks, transport distances, and service preferences. Interstate waste movement may also allocate disposal to facilities with lower real costs. Neither observation proves that a locality must privatize service, accept all waste, exclude outsiders, or use a particular financing device. It instead identifies the factual questions: Which costs arise locally? Which are exported? Who pays for the service? Are the charges based on risk or origin? Can the government obtain the same legitimate benefit without reserving an independent market for a favored operator?

There is a vital distinction between physical externalities and fiscal disadvantage. Pollution crossing a boundary is an externality. A competitor offering a lower price is not, without more, an uncompensated physical harm. Reduced demand for a municipal facility may create a fiscal problem, but describing that problem as an externality does not itself justify coercive protection from competition. Contractual debt obligations and public-service responsibilities are real facts deserving analysis; the constitutional question is whether they legally justify the challenged means.

Richard L. Revesz's *Rehabilitating Interstate Competition: Rethinking the “Race-to-the-Bottom” Rationale for Federal Environmental Regulation*, 67 New York University Law Review 1210 (1992), distinguishes interstate pollution from competition for mobile industry and disputes treating that competition as an automatic case for federal environmental floors. His theoretical analysis also examines how competition can migrate to other policy instruments. The article does not prove that interstate competition can never lower standards or that every State's environmental choice is socially optimal. It was available before OT1993 and directly supports a demand to identify the mechanism rather than invoke an undifferentiated race-to-the-bottom prediction. [Revesz, original article text hosted on the author's scholarly profile](https://www.academia.edu/17880261/Rehabilitating_Interstate_Competition_Rethinking_the_Race_to_the_Bottom_Rationale_for_Federal_Environmental_Regulation).

The legal implication is limited. These theories help expose a state's asserted justification as incomplete when it relies only on the discomfort of interstate competition. They do not give courts a constitutional license to select the optimal allocation of services. Congress's authority to regulate a genuine interstate problem and the dormant Clause's limitation on a discriminatory state solution remain separate propositions.

### Later evidence: spillovers, strategic behavior, capture, and experimentation

The empirical record supplies reasons to reject categorical factual assumptions on both sides. It does not yield a single answer to the OT1993 cases.

Daniel L. Millimet's *Environmental Federalism: A Survey of the Empirical Literature*, 64 Case Western Reserve Law Review 1669-1757 (2014), reviews mobility, preference heterogeneity, externalities, and political economy. The accessible December 2013 working-paper version concludes that evidence for transboundary effects and strategic policymaking is compelling, whereas evidence identifying a general race upward or downward is limited. It finds important gaps concerning comparative responsiveness, capture, and policy laboratories. Those are conclusions of the surveyed literature through that date, not claims that every later study agrees. [Millimet, full working-paper version](https://docs.iza.org/dp7831.pdf).

One original study surveyed is Hilary Sigman's *Transboundary Spillovers and Decentralization of Environmental Policies*, 50 Journal of Environmental Economics and Management 82-101 (2005). Its design examines water pollution in relation to state implementation authority and boundaries. It supports investigating geographically structured pollution incentives instead of treating States as sealed compartments. The full original manuscript was not reliably retrievable here, so no numerical causal estimate is adopted from a secondary report. [Sigman, publisher record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0095069604001342).

Another is Per G. Fredriksson and Daniel L. Millimet, *Strategic Interaction and the Determination of Environmental Policy across U.S. States*, 51 Journal of Urban Economics 101-122 (2002). The publisher abstract reports interdependence in states' environmental policymaking. The detailed discussion in Millimet's survey underscores that observed responsiveness to neighbors does not automatically identify harmful downward competition. Estimates depend on measures of regulatory stringency and on the institutional setting. [Fredriksson and Millimet, original study publisher page](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0094119001922399).

Pranab Bardhan and Dilip Mookherjee, *Capture and Governance at Local and National Levels*, 90 American Economic Review 135-139 (2000), construct a comparative model in which awareness, interest-group cohesion, electoral competition, inequality, and electoral arrangements can push relative capture in different directions. The authors explicitly treat the net effect as theoretically ambiguous and in need of contextual empirical assessment. This is a model, not proof of municipal corruption or federal impartiality. [Bardhan and Mookherjee, full article](https://people.bu.edu/dilipm/publications/BardhanMookherjee2000%20AER.pdf).

This means Stone may identify a regulation's concentrated beneficiaries and diffuse costs, but cannot infer pretext solely from that distribution. A private contractor can supply an effective public service; a public authority can protect its own revenue at outsiders' expense. The private/public label alone cannot settle either welfare or motive. The legally relevant facts are the actual franchise, coercion, governance, risks, price-setting arrangements, access rules, and the fit between regulatory burden and public-service need.

Koleman S. Strumpf's *Does Government Decentralization Increase Policy Innovation?*, 4 Journal of Public Economic Theory 207-241 (2002), models the informational spillovers from experimentation. Decentralized governments can underinvest because others benefit from the knowledge generated; the relative advantage of central and local arrangements depends on homogeneity, policy opportunities, and the model's institutional constraints. It is a theoretical warning against equating more governments with more useful experiments, not an empirical determination that centralization is superior. [Strumpf, full article](https://users.wfu.edu/strumpks/papers/Strumpf-2002-Journal_of_Public_Economic_Theory.pdf).

For Stone, state experimentation should remain a legitimate structural opportunity rather than a conclusive empirical premise. A national floor with local discretion, grants for pilot programs, shared information, or federal waivers can sometimes facilitate experimentation. None follows automatically from constitutional text. The judicial task is to preserve space that valid law leaves to state choice, not to create a free-standing “laboratory exception” to discrimination, enumeration, or preemption.

### Political safeguards, accountability, and the resources of cooperation

The classic political-safeguards account is associated with Herbert Wechsler, *The Political Safeguards of Federalism: The Role of the States in the Composition and Selection of the National Government*, 54 Columbia Law Review 543 (1954), and Jesse H. Choper's *Judicial Review and the National Political Process* (1980). The research here verified those sources and their use in *Garcia*, not their complete book-length arguments. The safe doctrinal foundation is *Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority*, 469 U.S. 528 (1985), including its majority's reliance on political process and its rejection of the traditional-government-function test. Powell's dissent challenges the adequacy of those safeguards. Those competing judicial assessments are not themselves empirical proof of how much federalism the process protects. [*Garcia*, complete opinions and scholarly references](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/469/528/).

Stone's *New York* baseline does not require repudiating all political safeguards. It preserves direct congressional regulation and lawful federal-state cooperation. The better distinction is between reliance on political process over the substance of ordinary national economic legislation and judicial enforcement of the structural allocation of responsibility for implementing that legislation. The argument is strongest if a prohibition on conscription preserves a State's capacity to set priorities for its own government rather than merely predicting that voters will always misunderstand jointly administered programs.

Roderick M. Hills, Jr., *The Political Economy of Cooperative Federalism: Why State Autonomy Makes Sense and “Dual Sovereignty” Doesn't*, 96 Michigan Law Review 813-944 (1998), frames federalism around cooperation among governments administering national policy and the circumstances in which state and local governments can refuse participation. The accessible publisher introduction establishes that practical problem; the full article was not reliably accessible in this research run. The bargaining analysis below is offered as an institutional inference, not represented as a detailed quotation from the unread article. [Hills, publisher introduction](https://michiganlawreview.org/journal/the-political-economy-of-cooperative-federalism-why-state-autonomy-makes-sense-and-dual-sovereignty-doesnt/).

The inference is straightforward. If Congress must regulate directly or secure cooperation, national officials confront implementation costs and state officials can negotiate around their administrative capacities and preferences. Compulsory use of state personnel can obscure those costs and displace state priorities. But it does not follow that any arrangement involving shared responsibility is constitutionally defective. Negotiated implementation can use local knowledge and existing infrastructure efficiently. Accountability is compatible with cooperation if legal responsibility, the choice to participate, and substantive authority are sufficiently identifiable.

The strongest objection to Stone's original voter-confusion explanation is therefore overbreadth. Direct federal preemption also produces political complexity; conditional grants may be more difficult for voters to understand than a simple federal command. Complexity cannot be the operative constitutional wrong without endangering cooperative arrangements the baseline permits. Stone should retain accountability as a reason supporting the rule while grounding the rule more precisely in compulsory appropriation of another government's legislative or administrative machinery.

That is a refinement, not abandonment of *New York*. The evidence examined does not establish that commandeering is always less efficient or always more confusing. The constitutional distinction should not depend on either empirical universal. Nor should Stone use the dormant Commerce Clause cases to decide in advance *Printz*, spending coercion, or sovereign-immunity cases. They involve different authorities, and the source file's later plans are not already precedents.

### Collective action: valuable diagnostic, insufficient constitutional source

Robert D. Cooter and Neil S. Siegel, *Collective Action Federalism: A General Theory of Article I, Section 8*, 63 Stanford Law Review 115-185 (2010), interpret Article I powers through interstate collective-action problems. The verified author abstract explains their argument that the economic/noneconomic distinction does not reliably track spillovers and that a collective-action account can illuminate national authority. It also advances a broader reading of the General Welfare Clause. Only the author abstract was reliably accessible here. [Cooter and Siegel, author publication page](https://scholarship.law.duke.edu/faculty_scholarship/2270/).

Stone can use this later theory to test reasoning without importing it as a new enumerated power. Ask whether a claimed externality actually crosses boundaries, whether individual States can realistically address it, and how the challenged means relates to the identified problem. A widespread local problem is not automatically an interstate collective-action problem. Conversely, a genuine spillover need not arise from conduct intuitively labeled economic. The theory is most useful as a demand for a demonstrated mechanism, least defensible as the proposition that every beneficial national solution is constitutionally authorized.

This is especially important in OT1993. The waste cases predominantly concern state regulatory and fiscal means; they do not present the validity of an act of Congress under a comprehensive post-*Lopez* test. Stone can acknowledge Congress's substantial authority over interstate waste transactions without announcing a general theory that reaches future guns, violence, health-insurance, or environmental disputes. He should reserve those questions until a record and a statutory scheme make them concrete.

### Later cases as stress tests, not instructions

#### Public operations: United Haulers and Davis

*United Haulers Association v. Oneida-Herkimer Solid Waste Management Authority*, 550 U.S. 330 (2007), upheld flow-control requirements benefiting public facilities while treating private facilities alike. Roberts's reasoning concerning public functions commanded a majority; his *Pike* analysis did not attract every justice in that coalition. The dissent argued that public favoritism can obstruct interstate commerce as private favoritism does. The decision does not erase the difference between proprietary purchases and sovereign flow-control law. [*United Haulers*, full opinions](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/550/330/).

*Department of Revenue of Kentucky v. Davis*, 553 U.S. 328 (2008), upheld preferential tax treatment for interest on Kentucky public bonds, stressing government borrowing and longstanding fiscal arrangements. Souter's market-participant discussion was a plurality section; the case should not be cited as a majority conversion of discriminatory taxes into ordinary purchases. The opinion also distinguishes public borrowing from private commercial favoritism. [*Davis*, full opinions](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/553/328/).

Norman R. Williams and Brannon P. Denning, *The “New Protectionism” and the American Common Market*, 85 Notre Dame Law Review 247-312 (2009), criticize both decisions. Their central practical concern is that government ownership, private management, mixed investment, and government contracting blur a public/private line and can allow protectionism to migrate through organizational forms. They specifically discuss privately operated public facilities. This is a doctrinal and institutional critique, not empirical proof that all predicted evasions actually occurred. [Williams and Denning, full article](https://ndlawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/07/Williams_Denning.pdf).

For OT1993 Stone, later history supplies a reason to reserve the public-facility question carefully in *Carbone*. Genuine governmental responsibility for providing an integrated service may be relevant. Title alone is inadequate. A municipality's own purchase of disposal service is analytically different from forcing independent firms' transactions through a protected facility. At the same time, it would be too fast to constitutionalize a universal requirement that municipalities fund all public services from general taxes or purchase all services on an open market.

The recommended reservation should identify the missing issues: actual public control, responsibility to serve, who carries operating and financing risks, whether the mandate reaches only the government's own service undertaking, treatment of private and outside public suppliers, and whether the benefit principally supports an integrated public service or selected commerce. These are evidentiary considerations for future adjudication, not a newly invented multi-factor immunity test. Stone should decide *Carbone*'s actual coercive preference and leave the later public structure open. That preserves his functional *Boston Harbor* distinction and avoids reverse-engineering *United Haulers*.

The cost of this reservation is uncertainty for public-private finance. The cost of a categorical title rule is manipulability and inconsistent treatment of economically similar arrangements. Neither public ownership nor competition can be assumed universally beneficial. The eventual choice needs the concrete later arrangement and governing precedent.

#### Alcohol: equal scrutiny without denying state regulatory authority

*Tennessee Wine and Spirits Retailers Association v. Thomas*, 588 U.S. 504 (2019), invalidated a durational-residency requirement for retail liquor licensing. The majority assessed whether the discriminatory condition actually furthered a legitimate health or safety objective, identified feasible nondiscriminatory alternatives, and found concrete evidentiary support lacking. It did not invalidate the basic three-tier system or every in-state operational requirement. The dissent presented a stronger account of state authority under the Twenty-first Amendment. [*Tennessee Wine*, full opinions](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/588/18-96/).

This is a direct stress test for Stone's anti-origin principle. A genuinely regulated field should not automatically immunize a preference. At the same time, constitutional provisions that confer additional state authority matter; applying the principle consistently requires interpreting those provisions, not treating all industries identically. The legal structure can differ while the demand for a real connection between discriminatory means and legitimate ends remains recognizable.

#### National Pork Producers: the danger of overstating a fractured holding

*National Pork Producers Council v. Ross*, 598 U.S. 356 (2023), rejected a proposed almost-per-se rule against state sales restrictions because of out-of-state production effects. The judgment upheld dismissal of a challenge to California's pork standard. Gorsuch wrote a Court opinion for Parts I, II, III, IV-A, and V; his treatment of incommensurable benefits and costs and parts of the *Pike* application lacked a majority. Sotomayor and Kagan expressly preserved *Pike*. Roberts, joined by Alito, Kavanaugh, and Jackson, would have allowed the *Pike* claim to proceed. Barrett found a substantial burden adequately pleaded but joined Gorsuch's concern about comparing unlike values. It is inaccurate to say the Court abolished *Pike*. [*Ross*, official complete opinions](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/21-468_5if6.pdf).

The useful retrospective lesson is methodological. Most production being out of State does not by itself turn an evenhanded sales condition into prohibited discrimination. Discrimination remains central, but the justices disagreed about whether the pleaded burdens were substantial and whether the particular benefits could be judicially compared. A defensible OT1993 rule should permit that factual and institutional debate rather than predetermine either invalidation or immunity for all national supply-chain regulation.

For Stone, a sweeping *Carbone* statement that States may never condition local transactions in ways that affect outside processing would be unstable. His opinion should instead focus on the reserved processing market and compulsory local preference actually present. That narrower reasoning remains defensible when the later law regulates a product standard with politically different goals and no admitted local favoritism.

## IV. The five central case studies

### Oregon Waste Systems, Inc. v. Department of Environmental Quality, 511 U.S. 93 (1994)

#### Actual dispute, mechanism, and historical record

Oregon was trying to finance a comprehensive system for waste management, risk reduction, recycling, monitoring, and eventual landfill liabilities. Its disputed charge was a public regulatory levy on the use of privately owned disposal facilities, not the price Oregon charged for selling a disposal service it itself supplied. Oregon Waste operated a landfill in Gilliam County. Columbia Resource transported Washington waste by barge under a twenty-year contract with Clark County to a landfill in Morrow County. Gilliam County joined the challengers. That local government alignment matters: the dispute was not a unitary Oregon community resisting outsiders, but a conflict among state regulators, local hosts, commercial facilities, and users of an interstate disposal market. [Supreme Court opinion, 511 U.S. 95-98](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/93/).

The legislation directed the Environmental Quality Commission to set a surcharge for solid waste generated outside Oregon based on otherwise uncompensated costs to Oregon and its subdivisions. The agency set it at $2.25 per ton. Oregon-origin waste faced a separate levy capped at $0.85. An easily missed procedural detail is that imports temporarily paid both the $2.25 surcharge and the $0.85 charge pending the litigation. The Court's comparison of $2.25 with $0.85 reflected the intended enduring structure and the particular surcharge under challenge, not a statement that imported waste's current total levy was only $2.25. The $0.85 charge was not itself challenged. The relevant provisions were Ore. Rev. Stat. §§ 459.297, 459.298, and 459A.110, and the implementing rule, renumbered during the controversy. [511 U.S. 96-97 & n.2](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/93/).

The asserted $2.25 costs were not invented from nothing. Rehnquist's dissent itemized $0.58 for statewide risk-reduction and management activities, $0.66 to reimburse tax credits and public subsidies, $0.05 for certification of reduction/recycling plans, $0.72 for environmental liability, $0.20 for lost disposal capacity, $0.03 for infrastructure, and $0.01 for transport nuisances. The problem was their relationship to the classification: the agency calculated disposal costs without reference to origin, and Oregon's economic consultant acknowledged that per-ton costs were the same for Oregon and imported waste. Oregon did not defend the surcharge on the ground that imports imposed higher costs or carried a distinctive health risk. [511 U.S. 101 n.5, 109 n.1](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/93/).

The Oregon Supreme Court had treated statutory cost linkage as sufficient to remove facial discrimination, applying the more accommodating framework for compensatory user fees. Its procedural jurisdiction under ORS 183.400 permitted facial review rather than resolution of factual disputes over the amount. It also resolved state-law legislative-veto and severability issues, excising committee approval provisions while preserving the agency's charge-setting authority. That history makes a record-development remand a serious alternative, but not necessarily the best one: Oregon's federal defense ultimately rested on general-tax compensation and resident cost spreading, not an untried claim of higher import-related costs. [*Gilliam County v. DEQ*, 316 Or. 99, 849 P.2d 500 (1993)](https://law.justia.com/cases/oregon/supreme-court/1993/316-or-99.html).

#### Historical result and competing approaches

The Supreme Court reversed and remanded, 7-2. Thomas wrote for Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg. Rehnquist dissented with Blackmun. There was no concurrence. The majority separated classification from justification. An origin-based difference in charges is discriminatory even if its intended purpose is cost recovery. Oregon therefore bore the demanding burden of showing a legitimate purpose unavailable through reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives. *Pike*'s ordinary incidental-burden balancing did not apply.

For a compensatory tax, the State had to identify the intrastate levy being offset; show that the interstate levy roughly approximated and did not exceed it; and establish substantially equivalent, mutually exclusive taxable events. Income taxation and waste disposal were not equivalent events. The surcharge even applied to Oregon businesses handling imported waste although they paid Oregon income tax. General taxes did not establish the asserted competitive equality. The majority also rejected reserving landfill capacity for residents through an import surcharge. It preserved genuine additional-cost distinctions, citing *Chemical Waste*, *Fort Gratiot*, and *Maine v. Taylor*. [511 U.S. 98-108](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/93/).

Rehnquist presented the strongest practical objection. Oregon citizens already financed a regulatory system and undertook recycling obligations. Requiring an identical disposal levy without accounting for those contributions could make imports free-ride on collective investment. He viewed a modest cost-based charge as incidental regulation of a scarce, publicly supported environmental resource. He also objected to language that seemed to prejudge differential pricing at government-owned facilities. His national landfill statistics and waste projections showed contemporary anxiety, not proof that each Oregon landfill faced an imminent capacity shortage. Nor did they establish that imported tons imposed different marginal costs. His prose's repeated $0.14-per-week illustration conflicts with his own footnote's $0.14-per-month calculation. No jurisprudential conclusion should rest on that arithmetic. [Rehnquist dissent, 511 U.S. 108-116](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/93/).

#### Stone baseline and initial prediction

The straightforward extension is invalidation. The inherited *Chemical Waste* entry expressly permits recovery of actual costs, risks, inspection, disposal, or environmental burdens while rejecting geographic origin as a proxy for a burden that identical local commerce avoids. *Fort Gratiot* supplies practical scrutiny of geographic barriers and blocks an answer based on local rather than state boundaries. *Kraft* and *Itel* establish that nondiscrimination does not confer immunity from ordinary taxes. *Boston Harbor* makes it inappropriate to call Oregon a market participant merely because government facilitates the disposal market through regulation.

This prediction is strong because the cases are close, not because Stone must be categorically hostile to state environmental programs. The file does not specify whether Stone accepts every element of the historical compensatory-tax test or whether he has considered general-revenue cross-subsidies. His philosophy therefore still needs an answer to Rehnquist's strongest point.

#### Reconsideration and serious alternatives

**Retain the anti-discrimination rule and invalidate.** This best fits the legal and factual record. A program can finance genuine public costs and still distribute those costs discriminatorily. Oregon could impose an origin-neutral per-ton levy for management, liability, infrastructure, and scarcity. It could assess demonstrable added inspection or transport burdens using the characteristics causing them. Border crossing can correlate with a cost without being the cost. The administration may use reasonable cost categories and estimates; the Constitution need not require measurement of every truck's exact incremental harm.

**Accept Rehnquist's fiscal-package approach.** This would treat local income taxes, fees, recycling obligations, and public investment as an implicit payment for disposal access, then allow a roughly compensating import assessment. The institutional problem it addresses is real: producing and maintaining regulatory capacity costs money before any particular transaction occurs. The legal difficulty is that a whole-budget accounting cannot identify who paid for what. Oregon-origin businesses can be nonresident-owned; local taxpayers can dispose of imported waste; nonresident generators also pay taxes and face recycling burdens elsewhere. The approach would authorize almost any border charge by pointing to some public expenditure supporting commerce. It is especially dangerous where the alleged offset taxes different people on different events. This is a defect of the mechanism, not a finding that the environmental purpose was a pretext.

**Remand for proof under a stricter compensation standard.** This would respect the facial-review posture and give Oregon a chance to establish actual equivalent burdens. The strongest version would preserve the invalidity of bare origin-based charges while requiring the agency to identify a discrete local counterpart. It is less justified where Oregon's asserted counterpart is general taxation, the taxable events plainly differ, and the State concedes equal disposal costs. A remand may resolve implementation or a newly lawful replacement charge, but should not imply that a better accountant can transform unrelated income taxes into a matched disposal fee.

**Adopt *Pike* or a de minimis safe harbor.** This would reduce judicial intrusion when the surcharge is economically small. But it supplies no principled constitutional threshold and requires judges to compare heterogeneous benefits and trade effects. A small tariff remains a tariff; a financially modest border penalty may be significant in competitive disposal markets. Conversely, a large neutral environmental charge is not necessarily unconstitutional. Amount is relevant to excessiveness and justification, not to whether the rule uses interstate origin to impose a disadvantage.

**Abandon judicial dormant-Commerce review.** Congress can authorize regional arrangements or national waste policy, and statutory solutions can account for political geography better than adjudication. But the case presents direct geographic discrimination of a sort already governed by longstanding precedent and Stone's inherited holdings. No new historical discovery, contrary empirical finding, or doctrinal breakdown demonstrated here warrants repudiating that entire legal structure. Congressional capacity to solve the problem does not establish that discriminatory interim rules are lawful.

#### Recommended position, change, and limits

Stone should vote to reverse and remand, invalidating this surcharge. He should join the core anti-discrimination and compensatory-tax reasoning, or write separately if the proposed majority refuses to reserve public-facility pricing. His reasoning should emphasize competitive equality in payment for the same regulated activity, with a demanding but workable opportunity to justify added costs. The purpose of the rule is to make legitimate environmental policy available while preventing a State from assigning a preferred tax price to locally originating commerce.

This is **clarification and doctrinal refinement**, not a vote change or abandonment of *Chemical Waste*. The new precision is that permissible cost recovery means identifying the cost-producing distinction or a genuinely equivalent local payment. A generalized claim that residents support the State does not suffice. The legal foundations are *Philadelphia v. New Jersey*, *Chemical Waste*, *Fort Gratiot*, *Henneford*, *Maryland v. Louisiana*, and *Armco*. The Court's primary opinion supplies and applies those authorities. No newly invented numerical threshold or motive test is necessary.

Stone should expressly reserve government-owned facilities and contractual purchases. The majority's footnote saying the charge would fail even as a user fee should be confined to a discriminatory sovereign levy of this kind. It need not decide whether an actual government proprietor may prefer its own taxpayers in selling its own services. The majority itself reserved market participation in footnote 9; Stone should prevent footnote 6 from obscuring that reservation. This respects *Boston Harbor* without automatically deciding *Carbone* or the future *United Haulers*.

The practical cost is that Oregon may have to increase visible charges on its own waste stream or finance environmental policy from a broader base. That is a political-accountability consequence, not a constitutional entitlement to inexpensive disposal. It may also face collection and monitoring costs in replacing an easy origin rule with a better cost proxy. Courts should acknowledge those costs and allow reasonable measurement, while refusing unrelated general-tax offsets.

#### Hindsight and downstream consequences

Subsequent experience cannot establish that Oregon's 1994 imports were harmless. The more defensible retrospective lesson is that integrated waste markets and environmental safeguards are distinct regulatory dimensions. Waste-flow economics can show that taxes alter disposal geography without establishing whether a particular change raises or lowers total social costs. Arik Levinson's 1999 study, *State Taxes and Interstate Hazardous Waste Shipments*, concerns hazardous waste and should not be mechanically generalized to the nonhazardous municipal stream at issue here. Its stated subject is the responsiveness of interstate shipments to disposal taxation. Even strong shipment responsiveness would prove neither that every tax is protectionist nor that an origin differential is necessary. [Original study citation](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.89.3.666).

The direct doctrinal path is to *Lohman* and *Fulton*, which require matched burdens rather than vague fiscal compensation. *Fulton* also explains why sophisticated economic-incidence assertions often cannot establish equivalence. A future public-facility exception remains possible because Oregon taxed private market transactions. It must remain tied to the government's actual operation, purchase, or provision of services. Calling all environmental regulation a publicly produced service would swallow the rule.

This decision does not imply a limit on Congress's affirmative authority to regulate interstate waste shipments, authorize an interstate arrangement, or establish national environmental standards. Nor does it expand anti-commandeering: invalidating a discriminatory state charge is enforcement of a constitutional limit, not conscription of a state legislature into administering a federal program. Direct connection to sovereign immunity is limited to remedy and forum questions not resolved here. Confidence is high on invalidity, medium on the boundaries of permissible differentiated cost recovery, and deliberately reserved on actual public-proprietor preferences.

### C & A Carbone, Inc. v. Town of Clarkstown, 511 U.S. 383 (1994)

#### Recommendation and source-status discipline

Stone should reverse the judgment enforcing Clarkstown's compulsory local-processing ordinance, but write separately to limit the rationale. The strongest basis is the town's use of sovereign compulsion to reserve a separable processing service in independently arranged private commerce to one designated local facility, including waste already sorted by another processor. The town's legitimate responsibility to arrange and finance safe disposal does not automatically authorize this particular restriction on where private customers purchase processing services. The opinion should preserve government procurement and leave a genuinely different public-service arrangement open. It should not declare that private title proves protectionism, that municipal title defeats it, that every monopoly is unconstitutional, or that flow control is economically unnecessary.

This is a moderate-confidence recommendation. Souter's case for affirmance is substantially stronger than the simulation's description suggests. The private contractor was building a facility for eventual municipal ownership; the town already guaranteed its income. A principled justice could accept Souter's classification of the enterprise as substantially municipal and uphold under deferential review. What is not defensible is mechanically treating the historical majority's word “private” as a complete answer while promising the opposite outcome for a formally public but operationally equivalent program.

The attached file contains recommended positions and roadmap entries, not a record of Stone-authored opinions or judicial votes. Its OT1993 entry already proposes invalidating *Carbone*. That is a proposal to test, not precedent requiring fidelity. Its later *SSC*, *United Haulers*, and public-facility positions cannot be imported into Stone's pre-OT1993 baseline. The recommendation below distinguishes refinement of that proposal from an actual reversal of an established Stone holding.

#### The actual regulatory problem

Clarkstown's existing municipal dump had repeatedly drawn environmental citations. An August 1989 consent decree with New York's Department of Environmental Conservation required closure and a replacement transfer station. At a transfer station, collection vehicles unload, recyclable material is separated, and the remainder is consolidated for shipment to disposal facilities. Clarkstown was trying to secure reliable, lawful disposal after closure of an environmentally deficient facility, acquire replacement infrastructure, and distribute its costs. The state appellate opinion identifies the consent decree, state permits, zoning amendments, and contract, rather than merely reciting a litigation claim that the ordinance promoted health. Its fuller account confirms a genuine public project. [*Town of Clarkstown v. C & A Carbone*, 182 A.D.2d 213, 215-221, 587 N.Y.S.2d 681 (1992)](https://www.elr.info/sites/default/files/litigation/23.20926.htm).

The project used a five-year build-operate-transfer arrangement. Clarkstown Recycling Center, Inc., a private contractor, would build and operate a station estimated to cost $1.4 million. The town guaranteed 120,000 tons annually, with an $81-per-ton tipping fee, and promised to cover a shortfall. After five years, the town could acquire the facility for $1. The guarantee matters: the town bore demand-related fiscal risk before acquiring formal title. The $81 financed more than an isolated sorting service; it helped secure the infrastructure and its availability. Multiplying guaranteed tonnage by the fee produces gross receipts, not profit or the cost of construction alone. The record does not support treating the entire fee as monopoly rent. [*Carbone*, 511 U.S. at 387, 411-412, 419 n.9](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511383/usrep511383.pdf).

Local Law 9 required acceptable nonhazardous waste generated or collected in the town to go to the Route 303 facility. Licensed recycling centers could continue operating, but had to deliver nonrecyclable residue to the designated station. Source-separated recyclables could be shipped elsewhere. Waste imported into Clarkstown was also subject to the arrangement. A separate zoning provision permitted only one transfer station. Violations could result in fines up to $1,000 and imprisonment up to fifteen days. This was legal compulsion affecting transactions between private generators, haulers, and processors, not just a direction to municipal employees or a term in a contract paid for by the town. [*Carbone*, 511 U.S. at 387-388, 395-401, ordinance §§2-7](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/383/).

Carbone itself operated a Clarkstown facility, charging approximately $70 per ton. It received waste from within and outside New York, separated recyclables, and shipped residue to out-of-state disposal facilities. Enforcement followed an accident involving one of its trucks and subsequent investigation of further shipments. The town sought an injunction because Carbone was bypassing the designated station. The ordinance required payment even on residue Carbone had already sorted, giving the case a particularly concrete private-market exclusion: additional patronage of the selected plant was compulsory regardless of whether the sorting function had already been performed. The two facilities' reported $11 price difference is not a reliable estimate of net social waste. Their permitted activities and service obligations differed, a point Souter raised. [District-court opinion, 770 F. Supp. 848, 850-851 (S.D.N.Y. 1991)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp/770/848/2129833/); [*Carbone*, 511 U.S. at 388, 424-425](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511383/usrep511383.pdf).

The beneficiaries were therefore several: the contractor received protected throughput and revenues; the town acquired a facility and reduced the likelihood of a contractual shortfall; residents received an available waste-management system; the town's taxpayers avoided some general-revenue financing. The immediate payers were haulers and processors, with possible pass-through to generators, including outsiders using Carbone's operation. Their exact incidence was not quantified. Outside processors lost the opportunity to substitute their service for the mandated local processing stage, but outside landfills could still receive the residual waste. Those are different markets. Souter's observation that garbage continued moving interstate does not answer the majority's concern about the foreclosed processing service; conversely, the existence of foreclosed processing opportunities does not prove aggregate economic injury or a fiscal burden primarily exported to nonresidents.

There were genuine externalities: unsafe disposal could harm health and the environment, and dependable reserve capacity might benefit generators who would otherwise use the public system only when private disposal became expensive. Yet the record does not establish that every bypass shipment created such a risk, that Carbone's destinations were less safe, or that forcing already sorted waste through Route 303 reduced a demonstrated cross-border harm. The consent decree establishes a governmental obligation, not by itself market failure in every available alternative. The public justification was genuine but incomplete as a justification for this instrument.

#### Historical litigation and competing opinions

The federal district court initially dismissed the antitrust claims under state-action principles but allowed the constitutional claim to proceed. Its preliminary injunction was limited to §5(a) insofar as the ordinance reached waste originating outside Clarkstown. The court assumed without deciding that locally generated waste could be regulated and noted the plaintiffs' then-concession regarding waste the town contracted to handle. The state trial court subsequently granted the town summary judgment; the Appellate Division affirmed, reasoning that the law imposed the same obligations regardless of the waste's origin and that financing the facility formed part of its health and safety purpose. The U.S. Supreme Court reviewed that state judgment, not the preliminary federal injunction. [770 F. Supp. at 851-855 & n.2](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp/770/848/2129833/); [182 A.D.2d at 218-225](https://www.elr.info/sites/default/files/litigation/23.20926.htm).

On May 16, 1994, the Supreme Court reversed and remanded, 6-3 in disposition. Kennedy wrote for five Justices: Stevens, Scalia, Thomas, Ginsburg, and himself. O'Connor concurred in the judgment. Souter dissented, joined by Rehnquist and Blackmun. Kennedy treated the ordinance as discriminatory local processing, citing *Dean Milk*, *Fort Gratiot*, *Pike*, and *South-Central Timber*. Giving the preference to one local enterprise rather than all local firms did not cure the exclusion. The majority applied demanding scrutiny, rejected revenue generation as sufficient justification, and identified nondiscriminatory safety rules, general taxation, and municipal bonds as alternatives. It did not undertake ordinary *Pike* balancing. [*Carbone*, 511 U.S. at 383-395](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511383/usrep511383.pdf).

O'Connor disagreed on discrimination. Local competitors, including Carbone, were excluded along with interstate rivals, leaving substantial local political opposition to excessive regulation. She nevertheless found the restriction excessive under *Pike*. Her reasoning emphasized the availability of alternative financing, closure of the processing market, the cumulative effect of similar laws, and possible conflicts between Clarkstown's routing requirement and New Jersey's requirement that recycling residue return there. She also examined and rejected purported congressional authorization under the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act. [Id. at 401-410](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511383/usrep511383.pdf).

Souter distinguished a municipal service monopoly from discrimination among private firms according to geography. The facility was substantially municipal because of its contractual role and impending transfer, and local waste generators largely funded the service themselves. He disputed proof of injury to out-of-state processors and the suggestion that courts should constitutionalize a preferred competitive structure. He accepted review for practical discrimination and unjustified economic isolation, but rejected an open-ended judicial comparison of ordinary regulatory costs and benefits. He also challenged O'Connor's actual-conflict claim: Carbone had not attempted to return waste to New Jersey, and local processing could in principle precede return. [Id. at 410-430, especially 419-427 & n.16](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511383/usrep511383.pdf).

These opinions disagree at the classification stage, before balancing. Is the protected comparator a local commercial facility, or the government itself undertaking a service all private suppliers are excluded from providing independently? The result cannot be settled by saying that everyone supports safe waste disposal or dislikes protectionism.

#### Stone's available baseline and the initial prediction

The simulation's *Chemical Waste* baseline rejects origin as a substitute for actual cost or risk. *Fort Gratiot* rejects redrawing the protected unit as a county to evade interstate nondiscrimination. Neither establishes that every exclusive municipal service concession is geographically discriminatory. They provide a reason to distrust local boundary rules, not a complete answer to Souter's comparator argument.

Stone's OT1992 *Boston Harbor* position is unusually important. It expressly distinguishes managing the government's own transaction from coercing outsiders and independent transactions, and says that the word “contract” cannot immunize regulation. Historically, *Boston Harbor* was an NLRA preemption case, so its precise holding is not a dormant-Commerce exemption. Its functional distinction is nevertheless supported in Commerce doctrine by *Reeves*, *White*, and *South-Central Timber*. Municipal purchasing can select services without a free-standing obligation to maintain an open private market; spending or ownership in one transaction does not automatically permit regulation of unrelated ones. [*Boston Harbor*, 507 U.S. 218, 227-233 (1993)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/507/218/); [*South-Central Timber*, 467 U.S. 82, 95-99 (1984), plurality analysis](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/467/82).

Stone's *Fabe* planning entry reinforces attention to what a provision does rather than its statutory or institutional label. His *New York* baseline supplies concern with political responsibility for policy, but anti-commandeering is not the legal rule here: no federal government is requiring Clarkstown to administer a federal program, and private haulers do not possess a Tenth Amendment immunity against municipal regulation. His state-tax positions also reject the proposition that interstate firms have an entitlement to cost-free public services. The legitimate fiscal burden should not disappear merely because the particular means of collecting it is invalid.

The natural initial prediction is invalidation. *Fort Gratiot* makes geographic line-drawing suspect, and *Boston Harbor* distinguishes the town's contractual financing obligation from coercion of unrelated private waste transactions. But this prediction must be qualified. The town's public undertaking and assumption of shortfall risk make the case closer to procurement than the file's description of a “favored private local enterprise” reveals. The source does not establish how Stone would resolve an integrated public concession in this form. Any claim that his prior reasoning mechanically decides that question would overread it.

#### What the initial position gets wrong if stated too broadly

First, a private contractor is not necessarily the substantive beneficiary in the relevant constitutional sense. Clarkstown was acquiring public infrastructure, not merely sheltering a resident business from competition. Souter noted that the record did not reveal whether local or out-of-state investors owned the contractor. Corporate ownership and plant location must not be conflated. A municipal agent can be privately incorporated, and a publicly owned enterprise can protect local jobs or suppliers. [*Carbone*, 511 U.S. at 418 n.7, 419-421](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511383/usrep511383.pdf).

Second, state-action antitrust immunity and Commerce Clause validity are distinct. The district court's antitrust dismissal shows why “anticompetitive” is an insufficient constitutional conclusion: state law may validly displace competition for public reasons. But state authorization of a monopoly does not equal congressional authorization of interstate discrimination. Stone should neither re-create federal antitrust policy under the Commerce Clause nor let antitrust immunity erase independent constitutional restrictions. [770 F. Supp. at 851-853](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp/770/848/2129833/).

Third, general taxation is not a costless functional substitute. It changes incidence, may conceal waste-management costs, and weakens the marginal incentive to reduce waste. Municipal borrowing faces legal and fiscal constraints; revenue bonds still need revenues. Purely competitive tipping charges may not fund education, household-hazardous-waste collection, or standby capacity. The relevant question is whether legitimate costs can be financed without the discriminatory processing condition, not whether a court imagines a cheaper government.

Fourth, local political opposition is evidence against protectionism, not a conclusive safe harbor. A concentrated contractor or incumbent coalition can defeat diffuse consumers even when most immediate costs remain local. That is an institutional possibility, not a finding of capture in Clarkstown. More fundamentally, *Fort Gratiot* and *Dean Milk* show that harming some local interests does not necessarily cure a geographically selective restriction. The converse matters too: ordinary health regulation is not unconstitutional simply because a local firm benefits from it.

Finally, the majority's statement about a town lacking power to direct waste away from environmentally harmful out-of-town sites should not become a sweeping rule against every regulation with effects beyond a border. The defect here can be resolved through discrimination and fit. A jurisdiction may have legitimate reasons to require evidence of safe disposal of waste it generates or purchases services to handle. One need not adjudicate every possible territorial issue to reject a mandatory commercial detour to finance Route 303.

#### Serious alternatives

| Approach | Legal attraction | Main cost or weakness | Implication for Stone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adopt Kennedy's broad discrimination rationale | Strong local-processing precedents; clear ex ante rule against forced local patronage | Risks equating monopoly with protectionism and understating municipal substance | Natural initial extension, but needs limiting explanation |
| Adopt O'Connor's *Pike* invalidation | Acknowledges equal treatment of private competitors and recognizes structural incompatibilities | Alternatives analysis may become judicial fiscal optimization; actual interstate conflict contested | Attractive only with a concrete structural burden, not the $11 price difference alone |
| Follow Souter and affirm | Respects genuine municipal responsibility, local cost-bearing, and freedom to organize services | Public sponsorship can become a manipulable exception to local-processing rules | Legally serious change in application, not abandonment of anti-protectionism |
| Vacate for fuller functional and burden findings | Avoids assuming private title or alleged burdens answer the question | Additional proceedings may merely postpone a legal choice on substantially undisputed mechanics | Appropriate if Stone makes public-service status dispositive, less necessary under processing precedents |
| Reverse on a narrowly specified local-processing rule | Preserves established nondiscrimination and procurement boundaries without announcing all public routing invalid | Leaves difficult concessions and municipal ownership questions unresolved | Recommended, with express reservations and moderate confidence |
| Leave correction entirely to Congress | Avoids judicial economic policymaking and recognizes affirmative Commerce authority | Requires abandoning or sharply cutting back precedents Stone has accepted without a new historical showing here | Disproportionate doctrinal change on this record |

A further alternative, suggested by later lower-court experience, is to examine competition for the exclusive concession rather than competition within its term. A municipality might select one operator through a genuinely open process, admitting bids proposing equivalent out-of-state service. This can be a more faithful nondiscrimination inquiry than asking whether the winning company's incorporation papers are private. But an opportunity for an out-of-state corporation to own the mandated local plant is not necessarily equivalent to an opportunity for an out-of-state plant to sell the service. Location restrictions in specifications can preserve the same exclusion. The Clarkstown record reviewed here does not supply enough about bidding or feasible off-site proposals to declare its selection neutral.

That distinction subsequently mattered in *Harvey & Harvey* and *Houlton Citizens' Coalition*. The First Circuit declined to treat every exclusive arrangement as invalid and emphasized genuinely equal opportunities for in-state and out-of-state bidders. It also acknowledged that compelled private transactions differ from government-funded service contracts. These are later doctrinal alternatives, not rules Stone already had in 1994. [*Houlton Citizens' Coalition v. Town of Houlton*, 175 F.3d 178, 186-190 (1st Cir. 1999)](https://www.elr.info/sites/default/files/litigation/29.21086.htm).

#### Recommended reasoning and disposition

Stone should concur in the judgment of reversal and remand, stating the following limited rule in ordinary doctrinal terms: when government leaves disposal transactions in private commerce but compels their processing at one favored local commercial facility before they may continue elsewhere, it imposes a local-processing restriction subject to the established demanding discrimination inquiry. A municipal financing contract and future acquisition right are relevant to purpose and remedy but do not, standing alone, transform every compelled transaction into the government's own purchase.

The comparison is between the designated local processing service and otherwise eligible out-of-town substitutes. It is not between favored shareholders' domiciles, and it is not between all local firms collectively and all outsiders collectively. The town's regulation guarantees that the indispensable intermediate commercial step occurs locally at its designated station. No showing establishes that this compulsory step, including on already sorted residue, is required to accomplish a distinct health function unavailable through nondiscriminatory requirements. Raising necessary revenue is legitimate; reserving that service geographically is not justified merely because it generates the revenue efficiently. The existing local-processing cases provide the rule. [*Dean Milk*, 340 U.S. 349, 354-356 (1951)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/340/349); [*Carbone*, 511 U.S. at 391-394](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/383/).

This opinion should expressly acknowledge the public concession. Even assuming the town is a substantial proprietor in the facility, the challenged command reaches a rival recycler's independently arranged interstate business. Procurement status must be tested against the challenged action, not simply the government's economic interest in the market. Otherwise a municipality could acquire a small stake or guarantee a private venture's debt and invoke that stake to force unrelated purchasers into it. *Boston Harbor* gives Stone a particularly direct reason to reject that move. Conversely, the opinion should not say that every publicly provided waste service is equivalent to this arrangement. Municipal collection and purchased disposal, a properly structured public service charge, and a different public authority's integrated operations must be assessed on their own facts.

Stone should decline to join two broader propositions: that all public waste routing is necessarily unconstitutional, and that title determines which flow-control laws survive. A future distinction must turn on operational responsibility, control, service obligations, funding and risk, the private transactions actually displaced, and the particular claim of geographic exclusion. Those factors guide classification; they should not become a newly invented multiprong constitutional test announced as settled law. In OT1993 the holding can remain within existing local-processing and proprietor/regulator doctrine.

O'Connor's congressional-authorization discussion should be adopted. RCRA's support for state planning and long-term supply contracts does not unmistakably authorize otherwise unconstitutional discrimination. A committee's discussion of flow control is relevant historical context but cannot substitute for a sufficiently clear congressional decision to displace the usual constitutional presumption. Congress retains authority to authorize appropriate flow arrangements or create regional systems. Its general encouragement of waste planning does not decide this case. [*Carbone*, 511 U.S. at 407-410 (O'Connor, J.); 42 U.S.C. §§6941, 6943(a)(5), 6948(d)(3), reproduced and analyzed there](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511383/usrep511383.pdf).

Relief should remove the injunction enforcing the invalid processing mandate and remand for state-law severability and any remaining issues. The ordinance's independent licensing, unsafe-disposal, and waste-handling provisions need not all fall. The Court should not decree a tax rate, select a replacement contractor, cancel the consent decree, extinguish contractual obligations, or award damages without the separate legal inquiries those actions require. The history of the federal action also warns against assuming the constitutional merits automatically settle forum, cause of action, damages, or antitrust issues.

#### Practical evidence and its limits

EPA's March 1995 *Report to Congress on Flow Control and Municipal Solid Waste* is the most directly relevant systematic governmental evidence. It is retrospective for Stone's May 1994 decision. It reviewed state authority, held stakeholder meetings, assembled market information, and used case studies. It did not perform a randomized evaluation or a causal before-and-after study of *Carbone*. Its national findings must not be presented as adjudicative findings about Clarkstown.

EPA found that flow control could efficiently support financing and integrated services but was not essential nationally to development of waste-management capacity or achievement of recycling goals. Its market analysis differentiated sectors: dependence was greatest in waste-to-energy, much smaller in recycling overall, and limited in landfill development. The report found declining landfill numbers did not necessarily mean declining total capacity because smaller facilities were replaced by larger regional operations. That qualifies the simple 1994 narrative of inevitable nationwide capacity scarcity, while leaving genuine local scarcity and financing constraints possible. [EPA, *Executive Summary*, ES-1-ES-3, ES-6-ES-12](https://archive.epa.gov/epawaste/nonhaz/municipal/web/pdf/execsum.pdf); [EPA, *Market Analysis*, III-79-III-83](https://archive.epa.gov/epawaste/nonhaz/municipal/web/pdf/chpiii-2.pdf).

EPA identified operational alternatives: municipal collection, purchased collection with destination terms, franchises, and special-purpose districts. Financing alternatives included general taxes, uniform service fees, unit-based fees, and market tipping charges. It also specified trade-offs. Taxes can be stable and administratively manageable but conceal service costs and do not price waste generation. Uniform fees can support debt and ancillary services but dilute marginal incentives. Unit fees relate payment to disposal but can reduce the very revenues needed for fixed obligations and impose administrative demands. Market prices alone may fail to finance the desired package. [EPA, *Market Analysis*, III-79-III-83](https://archive.epa.gov/epawaste/nonhaz/municipal/web/pdf/chpiii-2.pdf).

This evidence supports reconsideration of reasoning, not automatic reversal of result. It rebuts both the claim that flow control is inherently needless and the claim that no adequate waste system can exist without it. It strengthens Stone's obligation to recognize fiscal and administrative costs while limiting constitutional intervention to the legally objectionable mechanism. It does not establish that Clarkstown had sufficient unused tax capacity, what refinancing would have cost, whether its selected plant was optimally sized, or what actual adjustment occurred there after judgment.

The public-choice explanation must remain similarly disciplined. The designated contractor had a concentrated interest in maintaining throughput; dispersed residents had mixed interests as taxpayers, service users, and waste generators. Those facts make capture a plausible risk and also explain ordinary infrastructure finance. They do not prove corrupt motive, excessive profits, or a sham environmental objective. Anti-discrimination rules can avoid intrusive inquiries into motive, but the institutional reason for a rule is not evidence that misconduct occurred in this particular town.

#### Hindsight, doctrinal development, and costs of the recommendation

The immediate doctrinal path is clearest in *SSC Corp. v. Town of Smithtown*, 66 F.3d 502 (2d Cir. 1995). The court invalidated a criminally enforceable flow-control ordinance but upheld terms governing services the town itself purchased. Smithtown had a substantial fiscal stake in a privately owned incinerator, yet that did not convert its regulatory command into market participation. This supports the action-specific proprietor/regulator distinction. It also shows that invalidating general compulsion need not eliminate municipal use of designated facilities. It is later experience, not an OT1993 precedent. [*SSC*, 66 F.3d at 510-518](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-2nd-circuit/1034732.html).

The most difficult later test is *United Haulers Ass'n v. Oneida-Herkimer Solid Waste Management Authority*, 550 U.S. 330 (2007). The Court upheld routing to publicly owned facilities, distinguishing a public authority from private market participants; the four-Justice *Pike* discussion credited integrated services, recycling incentives, and enforcement. Scalia accepted the discrimination distinction without *Pike* balancing, and Thomas would abandon dormant-Commerce doctrine. Alito, joined by Stevens and Kennedy, argued that Clarkstown had already been functionally municipal and that formal passage of title could not justify the opposite result. That objection is directly relevant to the simulation's future roadmap. [*United Haulers*, 550 U.S. at 342-347, 348-355, 356-371](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/550/330/).

Stone should use that history as a warning against writing an ownership switch into *Carbone*. He could later uphold an integrated public authority if actual service responsibilities, its relationship to the relevant private market, and the record justify a distinction. But he must explain why Clarkstown's existing fiscal risk and future public ownership were insufficient. If the only material difference proves to be the date legal title passes, he should either apply *Carbone* to both or candidly reconsider *Carbone*'s classification. It would be dishonest to preserve both outcomes merely by labeling one facility “private.” The source's future public-facility disposition should remain provisional.

The later circuit focus on fair competition for contracts introduces another possible refinement: a nondiscriminatory concession is not automatically protectionist because one firm wins it. This distinction would matter whether the winner was a local family business, a national corporation, a worker cooperative, or a public authority. It would fail its own test if equally suitable out-of-state processing proposals were excluded because they were outside. Procurement conditions genuinely tied to the location of a municipal project remain different from insisting that independently purchased, mobile services occur locally.

*National Pork Producers Council v. Ross*, 598 U.S. 356 (2023), supplies a more distant but useful test. A nondiscriminatory condition on products sold in a state is not automatically invalid because compliance affects production elsewhere. Carbone's operative defect can be compulsory local processing that excludes out-of-state substitutes; it need not become a general extraterritorial-effects rule. That distinction protects the possibility of nondiscriminatory environmental regulation even when political valence changes. The later Court rejected the proposed almost-per-se extraterritorial rule; its *Pike* discussion was fractured and should not be reported as abolishing *Pike*. [*National Pork*, Parts II-III and the separate opinions](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/598/21-468/).

The recommended approach imposes real costs: possible stranded contractual commitments; greater dependence on taxes or fees; more administrative effort; litigation about public concessions and market boundaries; and uncertainty for municipalities arranging infrastructure. Those costs counsel clear, narrow reasoning and careful relief, not automatic constitutional validation. Conversely, affirmance would reduce financing risk but create the risk that sponsorship, revenue guarantees, and nominal future acquisition become easy devices for excluding rival processing services. Neither side can honestly claim its choice produces only benefits.

There is no direct consequence for the scope of Congress's affirmative Commerce power or anti-commandeering merely because Stone invalidates this local restriction. Congress's power over interstate waste markets remains a separate inquiry. Nor does municipal public status for this purpose establish Eleventh Amendment immunity. Any such downstream link would require independent constitutional and entity analysis, precisely the separation Stone's baseline favors.

#### Degree of change and remaining uncertainty

Relative to Stone's end-OT1992 resources, this is an application and clarification, not a repudiation: *Fort Gratiot*'s resistance to geographically partitioned markets and *Boston Harbor*'s transaction-specific distinction remain. Relative to the attachment's *Carbone* proposal, it narrows and corrects the explanation. The prior shorthand understated the public concession; the correction is warranted by the contract, town risk, consent decree, and later *United Haulers* disagreement, not by a desire to obtain a preferred future result.

The principle retained is a strong presumption against compulsory local processing in private interstate commerce. The proposition rejected is that the private or public label alone identifies the constitutional injury. The question reserved is when a genuinely public service arrangement makes government and private processors materially different comparators. The unresolved tension is that a functional inquiry can itself become uncertain and manipulable. Clear transaction boundaries help, but no existing baseline case eliminates all difficult concessions.

Confidence is high that the ordinance regulated independent transactions, that Clarkstown had a substantial public responsibility and fiscal interest, that the historical opinions divided over the correct comparator, and that RCRA did not unmistakably authorize the challenged restriction. Confidence is moderate that the narrow discrimination rationale is stronger than Souter's affirmance, because municipal service classification remains a real disagreement about constitutional structure. Confidence is low about the magnitude of competitive harm, actual contractor rent, the cost of Clarkstown's financing alternatives, or the causal effect of the decision on later waste-market outcomes. No conclusion here depends on pretending those missing facts are known.

### Associated Industries of Missouri v. Lohman, 511 U.S. 641 (1994)

#### Actual dispute and tax design

Missouri's legitimate problem was unequal sales and use taxation. A consumer could purchase outside Missouri, use the item inside Missouri, and avoid local sales taxes borne by an equivalent local purchase. Compensating use taxes ordinarily remove that distortion. The defect was how Missouri pursued parity: it imposed one statewide additional use-tax rate while leaving local sales-tax rates variable.

The State's ordinary sales and use taxes each totaled 4.225%; those matched statewide taxes were not challenged. The challenged addition was a 1.5% use tax under Mo. Rev. Stat. § 144.748, with an exemption for goods subject to the Missouri sales tax incorporated from § 144.615(2). Local sales taxes ranged from 0.5% to 3.5%, with at least one county imposing none. Thus a purchase used in a locality with a 0.5% local sales tax faced 4.725% total tax if purchased locally, but 5.725% if purchased outside the State. This example illustrates the statutory arithmetic; it is not a claim about a particular litigant's transaction. [511 U.S. 643-646](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/641/).

The challengers included Associated Industries, representing local and nonlocal businesses, and Alumax Foils, a Missouri manufacturer buying out-of-state inputs. Sellers required to collect the additional use tax and purchasers paying it were therefore not all outsiders. Local consumers and manufacturers could lose from protection of local sellers. The Court's concern was the competitive channel chosen for a transaction, not merely the domicile of the taxpayer.

The stipulated record showed that the additional use-tax rate exceeded local sales-tax rates in 53.5% of local taxing jurisdictions. The Missouri majority reported that 93.14% of local sales volume occurred where the local tax equaled or exceeded the addition, and 6.86% where it fell below the addition; it also emphasized higher statewide aggregate sales-tax burdens. Its separate disaggregation is arithmetically inconsistent, so these are reported aggregate figures rather than independently verified statistics. The U.S. opinion described the over-93% figure more loosely in terms of jurisdictions where the local rate exceeded the use tax. None of these figures establishes that only 6.86% of interstate purchases encountered the disadvantage. Chief Justice Robertson's state dissent correctly identified the missing denominator: the distribution of out-of-state purchases, not the distribution of local sales, would be needed to estimate how much interstate commerce was burdened. The affected jurisdictions contained approximately 26% of the State's population according to that dissent's account of the record. [Missouri Supreme Court majority and dissent, 857 S.W.2d 182, 185, 195-196](https://law.justia.com/cases/missouri/supreme-court/1993/75089-0.html).

#### Historical result

The Supreme Court reversed and remanded unanimously in result. Thomas wrote for eight Justices; Blackmun concurred in the judgment without a separate explanation. The Court held that a tax disadvantage in one locality is not compensated by advantages to different transactions elsewhere. *Fort Gratiot* foreclosed treating decentralized discrimination as constitutionally harmless. Equality was required in the relevant comparison, although a State remained free to tax interstate purchases less heavily. The compensating levies did not have to be enacted in the same statutory provision or by the same level of government. The proper inquiry was practical operation. [511 U.S. 646-655](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/641/).

The Court nevertheless rejected the challengers' demand to invalidate the tax everywhere under federal law. Where the local sales tax equaled or exceeded 1.5%, the challenged addition did not impose the identified competitive disadvantage. A possibility that those places might later lower their sales taxes did not establish present discrimination. The Court left remedy, predeprivation procedures, refunds, and other ways to achieve constitutional equality for the disputed period to the state courts under *McKesson*. It did not order a full refund to everyone paying the additional tax. [511 U.S. 654-657](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/641/).

#### Stone baseline and initial prediction

A natural extension of *Chemical Waste*, *Fort Gratiot*, *Quill*, and *Kraft* is to reject the surcharge to the extent it disadvantages interstate purchases. *Itel* supplies the other half: commerce must pay a lawful share of ordinary taxation. The inherited material does not support invalidating a compensating use tax merely because it is collected from an out-of-state purchase. The possibility of an initial overly broad invalidation is nevertheless real because the file's anti-origin language is compressed and the challenged tax expressly distinguishes goods subject to local sales tax from others.

*Fabe*'s functional classification method matters as an analogy, not as a new source of dormant-Commerce law. If provisions should be understood by what they actually do, then the court must identify which applications confer a local competitive preference rather than treating every provision bearing a geographic label alike. Stone's *PRASA* and *United States v. California* entries also caution against collapsing a substantive constitutional issue into a remedial entitlement. None establishes a Stone-specific rule on tax refunds; *McKesson* is the governing external authority.

#### The problem exposed by mechanical anti-discrimination rhetoric

The simplistic statement that geographic distinction equals unconstitutional discrimination is overinclusive here. Sales and use taxes must distinguish whether sales tax has already been paid. Their complementarity is the reason the distinction can produce equality. The opposite simplification, that a fiscal system is valid whenever interstate commerce as a whole pays no more, is underinclusive. It permits a State to give an import disadvantage to every local business in one region while using unrelated tax preferences elsewhere as an offset.

The remedy is to specify the comparator, not abandon nondiscrimination. The relevant question is whether equivalent goods acquired for equivalent use in the taxing locality bear a greater compulsory burden because the purchase is interstate. Comparator selection is not trivial. A purchaser might travel to a lower-tax locality within Missouri, and sourcing rules determine whether destination or point-of-sale comparisons are appropriate. The Court did not require a State to eliminate every tax difference among localities or decide every cross-local sourcing problem. Stone should use the case's presented pair of local and interstate acquisitions without constitutionalizing one universal sales-tax sourcing system.

This brings an administrability distinction into focus. Comparing stated rates and bases for genuinely corresponding sales and uses, even across hundreds of jurisdictions, is laborious but bounded. Comparing income taxes, payroll burdens, environmental expenses, property values, and public benefits across all state economic activity is conceptually indeterminate. The number of legal provisions is not itself the measure of whether an inquiry is judicially manageable. *Lohman* expressly makes that distinction in footnote 5. [511 U.S. 655-656 n.5](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/641/).

#### Alternatives and recommended position

**Statewide averaging** preserves a simple collection rule and local sales-tax autonomy. It may make the average imported purchase less taxed. But it cannot show that a disfavored purchaser received any offset, and it provides an easy architecture for creating local preferential trade areas. The State has delegated rate-setting, not a constitutional exemption.

**Invalidate the additional tax statewide as facially discriminatory** would provide a simple remedy and avoid monitoring changing local rates. It would also confer a windfall on purchases with no demonstrated tax disadvantage and constitutionalize statutory form. Neither the Constitution nor Stone's general hostility to protectionism requires that overcorrection.

**Apply *Pike* or forgive small disparities** responds to administrative costs, but again requires an invented tolerance for deliberate rate differences. Missouri could have matched local use and sales taxes or capped the additional use tax at the local counterpart. The record noted that numerous States already paired local taxes successfully. The better way to accommodate administrative limits is to allow state choices among parity-preserving systems and to leave severability and remedy open.

**Use statutory construction to impose the lesser of 1.5% and the local sales rate** is an attractive policy solution but is not automatically a court's authority. Federal constitutional invalidity does not delegate tax legislation to either the federal court or the revenue director. Whether the statute can support that construction belongs initially to Missouri law. Stone should identify permissible endpoints, not rewrite the statute.

Stone should therefore **join the judgment and principal reasoning**, reversing and remanding. The holding should bar applications that charge equivalent interstate acquisitions more than local ones, reject statewide averaging, and decline to hold lawful applications unconstitutional solely because future discrimination is possible. He should expressly preserve state-law severability, refund procedures, and the legislature's choice of how to achieve equality. A separate concurrence is useful only if a majority opinion obscures those limits; historical Thomas's opinion largely supplies them.

The jurisprudential change is **refinement**. Stone would retain the anti-protectionism principle, add a concrete comparator rule, and clarify that coordinated tax provisions must be integrated whether integration reveals discrimination or defeats an artificial claim of it. That last point is especially important alongside *West Lynn Creamery*. Functional integration cannot become an exclusively claimant-friendly technique. The same methodological discipline must let the State demonstrate genuine compensation.

#### Legal and practical foundations; strongest objections

*Henneford v. Silas Mason*, *Halliburton*, *Gregg Dyeing*, *Maryland v. Louisiana*, and *Fort Gratiot* provide the necessary authority. *General American Tank Car v. Day* offered the State some textual support for approximate aggregate equality, but its equal-protection discussion did not establish the modern dormant-Commerce rule the State proposed. Stone should candidly explain that its possible Commerce reading had been displaced by later cases instead of pretending all precedents always spoke with one voice. No need exists to overrule the entire earlier decision, which involved a different tax and competing constitutional claims. [511 U.S. 650-653](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/641/).

Missouri's best objection is that a flexible local sales-tax system plus a uniform use tax cannot be maintained without either local matching or a lower common ceiling. That is true. The constitutional rule removes one convenient design. But ease of collection does not identify why the specific interstate purchaser should bear extra tax. State legislators can choose a matching system, a lower statewide addition, an equivalent statewide sales-tax addition, or other neutral revenue sources. Those alternatives are policy possibilities; this analysis does not claim each could have been implemented immediately without state constitutional, statutory, or voter-approval constraints.

The remedy has its own limits. A litigant who shows discrimination does not necessarily obtain immunity from every lawful tax on the transaction. Conversely, a court cannot leave past unlawfully collected taxes unremedied merely to protect revenue if the State required payment before a meaningful challenge. That framework comes from *McKesson*, not a new Stone remedial theory. Reliance and orderly administration influence lawful remedial selection, but do not retrospectively make discriminatory liability constitutional.

#### Hindsight, actual adaptation, and downstream implications

The 1996 proceedings provide unusually useful evidence. The Missouri Supreme Court held that state severability and legislative intent required invalidating the entire additional use tax, even though the U.S. Constitution did not require every application to fail. The legislature had earlier adopted a local matching provision, never implemented it, and replaced it with the uniform statewide addition partly for administrative simplicity. The court concluded that preserving only some geographic applications would create the sort of patchwork the legislature had rejected. It declined to rewrite the law. [*Associated Industries v. Director*, 918 S.W.2d 780, 783-785 (Mo. 1996)](https://law.justia.com/cases/missouri/supreme-court/1996/77885-0.html).

This later result does not undermine the 1994 federal holding. It demonstrates why a federal remedial reservation was substantive federalism, not procrastination. Narrow federal invalidity preserves the State's authority to decide whether the remaining statute represents its legislature's choices. The practical cost may be greater than the federal court initially appears to require. Stone should not conceal that cost or respond by dictating a saving construction without state-law authority.

*Fulton* directly extends matched-burden scrutiny to an asserted corporate-income/intangibles-tax compensation scheme. It shows why the relevant incidence may differ from the named taxpayer. That does not contradict *United States v. California*: intergovernmental immunity asks whether a tax legally falls on the United States, while a compensatory-tax defense asks whether actual competing classes bear equivalent burdens. Different legal questions warrant different uses of incidence. [*Fulton*, 516 U.S. 338-344](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/516/325/).

*Wynne* later makes structural nondiscrimination visible through internal consistency, but Stone need not anticipate its entire income-tax rule here. The connection is direct at the level of identifying a border-caused burden, less direct at the level of how sovereign residence and source claims must be credited. *Wayfair* later removes *Quill*'s physical-presence collection rule while emphasizing legitimate state revenues and practical compliance concerns. *Lohman*'s parity rule remains compatible with that development: authority to collect a use tax and constitutionality of the tax's discriminatory rate structure are separate questions. [*Wynne*, 575 U.S. 542](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/575/542/); [*Wayfair*, 585 U.S. 162](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/585/17-494/).

Confidence is high on the proposed disposition and locality-specific comparison, medium on unpresented sourcing and administrative edge cases. There is no direct implication for affirmative congressional Commerce power, anti-commandeering, or sovereign immunity beyond retaining the distinction among duty, forum, and remedy.

### West Lynn Creamery, Inc. v. Healy, 512 U.S. 186 (1994): Stone case study

#### Recommendation and source discipline

Stone should vote to reverse the Massachusetts judgment and invalidate the challenged integrated financing order, but write a separate concurrence in the judgment. His opinion should identify a commodity assessment legally coupled to exclusive support of competing in-state production. It should reject a general constitutional entitlement to the economically cheapest producer, avoid treating inferred tax incidence as an established fact, and preserve ordinary neutral taxation and ordinary state appropriations. This is a refinement of the supplied OT1993 proposal and an application of the pre-OT1993 anti-discrimination baseline, not a reversal of an earlier adjudicated Stone holding.

The simulation file is a planning document, not a collection of signed opinions. It supplies Stone's proposed West Lynn disposition and short rationale, but no vote tally, actual authorship, joining coalition, separate opinion, or evidentiary findings. Its post-OT1993 roadmap cannot establish what Stone had already decided. The analysis below distinguishes express source positions, inferences from the earlier entries, and recommendations.

#### The regulatory problem and mechanism

Massachusetts was trying to arrest dairy-farm exit during a period of declining farm milk prices and high regional production costs. The federal milk marketing system already regulated minimum prices. It pooled receipts from milk used in different product classes into a producer blend price, while imposing different payment obligations on handlers according to their sales mix. Fluid drinking milk occupied the highest-priced class. Massachusetts added a state support program rather than replacing that federal system. The federal scheme allowed above-minimum prices; that permission did not itself authorize geographically discriminatory state financing. See [West Lynn, 512 U.S. at 188-191 and notes 1-8](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/186/).

The operative February 26, 1992 order assessed dealers on fluid milk sold for Massachusetts consumption without regard to origin. Its monthly assessment per hundredweight was one-third of the difference between $15 and the monthly federal blend price. Receipts went to the Massachusetts Dairy Equalization Fund and were distributed to Massachusetts producers according to their share of qualifying production. The program capped qualifying production at 200,000 pounds per producer and payments at a $15 total price per hundredweight; surplus receipts were returned to dealers. The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court upheld the scheme because dealer obligations were evenhanded and the producer subsidy's interstate effects, in its view, were incidental. See [415 Mass. 8, 11-19 (1993), including notes 8-10](https://law.justia.com/cases/massachusetts/supreme-court/volumes/415/415mass8.html).

The division by three matters. A $3 support-price gap did not mean a $3 dealer assessment. For illustration, if the blend price were $12, the levy would be $1 per hundredweight of covered fluid sales. With Massachusetts production approximately one-third of the assessed sales volume, those receipts could finance approximately $3 of support per hundredweight of eligible Massachusetts output, subject to the program's caps and refunds. This is an arithmetic illustration, not an estimate of the actual equilibrium price or the actual incidence of the program.

The legal taxpayer was the dealer. The subsidy recipient was the farmer. An out-of-state farmer did not write a check to Massachusetts merely by producing milk. Nor did the order legally exempt local farmers from a producer tax: there was no such producer tax. The simulation's statement that the program shifted the burden away from local producers and left interstate competitors bearing it compresses several economic propositions into one sentence. Stone should unpack them.

The assessment increased a covered dealer's marginal cost regardless of where the milk originated. The production payment improved eligible Massachusetts farmers' returns and capacity to remain in production. Who ultimately absorbed the assessment depended on bargaining, demand and supply responses, existing price floors, processing and retail margins, and the time horizon. Consumers could bear much of it. Out-of-state farmers could lose sales or future market opportunities even without receiving lower prices. The constitutional argument must concern the program's discriminatory competitive structure, rather than an unsupported conclusion that outsiders paid every dollar.

The contemporaneous record actually contested the strongest quantity and price predictions. The Commissioner found no supported reduction in prices paid to out-of-state farmers and reported that Massachusetts production had declined after implementation; uncertainty about the order's duration, investment requirements, and payment caps made expansion predictions speculative. These findings do not establish the no-program counterfactual, because a program can slow a decline without producing an absolute increase. But Stone should confront them explicitly and avoid calling a measured increase in local output an established fact. [415 Mass. at 17 n.13](https://law.justia.com/cases/massachusetts/supreme-court/volumes/415/415mass8.html).

The intervention also addressed interests beyond farmers' incomes. Rehnquist's dissent emphasized development pressure on agricultural land, open space, wildlife habitat, recreation, and preservation of a nearby milk supply. These were serious asserted objectives. They should not be dismissed as necessarily fraudulent because the chosen financing mechanism protected local production. The Massachusetts record documented a crisis; whether every prediction of imminent farm failure was accurate is a separate question. [Rehnquist dissent, 512 U.S. at 212-214](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/93-141.ZD.html).

Contemporaneous economic research confirms that nonmarket land benefits were a recognized subject of investigation before OT1993. John M. Halstead's 1984 study used an iterative willingness-to-pay survey in three Massachusetts counties to examine preservation values associated with agricultural land. That establishes a credible public-good problem and an available measurement approach. It does not establish the value of the particular milk order, eliminate survey biases, or prove that production-linked dairy payments preserved the desired acres. The accessible author abstract supports these limited propositions; no numerical results from the full paper are asserted here. [Halstead, “Measuring the Nonmarket Value of Massachusetts Agricultural Land: A Case Study,” 13 Journal of the Northeastern Agricultural Economics Council 12 (1984)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-northeastern-economics-council/article/measuring-the-nonmarket-value-of-massachusetts-agricultural-land-a-case-study/D3C8B24A3D0168138FA1EA233887BCB7).

An important inference follows: milk output was an administratively convenient proxy for preserving dairy farms, but it was not identical to acreage preservation, ecological stewardship, or reliable supply. More milk from an intensive operation need not preserve more open land. A payment cap can moderate concentration of benefits without solving that mismatch. These observations support examining alternative mechanisms, not a finding that judges know the optimal agricultural policy.

The majority itself cited earlier Massachusetts land-use research suggesting that substantial agricultural abandonment had led to forest regrowth rather than urban development. That was a legitimate challenge to assuming that every farm closure destroys undeveloped land, but statewide experience in 1951-1971 did not identify what would happen to the particular dairy parcels threatened in 1992. Stone should reject both automatic-development and automatic-reforestation assumptions. [512 U.S. at 204-205 n.20](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/186/).

#### Historical result and competing legal approaches

The Supreme Court reversed, 7-2. Stevens wrote for five Justices: O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg joined him. Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurred only in the judgment. Rehnquist, joined by Blackmun, dissented. The majority analyzed the tax and subsidy together as tariff-like discrimination; it also reasoned that the subsidy neutralized producer opposition to the assessment. The decision did not invalidate ordinary general-revenue subsidies categorically. [West Lynn, 512 U.S. at 192-207](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/186/).

Scalia accepted the outcome but attacked the majority's broader free-market formulations. Many lawful state programs improve the competitive position of local enterprises. He distinguished four mechanisms: a discriminatory tax; a neutral tax with discriminatory credits or exemptions; a neutral commodity tax earmarked for local-industry payments; and an ordinary subsidy financed from general revenues. The first three should fail; the fourth should survive. He grounded this boundary in precedent, administrability, and limited extension of the dormant Commerce Clause, not in a judicial calculation of political influence. His approach drew force from the difficulty of condemning every disturbance of comparative advantage while allowing subsidies at all. [Scalia concurrence, 512 U.S. at 207-212](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/93-141.ZC.html).

The dissent's strongest point was that dealers still had the same incentive to buy the least expensive available milk: the assessment did not vary with origin. Tax and subsidy recipients were different actors. Local dealers and consumers remained politically adverse to higher milk prices, so the majority could not simply assume that local checking mechanisms had disappeared. Rehnquist also defended state experimentation and questioned making constitutional validity turn on the financing form. His weakest inference was that these observations disposed of the question of integrated discrimination. They do not establish that a legally dedicated transfer from a commodity market to its resident producers is constitutionally indistinguishable from ordinary public expenditure. [Rehnquist dissent, 512 U.S. at 214-217](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/93-141.ZD.html).

#### Stone's baseline and initial prediction

The express earlier Stone positions supply several resources:

* **Chemical Waste and Fort Gratiot:** origin cannot justify an extra burden, and changing the geographic unit does not cure a practical barrier to interstate trade.
* **Kraft and Itel:** interstate or foreign commerce has no immunity from a legitimate local tax, but discrimination remains prohibited.
* **Boston Harbor:** analyze what government is doing, distinguishing its own purchase or project from sovereign control of independent market transactions.
* **Fabe:** classify a provision by its actual function, rather than by the statutory heading under which it appears.
* **United States v. California:** keep legal incidence distinct from ultimate economic cost. This methodological warning is particularly important here.

The natural inference is that Stone would reject the Massachusetts court's severed analysis and invalidate the combined program. His proposed OT1993 entry expressly confirms that result and preserves evenhanded taxes and ordinary general-revenue subsidies. However, the source does not explain the boundary between integration and ordinary spending, why a subsidy differs from a tax preference, or how much economic proof is required. Those are genuine gaps, not already settled elements of an elaborate Stone doctrine.

The initial prediction is therefore invalidation with reasonably high confidence. Predicting that Stone would join all of Stevens's reasoning would be unjustified. The earlier file supports functional scrutiny and anti-discrimination; it does not establish a constitutional commitment to laissez-faire, judicial optimization of agricultural production, or interest-group balancing.

#### Problems the initial formulation encounters

First, an unrestricted “look at practical economic operation” formula can swallow Stone's subsidy reservation. A $1 million appropriation and an economically equivalent tax concession can have similar competitive effects. Almost every general revenue fund includes taxes paid on interstate commerce. If any local competitive advantage plus any interstate-derived revenue suffices, ordinary education, infrastructure, research, and industrial assistance become constitutionally suspect.

Second, political-process reasoning helps explain why a rule against interstate discrimination exists but cannot sensibly become a case-by-case constitutional census of influential groups. Organized beneficiaries, dispersed consumers, and opposition by intermediaries appear throughout lawful economic policymaking. Courts lack both a stable constitutional metric and the evidentiary resources to calculate whether the remaining opposition is “enough.” The record's observation that consumers might not notice a small milk-price increment illustrates an accountability problem; it does not justify a general rule under which politically effective programs are unconstitutional.

Third, the majority's comparative-cost language understates the regulatory setting. Federal prices and pooling already shaped the market. Private production cost excludes possible public land benefits. A constitutional rule preserving an imagined unregulated competitive equilibrium would be both institutionally ambitious and factually misconceived.

Fourth, the validity of other actors' taxes cannot turn solely on ultimate economic burden. In Commonwealth Edison, Montana's neutral coal severance tax survived despite the predominance of out-of-state purchasers. That precedent supplies a boundary: exportation of economic cost alone is insufficient. Stone's earlier California reasoning points in the same methodological direction, although intergovernmental immunity and dormant-Commerce discrimination ask different constitutional questions. [Commonwealth Edison Co. v. Montana, 453 U.S. 609, 617-619 (1981)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/453/609/).

#### Serious alternatives

| Approach | Legal strength | Institutional or practical cost | Stone assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Uphold because dealer assessment is neutral and subsidy separately permissible | Protects spending autonomy and avoids speculative incidence calculations | Allows mechanically integrated commodity transfers to evade anti-discrimination law by separating payer from beneficiary | Reject for this order |
| Join the majority's full market-integration rationale | Strong response to protectionist circumvention | Threatens legitimate subsidies and invites judicial review of productive efficiency and political coalitions | Reject unnecessary breadth |
| Adopt Scalia's earmarked-fund/general-revenue distinction literally | Predictable, supported by familiar tax-credit precedents | Could make a bookkeeping transfer decisive even when legal operation remains unchanged | Useful starting point, insufficient explanation |
| Integrated anti-discrimination rule confined to linked competitive transfers | Preserves anti-circumvention and ordinary taxation/spending | Boundary litigation over legal linkage and comparator markets remains | Recommended |

| Reject the dormant Commerce Clause and await Congress | Takes textual objections seriously and maximizes political choice | Would abandon Stone's inherited decisions, settled tax constraints, and reliance without a demonstrated reason particular to this case | Not justified here |

The recommended position is not a novel tier of scrutiny. It identifies when established discrimination doctrine applies to multiple legally connected provisions. Once discrimination is established, the ordinary demanding justification rule applies. The government must show a legitimate objective for which reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives are inadequate; preservation of local market share itself cannot count as the qualifying objective. That framework already existed in New Energy. [New Energy Co. of Indiana v. Limbach, 486 U.S. 269, 277-279 (1988)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/486/269/).

#### Recommended reasoning and legal basis

Stone should explain that the challenged order creates one obligatory financing arrangement: liability arises from trade in the relevant commodity; the receipts are legally committed to payments to competing resident producers; eligibility turns on local production; and the assessment and distribution formulas are calibrated to operate together. The obligation is not payment for a service rendered to all assessed market actors. Nor is it a state purchase of milk for a state institution. Its function is to make participation in a commodity market finance an exclusive production advantage inside that same market.

The comparator is Massachusetts versus non-Massachusetts milk production in the market financed by the order. The fact that assessments are collected downstream does not eliminate that relationship. But downstream collection also does not mean the producer directly paid the tax. Stone can recognize both propositions without contradiction.

This has a recognizable doctrinal foundation. Maryland v. Louisiana examined related exemptions and credits in the actual operation of an energy-tax system rather than isolating its nominal levy. It did not require a complete econometric measurement of the discrimination before invalidating it. New Energy refused to turn a discriminatory tax credit into market participation merely because it supported local industry. Together they support examining linked rules while denying a general immunity for a state that describes regulation as subsidy. [Maryland v. Louisiana, 451 U.S. 725, 756-760 (1981)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/451/725/); [New Energy, 486 U.S. at 277-278](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/486/269/).

Legal linkage, not physical dollar tracing, should govern. Merely passing receipts through a general account should not save a program if law still mechanically commits the commodity assessment to geographically exclusive payments. Conversely, contemporaneous enactment, legislative enthusiasm for an industry, or the fact that some general revenues come from interstate sales should not establish linkage. An independently funded appropriation exposes the claimed priority to budgetary competition and does not condition commodity-market participation on financing the exclusive benefit. This is a modest anti-evasion clarification of the tax-credit analogy, not authorization to aggregate every tax and expenditure in a state budget.

The distinction imposes real costs. Legislators may reproduce similar economic consequences through conventional appropriations. They may incur additional budgetary or administrative burdens. Stone should acknowledge this, rather than pretend he has found an economic discontinuity wherever accounting categories differ. Constitutional law can permissibly regulate the legal means of financing a preference without guaranteeing that all economically comparable interventions receive identical treatment.

Subsequent scholarship clarifies the choice but does not supply binding OT1993 authority. Coenen argues that form, fiscal fairness, state experimentation, and political processes generally support preserving subsidies while condemning exceptional tax-linked arrangements. Hellerstein and Coenen likewise defend a significant distinction between subsidies and discriminatory tax relief, including the cautionary function of required form. Zelinsky resists generic claims that all taxes and direct expenditures are either equivalent or different: the constitutional context and actual program matter. Stone should adopt contextual discipline without importing an open-ended four-factor subsidy test. [Coenen, 107 Yale L.J. 965 (1998)](https://digitalcommons.law.uga.edu/fac_artchop/857/); [Hellerstein and Coenen, 81 Cornell L. Rev. 789 (1996)](https://digitalcommons.law.uga.edu/fac_artchop/274/); [Zelinsky, 112 Harv. L. Rev. 379 (1998)](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/faculty-articles/913/).

#### Practical alternatives and strongest objections

An ordinary appropriation could support farms during a temporary income collapse. Conservation easements or contracts could pay for maintaining particular land, habitat, or open-space services. Assistance based on demonstrated stewardship could target the nonmarket benefit more directly than milk volume. A local-supply program could specify actual contingency obligations rather than assume that the location of production guarantees emergency availability. These are analytically plausible alternatives, not findings that every one was immediately affordable or administratively ready in February 1992.

If a program buys a genuinely local land service, its territorial focus can follow the location of the service rather than disfavor an interchangeable out-of-state commodity. A Massachusetts easement cannot preserve a Massachusetts parcel by purchasing a Vermont parcel. That distinction must depend on the defined public service, contractual obligations, and ownership or stewardship interests, not on relabeling a farm-output subsidy “open-space procurement.” Boston Harbor supplies Stone's resource for that functional examination.

The strongest objection remains that earmarking does not necessarily cause a larger competitive distortion than a general appropriation. True. The recommended rule is defended as an administrable anti-circumvention boundary within settled anti-discrimination doctrine, not as a theorem of welfare economics. A second objection is that requiring general funding weakens innovative self-financing programs. Also true. That is a cost of preventing an industry-specific taxing mechanism from becoming an instrument of resident-only competitive redistribution. A third is the danger of extending “same market” too broadly. Stone should expressly reserve programs financing consumers, health services, pollution remediation, or noncompeting beneficiaries. Their competitive relationships require independent analysis.

Stone should reverse the state judgment and direct further proceedings consistent with invalidity of the integrated order. He should not judicially transform it into a different milk tax, design a statewide appropriation, or order equal subsidy payments to every out-of-state farmer. Those alternatives require policy and state-law choices. Any retrospective recovery question requires separate attention to the claims, governing remedial law, and procedural record; merits invalidity alone does not decide every refund issue.

#### Hindsight: what later experience establishes, and what it does not

Congress subsequently consented to the Northeast Interstate Dairy Compact, subject to stated conditions, in §147 of the 1996 farm legislation. This is concrete evidence that coordinated regional dairy support could move through national political authorization. It is not proof that Congress would necessarily have approved Massachusetts's exact program in 1993. It illustrates why protecting interstate equality by default can coexist with substantial congressional power to authorize a different regional arrangement. [Pub. L. 104-127, §147, 110 Stat. 919-920](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-104publ127/pdf/PLAW-104publ127.pdf).

The conditions were substantive: Congress prohibited barriers to milk from other production areas, required respect for inter-order sharing of receipts, and provided for compensation of federal commodity-purchase costs associated with specified excess production growth. This was supervised regional experimentation with attention to national spillovers, not blanket approval of interstate exclusion. Those details make the compact especially useful for Stone's allocation of responsibility between state initiative and Congress.

Later economic evidence counsels both against assuming no interstate effects and against exaggerating their magnitude. GAO's 2001 analysis modeled relatively small interregional effects from the actual six-state compact, potentially larger effects from wider compacts, and sensitivity to trade assumptions. It found that identifying the compact's contribution to retail-price increases and farm-structure trends was difficult. Farm numbers continued falling while herd size grew, but that did not prove the compact caused the decline or failed to slow it. This is evidence of causal uncertainty, not validation of either litigating side's strongest rhetoric. [GAO-01-866, especially pp. 20-31, 62-80](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-01-866.pdf).

Balagtas and Sumner's original economic model explicitly incorporated federal milk-marketing rules and compared compact outcomes against a no-compact federal-order baseline. Their accessible 2002 manuscript used 1999 data to model gains to participating producers, losses to local fluid-milk consumers and nonparticipating producers, and gains to other consumers through manufactured-milk markets. It is a simulation using specified elasticities, not a randomized causal estimate or a retrospective measurement of Massachusetts's 1992 order. Its contribution is the mechanism: regional support can change supply in connected product markets, so costs need not remain where the assessment is formally collected. [Balagtas and Sumner, manuscript, later published in Review of Agricultural Economics 25 (2003), 123-144](https://cail.ucdavis.edu/oa/compactsubmitted011302.pdf).

Nothing in these materials permits the claim that West Lynn itself caused dairy consolidation, saved consumers a specified amount, or destroyed a particular number of farms. The later compact differed in geography, eligibility, statutory authority, and timing. Hindsight strengthens the demand for factual care and national treatment of regional spillovers; it does not provide a numerical constitutional threshold.

#### Downstream implications and uncertainty

**Direct, high confidence:** the proposed rule reaches discriminatory tax refunds and structurally linked commodity transfers despite changes in administrative form. It preserves neutral commodity taxes whose receipts fund ordinary government. It does not create a requirement to prove lost market share before recognizing a legally geographic preference.

**Direct, high confidence:** Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturers of America v. Walsh demonstrates the importance of identifying actual competitors. The Court distinguished West Lynn because the Maine rebate system did not give a special benefit to in-state competitors of rebate-paying manufacturers; locating a factory in Maine would neither remove the obligation nor confer the subsidy. Stone's narrowed rationale handles that distinction well. It should not condemn every assessment on one commercial layer that supports another. [538 U.S. 644, 668-670 (2003)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/538/644/).

**Direct but contested:** Camps Newfound/Owatonna applied the discrimination principle to a property-tax exemption penalizing charities serving predominantly nonresidents. It retained the tax-preference/subsidy distinction and rejected an expansive characterization of the exemption as governmental purchasing. Stone's recommended West Lynn rationale supports serious scrutiny, while leaving room to distinguish a genuine contract purchasing services for residents from a tax penalty on interstate customers. Charitable status alone should not settle the issue. [520 U.S. 564, 588-594 (1997)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/520/564/).

**A genuine future tension:** Davis upheld a preference for the State's own public bonds and invoked the relationship between governmental financing and market participation. Stone's future roadmap favors a narrow public-financing distinction, but West Lynn does not itself prove that distinction. He would need a separate account of governmental debt, public responsibility, and treatment of private versus public competitors. Treating every fiscal preference as proprietary would conflict with the very distinction on which West Lynn rests. [553 U.S. 328 (2008)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/553/328/).

**Illustrative, lower confidence:** clean-energy credits expose the danger of treating all support for less competitive facilities as unconstitutional. A carbon-reduction attribute can define a legitimate product or service; a home-state preference among otherwise comparable qualifying generators presents another issue. Coalition for Competitive Electricity v. Zibelman did not resolve the dormant-Commerce merits: the Second Circuit rejected those plaintiffs' standing while upholding the program against preemption claims. It cannot be cited as a merits decision authorizing all local energy subsidies. [906 F.3d 41 (2d Cir. 2018)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca2/17-2654/17-2654-2018-09-27.html).

No direct implication follows for anti-commandeering or sovereign immunity. The modest connection to Stone's New York position is institutional: each sovereign should use legally available means and bear responsibility for its policy. That analogy does not transform a state subsidy into commandeering. Nor does invalidating this state program narrow Congress's affirmative power over the interstate dairy market.

#### Exact degree of Stone's development

The inherited principle, opposition to geographic discrimination combined with ordinary state taxing authority, survives. The proposed OT1993 disposition survives. The reasoning changes through **clarification and doctrinal refinement**, with a **factual reassessment** of its compressed incidence claim. Stone should replace the suggestion of a literal local-producer tax refund with analysis of linked assessment, production benefit, and comparator competition. He should decline any broader proposition that protecting local production from cheaper competition is always unconstitutional, because it would contradict the subsidy space he expressly preserves.

Confidence is high in invalidating this tightly integrated order and in rejecting an unbounded market-efficiency rationale. Confidence is moderate in any general formulation separating linked subsidies from independent appropriations. Precisely how to treat partial earmarking, mixed-purpose funds, tax-financed environmental attributes, or grants restricted to resident service providers remains open. Stone should acknowledge those boundaries rather than manufacture a comprehensive subsidy code in one dairy case.

### Barclays Bank PLC v. Franchise Tax Board, with Colgate-Palmolive Co., 512 U.S. 298 (1994)

#### The regulatory problem and the precise tax

California needed to attribute to itself a fair share of the earnings of integrated businesses operating across many jurisdictions. The difficulty is real: a multinational's profit does not necessarily have an objectively observable birthplace corresponding to separate legal subsidiaries' books. Central finance, management, brands, technology, customer networks, and intra-group transactions contribute to value across entity lines. Transfer prices can also move reported earnings among entities without moving the same amount of underlying economic activity.

California's disputed method combined the worldwide income of a unitary business, then apportioned it using the arithmetic average of the fractions of its payroll, property, and sales situated in California. The resulting California share, rather than worldwide income in full, became the state franchise-tax base. The challenged years were 1977 for Barclays and 1970-1973 for Colgate. Later changes, including California's 1993 decision to double-weight sales, did not describe the formula used in these tax years. Calling this a tax on all foreign earnings, or a tax imposed on every foreign affiliate as an independent taxpayer, misstates the mechanism. [512 U.S. 302-308](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/298/).

Barclays was a foreign-parented banking group with more than 220 entities in approximately sixty countries. Two components doing business in California sought refunds. The group conceded its worldwide unity for this litigation. Colgate was a U.S.-parented enterprise with foreign subsidiaries; its claim principally concerned federal foreign-commerce uniformity after *Container Corp.* had approved the method for a domestic-parented multinational. That distinction in conceded facts matters. Stone need not decide whether every multinational, every foreign affiliate, or every financial holding-company relationship is unitary.

Separate accounting, used in the federal system and internationally, was the competing method. It assigned earnings to individual entities and required arm's-length prices for controlled transactions under Internal Revenue Code § 482 and Treasury regulations. It was not a neutral absence of governmental allocation: the tax authority still had to estimate what related parties would have paid had they been independent. Formula apportionment replaced one approximation with another. Its virtue was resistance to certain intra-group transfers; its cost was that payroll, property, and sales are imperfect proxies for income production.

The governments and taxpayers faced several distinct burdens. California obtained revenue and protection against attribution strategies that understated its share. Domestic and foreign groups with relatively high California factors could pay more than under their separate accounts, while groups with other income-factor relationships could pay less. The immediate legal incidence fell on the California taxpayers. Ultimate incidence on owners, customers, or employees was not determined in the record. Foreign governments faced a conflict with their own asserted income allocations and concern about diplomatic retaliation. Businesses faced costs of converting currency, accounting standards, and languages across the group.

#### Historical result and all separate opinions

The Supreme Court affirmed both California judgments. The result was 7-2 for Barclays and unanimous for Colgate. Ginsburg wrote the Court opinion; Scalia joined all except Part IV-B, the one-voice discussion. Blackmun joined but wrote separately to reject reliance on congressional inaction. O'Connor, joined by Thomas, concurred in Colgate's judgment because of *Container Corp.* and reliance interests, while dissenting as to Barclays.

The majority applied *Complete Auto*: substantial nexus, fair apportionment, nondiscrimination, and relation to state-provided services. For foreign commerce it also addressed *Japan Line*'s heightened multiple-taxation and national-uniformity concerns. Nexus was undisputed for the actual California taxpayers. Fair apportionment survived because the taxpayers did not establish gross distortion and the formula was internally consistent if universally applied. Barclays' expensive-compliance claim failed because California accepted reasonable approximations, and the parties actually used them. The trial court's estimates of a new system costing over $5 million to build and over $2 million annually concerned literal compliance that the system did not in practice demand. The Court did not hold that such costs would be constitutionally irrelevant if actually imposed. Nor did it establish that reasonable approximation can systematically overtax foreigners. [512 U.S. 310-316](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/298/).

The California Court of Appeal had construed the approximation regulation to constrain administrative discretion. The U.S. Court relied on that construction, access to advance determinations, and the absence of a concrete rejected reasonable approximation. This is a consequential protection: taxpayers were not constitutionally required to seek standardless indulgence. [*Barclays Bank International v. FTB*, 10 Cal. App. 4th 1742, 1754-1766 (1992)](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/4th/10/1742.html).

On multiple taxation, the majority acknowledged the risk and *Container Corp.*'s acceptance that actual overlap had occurred. It did not establish that Barclays never encountered double taxation. It held that overlap was not the inevitable consequence of California's formula and that separate accounting would not eliminate it either because jurisdictions differ in their allocation rules. O'Connor agreed that the four *Complete Auto* factors and due process were satisfied but considered foreign corporate ownership decisive under *Japan Line*. California's nonconforming method could tax earnings already assigned to a foreign jurisdiction, while foreign taxpayers had weaker access to American political processes. Her disagreement primarily concerned international multiple taxation, not a decision to let executive disapproval control the case. She generally agreed that Congress, not precatory executive statements, controlled the relevant policy choice. [512 U.S. 316-320, 332-337](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/298/).

On national uniformity, Ginsburg relied on congressional nonenactment of proposed prohibitions, the Senate's reservation to the U.S.-U.K. treaty, and the absence of binding federal prohibition. The treaty as originally proposed would have restricted state combined reporting; the Senate withheld consent to that restriction as applied to subdivisions. The final treaty retained nondiscrimination obligations reaching state taxation without imposing the proposed accounting-method restriction. The Court held that executive letters, press statements, legislative proposals, and briefs lacked legal force to displace otherwise valid state taxation. It expressly reserved statutes delegating power, ratified treaties, and legally binding executive agreements. [512 U.S. 320-331](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/298/).

Blackmun regarded *Container Corp.* and the tax's character as sufficient, without inferring permission from legislative silence. Scalia would enforce dormant-Commerce restrictions against facial discrimination and indistinguishable previously invalidated taxes, rejecting the one-voice test. He criticized the notion that Congress could exercise its commerce authority by silence. These positions matter because Stone can uphold the tax without endorsing every part of Ginsburg's institutional reasoning.

#### Stone baseline and initial prediction

*Itel Containers* makes affirmance the more natural initial prediction: international use does not immunize local taxable activity, and an operative federal law or treaty must do the displacing. *Kraft* requires scrutiny of differential treatment of foreign commerce. *Quill* supports nexus, fair allocation, nondiscrimination, and protection against territorially excessive taxes, but its physical-presence collection rule does not itself determine attribution of the income of taxpayers physically present in California. *United States v. California* counsels against manufacturing federal law from a federal interest. *Easterwood* requires an actual conflict rather than a general invocation of national regulatory concern.

The countervailing baseline is equally explicit. Stone's *Itel* entry rejects serious multiple-taxation problems and taxation of value belonging elsewhere. If that means any overlap with a foreign sovereign's accounting allocation automatically invalidates the state tax, O'Connor's Barclays result becomes plausible. The file does not specify which meaning Stone intends. Pretending the baseline supplies an unequivocal answer would erase the term's most important tax ambiguity.

#### Reconsideration: what should change

Stone should distinguish **a tax structure that inherently penalizes interstate or foreign activity**, **an unreasonable attribution of extraterritorial value**, and **overlap caused by different otherwise permissible allocation systems**. The first two remain constitutional defects. The third does not automatically identify which sovereign acted unlawfully. A universal rule that whichever jurisdiction departs from prevalent international practice must yield would constitutionalize a contested accounting convention and let foreign choices determine a State's lawful revenue base.

This clarification must not become the claim that only mathematically inevitable double taxation matters. If a taxpayer can demonstrate a grossly distorted California attribution, actual discriminatory compliance burdens, or assessment of a nonunitary business, internal consistency does not save it. A formula can add to 100% across identical hypothetical jurisdictions and still badly misattribute a particular firm's income. Stone should retain both internal and external checks.

The constitutional source supports separate institutional treatment of direct discrimination and demands for harmonization. An origin-based penalty can be assessed against an equivalent domestic transaction by a court using established rules. Choosing the best global allocation regime requires coordination of tax bases, factor weights, credits, transfer-price enforcement, and treaty obligations. That is a strong reason for congressional responsibility. It is not a reason to abandon judicial review of discrimination.

#### Alternatives and recommendation

**Follow O'Connor and invalidate only for foreign-parented groups.** This has respectable support in *Japan Line*, *Container Corp.*'s express reservations, actual tax overlap, and the political vulnerability of foreign taxpayers. Its difficulty is the relationship between corporate domicile and the income allocation at issue. Once worldwide unity is conceded and California taxes only an apportioned share, ownership alone does not show that the share belongs elsewhere. Giving differently parented competitors different constitutional access to allocation rules could produce its own artificial incentives. Weak political representation justifies rigorous judicial nondiscrimination review; it does not establish that foreign corporate book income is necessarily the correct territorial measure.

**Require arm's-length separate accounting for all international income.** This would offer greater conformity with prevalent practice and remove one source of mismatch. It would also overturn or sharply restrict *Container Corp.*, require states to incur more transfer-pricing enforcement costs, and treat contested accounting estimates as constitutionally privileged. International uniformity is a strong policy interest, but neither constitutional text nor the cited precedents make one system uniquely valid.

**Accept the historical majority in full.** The result and much of the reasoning are defensible. The weakness is language equating failed legislation with congressional permission. Nonenactment may reflect agenda congestion, veto points, divided preferences, or pressure from States and firms. It does not satisfy the clear authorization needed to save otherwise discriminatory state laws. That distinction matters elsewhere in Stone's jurisprudence and should be maintained even when the silent-congress inference favors state autonomy.

**Adopt Scalia's limited-review position.** That would reduce discretionary judging, but it is broader than this case requires and would constrain review of nonfacial structural discrimination in future tax cases. Stone's file already emphasizes practical economic barriers, not merely statutory face. He can refuse speculative foreign-policy balancing without repudiating all dormant-Commerce scrutiny outside Scalia's categories.

**Affirm on a narrower, source-disciplined rationale. Recommended.** Stone should affirm both judgments, join the *Complete Auto*, compliance, and due-process analysis, and concur separately on international multiple taxation and uniformity. A concise independent rationale would be: the conceded unitary relationship and bounded allocation establish a constitutionally permissible California tax base; no discriminatory practical burden or gross distortion has been established; the Constitution does not command replacement of this permissible approximation by another merely because the latter is more common internationally; and neither the enacted treaty nor any binding federal measure prohibits the tax. Congressional silence is unnecessary to the holding and does not authorize discrimination.

For *Japan Line*, Stone should explain why its tax on foreign-owned instrumentalities already fully taxed at home differs from attribution of income within a genuinely unitary enterprise. He should not purport to overrule the one-voice inquiry across all foreign-commerce disputes. He can hold that this record supplies no judicially administrable necessity for uniformity beyond disagreement over a lawful income-allocation method, in the setting of a treaty that deliberately omitted the proposed restriction. Independent presidential power and binding executive agreements remain reserved. *Barclays* itself does not authorize saying that the Executive can never preempt state law.

#### Degree and justification of evolution

This is **clarification, with a conditional narrowing** of Stone's earlier double-taxation language. If the earlier phrase meant absolute freedom from overlapping claims, Stone should expressly narrow it. The legal reason is that *Container Corp.* already tolerated some overlap and exposed the flaw in treating separate accounting as an objective constitutional baseline. The institutional reason is the need for a principled allocation of harmonization decisions to political institutions. The practical reason is that both systems entail estimation and avoidance risks. There is no demonstrated reason to reject nondiscrimination, fair apportionment, or meaningful nexus.

Stone also refines *Itel*'s federal-conflict approach: absence of an operative prohibition defeats the asserted preemption claim, but does not establish affirmative congressional approval of every state practice. A law can be valid because it falls within retained state authority and violates no constitutional restriction. It need not be validated by a federal permission fiction.

#### Scholarship, empirical evidence, and hindsight

Contemporaneous economic theory warned against treating formula apportionment as costless. Gordon and Wilson's 1986 *Econometrica* model showed that factor-weighted apportionment changes firms' incentives concerning capital, payroll, location, sales, and integration. This is theory, not an estimate that California's Barclays tax caused a particular real-world relocation. It supports refusing to proclaim California's formula economically optimal while leaving a range of legally permissible fiscal choices. [Gordon and Wilson, *An Examination of Multijurisdictional Corporate Income Taxation under Formula Apportionment*, 54 Econometrica 1357 (1986)](https://gabriel-zucman.eu/files/teaching/GordonWilson86.pdf).

Subsequent research strengthens the practical case for preserving alternatives to separate accounting. Clausing's 2016 study used Bureau of Economic Analysis data for 1983-2012 and estimated large U.S. revenue losses from international profit shifting. Her $77-111 billion estimate for 2012 is a model-based federal estimate, not California revenue loss, not a Barclays finding, and not evidence available in OT1993. It supports the proposition that allocation and avoidance concerns did not disappear under internationally conventional accounting. It cannot establish that any particular state formula is fairly apportioned. [Clausing, *The Effect of Profit Shifting on the Corporate Tax Base in the United States and Beyond*, 69 National Tax Journal 905 (2016)](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.17310/ntj.2016.4.09).

Clausing and Avi-Yonah's 2007 proposal advocated a destination-sales formula for federal taxation. It is a reform proposal, not evidence that California's equal-weight 1970s formula worked optimally. Its own discussion recognizes transition, tax-base definition, mixed-system, and distributional difficulties. The disagreement with conventional separate accounting is therefore not between a demonstrably perfect system and an irrational alternative. Constitutionalizing either side would prematurely settle an ongoing technical and political dispute. [Clausing and Avi-Yonah, *Reforming Corporate Taxation in a Global Economy* (2007)](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/200706clausing_aviyonah.pdf).

The most immediate adaptation predates the judgment and must not be mislabeled hindsight. California offered a water's-edge election in legislation enacted in 1986, effective in 1988; in 1993 it removed the election fee and threat of disregard, effective January 1994. Foreign diplomatic objections and British retaliatory legislation already existed. Thus the State had substantially accommodated the practical controversy while litigation continued over old liabilities. This shows political capacity to respond, not that the Court's later affirmance caused reform. Nor does voluntary adaptation establish that the earlier system was unconstitutional. [512 U.S. 306-307, 324 n.22](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/298/).

#### Downstream consequences and residual risks

The direct future constraint is a serious unitary-business boundary. *MeadWestvaco* later rejected use of an independent operational-purpose slogan to tax the gain from a business without establishing the requisite unitary connection. That is a natural test of Stone's recommendation: actual integration must support worldwide attribution; regulatory convenience alone cannot. This preserves the file's territorial concern without imposing entity-by-entity formalism. [*MeadWestvaco*, 553 U.S. 16 (2008)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/553/16/).

*Wynne*'s internal-consistency analysis is compatible with tolerating overlap from different lawful systems while rejecting a structure whose replication systematically burdens interstate commerce. But Maryland resident-income credits present different taxing claims from multinational unitary allocation, so *Barclays* does not dictate *Wynne*'s result. The causal link is a diagnostic framework, not a binding prediction. *Wayfair* likewise can fit a jurisprudence of real economic connection and compliance protection without granting interstate businesses categorical tax immunity. Neither later case licenses silently overruling *Quill* in OT1993.

In foreign-affairs preemption, the recommended reasoning supports later provision-specific examination of actual federal enactments. It does not predetermine cases involving sanctions statutes, a legally binding executive agreement, or exclusive national diplomatic functions. An anticipated *Crosby* result would depend on conflict with Congress's sanction architecture, not on treating foreign complaints alone as a constitutional veto. The direct link to affirmative Commerce power is recognition that Congress may impose national allocation rules within its assigned foreign-commerce authority. There is no necessary extension to commandeering state officials or immunity from private damages.

The strongest unresolved objection remains the inadequacy of after-the-fact protection for a foreign business facing burdens it cannot politically resist, particularly where approximations depend on expensive negotiations and judicial proof of distortion is difficult. Stone's answer should make the safeguards enforceable: accepted approximate records, reviewable reasons, meaningful opportunities to contest attribution, and no blanket immunity for purportedly neutral reporting demands. That still leaves real litigation costs. Confidence is high on affirming Colgate, moderately high on Barclays under the conceded unity and actual approximation record, and lower on generalizing to different reporting systems or foreign groups that contest unity.

## V. Adjacent case studies

### Northwest Airlines, Inc. v. County of Kent, 510 U.S. 355 (January 24, 1994)

#### The actual problem and historical record

Kent County's airport allocated airfield costs to commercial and general aviation according to use, and terminal costs by occupied area. Commercial airlines paid their allocated costs; general aviation paid about 20% of its allocation. Commercial concessions paid market rents above allocated costs, funding the shortfall and reserves. Airlines wanted concession revenues credited to their charges and objected to favorable general-aviation treatment. This was a dispute over allocating joint infrastructure costs, not an origin surcharge on goods. [Opinion, 359-362](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/355/).

Congress's Anti-Head Tax Act, then 49 U.S.C. App. §1513, prohibited designated charges on air commerce but preserved reasonable airport charges to aircraft operators. The Court affirmed, 7-1, Blackmun not participating, using *Evansville*'s fair-approximation, nonexcessiveness, and nondiscrimination criteria. Thomas would have treated these fees as outside the Act and remanded the separate constitutional challenge. The Court left the implied statutory cause of action undecided because no cross-petition challenged the airlines' limited victory concerning crash/fire/rescue costs. [Opinion, 364-374; Thomas dissent, 374-382](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/355/).

Who benefits and pays must be stated carefully. General aviation benefits from under-recovery of allocated costs; concession renters finance the airport surplus and that gap in the accounting system. The record does not show commercial airlines themselves paying more than their allocated operating benefits. Indirect economic incidence may reach passengers or concession customers, but no quantified pass-through finding justifies assigning all costs to outsiders. Most importantly, general aviation was not proved to be an intrastate class. The airline argument cannot establish discrimination merely by relabeling a diverse category of users as local.

#### Stone baseline and initial prediction

*Itel* and the restrained *Quill* entry permit interstate businesses to pay for public services and valid local taxable events. *Chemical Waste* requires real evidence of differential burdens. *Boston Harbor* makes the county's operation of its own facilities relevant but does not immunize that operation from federal statutes. *Morales* requires full effect for actual aviation preemption; *PRASA* counsels keeping claim, jurisdiction, and remedial posture distinct. The natural prediction, an inference rather than an existing Stone vote, is to affirm the challenged charges while preserving a factually established discrimination claim in another case.

The tension is that Stone's enthusiasm for real costs could become a court-designed cost-accounting constitution. Common infrastructure costs are not uniquely attributable; allocating every indirect benefit to concessions would be an economic choice, not an inevitable legal answer. Conversely, allowing public ownership or a facially neutral accounting formula to end review would permit concealment of outsider burdens. Neither extension follows from his earlier cases.

#### Alternatives and recommendation

| Approach | Legal basis | Institutional and practical cost |
|---|---|---|
| Require concession-surplus credits and uniform recovery from all user classes | Robust reading of reasonable fees and equal treatment | Converts judges into airport rate regulators; treats unlike infrastructure uses as equivalent |
| Adopt Thomas's narrow statutory reading and remand Commerce issue | Avoids broad interpretation of indirect fees | Does not adequately account for Congress's express preservation of reasonable landing and service charges; litigation already afforded opportunity to prove discrimination |
| Affirm under a constrained reasonable-user-fee standard | *Evansville*, actual statutory categories, trial findings | Permits debatable allocations, but retains excessiveness and discrimination controls |
| Treat airport ownership or congressional permission as categorical immunity | Proprietor role or congressional authorization | Conflates market participation with federal statutory immunity; overreads ambiguous permission for discrimination |

Stone should affirm, joining the statutory disposition while writing or joining reasoning that rejects two overextensions. First, the Act's ordinary authorization of reasonable charges need not be treated as unmistakable authorization of discrimination. The constitutional claim fails on this record under the same user-fee criteria, so that harder authorization question can remain open. Second, the allowed allocation is a permissible approximation, not a judicial declaration that historical accounting cost is always the constitutional maximum or the sole economically legitimate basis for airport pricing.

For the statutory issue, Stone can accept that these specified aircraft-user charges fall within the prohibition-and-saving arrangement without treating every downstream expense eventually borne by passengers as a prohibited indirect tax. That avoids the very remote-effects expansion resisted in his *Morales* entry. With respect to the remedy, preserve the lower-court direction to allocate rescue-service costs among users, and the Court's reservation of the private-right question. An affirmance does not affirm every theory adopted below.

This is clarification and implementation, not reversal of Stone. It makes the distinction between proving actual discriminatory burden and demanding perfect distribution of all economic benefits. Federal standards constrain the county's own service operation; nothing commands it to regulate third parties for Washington.

#### Hindsight and downstream effects

Later DOT policy provides a concrete reason not to constitutionalize one cost formula. DOT issued a rates-and-charges policy in 1996; litigation and amendment continued. In 2010 the D.C. Circuit upheld 2008 policy changes permitting forms of congestion pricing. That opinion explains why an additional peak-time landing can impose costs on other users, why runway capacity cannot be expanded immediately, and why a weight-only fee may inadequately price a scarce landing opportunity. It also notes that much of the original policy had been vacated and that guidance remained deficient. Thus agency responsibility is a defensible allocation of comparative competence, not evidence of flawless agency performance. [2010 primary opinion](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/airports/airport_compliance/compliance_guidance/uscourtappeals-airports-rates-charges-jul2010.pdf); [1996 policy](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/airports/airport_compliance/compliance_guidance/obligation_rates96.pdf); [2013 policy](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2013-09-10/pdf/2013-21905.pdf).

The retrospective conclusion is limited: real operating benefits and external costs can differ from a narrow account ledger. The later regulatory episode does not establish that Kent County's particular cross-subsidy was economically optimal. It supports preserving administratively supervised flexibility under statutory limits.

Direct doctrinal consequence: user-fee review remains distinct from origin discrimination in *Oregon Waste* and from compelled exclusive dealing in *Carbone*. Plausible broader consequence: *Boston Harbor*'s functional proprietor inquiry can coexist with nondiscrimination and federal statutory regulation. No sovereign-immunity holding follows. Strongest counterargument: airlines may be captive to an airport with monopoly power and therefore unable to discipline excessive charges by exit. Answer: this justifies meaningful statutory/agency review and evidence-based constitutional review, not a court-selected universal allocation formula. Confidence: high on affirmance and preservation of review, medium on optimal administrative design, low on incidence claims beyond the trial record.

### Department of Revenue of Oregon v. ACF Industries, Inc., 510 U.S. 332 (January 24, 1994)

#### The actual tax and historical decision

Oregon taxed railroad cars leased by eight companies while exempting categories including agricultural equipment and business inventories. Timber and motor vehicles received different fiscal treatment. Congress's 4-R Act barred specified property-assessment and rate discrimination and, separately, another tax discriminating against a rail carrier. Congress acted against a record of railroads' immobile investments and vulnerability as nonresident tax targets. Historical holding, 8-1: general property-tax exemptions were outside the residual prohibition; reverse and remand. Stevens dissented. [Opinion and dissent](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/332/).

The Court's structural argument is serious: the first three provisions compare rail property with taxable commercial/industrial property, expressly excluding exempt property from the defined comparison class. A residual clause should not nullify that deliberate limitation. The opinion also invokes traditional state taxation and a presumption against preemption, and reserves taxes that single railroads out for discriminatory treatment. Stevens argues that the residual clause independently reaches devices omitted from the strict numerical tests; he rejects both automatic most-favored-taxpayer treatment and the Ninth Circuit's automatic entire-tax refund.

The economic problem is not proved simply by listing exemptions. Agricultural machinery, inventories, vehicles paying alternative charges, and mobile railroad cars may differ in ways relevant to tax design. The challengers must establish a proper comparator and material disadvantage; the State must be able to defend genuine distinctions. Yet characterizing every exemption as an untouchable definition of the tax base would enable a state to reproduce a higher effective tax on a vulnerable interstate industry while announcing uniform rates.

#### Stone baseline and initial prediction

Two resources point in different directions. *Itel*, *Quill*, and the tax-incidence discussion in *United States v. California* favor room for workable state revenue systems; *Easterwood* and *Cipollone* refuse preemption beyond enacted scope. But *Chemical Waste*, *Fort Gratiot*, and *Fabe* require attention to functional burdens rather than the mechanism's label. A mechanical prediction is therefore uncertain: a narrow majority position is plausible if statutory structure dominates, but a functional statutory-antidiscrimination reading is equally rooted in Stone's actual materials. Do not describe an 8-1 historical result as making Stone's vote obvious.

#### Alternatives and recommended evolution

| Approach | Legal advantage | Principal cost |
|---|---|---|
| Majority's general exemption exclusion with targeted-tax reservation | Definite statutory structural rule and broad fiscal discretion | The difference between an exemption and tax-base manipulation may become unstable; large substantive disparities can avoid review |
| Automatic extension of every favorable exemption to railroads | Simple remedy and maximum protection for interstate infrastructure | Far exceeds a ban on discrimination; erases legitimate distinctions and creates windfalls |
| Permit residual discrimination claim, require meaningful comparator and justification, reject automatic exemption | Gives residual clause independent work and preserves legitimate exemptions | More factfinding and judicial judgment; the property/excise structure remains a strong objection |

Recommended Stone position: vacate the Ninth Circuit's automatic favorable-tax-treatment remedy and remand for a genuine statutory discrimination inquiry; reject the historical Court's categorical exclusion of general property-tax exemption schemes. He should explain that his disagreement concerns cognizability, comparator, and remedy, not a declaration that Oregon's tax is necessarily invalid. His separate opinion would agree that the appellate remedy cannot stand while disagreeing with the Court's categorical limitation of the federal cause.

Legal basis: the residual clause uses the established legal concept of discrimination; the specific property provisions supply strict numerical tests and a defined comparison class for those tests, while a residual inquiry can require proof of unjustified material disadvantage without pretending every untaxed asset bears a calculable tax rate. Congress already explicitly intruded upon discriminatory state railroad taxation. A presumption against displacing traditional state powers should help resolve actual ambiguities; it should not require Congress separately to name each device capable of producing the forbidden treatment. *Davis v. Michigan Department of Treasury*, 489 U.S. 803, and *Bacchus*, 468 U.S. 263, show that exemptions can be discriminatory without using a formally higher rate. Those precedents establish a recognized concept, not that their precise statutory or constitutional comparator automatically governs the 4-R Act.

The limiting rule should track substantially comparable property and evidence of burdens, not invent a mandatory numerical share of taxpayers that must be exempt before scrutiny begins. Legitimate distinctions, including genuinely equivalent alternative taxes where pertinent, can defeat the discrimination inference. The statute protects railroads against discriminatory treatment, including relative to other interstate actors; its object is not identical to the dormant Clause's state-border discrimination rule. Consequently Stone should not import the strict compensatory-tax doctrine wholesale or establish a constitutional entitlement to every exemption.

Degree of change: doctrinal refinement of Stone's source discipline and functionalism. No prior Stone holding is reversed. The real reconsideration is of a tempting implementation, namely treating deference to state fiscal choice as a reason to immunize a device from the federal prohibition altogether. Costs: comparison litigation, judicial disagreement over tax incidence, and potential pressure on beneficial state exemptions. Benefits: fewer opportunities to defeat an enacted protection through form. Confidence is moderate because the majority's express statutory-structure argument is substantial and should be answered, not dismissed as formalism.

#### Hindsight, opposing view, and consequences

In *CSX Transportation v. Alabama Department of Revenue*, 562 U.S. 277 (2011), the Court rejected the Eleventh Circuit's extension of *ACF* to sales/use tax exemptions. It preserved *ACF* for the specific property-tax structure, but held that exemptions could constitute forbidden discrimination under the residual clause. Its opinion specifically warns that federalism does not narrow an express federal prohibition. [CSX 2011](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/562/277/). In 2015 the Court further recognized that an alternative, roughly equivalent tax may matter when evaluating an excise-tax exemption and that transportation competitors may be suitable comparators. [Alabama Department of Revenue v. CSX, 575 U.S. 21](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/575/21/).

This later history demonstrates the danger of generalized immunity for exemptions. It does not prove the 1994 Court misread the property-specific provisions; indeed the later Court expressly retained that part of *ACF*. A Stone choosing the historical majority could defensibly state in 1994 that the holding is property-structure-specific, exemptions are often discriminatory elsewhere, and statutory anti-evasion challenges remain open. That is the strongest alternative and preferable to a sweeping state-tax carveout.

The recommendation's direct downstream path would make both property and excise exemption challenges legally cognizable under their respective statutory rules. Whether any tax actually fails would remain a separate question. Its relation to *West Lynn* is methodological: examine integrated real burdens. It does not make general-revenue subsidies unconstitutional. Its relation to *Lohman* is similarly limited: the compensatory inquiry there is constitutional and tied to interstate discrimination, whereas Congress can protect railroads more specifically here. Anti-commandeering and state immunity remain separate issues; neither is adjudicated by interpreting this prohibition or its existing enforcement route.

### John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank, 510 U.S. 86 (1993)

#### Inclusion, actual dispute, and regulatory mechanics

The case belongs because it directly tests Fabe's functional classification of insurance, Greater Washington's ERISA boundaries, and the question whether state regulatory authority displaces or coexists with an enacted national fiduciary regime. The relevant federalism issue is statutory allocation between federal pension regulation and state insurance regulation. There was no litigated claim that Congress lacked power to regulate the pension arrangement.

Sperry's retirement plan used Group Annuity Contract 50, originating in 1941 and amended repeatedly, to place pension contributions with Hancock. The funds were commingled in the insurer's general account. The “Pension Administration Fund” was a bookkeeping measure, not a segregated trust containing identified investments. Hancock allocated a share of investment gains and losses to it. Another bookkeeping entry recorded liabilities for benefits already guaranteed. A minimum operating level included those liabilities and a five-percent cushion. Amounts above that level were the “free funds.” Retirement benefits could become guaranteed when converted under the contract; a 1977 amendment also permitted payment of expressly nonguaranteed benefits from free funds. The plan challenged Hancock's management and valuation of those funds under ERISA. [Majority's agreed-record account, 510 U.S. 90-94](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/86/).

This was not simply an insurer dissipating pension money. Thomas emphasized that investment accumulation had outperformed expectations and that the plan wanted to escape an unattractive bargain. Termination and withdrawal provisions were expensive; contract assumptions fixed in earlier years could make replacement annuities costly relative to later market rates. The plan alleged, among other things, wrongful valuation and refusal to change contract terms. Those allegations must be distinguished from proved fiduciary misconduct. The judicial issue was fiduciary coverage, not an adjudication that Hancock had breached a duty or that ERISA required renegotiation of every unfavorable term. [Thomas dissent, 510 U.S. 121-123](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/86/).

The competing regulatory purposes were genuine. Federal fiduciary law protects plan beneficiaries against conflicted discretionary asset management. State insurance law protects solvency and equitable treatment of all policyholders and creditors. A general account finances multiple products and obligations. Giving one pension plan exclusive-benefit rights over every commingled dollar could conflict with the insurer's responsibilities to others. Conversely, calling a discretionary pension investment “insurance” could remove protections that an ordinary investment manager would have to observe.

Benefits and burdens did not divide along state borders. Pension beneficiaries gained fiduciary safeguards if free funds were covered. Insurers faced fiduciary and prohibited-transaction obligations, and other policyholders could face transferred administrative costs. Interstate spillovers arose from nationally distributed pensions and insurers, not a protectionist classification. No record finding establishes the net welfare effect or quantifies who ultimately paid new costs.

#### Historical decision and serious alternatives

The Court affirmed the Second Circuit, 6-3. Ginsburg wrote the majority; Thomas dissented with O'Connor and Kennedy. ERISA §401(b)(2), 29 U.S.C. §1101(b)(2), exempts insurer assets attributable to a guaranteed benefit policy only to the extent the contract actually provides guaranteed benefits. The majority rejected blanket general-account exclusion and examined components by who bore investment risk. Already-guaranteed benefits qualified; the free-fund component did not. The Court recognized complementary federal/state regulation, with federal law controlling an actual incompatibility. It rejected an agency interpretation inconsistent with the limited statutory exclusion. [Majority, 510 U.S. 95-110](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/86/).

Thomas also rejected blanket immunity for all general-account assets. His disagreement was more precise: “provides for” can include a present contractual arrangement for future guaranteed benefits, even before particular benefits become fixed. He questioned importing Securities Act risk-allocation precedents into a differently phrased ERISA provision and warned that a nominally narrow duty over free funds could reach the whole commingled enterprise. He would remand to examine the 1977 amendment rather than affirm the majority's broader classification. His approach is a serious alternative, not an argument that state insurance regulation always controls. [Dissent, 510 U.S. 111-123](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/86/).

The Department of Labor's position offered a third route: substantial deference to its general-account interpretation and industry reliance. But the majority found the 1975 interpretive bulletin concerned prohibited transactions, lacked an explanation reconciling a blanket exclusion with the statutory limitation, and had not generated a consistently maintained agency resolution of the precise dispute. Stone should evaluate the quality and scope of that interpretation rather than equate agency inconsistency with automatic loss of all deference. Chevron and Skidmore were then applicable law; later administrative-law doctrine should not be silently inserted into OT1993.

#### Stone baseline and initial prediction

Fabe strongly suggests disaggregating the instrument by actual function rather than treating every insurer-related fund identically. Cipollone and Easterwood reinforce analysis of the precise duty. Greater Washington shows that Stone will enforce ERISA when the relevant statutory connection exists, but does not endorse sweeping displacement by relational language. His initial prediction is therefore affirmance of fiduciary coverage for the nonguaranteed investment component, with state insurance regulation preserved wherever compatible. This prediction is an inference, since the simulation contains no Hancock entry.

An uncritical extension would nevertheless be inadequate. Fabe's insurance-benefit test does not answer the different words of §401. The contractual expectation of future guaranteed benefits is not necessarily fictitious. Nor is the problem of allocating duties in a common asset pool imaginary. Most importantly, imposing fiduciary status does not alone identify the fiduciary act, establish breach, or supply a remedy. Stone's PRASA/California separation of legal questions should operate here too.

#### Recommended disposition and reasoning

**Affirm, with a narrowing concurrence or equivalent limiting language in an opinion Stone can join.** Reject the proposition that depositing pension funds in the general account immunizes discretionary management. Apply the enacted “to the extent” limitation to each contract component. An enforceable guarantee can relate to future benefits; immediate payment or present individual vesting is unnecessary. But the mere possibility of later purchasing an annuity cannot transform an otherwise nonguaranteed investment pool into present guaranteed-benefit insurance. Hancock's free funds lacked a genuine guarantee of benefit amounts during the relevant accumulation phase, particularly with the expressly nonguaranteed payment option.

Risk allocation is evidence explaining whether a benefit is guaranteed, not an independent federal rule about how insurers ought to invest. Stone should not announce a judicially selected minimum “reasonable” investment yield for every valid guarantee. The majority's reasonable-return language is defensible as a warning against a merely nominal guarantee that leaves the investment component effectively unprotected; treated as a free-standing rate requirement, it exceeds what this contract requires the Court to decide. Preserve the possibility of different genuine guarantees under different contract terms.

The federalism reasoning should be exact. McCarran-Ferguson preserves state insurance law against federal statutes that do not specifically relate to insurance. The relevant ERISA provisions specifically address insurance contracts, so the Act does not reserve this entire relationship exclusively to States. ERISA's own insurance saving clause remains operative. Harmonize the relevant state and federal duties where possible, and identify an actual conflict before declaring a particular state requirement displaced. Do not turn the case into a general declaration that federal pension interests outrank every state insurance interest. [Statutory reasoning, 510 U.S. 97-100](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/86/).

On further proceedings, the lower court should identify the assets or interests and discretionary acts at issue, the applicable §3(21)(A) fiduciary function, each alleged breach, defenses, and causation/remedy. An unfavorable locked-in contract is not by itself misconduct. Nor does a fiduciary holding automatically give pension beneficiaries ownership priority in an insurer insolvency. These limitations clarify the adjudicated question and use existing statutory categories; they do not invent a new immunity.

#### Change, hindsight, objections, and downstream effects

This is **extension and refinement**, not repudiation of Fabe. What changes is the application: state regulatory primacy under a congressional savings arrangement has limits where another federal provision specifically regulates the disputed function. Stone's functional method is also disciplined by the actual statutory language and distinct remedial questions.

**Retrospective evidence:** Congress added §401(c) in 1996. It directed a transitional regulatory regime for policies issued by December 31, 1998, required administrative feasibility and beneficiary protection, prescribed disclosure and independent fiduciary approval, and required prudent general-account management taking account of all obligations supported by the enterprise. It provided compliance protection and substantial retrospective relief, while expressly preserving civil actions commenced before November 7, 1995. This was not a complete restoration of blanket general-account immunity, and the pending Hancock action was not erased by the transition. [29 U.S.C. §1101(c) and amendment/effective-date notes](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/29/1101).

The implementing rule protects qualifying transition policies through specified disclosure, transfer/termination, and related requirements; it differentiates general and separate accounts, their investment risks, and their insolvency positions. [29 C.F.R. §2550.401c-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/2550.401c-1). This subsequent response validates the dissent's concern that classification could require administratively complex adaptation. It also demonstrates that Congress and the agency could tailor relief while preserving protections. It does not prove the original interpretation wrong or establish a quantified economic loss caused by the ruling.

The strongest counterargument remains Thomas's textual point that a contract exclusively funding future guaranteed individual benefits can “provide for” them before conversion. Stone should concede that general proposition and explain why the actual nonguaranteed investment component still falls outside the exemption. A second objection is practical: limiting responsibility verbally does not segregate a commingled balance sheet. That calls for careful lower-court implementation and authorized regulatory accommodation, not treating the whole contract as exempt merely because implementation is difficult.

**Direct downstream consequence:** component-specific insurance/pension classification and the requirement to preserve compatible state duties. **Plausible broader consequence:** an ERISA method resistant both to unlimited “relates to” displacement and to categorical insurance immunity. **No demonstrated consequence:** a new limit on Congress's Commerce power, changed sovereign immunity, or a constitutional duty to privilege pension funds over other creditors. Confidence is high on rejecting blanket exclusion, moderate on the best boundary for pre-conversion guarantees, and lower on the optimal mechanics of retrospective liability because the Supreme Court did not adjudicate every alleged breach or their economic effects.

### City of Chicago v. Environmental Defense Fund, 511 U.S. 328 (1994)

#### Inclusion, actual dispute, and environmental operation

This case belongs because it tests the distinction between regulating a local government's own waste-producing operation and requiring that government to implement federal regulation of third parties. It also applies Stone's Fabe/Easterwood method to a statutory exemption and supplies direct context for the waste-management financing cases. It is **statutory construction**, not a historical adjudication sustaining RCRA against an affirmative Commerce Clause or anti-commandeering challenge.

Chicago operated the Northwest Waste-to-Energy Facility. The record described approximately 350,000 tons of annual incoming municipal solid waste, energy used internally and sold, and 110,000-140,000 tons of annual combustion ash. Chicago disposed of ash in landfills not licensed for hazardous waste. EDF brought a RCRA citizen suit alleging that the residue was sufficiently toxic to qualify as hazardous under the regulatory tests. Chicago did not claim compliance with Subtitle C's hazardous-waste requirements; it claimed an exemption. The Court did **not** determine that all municipal ash, or every sample from Chicago's facility, was hazardous. [Opinion, 511 U.S. 330-331](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/328/).

Municipal incineration can reduce the amount of material requiring final disposal and recover usable energy. Those purposes do not establish the harmlessness of the residual material. The relevant choice was between a broad source/process exemption and characteristic-based controls on whatever hazardous residue emerged. Subtitle C distinguishes generators, transporters, and treatment/storage/disposal facilities; the full facility permit regime is more onerous than generator duties. Subtitle D supplies a different framework for nonhazardous solid waste. Treating these obligations as a single indivisible ban on municipal incineration misstates the dispute.

The 1980 household-waste regulation excluded household waste through its management stream, including treatment residue. Chicago burned household and nonhazardous commercial/industrial waste. Congress's 1984 §3001(i), entitled clarification of the household-waste exclusion, specified protection for qualifying resource-recovery facilities receiving and burning those inputs and taking precautions against prohibited hazardous inputs. The statutory list exempted treating, storing, disposing, and otherwise managing hazardous wastes, but omitted generating. The defined terms did not simply subsume generation. [RCRA framework and text, 511 U.S. 331-337](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/328/); [42 U.S.C. §6921(i)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/6921).

The city's objective was a financially viable public disposal and energy service, supported by a substantial legal argument about the exemption's history. Calling that argument protectionist or pretextual would be unfounded. Compliance costs would fall initially on facility operators and could reach residents, customers, or other municipal taxpayers. Avoided harmful releases benefit exposed communities and environmental resources. Border-based insider/outsider discrimination was not the mechanism under review; the duties followed the nature of waste and operations. Interstate disposal markets provide important background, but the Court did not make a finding that Chicago's particular ash crossed a state border or caused a proven interstate plume.

#### Historical decision and competing approaches

The Court affirmed, 7-2. Scalia's majority held that the statutory facility exemption did not exempt hazardous ash the facility generated. Stevens, joined by O'Connor, dissented. The majority relied on the enacted distinction between managing inputs and generating a hazardous output, the precise statutory definitions, and omission of generation. A Senate report expressly mentioned generation, but did not alter the operative enacted text. EPA's 1992 interpretation favoring exemption was outside the ambiguity the majority considered available. [Majority, 511 U.S. 331-339](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/328/).

Stevens offered a serious contextual account: Congress was clarifying the pre-existing household-waste exclusion, not covertly imposing a new generator regime. The title, Senate report, conference treatment, and regulation's background supported continuity. He also noted the counterintuitive result that mixing household waste with nonhazardous commercial waste might destroy a broader exclusion even though the added material was not itself hazardous. This was a disagreement about statutory continuity and Congress's practical intent, not simple refusal to regulate toxic material. [Dissent, 511 U.S. 340-349](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/328/).

Below, the Seventh Circuit reinstated its judgment after the Supreme Court directed reconsideration in light of EPA's September 1992 memorandum. Judge Ripple dissented: changing an agency interpretation need not eliminate Chevron deference, especially in a technical area left incompletely specified. That is a third approach: accept reasonable agency resolution of ambiguity rather than choose between competing judicial accounts of clarity. [985 F.2d 303, 303-305](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/985/303/206976/); [earlier opinion, 948 F.2d 345](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/948/345/286366/).

#### Baseline, initial prediction, and problems

Stone's Fabe method directs attention to what the exemption actually covers; Easterwood rejects extension from a federally regulated subject to every associated duty. Boston Harbor permits government operation of its own project but expressly disclaims immunity from valid federal statutes. New York preserves direct regulation while rejecting conscription of governmental regulatory machinery. The natural prediction is therefore no general ash exemption, together with a refusal to infer a constitutional municipal privilege from public ownership. This remains inference, not a recorded Stone vote.

The problem with an automatic prediction is that functional decomposition can become artificial when Congress actually intended a whole waste-stream exclusion. The dissent's legislative history cannot be dismissed as irrelevant merely because Stone values enacted sources. A genuine inquiry must decide whether the technical terms and structure overcome that context. Also, sudden application of hazardous-waste requirements poses testing, storage, disposal-capacity, and financing problems. The Court cannot infer that these will be negligible. Conversely, those burdens do not authorize an exemption the statute does not supply.

#### Recommended position and legal/practical grounds

**Affirm the rejection of the claimed categorical ash exemption**, substantially accepting the majority's statutory result while making three limits explicit.

First, Congress differentiated facility operations from generation. The specific statutory vocabulary and separate generator regime give the distinction a legal basis beyond a judge's environmental preference. An exemption from treatment-facility status for burning specified inputs need not exempt a resulting hazardous material from downstream generator and disposal duties. This gives the enacted exemption meaningful work while preserving the independently enacted hazardous-output controls.

Second, actual classification matters. Hazardous-ash requirements attach only if the applicable identification rules are met. The decision does not establish the toxicity of all ash, the sufficiency of particular sampling, or the exact point at which every facility's operations become subject to each obligation. These implementation questions should remain with the proper administrative process and subsequent fact-specific review. No invented constitutional concentration threshold or universal judicial facility design is needed.

Third, the city's ownership does not itself change the statutory duty. The regulated activity is Chicago's production and management of its own residue. The federal government is not requiring Chicago to legislate or enforce federal rules against independent waste operators. The practical obligation to test its output and dispose of it lawfully is analogous to an owner's own compliance, not conscription of a state regulatory apparatus. This is explanatory federalism context, not a necessary separate constitutional holding in a case that presented a statutory exemption.

Municipal public-service responsibilities deserve recognition, but the appropriate response is lawful implementation flexibility, appropriate compliance schedules/enforcement choices, and neutral funding or rates. The Court should not promise that broader costs are recoverable through unconstitutional discrimination in another case. Equally, its rejection of the ash exemption does not settle the constitutionality of any particular municipal flow-control system.

#### Degree of change and hindsight assessment

This is **clarification and application** of existing distinctions. It refines Fabe's functional method by connecting each category to statutory language. It confirms Boston Harbor's reservation that public proprietors remain subject to valid federal law. It does not prematurely decide Printz, a coercive-spending test, or a comprehensive post-Lopez theory of environmental regulation.

**Retrospective evidence:** EPA's March 22, 1995 implementation memorandum expressly recognized that Chicago left the starting point of ash jurisdiction undecided. EPA placed it at exit from the resource-recovery facility, including connected enclosed equipment. Its examples permitted fly ash and bottom ash to be combined before the determination when handled within the enclosed facility. Open ash handling could require separate determinations. EPA had also adjusted early enforcement priorities to permit testing programs and disposal transition, and then expected full compliance for ash determined hazardous. [EPA530-F-95-013, March 22, 1995](https://nepis.epa.gov/Exe/ZyPURL.cgi?Dockey=10000LBT.TXT).

This is observable administrative adaptation, not proof of a pollution benefit or net economic gain caused by the judgment. It supports limiting the judicial holding to the legal exemption and leaving a technically important implementation margin. It also exposes a possible weakness: jurisdiction tied to enclosure/configuration and combined testing can materially affect classification and incentives. Whether those practices adequately track exposure is a question requiring technical evidence; a court cannot answer it from the word “ash.” No verified causal empirical study in this research establishes Chicago's effect on aggregate incineration capacity, municipal fiscal distress, or health outcomes. Such consequences must not be asserted as fact.

The strongest objection remains Congress's apparent expectation of continuity with the household-waste exclusion, reinforced by the facility's financing reliance. On balance, the enacted technical omission of generation, and the meaningful distinction between a facility permit and hazardous-output obligations, are the stronger legal basis. Stone can respect the dissent's institutional argument without inventing authority for a wholesale exemption. Confidence in the result is moderately high, lower concerning precise technical implementation and net environmental/economic effects.

**Direct downstream implications:** source-specific exemptions, actual hazardousness, and ordinary federal duties on government operators. **Plausible doctrinal implication:** this supplies a useful factual contrast for later direct-state-activity cases such as Reno v. Condon, without predetermining their limits. **No direct implication:** a public-facility dormant-Commerce exception, state sovereign immunity from citizen suits, or a sweeping congressional power over every local environmental externality. Those require their own constitutional and statutory analyses.

### PUD No. 1 of Jefferson County v. Washington Department of Ecology, 511 U.S. 700 (1994)

#### Dispute, mechanism, and historical decision

The Elkhorn hydroelectric proposal would divert water from a 1.2-mile reach of Washington's Dosewallips River, generate electricity, and return the water downstream. The proposed residual flow varied seasonally from 65 to 155 cubic feet per second. Washington's Department of Ecology required 100 to 200 cfs to protect salmon and steelhead habitat. Those figures describe the competing proposals, not constitutional thresholds. The state hearings board and reviewing courts disagreed about whether the condition enhanced the fishery or preserved its existing use; the state courts ultimately sustained preservation as the basis. The Supreme Court did not independently conduct a fisheries study. [Opinion, 511 U.S. at 708-710](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/700/).

This was a dispute between public project sponsors and a state environmental regulator. Calling it a victory for either government or business misses its institutional structure. The utility and prospective electricity users benefited from generation; fishery users and others dependent on river habitat bore part of the diversion's cost. More water in the bypass reach meant less available for generation. No finding established discrimination against an out-of-state generator. Salmon migration and an interconnected electricity system supplied reasons why neither economic nor ecological effects stopped neatly at a jurisdictional boundary, but the case did not adjudicate the outer reach of the Commerce Clause.

Federal Power Act licensing ordinarily places substantial authority in FERC. The Clean Water Act nevertheless makes state certification a condition of specified federal permits. Section 401(a) identifies activities that may cause a discharge; §401(d) authorizes conditions necessary to assure the applicant's compliance with listed federal provisions and appropriate state-law requirements. Washington invoked EPA-approved §303 water-quality standards, including designated uses and antidegradation requirements. The relevant federal regulations included 40 C.F.R. §§121.2 and 131.12 as they stood in 1993. This was a claimed exercise of authority within a federal statute, not a freestanding state veto asserted against that statute. [Statutory and regulatory text reproduced in the opinion, pp. 704-708, 711-719](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/700/).

The historical Court affirmed, 7-2. O'Connor distinguished the discharge prerequisite from the permissible scope of conditions on the activity. Stevens concurred and would read the state-law reference more broadly. Thomas, joined by Scalia, argued that the conditions must be discharge-related; he also argued that the majority displaced FERC's carefully constructed authority over stream flows. His separate criticism of the majority's reliance on Chevron deference was substantial: the cited regulations did not speak with complete clarity, and the Government offered a narrower theory assuming for argument that conditions had to relate to a discharge, without necessarily conceding that statutory limitation. FERC had not yet acted, so the historical majority treated the asserted licensing conflict as premature. [Majority, concurrence, and dissent, pp. 710-737](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/700/).

#### Stone baseline and initial prediction

*Gade* and *Easterwood* direct Stone to identify the particular federal allocation before finding preemption. *Fabe* shows that federal legislation can preserve a bounded state role even against another federal rule. *New York* preserves cooperative arrangements. Those resources point toward affirmance if §401 actually supplies the authority. They do not establish that state environmental interests prevail whenever they conflict with federal energy policy. That conclusion would contradict Stone's own distinction between the importance of an interest and its legal source.

The mechanical prediction is therefore qualified affirmance, with medium confidence before statutory examination. There is no entry recording Stone's prior views on §401, the Federal Power Act, or Chevron. One cannot invent them from his later *Michigan v. EPA* roadmap.

#### Alternatives, recommendation, and degree of change

Three approaches merit serious consideration. A discharge-only reading offers a definite boundary and protects FERC's integrated licensing function. An activity-wide, water-quality-bound reading prevents a project from destroying the protected use of a water body while technically satisfying a limited list of discharge criteria. A broad reading of any appropriate state requirement would maximize state control but risks converting certification into general project regulation.

Stone should affirm and write a concurrence limiting the rationale to the second approach. The text changes from discharge in subsection (a) to applicant in subsection (d); that distinction does real work. Conditions must secure compliance with identifiable, applicable water-quality obligations. Designated uses are part of the statutory water-quality standard, not merely political aspirations external to the Act. A river with insufficient flow can fail its fish-habitat standard without a newly added chemical contaminant. The condition has to be supported by the relevant administrative record and remain reviewable; the existence of a discharge is not permission to attach unrelated economic, labor, or land-use demands.

Stone should answer Thomas's Federal Power Act objection more directly than the historical majority's prematurity response. This is recommended reasoning: a valid §401 condition binds the federal license because Congress specified that consequence. *California v. FERC*, 495 U.S. 490 (1990), rejected state stream-flow regulation under the authority asserted there; it did not repeal a different federal statute's express certification function. A generally important federal energy program does not nullify Congress's water-quality condition. Nor does this reading make all FERC fish-and-wildlife balancing redundant: §401 is confined to applicable water-quality compliance, while the FPA covers a wider set of project considerations. [PUD majority at 721-723 and Thomas dissent at 732-737](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/700/).

This is clarification and development of statutory federalism, not a reversal of an earlier Stone vote. The narrow concurrence should rest on the statute and its structure, with the contemporary agency view as corroboration, avoiding unnecessary dependence on disputed deference premises. It should reserve the reach of state requirements unrelated to water quality, and any specific claim that the flow determination lacks evidentiary support.

#### Practical basis, hindsight, and counterarguments

The practical case for contextual standards is strong. A statewide list of numeric criteria cannot anticipate every river, fish population, diversion, and seasonal relationship. Conversely, a designated-use standard without reasoned application can become unpredictable. The appropriate constraint is a demonstrated connection between condition and legally protected use, not a judicially invented insistence that all enforceable standards be numerical. Alternatives include redesigning the diversion, revising the generation proposal, mitigation consistent with the protected use, or declining the project; their feasibility is an administrative question, not an assumption that any alternative is costless.

Retrospectively, *S. D. Warren Co. v. Maine Board of Environmental Protection*, 547 U.S. 370 (2006), confirmed that a dam's release of water can be a discharge without an added pollutant. That strengthens the functional account of hydrological harm. It does not authorize all state objections to federally licensed projects. [S. D. Warren](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/547/370/).

The 2020 and 2023 EPA rulemakings illustrate continuing conflict over discharge-only and activity-based review. The 2023 rule returned to an activity approach bounded by water-quality requirements and acknowledged that its incremental economic analysis was qualitative because of data limitations. This is evidence of administrative contestability, not proof of either rule's net welfare effects, and the 2023 rule is cited as a dated development rather than a representation of the regulatory position in September 2026. [88 Fed. Reg. 66558, 66559-66561 (Sept. 27, 2023)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/09/27/2023-20219/clean-water-act-section-401-water-quality-certification-improvement-rule).

The strongest objection is that a state can use a plausible water-quality rationale to export generation costs to other jurisdictions. That concern requires genuine scrutiny of statutory fit and the record. It does not justify judicial removal of the state role Congress enacted. The same principle must sustain certification by a state whose environmental preferences Stone dislikes and invalidate conditions outside the statutory assignment even when he favors their aims.

The direct downstream consequence is a bounded state certification role within national regulation. Any connection to anti-commandeering is secondary: the decision does not compel a state legislature to enact a program or establish a comprehensive constitutional test of voluntary cooperation. Confidence is high on the recommended statutory result, moderate on the precise limiting language, and low on unmeasured economic consequences of alternative flow levels.

### Livadas v. Bradshaw, 512 U.S. 107 (1994)

#### Dispute, mechanism, and historical decision

California required prompt payment of final wages and provided a waiting-time penalty. Karen Livadas received wages three days after discharge from Safeway and sought the state administrative remedy. The Labor Commissioner refused to enforce the claim because her employment was covered by a collective-bargaining agreement with an arbitration clause. Determining the amount required looking up the wage rate, not resolving a dispute about the meaning of that agreement. California's minimum substantive protections were nonwaivable, and this agreement expressly allowed independent wage claims to proceed before authorized agencies. Substantive nonwaiver does not by itself prohibit arbitration of a statutory claim; the agreement's actual terms and the independent source of this duty matter. The asserted arbitration policy withheld enforcement without implementing an agreed exclusive substitute. [Opinion, pp. 110-115, 123-125](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/107/).

The practical beneficiaries were employers relieved of this administrative enforcement and an agency seeking to avoid perceived federal preemption difficulties. The burden fell on a class defined by federally protected collective bargaining. This was not an insider-outsider trade classification. The relevant institutional distortion was a state-created disadvantage attached to exercising a federal labor right.

The Ninth Circuit accepted the Commissioner's position; Judge Kozinski dissented, distinguishing ordinary enforcement discretion from discretion exercised on a prohibited basis. The Supreme Court unanimously reversed in Souter's opinion. The policy conflicted with federal labor law, the underlying wage claim was not preempted by LMRA §301, and §1983 furnished a route for enforcing the relevant federal right. The Court left remedial issues for remand, expressly noting the problem with retrospective official-capacity monetary relief. [Ninth Circuit, 987 F.2d 552](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/987/552/240782/); [Supreme Court, pp. 115 n.9, 116-135](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/107/).

#### Baseline, initial prediction, and the real tension

*Cipollone* and *Easterwood* strongly support preserving an independently grounded state wage duty. *Boston Harbor* rejects blanket labor preemption. *PRASA* warns that a right, cause of action, immunity, and remedy are different questions. Mechanical extension suggests reversal on the preemption issue. The §1983 and remedial conclusions are less determinate from the simulation alone.

A compatibility question for Stone's proposed reasoning, which the historical *Livadas* decision did not adjudicate as an anti-commandeering challenge, comes from *New York*: does ordering a state officer to administer a remedy conscript state government? Stone must distinguish a federal demand to create or operate a national program from a federal prohibition against administering an existing state program on a basis that penalizes a federal right. The mere fact that compliance requires administrative work cannot decide the issue. Otherwise the state could immunize its own discriminatory administration by invoking accountability.

#### Alternatives and recommended position

Stone could accept the Commissioner's categorical abstention as administrative caution; require use of contractual arbitration; invalidate the policy but reject a private federal remedy; or recognize the specific federal right and §1983 channel while separately assessing relief. The first approach permits a mistaken preemption rule to become a new preemption rule. The second sends this independent statutory claim into a contractual forum despite the agreement's express reservation of agency enforcement for such claims. The third requires a reason for treating the established federal remedial route as unavailable.

Stone should reverse and join the core holding, adding a narrow remedial concurrence if necessary. *Lingle v. Norge Division*, 486 U.S. 399 (1988), supplies the distinction between interpreting a collective agreement and merely consulting an undisputed wage term. *Nash v. Florida Industrial Commission*, 389 U.S. 235 (1967), and *Golden State Transit Corp. v. Los Angeles*, 493 U.S. 103 (1989), support the prohibition on state penalties for protected federal labor conduct and the remedial analysis. Section 1983, rather than the Supremacy Clause standing alone, supplies the cause of action for the sufficiently definite federal right. [Livadas, pp. 116-125, 132-135](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/107/).

The decree should remove the federally impermissible exclusion and leave lawful administrative choices to the State. It should not constitutionalize the wage amount, mandate creation of a state benefit program, guarantee enforcement of every meritorious state claim, or automatically award damages against the state treasury. Whether state officials could lawfully redesign or discontinue the program is distinct from whether they can selectively exclude workers because of federal labor activity.

This is a refinement of *New York*'s actor-and-duty distinction and an application of *Easterwood*, not abandonment of anti-commandeering. If the earlier accountability language were read to bar all federal orders affecting state administration, that overbroad reading should be expressly rejected. The legally established national right, the State's own administration of its program, and the character of relief justify the distinction. The distinction does not depend merely on describing an order as prohibitory rather than affirmative; later *Murphy* rejects that formal shortcut. The decree enforces the federal labor right, rather than California law solely because it is California law. [Murphy v. NCAA](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/584/16-476/).

#### Practical basis, hindsight, objections, and consequences

A routine wage calculation has much lower interpretive demands than adjudicating a disputed contractual entitlement. Categorical refusal simplifies agency screening but creates predictable overexclusion. Claim-specific review costs more at the margin; here it avoids sacrificing a statutory entitlement merely to reduce the possibility of a preemption error. This is an institutional comparison supported by the dispute's operation, not an empirical estimate of statewide agency workloads.

Later limitations on §1983, including *Gonzaga University v. Doe*, 536 U.S. 273 (2002), reinforce the need to identify a right rather than any federal interest. *Armstrong v. Exceptional Child Center*, 575 U.S. 320 (2015), separately cautions against treating supremacy as a universal private cause of action. Neither proposition requires rejecting the specific NLRA right recognized in *Livadas*. [Gonzaga](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/536/273/); [Armstrong](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/575/320/).

The strongest counterargument is that even a nondiscrimination order can dictate resource allocation. True, but a structural rule cannot disable otherwise lawful judicial enforcement simply because officials must work to comply. The limiting inquiry is what federal duty is enforced and what the decree requires. Direct consequences include preservation of independent labor standards and separation of preemption from remedies. Possible implications for later officer-relief cases are significant but not dispositive of sovereign immunity, which involves additional sources and rules. Confidence is high on reversal and the independently grounded wage duty, medium-high on the narrowly specified §1983 route, and deliberately limited on the remand's monetary relief.

### Hawaiian Airlines, Inc. v. Norris, 512 U.S. 246 (1994)

#### Dispute, historical decision, and Stone's baseline

Grant Norris alleged that Hawaiian Airlines discharged him after he refused to sign a maintenance record for an aircraft he considered unsafe and reported concerns to the FAA. His mechanic's federal license carried certification responsibilities independent of his employment contract. He asserted state wrongful-discharge and whistleblower claims. The airline invoked the Railway Labor Act's mandatory arbitration of minor disputes arising from collective agreements. Hawaii's Supreme Court preserved the claims; the United States Supreme Court unanimously affirmed in Blackmun's opinion. [Opinion, pp. 248-252](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/246/).

The employer sought a uniform contractual dispute channel. The employee sought protection of state rights independent of that contract. The federal arbitral system promotes continuity in interstate transportation; independent retaliation law addresses the incentive to suppress reports of unsafe work. Shared facts do not establish shared legal duties. The case neither decided the truth of Norris's allegations nor found the aircraft unsafe.

Stone's *Cipollone* and *Easterwood* methodology naturally predicts affirmance. *Morales* supplies a warning against saying that airline employment is local and therefore unpreemptable. The actual federal statute matters: this case concerns RLA arbitration, not an automatic answer under the Airline Deregulation Act or every federal aviation-safety provision.

#### Alternatives, recommendation, and justified refinement

A broad employment-dispute rule would send nearly every discharge controversy to RLA arbitration. An employer-defense rule would do the same whenever the employer invokes a just-cause provision. The narrower approach asks whether the asserted state right exists independently and whether adjudication requires resolving the meaning of the collective agreement. The historical opinion adopted the latter and expressly disavowed expansive language from *Elgin, Joliet & Eastern Railway v. Burley* suggesting that all independent employment claims are minor disputes. The RLA and LMRA are not identical, but the Court explained the convergence of the relevant precedents rather than deriving one statute's result solely from another statute's wording. [Opinion, pp. 252-266](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/246/).

Stone should affirm and join that analysis. An employer's assertion that it could discharge someone under the contract does not resolve whether it discharged the person for an independently unlawful reason. Conversely, a plaintiff cannot evade federal arbitration merely by relabeling a disputed contractual entitlement as a tort. Courts should identify the elements of the state claim, the contractual issue alleged to be indispensable, and whether that issue actually needs interpretation. This is a refinement in application, not a new presumption that all workplace torts survive.

This case also clarifies *Greater Washington Board of Trade*. Using an undisputed contract wage to calculate an independently specified legal remedy differs from making an ERISA plan's benefit design the operative source of a mandatory state benefit. Both cases involve a reference to a federally regulated arrangement; the legal function of the reference differs. Stone cannot use the word reference as an all-purpose rule in either direction.

#### Practical assessment, hindsight, and downstream limits

Uniform arbitration is valuable where disagreement concerns the bargain. Its value does not establish congressional intent to displace independently created worker protections. Courts routinely distinguish factual disputes about motive from disputes about contractual meaning, though difficult boundary cases impose screening costs. The strongest objection is that parallel proceedings can produce inconsistent factual findings and additional expense. That is a cost of concurrent rights, not proof that Congress abolished one set of them.

*American Airlines v. Wolens*, 513 U.S. 219 (1995), later preserved enforcement of an airline's voluntary contractual undertaking while displacing state-imposed consumer-fraud regulation under a different statute. The juxtaposition teaches source specificity: sometimes the contract is the surviving duty; sometimes the independent state right is. There is no general rule privileging contract over tort or private over public norms. [Wolens](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/513/219/).

The direct consequence is a workable interpretation-versus-consultation distinction in labor preemption. Application to medical-device or transportation torts remains analogical because each preemption clause differs. There is no adequate evidence here to quantify the effect on aviation safety, settlement behavior, or arbitration costs. Confidence is high in the disposition and test, lower in predictions about those consequences. No additional constitutional holding is needed.

### Department of Taxation and Finance of New York v. Milhelm Attea & Bros., 512 U.S. 61 (1994)

#### Mechanism, historical record, and disposition

New York's cigarette tax distinguished taxable purchases by non-Indians from protected purchases by enrolled tribal members on reservations. Licensed wholesalers normally prepaid the tax through stamps. The challenged rules limited deliveries of unstamped cigarettes by reference to probable exempt demand, required certificates and records, and preapproval of exempt deliveries. They did not prohibit sales exceeding the exempt allotment; additional sales could be made with tax stamps. The respondents were federally licensed, non-Indian wholesalers asserting that the Indian Trader Statutes barred the regulatory burdens. [Opinion, pp. 64-70, 75-77](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/61/).

The mechanism sought to prevent untaxed reservation sales from becoming an evasion channel for otherwise taxable consumers. New York and compliant retailers benefited from effective collection. Wholesalers bore paperwork and financing burdens; tribes and members risked curtailed access to genuinely exempt goods if estimates or approvals were deficient. The distinction was one of tax status within a special federal-tribal framework, not geographic discrimination under the dormant interstate Commerce Clause.

The New York Court of Appeals invalidated the rules. The Supreme Court unanimously reversed in Stevens's opinion, resolving a facial challenge under the Indian Trader Statutes. The Court relied on *Moe*, *Colville*, and *Potawatomi* to distinguish collecting a valid tax on nonmembers from directly taxing protected tribal trade. It explicitly reserved inadequate quotas, delays, allocation problems, and a Seneca treaty argument not properly presented below. The United States supported the challengers' objections, a reminder that deference to federal law is not deference to every litigation position of the federal Executive. [Opinion, pp. 69-78 and n.11](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/61/).

#### Baseline, alternatives, and recommendation

*Itel* and *Quill* reject tax immunity merely because commerce crosses a protected boundary. *Fabe* and *Easterwood* require examining what the federal statute protects and what the state rule actually imposes. Those suggest upholding a genuinely ancillary collection mechanism. Nevertheless, the simulation records no developed Stone theory of tribal sovereignty. *New York* cannot simply be transposed to tribes, whose constitutional and treaty position differs from states'. The initial prediction is cautiously favorable to the State, with a separate examination of federal Indian law indispensable.

The alternatives are categorical immunity for federally licensed traders, broad state enforcement power whenever taxable customers exist, or tailored ancillary regulation preserving actual exempt demand. Stone should choose the third and reverse the facial invalidation. The State must have an independently valid tax; regulation must be reasonably necessary to collecting it; federal licensing cannot be made dependent on a discretionary state veto; and exempt consumption must remain practically available. *Warren Trading Post*'s broad language should be read in light of its direct-tax facts and the intervening decisions permitting collection duties. That is precedent-sensitive refinement, not a free-standing judicial balancing of whichever sovereign seems more deserving.

The legitimate alternative of collecting directly from every ultimate purchaser is administratively weak in a high-volume cash retail market. Moving collection upstream can reduce the number of transactions that officials must monitor. Agreements with tribes, revised allotments, prompt adjustments, and workable exemption procedures can address implementation problems. None of this permits courts to assume that a badly administered quota is adequate merely because it was facially permissible.

The doctrinal development for Stone is source-bound anti-evasion reasoning: a valid substantive obligation may justify fitted collection measures. This is not yet the later *Hale* or *Raich* doctrine. Those cases would involve congressional power to prohibit conduct, different statutes, and different relationships between local conduct and interstate markets. Here the substantive tax's validity on the relevant sales was the starting point, and the disputed issue was federal statutory displacement of collection methods.

#### Hindsight, objections, and uncertainty

Later New York litigation shows the distinction between formal tax incidence and practical collection burdens remained consequential. *Oneida Nation of New York v. Cuomo*, 645 F.3d 154 (2d Cir. 2011), addressed a revised system and sustained the State against preliminary-injunction challenges, relying on *Milhelm Attea* while examining collection and exemption mechanisms. *White v. Schneiderman* (N.Y. 2018) likewise distinguished tax prepayment from direct taxation of tribal retailers. These decisions demonstrate adaptation and continuing litigation, not proof that every quota or prepayment burden is modest. [Oneida Nation](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca2/10-4265/10-4265_opn-2011-05-09.html); [White](https://law.justia.com/cases/new-york/court-of-appeals/2018/59.html).

The strongest objection is that upstream regulation can control tribal commerce in practice despite a lawful tax's nominal incidence elsewhere. Stone should take it seriously by preserving as-applied review of shortages, delay, entry restrictions, and costs. He should not minimize a sovereign interest merely because a market advantage arose partly from tax treatment. Conversely, protected tribal commerce does not entail an unlimited right to sell an exemption to nonexempt purchasers under the precedents governing this case.

Direct implications concern tax collection and the functional scope of Indian-trader preemption. Connections to later anti-evasion doctrine are analogical, with moderate confidence. No general conclusion about tribal immunity, state authority in Indian country, treaties, or commandeering follows. Confidence is high on reversing this facial challenge and substantially lower on applications not established in the record.

### American Dredging Co. v. Miller, 510 U.S. 443 (February 23, 1994)

#### Actual dispute and competing historical accounts

A Mississippi seaman injured on the Delaware River sued a Pennsylvania company with New Jersey headquarters in Louisiana. Louisiana excluded Jones Act/general maritime claims from its otherwise available forum-non-conveniens dismissal procedure. The company argued federal maritime law required consideration of inconvenience. Historical result, 7-2: affirm Louisiana. Scalia's majority found no displacement; Souter stressed substance/procedure as a useful but imperfect proxy. Stevens joined the statutory reasoning and judgment, criticizing the *Jensen* maritime-preemption framework. Kennedy, joined by Thomas, would require state courts at least to consider the defense. [Opinion and all separate opinions](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/443/).

The practical conflict is genuine. Louisiana offered injured seamen access to its courts and accepted the associated public docket expense. Litigants and local legal services could benefit; remote defendants and witnesses could bear travel, evidence, and litigation costs. But the record does not establish that the statute was enacted to enrich local lawyers or that a specific volume of trade was lost. Kennedy's concern about shipping defendants facing distant litigation is structurally plausible; those are not proved empirical consequences of this particular law.

The statute selectively treated maritime cases, so an account of neutral housekeeping is incomplete. Yet the case involved domestic parties; seizure of a foreign ship, international diplomatic conflict, and absence of constitutionally sufficient personal jurisdiction were not the issues decided. The company did not pursue personal-jurisdiction objections in the Supreme Court. Those distinctions matter when weighing the defense's importance.

#### Stone's resources and mechanical implication

*Cipollone*, *Easterwood*, and *Gade* require an identified source and subject of federal displacement. *United States v. California* rejects federal common law manufactured merely from an important federal interest. That suggests affirmance, but the analogy cannot simply erase admiralty's distinctive constitutional and historical authority for federal common law. Article III and the saving-to-suitors framework are themselves sources that must be evaluated. The Constitution's Admiralty Clause is not merely a Commerce Clause nickname.

A mechanical version of Stone might ask whether Congress enacted a federal rule and, finding none, let Louisiana prevail. That would be too narrow: maritime common law can be valid federal law. Another mechanical version could invoke national maritime uniformity and displace every state procedural variation. That would disregard the express preservation of concurrent remedies and statutory treatment of forum choice. The correct question is what valid maritime or statutory rule requires uniformity on this particular issue.

#### Recommendation and legal justification

Stone should affirm and concur in the judgment on a source- and subject-specific ground, closely aligned with Part II-C of the majority and the restrained core of Stevens's concurrence. The Jones Act incorporates FELA liability doctrine; *Missouri ex rel. Southern Railway v. Mayfield*, 340 U.S. 1, permits state forum-non-conveniens policy for FELA claims; *Bainbridge*, 287 U.S. 278, treats state-court Jones Act venue as governed by state law. Federal law itself therefore gives substantial evidence that this question can remain local. Having Jones Act claims remain while forcing dismissal of associated unseaworthiness/maintenance-and-cure claims would create practical dissonance, not meaningful substantive uniformity.

He should reserve broad reconsideration of *Jensen*, 244 U.S. 205. A rule under which any characteristic federal practice displaces a state rule is insufficiently precise, and the origins of the practice do not settle its current importance. But the parties did not ask the Court to overrule the entire maritime framework and this case can be resolved under either framework. Stone can decline an unnecessary re-endorsement of *Jensen* without purporting to overrule it in dictum.

Alternatives must be answered explicitly. Kennedy's minimum-procedure approach is narrower than making federal discretion dictate every dismissal; it targets Louisiana's refusal to consider hardship at all. It is therefore the strongest contrary position. Still, the statutory architecture allows state administration of these proceedings, and the alleged burden here does not establish conflict with an identifiable substantive maritime entitlement. Souter's functional interference inquiry is preferable to a categorical substantive/procedural label, but an unbounded balancing test simply restating free maritime commerce could recreate the uncertainty Stone objects to. Full Stevens repudiation of *Jensen* should wait for briefing and necessity. Full historical majority joinder is defensible if Stone expressly treats procedure as a proxy and preserves exceptions.

Degree of change: clarification and domain-sensitive extension of operational preemption. He learns that statutory and recognized federal-common-law sources differ, but both require careful identification; neither state autonomy nor uniformity decides a case by itself. State institutional autonomy extends to genuine courtroom procedure, subject to controlling federal rights and federal constitutional limits. This does not create a right for states to discriminate against federal causes of action or deny them adequate enforcement.

#### Hindsight, scholarship caveat, and downstream paths

*Yamaha Motor Corp. v. Calhoun*, 516 U.S. 199 (1996), later permitted state wrongful-death/survival remedies for nonseafarers killed in territorial waters absent a congressionally prescribed exclusive regime. That illustrates the durability of identifying the particular maritime problem, not announcing that admiralty jurisdiction automatically excludes state law. [Yamaha](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/516/199/).

The opposite-direction stress test is *Great Lakes Insurance v. Raiders Retreat*, 601 U.S. 65 (2024), unanimously enforcing a recognized federal maritime presumption favoring contractual choice-of-law provisions. A Stone principle that automatically gives state procedure or state insurance policy priority would perform poorly there. The defensible rule asks whether an established federal maritime rule actually governs this question and what authority supports it. Preserving Louisiana's state-court forum-non-conveniens policy does not mechanically decide a later bargained-for choice-of-law dispute. [Great Lakes primary opinion](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/601/22-500/).

Contemporaneous intellectual disagreement appears directly in the opinions. Stevens draws on David Currie's critique of maritime federalism's unpredictability and on historical work by Robert M. Castro questioning expansive uniformity claims; Kennedy draws on Alexander Bickel's account of maritime forum inconvenience. These sources are cited and characterized in the primary opinions; their independent texts were not retrieved in this subtask, so do not represent them as independently verified scholarship. The disagreement is real: preservation of a national trading system can support federal adjudicative rules, but the extent of displacement must still be justified.

No reliable empirical estimate of trade losses or litigation migration caused by the Louisiana rule was obtained. Hindsight supports doctrinal distinctions, not a causal claim that this result improved commerce. Confidence: high that narrow affirmance is defensible; medium that the majority's procedural explanation suffices; low that one can prove economic superiority over Kennedy's approach. Direct consequence is a restrained maritime-preemption rule. Broader consequence, plausible rather than certain, is resistance to all-purpose uniformity arguments in later preemption cases. No conclusion about affirmative Commerce Clause aggregation or executive commandeering follows.

### O'Melveny & Myers v. FDIC, 512 U.S. 79 (June 13, 1994)

#### Regulatory and litigation reality

The FDIC, succeeding earlier FSLIC receivership roles, pursued state-law negligence and fiduciary claims against lawyers after a federally insured California savings institution's insiders engaged in fraud. The dispute was whether the insiders' knowledge/inequitable conduct could be attributed to the institution and invoked against its federal receiver. The FDIC sought a favorable federal rule. The Court unanimously reversed and remanded for California law; Stevens's concurrence emphasized that state courts could legitimately develop creditor-protective rules although federal courts lacked general lawmaking authority. [Opinion](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/79/).

This concerns receivership recovery and professional gatekeeper responsibility during a real financial crisis. On the FDIC's view, attributing fraudulent managers' wrongdoing to a receiver could deprive innocent creditors and the insurance fund of recovery from negligent professionals. On the law firm's view, federal judicial alteration of defenses would impose different obligations from those attached to the state-created cause of action. Neither an asserted attorney breach nor a complete equitable defense had finally been adjudicated by the Supreme Court.

The distinction between federal receiver and federal insurer is critical. The FDIC was asserting the failed institution's claim, not suing upon a general federal damages right owned by the United States. The ultimate federal fiscal stake did not answer what law defined that claim. No insider/outsider commercial discrimination was at issue; public finance and institutional source, not the dormant Clause, create the connection to Stone.

#### Stone baseline, initial result, and difficulty

Stone's *United States v. California* entry nearly directly predicts this result: a federal fiscal interest does not itself create federal law. *Fabe* requires classification according to function, making FDIC-as-receiver distinct from FDIC-as-regulator/insurer; *Cipollone* and *Easterwood* demand actual displacement, not a general national-purpose appeal. *PRASA* further supports separating the identity of a public entity from each legal consequence of that identity. Initial prediction is therefore strongly for reversal of federal-common-law displacement.

The concern is substantive, not an inconsistency to be imagined away. Financial losses and benefits of weak professional-liability rules can cross state boundaries when federal insurance absorbs the loss. State law need not fully internalize that effect. Congress may thus have a powerful case for national liability standards. But that reason supports congressional legislation; it is not automatically an existing judicial delegation. The FDIC's proposed rule also sits inside an intricate congressional statute containing express special federal rules for limitations, repudiated contracts, director liability, and some undocumented agreements. Judicially adding one more exception may alter a compromise.

#### Alternatives and recommendation

| Approach | Supporting rationale | Main difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| National nonimputation rule for every corporate victim of insider wrongdoing | Uniform professional liability and creditor protection | Recreates general federal corporate/common law far beyond receiverships |
| Federal exception only when FDIC sues as receiver | More closely tied to federal insurance losses | Still modifies state causes and defenses without adequate source or demonstrated conflict |
| Apply California law, including its own receiver-protection exceptions | Erie, statutory succession, absence of a demonstrated special conflict | Allows interstate diversity and may complicate recoveries |

Stone should reverse and remand, join the core holding, and adopt Stevens's distinction between a rule's merits and the institution empowered to make it. A state-law answer protective of receivers remains possible. Federal courts should decide unsettled state questions using legitimate state-law methods, including certification when available and useful, rather than converting perceived sound policy into federal common law.

The legal basis is *Erie*, the limited federal-common-law cases, *Kimbell Foods*, and the FIRREA succession arrangement. But do not rest solely on retroactive application of FIRREA: the receivership predated the 1989 enactment, and the Court expressly refused to settle that temporal issue without briefing. Even without FIRREA, no sufficiently specific conflict justified the asserted rule. Stone should likewise decline any categorical rule that all unmentioned matters in every comprehensive federal statute necessarily belong to state law. Some statutes provide delegated gap-filling authority; some established federal-common-law enclaves remain. The necessary point is narrower: there is no such basis for this asserted receiver preference.

This is preservation with clarification. No earlier Stone position must be abandoned. It strengthens his distinction between a desirable federal policy and enacted or otherwise legitimately created federal law. The cost is administrability across numerous state liability regimes; the countervailing benefit is stable expectations attached to state-created rights and accountable federal legislative choice. A broad remedial gap that endangered the federal program could justify a differently supported federal rule, but the Court did not find that predicate here.

#### Hindsight and downstream significance

The most revealing subsequent fact is the same litigation's remand. In 1995 the Ninth Circuit again allowed the FDIC's case to proceed, this time identifying California receiver-protection principles as the basis for rejecting certain equitable defenses. The court vigorously disputed the Supreme Court's description of its earlier state-law reasoning while acknowledging the change required to the specifically federal portion. Thus refusing federal common law did not itself require allowing the professionals to escape the claim. [FDIC v. O'Melveny & Myers, 61 F.3d 17 (9th Cir. 1995)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/61/17/493343/); [original 1992 opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/969/744/238508/).

In *Atherton v. FDIC*, 519 U.S. 213 (1997), state standards governed even a federally chartered bank, subject to a statutory gross-negligence floor; stricter state standards could remain. That makes the same method operate in favor of broader liability rather than immunity. [Atherton](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/519/213/). In *Rodriguez v. FDIC*, 589 U.S. 132 (2020), the Court rejected a judge-created tax-refund-allocation rule among affiliated corporations despite federal tax and bankruptcy context. [Rodriguez](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/589/18-1269/).

These developments validate the coherence of source-specific federalism, not an empirical conclusion that state professional-liability systems always optimally protect federal funds. No controlled evidence establishes the financial effects of *O'Melveny* itself. The direct doctrinal path concerns receiver claims and federal-common-law displacement. A plausible broader path is duty-specific preemption and remedial separation. It would be a mistake to cite this case as controlling whether Article I can abrogate state immunity, whether a plaintiff has a Supremacy Clause action, or whether Congress possesses affirmative commerce power. Those questions require independent law. Confidence is high on legal recommendation, moderate on general administrability, and deliberately low on unmeasured fiscal consequences.

### BFP v. Resolution Trust Corp., 511 U.S. 531 (1994)

#### Dispute and historical competing positions

A California home was sold at a noncollusive mortgage-foreclosure sale for $433,000. BFP, subsequently a Chapter 11 debtor in possession, alleged that the property was worth more than $725,000 and sought avoidance under the Bankruptcy Code's constructive-fraudulent-transfer provision. The higher value was an allegation, not a Supreme Court finding. The question was whether compliance with state foreclosure procedure made the price conclusively reasonably equivalent value. The federal statute expressly covered involuntary transfers and defined transfer to include foreclosure of the equity of redemption. The relevant historical avoidance period was one year; today's differently amended provision should not be imported. [Opinion, pp. 533-537](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/531/).

The historical Court affirmed, 5-4. Scalia's majority treated the price produced by a noncollusive, regularly conducted real-estate mortgage foreclosure satisfying state law as sufficient. It rejected fair-market-value benchmarks and invoked longstanding state control of property and the stability of titles. Souter, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, and Ginsburg, dissented: the federal statute requires an inquiry into value, not merely compliance with state procedure. Rejecting an inflexible percentage-of-market-value rule does not establish that every lawful auction necessarily satisfies the federal standard. [Majority and dissent, pp. 537-569](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/531/).

The distributional conflict includes more than debtor versus lender. Junior creditors and unsecured creditors can lose equity when assets leave the estate at a depressed price; buyers and mortgage lenders value finality; all parties bear uncertainty and litigation costs. A lender bidding its debt need not have the same incentive as the estate to obtain the highest realizable price. Those mechanisms were argued in 1994. The case did not supply a general empirical estimate of bankruptcy review's effects on mortgage availability.

#### Stone baseline, mechanical prediction, and the flaw it exposes

Two Stone tendencies pull in different directions. *United States v. California* disfavors judicially manufactured federal rules based on federal fiscal importance; *Fabe* respects carefully preserved state systems. Those suggest caution about upsetting foreclosures. But *Cipollone*, *Easterwood*, and *Gade* insist that actual enacted federal law controls. Here Congress enacted an avoidance standard and expressly included foreclosure transfers. This is not a request to make federal common law because a federal fund would benefit.

There is no honest high-confidence mechanical prediction. A shallow state-autonomy extension predicts the historical majority; a stronger source-specific extension predicts a fact-sensitive federal inquiry. This is an unresolved methodological choice within the baseline, not evidence that Stone previously voted against bankruptcy avoidance.

#### Alternatives and recommended position

The serious alternatives are the majority's conclusive state-process rule, a fixed minimum percentage of ordinary market value associated with *Durrett*, and the *Bundles* approach using all relevant circumstances with a rebuttable presumption favoring a regular foreclosure. A fourth option would decide only that conclusiveness is wrong and leave elaboration of the valuation standard to remand. The percentage rule invents precision that Congress did not enact and undervalues legitimate forced-sale constraints. Conclusiveness removes substantive work from the federal words. A contextual inquiry best respects both.

Stone should dissent from the historical affirmance and vote to vacate the judgment and remand for an actual §548 inquiry. State-compliant, noncollusive procedure should be strong evidence of adequacy, not an irrebuttable substitute for it. The inquiry should account for the actual forced-sale setting, marketing, competitive exposure, encumbrances, condition, timing, and credible valuation evidence. Fair market value may inform the analysis without automatically controlling it. The debtor or trustee must prove the statutory inadequacy; no constitutional or statutory percentage floor should be announced. The recommendation does not establish that BFP wins on remand.

This is a clarification of the limits of federalism-based interpretation. An established state field is a reason to read federal language carefully, not a reason to replace a federal substantive standard with the State's procedural rules after Congress has addressed the transaction. Bankruptcy's constitutional source, Article I, §8, cl.4, makes national adjustment of otherwise lawful debtor-creditor relationships central rather than anomalous. No Commerce Clause holding or immunity doctrine is needed. [Souter's statutory and federalism objections, pp. 550-568](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/531/).

#### Counterarguments, evidence, and hindsight

The majority's best argument combines statutory ambiguity with institutional costs. Scalia did not require express displacement in every instance; he allowed sufficiently unambiguous implicit displacement. Stone's answer must be that Congress's explicit inclusion of foreclosures, combined with its value standard, resolves the relevant ambiguity against a conclusive exemption, even though valuation remains contextual. Uncertain avoidance can reduce auction bids, increase litigation, cloud titles, and ultimately raise the cost of credit. A contextual rule can therefore undermine the value it aims to preserve. Stone should acknowledge those mechanisms and the reliance of buyers, but the record does not establish that an irrebuttable rule is the only feasible response. The *Bundles* approach already existed, so remand would not invent a novel judicial apparatus. The statutory cause of action, limited avoidance period, burden of proof, and weight accorded regular procedures constrain review. These safeguards reduce, but cannot eliminate, uncertainty.

Ronald Mann's retrospective account interprets *BFP* as a conflict between statutory language and the Court's concern for nonbankruptcy secured-creditor remedies. That supports recognizing the institutional tradeoff; it does not prove improper judicial motives or settle the text. [Mann, The Supreme Court, the Solicitor General, and Bankruptcy: BFP v. Resolution Trust Corporation (2006), author abstract](https://scholarship.law.columbia.edu/faculty_scholarship/1426/).

Campbell, Giglio, and Pathak's later study of Massachusetts house transactions found substantial discounts on sales of houses following foreclosure, averaging 27 percent in their analysis, and neighborhood price effects. That is evidence that forced-sale conditions matter and that consequences can extend beyond the immediate parties. It is not evidence that a 27-percent discount is unlawful, that California's BFP auction had the same characteristics, or that *BFP* caused those discounts. This is a post-foreclosure resale estimate, not an estimate of the discount at the foreclosure auction and not a controlled comparison of competing §548 rules. Its implication cuts both ways: account for forced-sale conditions, but do not assume procedure mechanically proves substantive equivalence. [Forced Sales and House Prices, 101 American Economic Review 2108 (2011), publisher abstract and study information](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.101.5.2108).

The historical majority expressly reserved tax foreclosures and other forced sales. Stone should also reserve them: different sale mechanisms may retain surplus, auction different interests, or give very different opportunities for competitive bidding. No result in a later tax-foreclosure case follows simply from disliking *BFP*. Nor does this reasoning dictate *Katz* or state bankruptcy immunity; federal substantive priority and the forum for enforcing it remain distinct.

Confidence is moderate. The text and avoidance function favor rejecting conclusiveness; institutional costs favor restraint in the replacement standard. The recommendation is defensible without assuming the financial crisis, later foreclosure practices, or a particular future litigant. Its direct consequence is preserving Congress's actual avoidance standard; the wider cost of credit effect is uncertain.

### National Organization for Women, Inc. v. Scheidler, 510 U.S. 249 (1994)

#### Actual issue and historical decision

Two clinics alleged that an organized campaign against abortion providers violated RICO through specified predicate offenses; NOW was a co-plaintiff in the broader action but did not itself assert the RICO claims. Lower courts dismissed the RICO theory because the enterprise or racketeering lacked an economic motive. The Supreme Court unanimously reversed in Rehnquist's opinion. Souter, joined by Kennedy, separately emphasized that motive is a poor proxy for First Amendment protection and that defendants could raise actual speech defenses in later proceedings. The holding removed an unwritten economic-motive requirement; it did not establish the predicate offenses, pattern, ultimate liability, or every constitutional application. [Opinion, pp. 252-264](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/249/).

The regulatory problem was coordinated interference with operating clinics. The federal cause of action offered broader remedies and organizational reach than scattered state actions; its use against protest activity also created serious risks of expansive pleading and expensive litigation. Those risks required attention to statutory elements and actual protected expression, not an assumption that every ideologically motivated campaign was exempt or every interference with commerce was racketeering.

#### Stone baseline, alternatives, and recommendation

The pre-OT1993 file gives no settled Stone test for noneconomic local conduct under the affirmative Commerce Clause. *New York* accepts broad direct regulation within enumerated power, but it did not settle the later *Lopez* questions. The baseline's more immediate resource is accurate statutory classification: *Fabe* rejects labels in favor of legal function. An actor's motive does not determine whether the actor conducts an enterprise, commits a specified predicate, or affects commerce in the required manner.

Stone should reverse the motive-based dismissal and join the narrow statutory holding, with a concurrence reserving power and application questions. Alternatives include an economic-motive exemption, categorical immunity for political organizations, or a broad pronouncement sustaining federal regulation of all local conduct with economic consequences. The first two lack a sound statutory basis and misidentify speech protection; the third exceeds the question presented. The act's text and *United States v. Turkette*, 452 U.S. 576 (1981), support the limited holding. [Scheidler, pp. 256-264](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/249/).

Stone should avoid relying on the opinion's suggestion that harming a business drains money from the economy as a complete constitutional explanation. That observation answers the narrow motive argument, but generalized economic loss cannot by itself decide the full reach of enumerated power. A later case might present deliberate interference with interstate service transactions, a nationally organized enterprise, or a much more attenuated nexus. OT1993 need not erase those distinctions or prematurely decide them.

#### Hindsight and downstream discipline

Later *Scheidler* decisions in 2003 and 2006 rejected the asserted Hobbs Act predicates because deprivation or obstruction without obtaining property did not satisfy extortion and because the statute's violence provision was not a free-standing prohibition on all violence affecting commerce. That history strongly validates careful reservation in 1994: removing an unsupported motive requirement did not establish the elements Congress actually enacted. [537 U.S. 393](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/537/393/); [547 U.S. 9](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/547/9/).

The strongest counterargument is that narrow statutory reservations do too little to prevent the costs of prolonged litigation against expressive organizations. Courts should enforce pleading, predicate-offense, causation, and First Amendment requirements. An invented economic-motive exemption would both protect unprotected violence and leave some protected fundraising or commercial advocacy exposed, as Souter observed. The rule must apply to labor, environmental, civil-rights, and antiabortion organizations alike.

This is a modest addition to Stone's jurisprudential resources: motive, statutory coverage, constitutional power, individual rights, and remedies are separate inquiries. It does not constitute Stone's first *Lopez* holding, and it does not settle the file's future FACE cases. Confidence is high on the statutory disposition and reservations, low on any prediction of later Commerce holdings derived from this case alone.

### Beecham v. United States, 511 U.S. 368 (May 16, 1994): limited allocation study

Case and mechanism: Federal firearms law disqualified persons with qualifying convictions but recognized specified expungements, pardons, set-asides, and civil-rights restorations. Section 921(a)(20) directed attention to the law of the convicting jurisdiction. The defendants sought recognition of state restorations to remove disability arising from federal convictions. One lower court used the state where the earlier federal offense occurred; another used the defendant's residence. This disagreement itself exposed the administrability problem in a free-floating state-restoration rule. Historical Court: unanimous affirmance of the Fourth Circuit, requiring federal-law restoration for a federal conviction, while expressly reserving which federal restoration procedures might be available. [Full opinion](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/368/).

Stone baseline and initial prediction: No prior firearms or clemency vote is supplied. Nevertheless *New York* distinguishes governments' own authority and responsibility, and *Easterwood*/*Fabe* require reading operative provisions together according to their function. The most natural inferred position is to enforce the statute's selected source of law rather than permit whichever state's restoration produces a favorable outcome. Confidence in this prediction is only moderate because it extends a methodological commitment to a new substantive field.

The real problem includes reintegration as well as firearms control. States may restore voting and civic status; federal law may attach a separate consequence to a conviction. The strongest contrary argument is that civil rights are largely state-defined, Congress likely meant restoration to have practical effect, and denial of state restoration for federal offenders can leave them with few realistic avenues. That is a serious statutory-design criticism. It is not proof that Congress gave every state power to undo federal firearms consequences from a federal conviction. Nor can geographic federal-court location reliably identify one state's law as superior to current domicile.

Recommendation: affirm on the choice-of-law and exemption clauses read together. State restorations control the federal consequence when the statute incorporates the convicting state's law; the federal conviction requires the corresponding federal-law inquiry. Treat this as statutory source allocation, not an inherent constitutional bar on Congress recognizing state restoration. Congress can adopt a different incorporation rule within its powers. Reserve the availability and sufficiency of federal restoration, any constitutional challenge to firearms restrictions, and the interstate-commerce nexus of a later prosecution.

Degree of change: clarification, no reversal. The practical virtue is a predictable convicting-jurisdiction rule, with the practical cost of uneven restoration avenues. The opinion itself already recognized unequal availability among jurisdictions; no later trend is needed to invent that concern. The strongest alternative is a broader state-restoration reading founded on rehabilitation and federal reliance on state civil status, but it needs a defensible answer to which state's law governs. Downstream implications are direct for statutory incorporation, weak for Commerce power, and nonexistent for any categorical federal right to regulate all gun possession. Overall recommendation confidence: moderately high; uncertainty remains over relief procedures and constitutional claims not presented.

Concrete hindsight: *United States v. Bean*, 537 U.S. 71 (2002), records that congressional appropriations restrictions had blocked ATF processing of individual §925(c) applications since 1992 and holds that unprocessed applications did not supply the actual denial required for judicial review under that provision. The funding restriction is contemporaneous background; the later adjudication is retrospective evidence that reliance on an administrative route could be practically empty. This makes Stone's reservation about available federal relief consequential and counsels against describing federal restoration as an easily available substitute. It does not establish that no other relief, such as a pardon, existed, nor that the choice-of-law statute silently transferred relief authority to a state. [Bean primary opinion](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/537/71/).

### What should actually change, and why

The following ledger distinguishes changes to an established working position from corrections to an OT1993 proposal or restraint of a possible inference. This distinction prevents the appearance of evolution from being manufactured by first overstating Stone's baseline.

#### 1. Correct the source record before changing doctrine

**Previous account:** the attachment places legal/economic tax incidence at the center of *United States v. California* and compresses *Fabe*'s priority ordering. **Problem and evidence:** the full opinions identify a restitution/subrogation dispute in the former and four categories ahead of federal claims in the latter. **Response:** correct the account while retaining Stone's supported source-discipline and functional-classification commitments. **Degree:** factual and doctrinal reconstruction correction, not a new vote or repudiation. **Cost:** a less tidy narrative and a need to distinguish Stone's useful proposition from the actual vehicle. **Effect elsewhere:** strengthens *O'Melveny*, *John Hancock*, and the refusal to derive broad immunity from fiscal incidence. It does not convert legal incidence into a rule governing every kind of tax comparison.

#### 2. Replace loose cost-recovery permission with doctrine-specific proof

**Previous position:** *Chemical Waste* permits charges for actual costs, risks, inspections, and environmental burdens while rejecting origin as a proxy. **Problem:** diffuse environmental costs and accumulated public spending can be redescribed as an almost unlimited outsider surcharge. **Evidence:** Oregon's consultant attributed the relevant environmental costs equally per ton; the proposed general-tax counterpart did not identify an equivalent local event. *Lohman*, in the other direction, demonstrates that corresponding sales taxation can justify a use tax even though the legal events differ. **Response:** preserve cost recovery, require the legally relevant comparison, and distinguish a regulatory levy, compensatory tax, general revenue tax, and genuine user fee. **Degree:** clarification and refinement. **Legal basis:** existing discrimination, compensatory-tax, *Complete Auto*, and user-fee doctrine. **Cost:** proof and classification disputes. **Cross-case constraint:** Stone may not demand individualized cost invoices for ordinary general taxation or exact accounting where *Northwest* permits fair approximation.

#### 3. Make Carbone's operative act, not title, central

**Previous position:** *Boston Harbor* protects the government's own transaction while rejecting contractual disguise for coercion of independent transactions; the proposed OT1993 *Carbone* rationale emphasizes a favored private facility. **Problem:** Clarkstown already bore demand risk and arranged eventual public ownership. A title-based rule could change the Constitution's answer without changing the transaction. **Evidence:** the build-operate-transfer contract, throughput guarantee, town shortfall obligation, and Souter's contemporary dissent; later *United Haulers* makes the unresolved comparison explicit. **Response:** invalidate compulsory local processing in the independent transactions actually regulated, recognize the public undertaking, and reserve a genuinely different public-service case. **Degree:** narrowing and factual correction of the OT1993 proposal, not reversal of *Boston Harbor*. **Legal basis:** local-processing precedents and transaction-specific proprietary analysis. **Cost:** harder classification and financing uncertainty. **Cross-case constraint:** public ownership cannot erase *Chicago*'s statutory duties or establish sovereign immunity. If a future case is materially indistinguishable, Stone must apply the same rule or openly reconsider the earlier classification.

The attachment already warns that public ownership is not magical. This recommendation takes that warning seriously; it does not accuse the file of expressly adopting the very categorical rule it disclaims. The change is to prevent the planned sequence of opposite outcomes from doing work that only demonstrated factual and legal distinctions can do.

#### 4. Correct West Lynn's economic explanation and specify integration

**Previous proposal:** the neutral dealer assessment and producer subsidy shift the burden away from local producers and leave interstate competitors bearing it. **Problem:** dealers paid the tax, farmers received payments, and the record did not prove the asserted price effect on outsiders. An unlimited aggregate-incidence inquiry would also threaten ordinary budgets and subsidies. **Evidence:** the order's formula and earmarking, federal milk-market regulation, the separate legal actors, and the Commissioner's findings. **Response:** identify a legally integrated levy-and-payment program that grants a competitive preference within the same commodity market; do not call it a literal refund or require proof of every ultimate incidence effect. Preserve independent general-revenue subsidies. **Degree:** factual reassessment and doctrinal refinement. **Legal basis:** established practical discrimination and integrated-tax cases, with Scalia's administrability concern taken seriously. **Cost:** boundary litigation over formally separated but legally linked schemes. **Cross-case constraint:** account separation cannot automatically cure the same mechanism, and government assistance outside that mechanism cannot automatically be condemned.

#### 5. Specify which multiple taxation is constitutionally defective

**Previous position:** *Quill* and *Itel* oppose duplicative or territorially excessive taxes, without supplying a complete definition. **Problem:** different permissible systems may overlap; automatically subordinating California to separate accounting makes foreign law the constitutional baseline. **Evidence:** *Container Corp.*, the conceded unitary businesses, California's accepted approximations, and the limitations of both allocation methods. **Response:** retain review of discrimination, internal inconsistency where applicable, gross external distortion, and nonunitary attribution; do not treat every overlap as automatic invalidity. **Degree:** clarification, or narrowing if the previous language meant an absolute guarantee against overlap. **Legal basis:** existing apportionment and foreign-commerce precedents. **Cost:** imperfect coordination and expensive distortion claims remain. **Cross-case constraint:** this must not excuse Missouri's unequal local treatment or a later tax structure inherently penalizing interstate activity. The source's future *Wynne* direction remains possible, but requires its own analysis.

#### 6. Separate nonpreemption from congressional permission

**Previous position:** *Itel* demands an actual federal source for displacement. **Problem:** affirming *Barclays* by treating legislative inaction as authorization would undermine the clear-authorization requirement used against Clarkstown. **Evidence:** the enacted treaty's scope, failed legislative proposals, nonbinding executive communications, and Blackmun's express objection to silence reasoning. **Response:** uphold a constitutionally permissible tax in the absence of a binding contrary rule, without deeming it affirmatively authorized. **Degree:** refinement, no changed disposition. **Legal basis:** the difference between retained state authority, constitutional validity, and congressional authorization. **Cost:** the Court cannot use silence as an easy resolution of political uncertainty. **Cross-case constraint:** preserve the possibility of actual treaty, statutory, or binding executive displacement; do not announce that the Executive can never preempt.

#### 7. Clarify that federalism does not rewrite statutory standards

**Previous resources:** state-law respect in *California* and functional preservation in *Fabe*, alongside enforcement of actual federal displacement in *Morales*, *Gade*, and *Easterwood*. **Problem:** deference can become substantive erasure when Congress has already addressed the transaction. **Evidence:** *BFP*'s express inclusion of foreclosure transfers and value standard; *ACF*'s residual prohibition on discriminatory taxes and the dispute over property exemptions. **Response:** preserve the actual federal inquiry while constraining remedy and proof. **Degree:** resolution of a baseline tension, producing disagreement with historical results/reasoning rather than reversal of an earlier Stone vote. **Legal basis:** statutory text, structure, contemporary dissents, and existing alternatives such as *Bundles*. **Cost:** valuation uncertainty, title reliance, and tax-comparator litigation. **Cross-case constraint:** these decisions do not authorize federal common law whenever a federal creditor would benefit. Congress's enacted standard is precisely what *O'Melveny* lacks.

The cases differ in strength. *BFP* squarely presents a federal value inquiry displaced by a conclusive procedural proxy. *ACF* presents a more difficult question whether Congress deliberately excluded exempt property from one part of its discrimination scheme. Stone should acknowledge that textual objection and choose a limited remand rather than assert that all tax exemptions must be shared with railroads. The narrower and more candid recommendation is stronger than a categorical federal victory.

#### 8. Make accountability structural without depending on a universal voter psychology

**Previous position:** *New York* ties anti-commandeering to political accountability and voter uncertainty about responsibility. **Problem:** voluntary cooperation, federal preemption, and ordinary judicial relief can also make responsibility complex. Complexity cannot be the operative constitutional wrong if these remain valid. **Evidence:** *New York*'s own preserved alternatives, *Hodel*, *Garcia*, *FERC*, and *Livadas*'s actual enforcement problem; scholarship does not establish universal confusion or inefficiency from commandeering. **Response:** retain accountability as a reason, emphasize control over governmental priorities and resources, and distinguish conscription from compliance with an independently valid federal duty. **Degree:** refinement of rationale, no change in the take-title result. **Cost:** difficult boundary cases remain. **Cross-case constraint:** do not decide *Printz*, spending coercion, or state-court obligations by a loose analogy; do not use a merely prohibitory grammatical form as the distinction.

#### 9. Expand the source vocabulary without abandoning source discipline

**Previous position:** Stone repeatedly insists on enacted displacement in statutory preemption cases and rejects federal fiscal interest as a substitute for law. **Problem:** universalizing that language would deny legitimate maritime common law, direct constitutional restrictions, and valid regulations or treaties. **Evidence:** *American Dredging*'s actual maritime framework and *O'Melveny*'s recognition of limited federal-common-law domains. **Response:** require an identified valid federal source and show its scope, including any legally established field or protected deregulation. **Degree:** clarification and extension, not abandonment. **Cost:** the boundaries of recognized federal common law require judgment. **Cross-case constraint:** avoid both automatic federal exclusivity and the equally inaccurate requirement of an identical affirmative federal rule for every preempted state duty.

#### 10. Keep functional classification subordinate to the relevant statute

**Previous position:** *Fabe* classifies provisions by their policyholder-protection function. **Problem:** its test cannot simply decide ERISA's guaranteed-benefit language or RCRA's listed exemptions. **Evidence:** Thomas's textual future-guarantee argument in *John Hancock* and Stevens's continuity argument in *Chicago*. **Response:** disaggregate the particular contract or regulated act using the operative statutory categories; distinguish status, act, breach, and remedy. **Degree:** extension with limits. **Legal basis:** ERISA's component limitation and RCRA's technical terms. **Cost:** implementation is complex; transition and reliance deserve lawful accommodation. **Cross-case constraint:** a functional method is not judicial permission to disregard legal form whenever the judge prefers an economic description.

No preterm Stone principle needs full repudiation on the supplied evidence. Some broad readings and proposed explanations should be expressly rejected. That conclusion results from the source's limited commitments and the research, rather than a premise that consistency must be preserved. If a fuller simulation opinion later establishes a categorical title rule, absolute immunity from international overlap, or immunity from all federal administrative orders, the degree of change would become greater. This report does not invent those earlier holdings to create dramatic reversals.

The case studies link the underlying materials and distinguish their methods. The common lesson is restraint in empirical claims. Several later developments support the proposed legal distinctions, but often they chiefly falsify an overbroad assumption. That is a legitimate use of hindsight even where it does not select a unique constitutional outcome.

### Downstream paths and tests across changed facts

The most reliable downstream consequences concern the same legal mechanism. More distant analogies can guide questions but should not be presented as predicted holdings.

**Discrimination and public services.** The recommended *Carbone* rationale directly supports examining the difference between Smithtown's coercive ordinance and its purchased services in *SSC*. It leaves *United Haulers* genuinely open. If Stone later upholds a publicly owned facility, he must identify a relevant operational difference, not simply quote the word public. The same problem follows into *Davis*: government borrowing may present a distinct fiscal relationship and strong reliance interests, but neither public identity nor tradition supplies a general constitutional exemption. Confidence in the relevance of this issue is high; confidence in one predetermined outcome for every public-service case is moderate or lower.

The test must also work when the selected operator is an out-of-state corporation that owns a local plant. A local-processing reservation can exclude out-of-state services even if its shareholders live elsewhere. Conversely, an open, geographically neutral procurement does not become unlawful merely because one local contractor wins. The relevant comparison concerns the service or transaction, rather than an intuition about the political identity of its beneficiary.

**Subsidies and tax integration.** *West Lynn* directly informs later schemes whose levies and preferential payments are legally joined. It does not decide unrelated general subsidies, environmental-service purchases, or benefits lacking the same competitive link to disadvantaged interstate suppliers in the assessed commodity market. *Walsh* illustrates the significance of the beneficiary relationship; *Camps Newfound* tests whether noncommercial form erases an interstate preference. These later cases should probe the rule's scope, not turn every favorable state expenditure into a constitutional tax credit. The hardest unresolved issue is how much legal coordination suffices when a legislature changes accounts, enactment dates, or formal labels. A physical tracing requirement is too manipulable; an entire-budget netting rule is too broad.

**State tax structure.** *Lohman* and *Barclays* together support testing the source of the interstate disadvantage. A State cannot offset a discriminatory burden on one transaction by favorable treatment of unrelated transactions elsewhere; it can, however, identify a genuine corresponding local tax or use an otherwise reasonable apportionment method. Later *Jefferson Lines*, *Wynne*, and *MeadWestvaco* test different parts of this architecture: taxable event, structural interstate disadvantage, and actual unitary connection. Those links are direct doctrinal concerns but do not determine every result. *Wayfair* would require a separate evaluation of the physical-presence rule and reliance, because this source never establishes Stone's *Quill* vote.

**Statutory preemption.** *Hawaiian Airlines* and *Livadas* give Stone useful resources for the source and interpretation of a state duty. They do not establish that contracts always survive or torts always fail. The attachment's later *Wolens*, *Myrick*, *Medtronic*, *Geier*, *Crosby*, and *Arizona* sequence remains plausible only through examination of the particular statute and challenged duty. A lawful federal choice to preserve options can create a conflict; federal silence without such a choice cannot do that work automatically. A state law with a sympathetic aim can still alter Congress's chosen system. A state law inconvenient to a national firm can still survive.

**Direct federal regulation and cooperation.** *Chicago* provides a factual example of a government operating a facility subject to a national statutory rule; *PUD* supplies a congressionally assigned state role; *Livadas* supplies enforcement of a specific national constraint on state administration. These distinctions can inform later direct-state-activity and officer-relief cases. They do not constitute an advance holding in *Reno v. Condon* or *Printz*. Nor do they turn the expensive consequences of federal fallback into the attachment's mature spending-coercion rule. The legal forms of the arrangements, the federal sources, and the actual institutional demands remain indispensable.

**Affirmative Commerce power.** *Scheidler* removes a statutory economic-motive requirement; *Milhelm Attea* sustains a bounded collection mechanism against a particular facial preemption challenge. Neither establishes Congress's power over every noneconomic act or validates every anti-evasion rationale. These cases can train Stone to identify an actual mechanism, but *Lopez*, *Morrison*, *Raich*, and the source's lower-court simulation vehicles still require independent constitutional work. There is no defensible causal chain from invalidating Oregon's surcharge to a predetermined vote about guns, violence, local production, or compulsory market entry.

**Immunity and remedies.** *PRASA*, *Livadas*, *O'Melveny*, and *BFP* support separating a substantive duty from who may enforce it, where, and with what relief. They do not decide *Hess* entity status or *Seminole Tribe* abrogation. Public-service status under one doctrine cannot serve as an all-purpose immunity classification. Likewise, *BFP*'s Bankruptcy Clause reasoning does not answer *Hood* or *Katz*: adjustment of substantive property rights and immunity from suit are different questions even when bankruptcy supplies the setting. The relevant connection is methodological, not a claimed doctrinal deduction.

Three further tests expose whether the architecture is opportunistic. First, if Congress validly and clearly authorizes a specified discriminatory waste or milk arrangement, Stone's dormant-Commerce objection must respond to that authorization rather than preserve his preferred policy outcome; other constitutional constraints remain separate. Second, if a neutral environmental or labor standard burdens mostly out-of-state producers without preferring local competitors, Stone must demand an actual interstate burden under the applicable doctrine rather than infer protectionism from geography alone. Third, when federal law expressly preserves state choice, Stone must respect that choice even if uniform national administration seems easier. The principles need to survive each change in political valence.

### Stone after OT1993: the architecture, not a label

#### What the Commerce Clause structurally protects

Stone should now articulate a national commercial relationship in which state boundaries ordinarily cannot be used to allocate commercial privilege or impose a special competitive handicap on outside transactions. That is an inference from constitutional structure, developed through precedent and subject to Congress's proper authority; it is not an explicit textual ban on all economic inefficiency. Courts protect that relationship by examining actual markets and fiscal counterparts. They do not guarantee the cheapest supplier access to every government contract, freedom from public-service financing, or uniform regulation throughout the country.

This account answers a tension left unexplained in the baseline. Stone can accept a limited negative-Commerce inference while resisting judicial invention of federal exclusivity in ordinary statutory and common-law disputes. The former rests on an established constitutional doctrine concerning relations among States, together with Congress's power to authorize exceptions. The latter asks whether a valid source actually displaced a particular state rule. The distinction does not eliminate the serious textual objection to dormant-Commerce doctrine. It explains why Stone's response should be constrained enforcement of its established core, rather than either unlimited judicial economic review or unexamined abolition.

#### The preserved domain of state authority

States retain room to tax connected activity, regulate hazards, enforce independent labor and private-law duties, purchase services, provide infrastructure, choose among reasonable fiscal methods, and exercise authority Congress has preserved. This space is legally significant even where state decisions affect national firms or international markets. It is not a reward for wise policy. Constitutional permission includes imperfect experiments and tradeoffs a judge might not choose.

State experimentation, local knowledge, and diversity help explain why preserving such space can be valuable. They are not universal empirical propositions or exceptions to otherwise applicable law. Cross-border harms, capture, resource inequality, and underinvestment in useful experimentation can point in the other direction. The report's scholarship supports an inquiry into those mechanisms, not an automatic decentralization theorem.

#### Discriminatory and nondiscriminatory regulation

Discrimination triggers demanding review. Stone should identify the relevant commercial comparison, allow the State to prove a genuine legitimate purpose and inadequate nondiscriminatory alternatives where existing doctrine permits, and reject revenue needs or sincere public motives as sufficient by themselves. Origin words are highly probative, but the analysis also reaches a legally integrated competitive preference or a local-processing reservation.

Nondiscriminatory regulation belongs in a different inquiry. Stone should retain restrained *Pike* review, requiring a demonstrated burden on interstate commerce rather than treating every compliance expense as constitutionally cognizable in the same way. Market exclusion, incompatible interstate requirements, or duplicative compelled facilities can matter. The court must evaluate the actual evidence and institutional comparability of burdens and benefits. It should not assume every national supply chain has a constitutional entitlement to the least demanding State's law. This is an articulation of how to use an existing standard, not a new numerical or categorical test.

The unresolved tension is unavoidable: a narrower standard reduces economic policymaking by judges but can miss sophisticated barriers; a broader standard risks recreating judicial supervision of ordinary economic policy. Stone should make the claimed interstate mechanism explicit and explain the standard applied, rather than conceal this choice behind the word balancing. No core OT1993 holding requires abolishing *Pike*.

#### Taxation and public finance

Stone's tax theory now has distinct components. Connection identifies what the State may tax. Fair attribution limits the portion properly assigned to it. Nondiscrimination checks the relative burden on interstate activity. A genuine fiscal counterpart can justify a use tax; unrelated favorable treatment cannot average away discrimination. Fees can use defensible approximations. General taxation need not track individualized service costs. International overlap demands analysis of its cause rather than automatic immunity or automatic deference.

Public finance is a legitimate governmental function but not an all-purpose defense. *Carbone* and *West Lynn* invalidate particular methods while recognizing real financing problems. *Northwest* and *Barclays* preserve methods that survive the relevant legal inquiry. Congress and state institutions choose replacement systems within lawful bounds; courts should avoid directing a tax rate, financing instrument, contractor, refund plan, or liability allocation beyond what the remedy requires.

#### Courts, Congress, and federal sources

Courts should enforce constitutional nondiscrimination and enacted legal choices, identify legitimate federal-common-law domains, and preserve remedial limits. Congress supplies national regulation, harmonization, appropriate authorization, and changes to statutory designs that experience exposes as costly. States govern within the space left by constitutional limits and controlling federal law. Legislative inaction supplies neither an automatic federal veto nor fictional permission for a constitutional violation.

Federal displacement should be explained in terms of a valid source, its subject, and the challenged duty. That includes express, field, and conflict preemption where actually supported. It also includes the legal preservation of state authority. “National market” cannot substitute for the analysis; neither can “traditional state field.” The different recommendations in *O'Melveny* and *BFP* are the clearest demonstration: a federal litigant's interest does not create law, but an actual federal standard cannot be erased to protect state procedure.

#### Sovereignty, accountability, and remedies

The take-title holding remains. Its rationale becomes more precise: national government may pursue its authorized ends through lawful means without appropriating another government's regulatory machinery merely because that route is convenient. Accountability supports this allocation of institutional responsibility. It does not prohibit all cooperation or every order that makes state officials do work.

The term adds a useful contrast between a State administering its own program unlawfully, a public operator complying with a valid federal duty, and a government asked to legislate for independent third parties. It leaves significant boundary questions open, including executive commandeering, conditions on major funding streams, compulsory consideration procedures, and the distinct obligations of state courts. That incompleteness is legally preferable to announcing the future roadmap without a presented dispute.

Remedies remain independent. A substantive federal rule does not automatically create a cause of action, establish state-entity status, waive immunity, authorize retrospective treasury relief, or displace a specialized enforcement system. Conversely, immunity does not itself erase a substantive duty. OT1993 strengthens this method but does not yet establish all twelve questions in the attachment's mature immunity framework as adjudicated Stone law.

#### Remaining tensions and the honest limit of the synthesis

The public-service comparison remains the largest unresolved dormant-Commerce question. Clarkstown's substantial public characteristics mean Stone cannot promise that later public title alone will distinguish *United Haulers*. Subsidy integration remains difficult at the boundary between a coordinated preference and an ordinary budget. *Pike* remains less administrable than a categorical discrimination rule. International attribution remains imperfect. ERISA's distinction between duties constituted by plan terms and duties merely calculated with reference to them requires continued care. Federalism-based interpretation remains contested in the close statutory cases, especially *ACF* and *BFP*.

Those are genuine costs and open questions, not omissions to cure with invented doctrine. The resulting jurisprudence is strongest when it identifies the legal relationship being protected, examines actual operation within that relationship, and explains why the governing rule permits or forbids the particular action. It is capable of revising a factual assumption, rejecting an overbroad proxy, and accepting a difficult statutory consequence without giving up its central commitments or turning hindsight into an outcome-selection device.
