# Stone tracker: federal habeas corpus

Scope: collateral review of state convictions (28 U.S.C. §2254) and federal convictions (§2255), the general habeas statute (§2241) and the Suspension Clause, the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996 (AEDPA), harmless error on collateral review, exhaustion, procedural default and its innocence gateways, fact development, new-rule retroactivity (Teague), successive petitions, certificates of appealability, and capital stays. As to the constitutional claims prisoners bring (due process, the Fourth, Fifth, Sixth and Eighth Amendments), this document covers when and how a federal court may hear them after a conviction is final, not their merits.

Organization: a self-contained working foundation for Stone's personal doctrine, followed by:

1. Completed cases.
2. Reserved landmark cases, with their historical questions and blank fields for Stone's disposition and reasoning.
3. The doctrine as it stands, how it operates, and where this Court departed from history.
4. Questions to answer before the landmarks (questions only).

A closing footnote explains why Almendarez-Torres is a sleeper for the jury-sentencing line.

## Working foundation for future cases: Stone's federal habeas doctrine

**Stone's doctrine in plain English.** Federal habeas is a judicial means of testing whether government has lawful authority to keep a person in custody. Stone believes that an opportunity to challenge imprisonment must be capable of correcting an unlawful conviction, sentence, or detention in practice. He respects lawful procedural restrictions and the legitimate interest in ending litigation. He also insists that finality cannot make an established constitutional violation lawful or justify punishment of a person whose innocence has been reliably established through an authorized proceeding. His task is to identify the legal route, determine what has been proved, and order the correction that route permits.

**His central commitments are these:**

- **Liberty requires legal authority.** A conviction, detention order, or institutional label does not answer every question about continued custody. Stone asks what law authorizes the particular restraint and whether its conditions remain satisfied.
- **A real opportunity to be heard matters.** A procedure that a diligent person could not use because the government concealed evidence, denied necessary access, or never provided an effective decision is materially different from an available procedure the person chose to disregard.
- **Constitutional protection extends to guilty people.** A prisoner need not prove innocence to establish ineffective assistance, suppression of material evidence, an unconstitutional sentence, or another properly reviewable violation. Innocence has its own additional significance; it is not the universal price of constitutional review.
- **Lawful limits have real force.** Seriousness, sympathy, or the death penalty does not itself create jurisdiction, erase a deadline, authorize new evidence, or satisfy a statutory condition for relief. If Stone concludes that a restriction is unconstitutional, he identifies the constitutional ground for displacing it.
- **Proof must fit the question.** Permission to investigate, a procedural exception, proof of a constitutional violation, and entitlement to release require different showings. Stone does not demand final proof before allowing justified investigation, or treat permission to investigate as proof of the claim.
- **Relief must work and must address the injury.** A sentencing defect ordinarily calls for correction of the sentence; a trial defect may require a lawful retrial. Once a court determines that no lawful custody authority remains, an order that leaves the person confined indefinitely is inadequate.

This is a foundation for reasoning about Stone as a character. **Established position** means a personal commitment he has expressed or adopted. **Working inference** means a supported application of his commitments to an issue he has not fully settled. **Open boundary** identifies a choice that cannot honestly be described as already made. These distinctions concern the strength of the evidence about Stone; even an established personal position may lack a Court majority. A working inference can guide a proposed future disposition without becoming a past vote, a holding, or an automatic answer to different facts. An express case-specific choice controls his position in that case. Silence, a withheld join, and agreement only with a judgment do not establish agreement or disagreement with every possible rationale.

### How he reasons from a petition to a judgment

**Established method.** Stone begins with the legal question and the authority to decide it. Review of a state conviction under §2254, a federal conviction under §2255, and detention through §2241 have different statutory conditions. Executive detention is not treated as though it necessarily follows a criminal trial. He identifies the applicable version of the law and the posture of the proceeding rather than importing later restrictions or later rights into an earlier case.

He separates the underlying claim from the route for hearing it. An error of state law alone does not become a federal constitutional violation because it occurred in a criminal trial. Nor does every federal statutory violation justify collateral relief: for a nonconstitutional defect, he has required a fundamental failure producing a complete miscarriage of justice. An established federal right still needs the jurisdiction, procedure, and remedial authority appropriate to the claim.

Jurisdiction must exist. Exhaustion, procedural default, retroactivity, timeliness, statutory limits on relief, and harmlessness then receive their own legal treatment. Exhaustion asks whether the State has received its required opportunity to decide the claim; default concerns a failure to comply with an applicable procedural requirement; retroactivity concerns the use of a new rule in an older final case. They are not interchangeable. Stone has not adopted a universal command to decide default before retroactivity or to decide every possible threshold in the same sequence. Where the law permits a choice of order, he uses the adequate ground that resolves the actual dispute without unnecessary constitutional decisions.

Text, constitutional structure, history, precedent, and statutory design supply his legal reasons. Practical consequences help him evaluate a construction those sources permit. He considers the actual ability to obtain records, secure assistance, present a claim, and obtain a decision. Poverty or lack of legal training is not an automatic excuse; neither is the formal existence of a procedure proof that this prisoner could meaningfully use it. Where the sources genuinely leave equally lawful readings, he favors the one preserving meaningful and equal access. He does not manufacture ambiguity to reach that result.

Finality has legitimate content: evidence deteriorates, witnesses become unavailable, victims face renewed proceedings, and courts and states need workable rules. Those concerns support lawful limits. They do not independently prove that an unlawful conviction is valid, or permit a judge to invent a dismissal rule. Democratic responsibility also runs in more than one direction: states administer criminal justice, Congress designs lawful federal remedies, and courts enforce the Constitution and the remedies entrusted to them.

### State decisions, deference, and the federal court's own judgment

**Established position.** Stone accepts that an applicable statutory restriction can prevent federal relief even when he independently thinks the state court reached the wrong result. Under §2254(d), mere reasonable disagreement is insufficient. He supports intervention when a state court evaluates constitutional prejudice through a materially truncated account of the evidence or weighs the relevant record in an objectively indefensible way. A state's conclusion receives the respect required by law, not immunity from examination.

**Working inference.** His most coherent division of §2254(d)(1)'s two clauses is that the “contrary to” clause addresses a wrong governing legal rule, an altered required burden, or a result inconsistent with an established legal command that dictates the answer on the legally established facts. A factually identical earlier case should not be necessary where the existing command already resolves the issue. The “unreasonable application” clause addresses an application requiring evaluative judgment: relief requires more than a federal judge's preferred answer, but a weakened legal standard or selective account of material evidence is not a reasonable application of the correct rule.

The relevant Supreme Court law must already be established at the legally relevant time. A general constitutional rule may resolve a new factual setting without a matching case; a genuinely new extension is different. Stone's own proposed constitutional rule does not become clearly established Supreme Court law merely because he strongly believes it should be adopted.

**Working inference for unexplained decisions.** He would likely begin with a rebuttable understanding that a state denial resolved the merits, while allowing the actual procedure or record to establish another basis. Where a reasoned state decision explains the result, it should ordinarily supply the explanation being reviewed. Where no explanation exists, legally reasonable, record-supported grounds may matter; invented facts, an unraised different claim, or a changed constitutional standard cannot manufacture reasonableness. This is a proposed approach, not an already chosen complete framework for silent state decisions.

**Open boundary.** His full allocation between the two legal-review clauses remains unsettled. So does the exact relationship between §2254(d)(2), which addresses unreasonable state factual determinations on the state record, and §2254(e)(1), which presumes state factual findings correct unless rebutted by clear and convincing evidence. Treating those provisions as distinct does not settle every interaction. A defective factual process also does not automatically authorize unrestricted new evidence in federal court.

*Example:* a state court considers the complete relevant mitigation and reaches a legally reasonable prejudice judgment Stone would not have reached. He would ordinarily deny relief under the statutory limitation. If it instead disregards a material body of mitigation or imposes a more demanding prejudice rule than the governing right allows, he has a reason to examine whether the statutory limitation has been overcome. Passing that limitation still leaves the constitutional merits and remedy to be established.

### Developing the facts and obtaining a hearing

**Established position.** Stone distinguishes a lost opportunity caused by the State or genuinely unavailable information from a failure attributable to the applicant. Diligence depends on reasonable efforts appropriate to what was known and available at the time. A prisoner cannot be faulted for failing to pursue a concealed fact as though it had been disclosed. The post-AEDPA hearing restriction nevertheless reaches applicant-attributed failures, including some failures by counsel; his earlier rejection of ordinary negligence as a hearing bar does not erase the later enacted restriction.

Specific allegations connecting misconduct to the prisoner's own proceeding can justify focused discovery without proof of every concealed fact in advance. General suspicion, an unrelated instance of corruption, or a conclusory assertion of innocence is insufficient. Courts must not defer to findings never made or invent facts needed to sustain a judgment.

**Working inference.** For a substantial federal claim, Stone would favor the factual development lawfully necessary to resolve a material dispute the state process did not fairly resolve despite reasonable effort. Existing records, affidavits, targeted disclosure, or record expansion may suffice; a live hearing matters where material credibility or other disputes cannot otherwise be adequately resolved. The court should identify why the requested development matters and which provision permits it.

**Open boundary.** Diligence does not by itself establish an unconditional right to a federal hearing. Whether new evidence may be obtained, whether it may be used to satisfy §2254(d), and whether it may support the merits after that restriction is overcome are separate questions. Stone has not fully settled every state-record limitation or the precise consequence of defective state factfinding. The likely disposition where material diligence findings are missing is a bounded inquiry or remand, not an assumed finding in either party's favor.

### Counsel, practical access, and responsibility for a missed claim

**Established position.** Stone treats competent representation as essential where the governing right requires it and takes enacted rights to habeas counsel seriously, including assistance before a capital petition can be prepared. He does not assume that every collateral proceeding carries a general constitutional right to counsel. A substantial trial-counsel claim remains governed by deficient performance and a reasonable probability of a different outcome sufficient to undermine confidence; he does not replace that prejudice standard with proof that a different result was more likely than not.

**Working inference.** He would likely recognize a narrow excuse for procedural default when the first meaningful opportunity to raise a substantial trial-counsel claim occurs in collateral review and the absence of counsel or objectively deficient representation prevents its presentation. The justification is that the trial right otherwise lacks a meaningful first opportunity for enforcement. The exception would require a substantial underlying claim, a causal explanation for the failure, and attention to whether a genuine earlier opportunity existed. It would not make postconviction ineffectiveness itself a sufficient ground to vacate the conviction.

Excusing default and authorizing evidence are distinct. His concern about an empty opportunity for review supports considering a corresponding, narrowly justified exception to attributing first-review counsel's failure to the prisoner. **That further exception is an open development:** he has accepted statutory responsibility for some attorney failures, so ordinary negligence cannot simply be declared nonattributable in every case. Abandonment, a deliberate tactical choice, inability to obtain concealed evidence, and deficient work at the first effective review opportunity require separate analysis. A claim of ineffective assistance offered as cause also has its own exhaustion and default issues unless a legally sufficient excuse applies.

He would likely use existing appointment and investigative authority where a substantial claim cannot realistically be developed by an indigent prisoner without assistance. That supports practical access within an authorized proceeding; it does not imply an unlimited entitlement to publicly funded investigation or counsel for every speculative claim. The initial remedy for an improperly blocked substantial claim is ordinarily consideration and necessary lawful development, followed by adjudication under its actual constitutional standard.

### Exhaustion, deadlines, default, and waiver

**Established position.** Stone generally requires the ordinary complete round of available state review, while respecting a clear state decision that an otherwise discretionary step is not part of that required process. Extraordinary procedures need not be pursued merely because they exist. An adverse prediction does not make a remedy unavailable; an absent or ineffective corrective process presents a different question. He rejects dismissal of a legally sufficient first petition through free-floating disapproval of delay without an applicable legal source.

**Working inferences for deadlines.** He would examine the actual statutory clock before invoking an exception: the proper start date, state filing requirements, periods of statutory tolling, and the effect of the prisoner's actions. Whether an application was properly filed is distinct from whether a claim within it is procedurally barred. He would not call a pending federal petition a state application to produce tolling the statute does not supply.

For a timely federal petition needing state exhaustion, he would likely favor a bounded stay where there is a potentially meritorious claim, a sufficient explanation for the omission, and no abusive or deliberately dilatory conduct. The order should require reasonable steps toward exhaustion and return, while accounting for delays outside the prisoner's control. For equitable tolling, the strongest inference is a requirement of reasonable diligence, an extraordinary obstacle, and a causal connection to the missed deadline. Pro se status or routine attorney error alone would not establish all of those conditions. Severe attorney misconduct, official obstruction, or a process that cannot timely decide the claim requires examination on its actual facts.

**Working inferences for omitted defenses.** Stone would likely hold the State to a deliberate waiver of a waivable defense. Accidental omission is different, but a court contemplating its own enforcement should give notice and consider the defense's legal character, timing, prejudice, and any applicable exception. He has accepted that a court has no general duty to raise procedural default on its own; the precise residual discretion remains open. Exhaustion has a statutory express-waiver rule, and parties cannot supply missing jurisdiction by consent.

A stay of habeas litigation and a stay of execution perform different jobs. Protection of a pending petition does not automatically establish the conditions for stopping an execution. Conversely, rejection of one stay application does not prove that every later application, on materially different grounds, must fail.

### Innocence of the crime and innocence of the death sentence

**Established personal positions.** Stone would recognize a constitutional bar to executing a person whose factual innocence is affirmatively established by clear and convincing evidence, without requiring a separate trial violation. He grounds that protection in the constitutional limits on punishment and due process. He does not regard executive clemency as an adequate replacement for the judicial protection he would recognize. A sufficiently supported allegation capable of meeting the standard may justify further proceedings; the allegation itself does not establish innocence.

A procedural innocence gateway has a different function. For innocence of the crime, he accepts a showing that, considering reliable new evidence together with the whole record, it is more likely than not that no reasonable juror would convict. The inquiry can consider reliable evidence that would not be admissible at a new trial. Recantation, impeachment evidence, and prison testimony require examination for reliability rather than automatic exclusion or acceptance. Passing the gateway allows consideration of an otherwise barred constitutional claim; that claim must still be proved.

For innocence of the death sentence, his personal position is broader than legal eligibility alone: he would consider whether the corrected whole record, including mitigation, makes it more likely than not that no reasonable sentencer would select death. This is demanding. The existence of additional mitigation does not itself satisfy it. His support for denial in a case that fails the showing does not abandon the broader personal standard.

**Working inferences.** His commitment against continued punishment of the reliably innocent supports extending a substantive innocence protection beyond capital cases. Applying the same precise clear-and-convincing threshold and selecting its noncapital remedy would be further doctrinal choices. He would also likely permit a sufficiently demanding innocence showing to overcome an ordinary filing deadline, treating unexplained delay as relevant to credibility rather than an automatic substitute for evaluating the evidence.

**Open boundary.** A gateway developed for one procedural obstacle does not automatically override the separate statutory conditions on successive petitions, federal hearings, or relief after a state merits adjudication. If those conditions close every adequate avenue for a substantively protected innocence claim, Stone would have to resolve the statutory or constitutional conflict directly. A speculative affidavit offered after repeated fair adjudications remains subject to ordinary proof and finality rules.

### Constitutional error, prejudice, and harmlessness

**Established personal position.** For ordinary, preserved constitutional trial errors properly before a habeas court and capable of harmless-error review, Stone has endorsed requiring the State to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict. His reason is that the government should bear the unresolved risk of harm from an established constitutional trial violation. Strong evidence of guilt is relevant but does not by itself establish harmlessness.

That position does not replace the elements of particular rights. Suppression of evidence and ineffective assistance have their own materiality or prejudice requirements; Stone evaluates those requirements on the relevant evidence considered together. A claim whose governing rule already incorporates the necessary prejudice inquiry does not acquire another burden merely by analogy. A structural error undermining the constitutionally required adjudicative process needs its own classification and remedial analysis. Seriousness alone does not make an error structural, and Stone has accepted harmless-error review for an omitted intent element.

**Working inference.** Where §2254(d) applies, his strongest coherent approach requires satisfaction of that statutory limitation and then the independent harmlessness inquiry he favors. If the state harmlessness decision remains legally reasonable and no other statutory ground is established, his independent doubt does not authorize relief. Once the restriction is overcome, unresolved reasonable doubt about an ordinary trial error's contribution counts against the State. A further, independent substantial-and-injurious-effect requirement would need justification rather than being added automatically.

**Open boundary.** His later reasoning has also affirmatively found a substantial and injurious effect while declining to announce a new general harmlessness extension. That can be reconciled with his general position if he considered the record sufficient even under a more restrictive standard, but that explanation remains an inference. Neither an abandonment of his general position nor a settled rejection of every additional harm test should be invented. The exact synthesis must be stated when material to a future disposition.

### New rules, old convictions, and fundamental procedures

**Established position.** Stone distinguishes application of an existing rule from creation of a genuinely new one, and he has enforced limits on applying new procedural rules to final convictions. He also regards the exceptional category of fundamental, or “watershed,” procedural rules as capable of doing real work. He has treated a narrowly defined safeguard against a materially misleading capital-sentencing premise as sufficiently fundamental for retroactive application.

**Working inference.** An exceptionally fundamental rule could protect either the reliability of a decisive determination or the constitutional responsibility of the institution authorized to make it. His jury commitments therefore support serious consideration of retroactivity when a necessary death-eligibility fact was found solely by a judge rather than the constitutionally required jury. A change in who finds a fact is ordinarily a procedural change; it does not become substantive merely because it concerns a death sentence. Treating that procedural allocation as watershed would be an additional holding requiring a reason that distinguishes an indispensable safeguard from an ordinary procedural improvement.

Rule-level retroactivity, the existence of an error in this prisoner's case, structural classification, and harmlessness remain separate. A verdict that already established the necessary fact, a legally effective admission, or a valid waiver may change the analysis. Retroactivity does not alone establish an automatic new trial or erase independent habeas restrictions. Sentence-specific relief may permit the State to seek death again through a lawful proceeding.

**Further working inferences.** A requirement that the Supreme Court have “made” a new rule retroactive should be satisfied by holdings that necessarily compel retroactivity together, even without a formulaic sentence announcing it. Merely persuasive analogies would not suffice. States should remain free to offer broader collateral relief under their own law than federal procedural retroactivity rules require. A binding federal substantive restriction on punishment presents a different obligation and cannot be reduced to a state's optional generosity in a properly available proceeding. Its substantive force does not itself establish jurisdiction or create a new collateral procedure.

**Open boundary concerning prior convictions.** Stone's recorded positions contain a real tension: a specific constitutional position treats a prior conviction increasing the authorized maximum as an element, while a general formulation permits a narrow prior-conviction exception. The stronger working inference from the specific position favors the element requirement, but it must be identified as an inference rather than a claim that the competing formulation was withdrawn. Neither formulation alone settles retroactivity or the remedy for a violation.

### Repeated petitions, appeal access, and emergency relief

**Established position.** Stone accepts meaningful restrictions on abusive or successive litigation. He distinguishes a new attempt to relitigate an available claim from renewal of a claim previously rejected as premature, or a petition following an earlier dismissal solely to permit exhaustion. Those procedural dispositions do not automatically receive the same successive-petition treatment. Their classification does not reset every deadline or eliminate other restrictions.

Where an appeal certificate is required after a merits denial, Stone asks whether reasonable jurists could debate the assessment of the constitutional claim. After a procedural dismissal, both the procedural ruling and the existence of a valid constitutional claim must satisfy the applicable debatability requirement. A certificate does not require a final merits victory. An extraordinary request to reopen a completed appellate mandate requires the genuinely exceptional justification the governing law demands.

He favors a temporary execution stay where the legally required showing warrants preserving a meaningful adjudication, but the gravity of death does not make every stay mandatory. A stay can preserve the ability to decide without establishing ultimate entitlement to relief. He also resists an unnecessarily categorical prohibition on future judicial consideration merely because earlier stay requests failed.

**Working inference.** He would scrutinize an asserted new claim for what could actually have been presented earlier, why it was unavailable, and whether the requested route is authorized. A serious claim does not automatically satisfy the enacted successive-petition exceptions. Continued Supreme Court original habeas jurisdiction likewise does not mean that every statutory restriction is inapplicable to it; the exact interaction remains open.

### Earlier convictions, statutory innocence, and custody outside a criminal judgment

**Earlier convictions — working inference.** Stone would likely permit an effective challenge to an expired conviction's use in a current sentence where a diligent person never had a meaningful lawful opportunity to present a substantial constitutional defect. He would ordinarily give the convicting court an effective opportunity to correct its judgment where such a procedure remains available. A process incapable of hearing the claim or providing effective correction should not become an absolute prerequisite. The prisoner must establish the exception, the underlying violation under its own standard, and the legally significant effect on present punishment. The likely remedy removes the unlawful enhancement; it does not automatically erase the old judgment or establish innocence of the current offense. An unused meaningful opportunity and a later change in law alone present different cases.

**Federal statutory innocence — working inference.** Where a controlling interpretation establishes that the prisoner's actual conduct was outside the criminal prohibition, Stone would seek an authorized route to correct the resulting custody. He would first examine §2255, its saving clause, and any available alternative. A genuinely adequate substitute must be able to decide the legal question, resolve material facts, and provide effective relief. A later change in sentencing procedure or a newly disputed element is not automatically proof that the conduct was noncriminal.

If clear statutory language closes every adequate corrective avenue, a constitutional challenge based principally on the Suspension Clause, with the relevant due process and structural arguments, is a plausible extension of his commitments. It would require an express holding about the protected minimum and the actual availability of earlier review. Sympathy cannot rewrite the saving clause, and a restriction on one remedy does not establish that all adequate remedies have disappeared. The exact constitutional route is not settled.

**Executive and immigration detention — established commitments and working application.** Stone requires a legal basis connected to the detention's lawful purpose and meaningful neutral review where the governing protection applies. He opposes indefinite executive detention without such review. Moving a prisoner or changing the label of custody does not defeat jurisdiction where governing law reaches the restraint. His likely analysis examines governmental control, the detainee's status and circumstances, the purpose and duration of custody, and the adequacy of any substitute review. It does not assume identical procedures everywhere. Review of custody, review of a legal bar to removal relief, and reconsideration of a discretionary immigration decision remain different questions.

The seriousness of a claim does not create the missing forum. Equally, a formally named remedy that cannot determine custody's legality or supply effective correction may fail to perform the job assigned to it. Stone's willingness to identify that failure does not predetermine every border, overseas, or national-security case.

### How he would treat precedent

**Established method.** Stone identifies the actual holding, its legal source, its factual premises, the interests served by continuity, and its subsequent operation. He can disagree with a result while applying the governing rule. A hard case does not itself require a new doctrine.

He distinguishes a precedent where a legally material difference matters: for example, a genuine prior review opportunity rather than government concealment that prevented one. He narrows an overbroad formulation where its source supports a defined boundary. Where the rule itself conflicts with superior law, contradicts itself, becomes unworkable, rests on a collapsed premise, or systematically defeats its own constitutional protection, he is willing to propose overruling it. He does not disguise elimination of a rule as faithful application.

His replacement must address the demonstrated defect and rest on the relevant legal source. Statutory precedent normally carries added weight because Congress can amend the statute, but that is not an absolute prohibition on correction. Concrete reliance and legitimate repose matter. Administrative convenience and a desire to keep winning cases are not sufficient legal reasons to preserve a defective rule.

A change must explain the previous rule, the reason it fails, the replacement boundary, the treatment of reliance, and the resulting remedy. A narrower remedy does not conceal that the legal rule changed. Later historical decisions may illuminate a comparison but do not become binding earlier decisions merely because their outcomes are known.

### Likely dispositions and remedies in difficult cases

Stone's remedial commitments are often firmer than the exact formulation of a new exception. He distinguishes obtaining a hearing, proving a violation, and receiving relief from custody.

| Situation established by the record | Likely personal disposition | Reason and remedial limit |
|---|---|---|
| The state court's application of the correct constitutional rule is reasonable, no other §2254(d) ground is established, and Stone would decide differently. | Deny relief under an applicable §2254(d) restriction. | Independent disagreement does not satisfy the statutory condition; a necessary constitutional challenge would need its own adjudication. |
| Specific evidence suggests a substantial violation, but the State concealed the material needed to resolve it. | Permit authorized investigation or remand for the necessary diligence and factual determinations. | Access to proof cannot depend on already possessing concealed proof; entitlement to a writ remains separate. |
| A prisoner deliberately withheld an available claim and later repeats it without a legally sufficient excuse. | Enforce the applicable default, timeliness, or successive-petition restriction. | Meaningful opportunity carries responsibility to use it; the particular restriction and exception must actually apply. |
| A preserved ordinary constitutional trial error is established, statutory restrictions are overcome, and the State cannot establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. | Under his general personal position, grant or direct appropriate conditional relief. | The ordinary correction is a lawful retrial or sentencing proceeding, subject to any distinct remedial rule governing the violation. |
| A necessary death-eligibility determination was assigned to an unauthorized decisionmaker. | Examine the jury violation, retroactivity, applicable barriers, and the proper error treatment separately. | If relief is warranted, correct the sentence; do not infer invalidity of the murder conviction or an automatic prohibition on lawful resentencing. |
| A diligently undiscoverable constitutional defect in an old conviction materially increased the current sentence. | Under the meaningful-opportunity inference, allow the claim to be examined and correct the enhancement if proved. | Access alone does not invalidate the predicate, and the court must have authority over the present relief. |
| A court lawfully determines that no valid basis for the challenged custody remains. | Require effective termination of that custody. | Government cannot use an indefinite remedial delay to continue unauthorized confinement; independent lawful custody remains a separate matter. |

For a constitutional trial violation, a conditional writ ordinarily gives the responsible government a reasonable opportunity to retry the person lawfully or end the custody supported by the defective judgment. For a sentencing violation, it ordinarily allows constitutionally adequate resentencing or another lawful sentence within the governing authority. A finding of substantive innocence has consequences different from a finding that an otherwise permissible trial contained error; the available order must implement the particular protection established. A remand should identify what remains to be decided, the applicable standard, and why those findings matter. An emergency order should preserve the necessary opportunity for decision without deciding unproved merits.

**Using this foundation in a future case.** State the likely disposition, identify which established commitment or labeled inference supports it, explain the strongest contrary legal argument, and specify the fact or legal choice that could change the result. Then state whether the existing rule is being applied, distinguished, narrowed, or replaced, and give the remedy that follows from the actual finding. Where a material choice remains open, reason conditionally and identify that choice; do not transform it into a past commitment. This permits an educated account of what Stone would think without inventing a vote, attributing his position to the Court, or requiring every future factual variation to have been decided in advance.

## 1. Completed cases

OT1991
- Estelle v. McGuire
  > Held (White for the Court, 5-3): On habeas review, admitting evidence of the child's prior nonaccidental injuries to show the killing was intentional did not violate due process, and the instruction created no reasonable likelihood that the jury skipped proof of an element. The Ninth Circuit's grant of relief was reversed.
- Stringer v. Black
  > Held (Kennedy for the Court, 6-3): Teague did not bar the vague-aggravator claim, because Godfrey already forbade unguided heinousness aggravation before Stringer's sentence became final in 1985, and applying it to Mississippi's weighing system was not a new rule. The case was reversed and remanded, with the State's default defense still open.
- Gomez v. United States District Court and Vasquez v. Harris (stay applications)
  > Held (Gomez: per curiam, 6-3; Vasquez: unsigned orders, 7-2 and 6-3): Gomez vacated the Ninth Circuit's execution stays because Harris's unexplained delay in raising his cyanide-gas challenge through four earlier federal habeas proceedings left them without equitable support, apart from any habeas bar. The Vasquez orders vacated two later stays (7-2) and directed that no federal court stay Harris's execution again without this Court's order (6-3, Stone dissenting from that direction).
- Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 5-4): Under Townsend v. Sain, a habeas petitioner whose material facts were not adequately developed in state court loses a federal hearing only by deliberate bypass, not by counsel's ordinary negligence. Tamayo-Reyes was entitled to a hearing limited to whether the manslaughter mental-state element was explained to him.
- Coleman v. Thompson (stay application; petition disposition)
  > Held (per curiam, 6-3): The stay of execution was denied because Coleman's innocence presentation showed no significant possibility of overcoming the independent state default and successive-petition bars, deciding neither innocence nor a general innocence rule. Certiorari was later denied on June 15, 1992.
- Wright v. West
  > Held (no opinion of the Court, 9-0): The trial evidence let a rational juror find larceny under Jackson v. Virginia, so West's insufficiency claim warranted no habeas relief, and the judgment was reversed and remanded. No majority adopted a general rule of deference to state-court rulings on legal or mixed questions.
- Sawyer v. Whitley
  > Held (Kennedy for 5, 9-0 judgment): To reach a barred claim by showing innocence of the death penalty, a prisoner must show by clear and convincing evidence that, absent the error, no reasonable juror would find him eligible for death; mitigation evidence does not count. Sawyer's evidence fell short, so his claims remained barred.

OT1992
- Grubbs v. Delo (administrative stay)
  > Held (unsigned order; internal tally 9-0, not published): The Court temporarily stayed the execution pending its further order so it could examine the application and the State's response. The order decided no habeas claim and no entitlement to a longer stay.
- Parke v. Raley
  > Held (O'Connor for 8, 9-0 judgment): Due process let Kentucky, once it proved a prior conviction, require the defendant to produce evidence that the guilty plea was invalid, and did not demand clear-and-convincing proof of validity. Under former §2254(d) the state findings were presumed correct, Raley's 1981 plea was valid, and the Sixth Circuit's conditional writ was reversed.
- Dobbs v. Zant
  > Held (per curiam for 6; 8 for reversal, White would have granted full review): An appellate court may not exclude, without justification, a recovered capital-sentencing transcript that materially contradicts the facts behind an earlier habeas ruling and then rely on law of the case. The judgment was reversed and remanded for consideration of the transcript.
- Graham v. Collins
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 5-4): Teague did not bar Graham's claim, because Jurek, Lockett and Eddings already required before his 1984 finality that Texas's special issues allow effective use of youth and background mitigation. The special issues gave his youth no adequate route, so he was granted conditional relief from the death sentence.
- Herrera v. Collins
  > Held (no opinion of the Court, 5-4): Herrera's late and inconsistent affidavits did not entitle him to federal habeas relief or justify the innocence-based stay, even assuming a truly persuasive innocence showing could bar an execution. No majority decided whether a freestanding innocence claim exists or what it would require.
- Lockhart v. Fretwell
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 6-3): Counsel's failure to win the benefit of a rule later rejected is not Strickland prejudice when the lost benefit is one the law does not recognize, and Teague does not require ignoring that later correction. The rule adds no general unfairness requirement to ordinary prejudice analysis; habeas relief was reversed.
- Delo v. Lashley
  > Held (per curiam, 7-2): The Constitution does not require an instruction on a mitigating circumstance that no evidence supports, so Missouri could refuse the no-significant-criminal-history instruction. The grant of habeas relief on that ground was reversed and remanded.
- Brecht v. Abrahamson
  > Held (White for the Court, 5-4): Impeaching Brecht with his silence after Miranda warnings was Doyle trial error, and on habeas Chapman governs this preserved error: the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that it did not contribute to the verdict. Kotteakos was not applied, and the case was vacated and remanded for Chapman review.
- Withrow v. Williams
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 6-3): Stone v. Powell's bar does not extend to Miranda claims, so a full and fair state hearing does not foreclose federal habeas review of them. Unanimously, relief on an unpleaded coercion theory was reversed because the State never had a fair chance to litigate it.
- Gilmore v. Taylor
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 7-2): Falconer's rule on these murder and voluntary-manslaughter instructions was a new rule under Teague, the State's concession of error did not waive its Teague defense, and neither exception applied. The grant of habeas relief was reversed.
- Delo v. Blair (stay application)
  > Held (equally divided Court, 4-4): The State's application to vacate the Eighth Circuit's stay of execution was denied, so the stay remained in effect. The division set no precedent and decided nothing about Blair's innocence showing.

OT1993
- Burden v. Zant
  > Held (per curiam, 9-0): A habeas judgment resting on deference to a district-court finding that was never made must be reversed. On remand the courts must decide whether trial counsel had an actual conflict of interest that adversely affected his performance, without presuming it from joint representation.
- Schiro v. Farley
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 7-2): Teague is not jurisdictional, so when the State first raises it in merits briefing the Court may decline to consider it, and it did so here. On the merits the sentencing proceeding did not violate double jeopardy, and the denial of habeas relief was affirmed.
- Caspari v. Bohlen
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 8-1): The State's preserved Teague defense was fairly included in the question presented, and a rule barring renewed proof of persistent-offender status was new at Bohlen's 1986 finality and fit neither exception. The grant of habeas relief was reversed without deciding the double jeopardy merits.
- Custis v. United States
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 6-3): At sentencing under the Armed Career Criminal Act, a defendant may attack a prior state conviction only for a complete denial of appointed counsel, not for ineffective assistance or an involuntary plea. He may seek review of those convictions through other available procedures and, if he succeeds, seek relief from the enhancement.
- Reed v. Farley
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 5-4): A claimed violation of the Interstate Agreement on Detainers is a federal-law claim cognizable under §2254, but a nonconstitutional error supports habeas relief only if it is a fundamental defect causing a complete miscarriage of justice. Reed's assumed timing violation did not meet that standard, so the denial of relief was affirmed.
- McFarland v. Scott
  > Held (Stevens for the Court; 7-2 on counsel, 6-3 on the stay): A capital defendant may obtain appointed counsel under §848(q)(4)(B) before filing a habeas petition, and that request starts a proceeding in which a federal court may stay the execution under §2251. A stay is discretionary, not automatic.

OT1994
- Schlup v. Delo
  > Held (Stevens for the Court, 6-3): To pass through successive or abusive petition barriers by showing innocence of the crime, a prisoner must present reliable new evidence showing it more likely than not that no reasonable juror would convict, considering all the evidence. Sawyer's clear-and-convincing test is confined to death eligibility; the judgment was vacated and remanded.
- O'Neal v. McAninch
  > Held (O'Connor for 5, 7-2 judgment): On habeas, Chapman governs a preserved error in instructions and argument that let the jury convict without finding the defendant's own required intent, so the State must prove harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. The Sixth Circuit's judgment was vacated for review under that standard; no broader rule for other errors drew a majority.
- Kyles v. Whitley
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 6-3): Suppressed evidence is material when its combined effect creates a reasonable probability of a different result, the prosecutor answers for evidence known to the police, and a material Brady violation needs no second harmlessness review. The Court ordered a conditional writ requiring release unless Louisiana retries Kyles.
- Garlotte v. Fordice
  > Held (Ginsburg for the Court, 8-1): A prisoner serving consecutive sentences remains in custody under an earlier conviction in the series, even after its term is served, when invalidating it would advance his release. The judgment was reversed and remanded for the remaining habeas inquiries.

OT1995
- Tuggle v. Netherland
  > Held (Stevens for the Court, 9-0): In a nonweighing capital system, a surviving valid aggravator does not by itself defeat a claim of denied psychiatric assistance under Ake or show that the denial did not affect the death sentence. The ruling was vacated and remanded without choosing a harmless-error standard for habeas.
- Thompson v. Keohane
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 8-1): Whether a suspect was in custody for Miranda purposes is a mixed question that federal habeas courts decide independently; the §2254(d) presumption of correctness covers only historical facts and credibility findings. The judgment was vacated and remanded for independent review.
- Allridge v. Scott
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 9-0): Simmons does not entitle a capital defendant to a prediction of release when the alternative sentence allows ordinary parole, but a court may not reject admitted mitigation merely because it fails to show a severe permanent disability. That mitigation ruling was vacated and remanded; no sentencing writ issued.
- Lonchar v. Thomas
  > Held (Stevens for the Court, 9-0): A first federal habeas petition that meets jurisdictional and pleading requirements may not be dismissed on general equitable grounds for delay; delay supports dismissal only under Rule 9 or another lawful source. The order vacating the district court's stay was set aside and the stay restored pending redetermination.
- Bowersox v. Williams (stay application)
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 5-4): A stay pending a second or successive habeas petition requires substantial grounds on which relief might be granted, and Williams's identified claims supplied none. Missouri's application was granted and the Eighth Circuit's stay vacated, with Stone dissenting.
- AEDPA Title I (admitted source: enacted April 24, 1996)
  > Effect: Enacted April 24, 1996, Title I added one-year limitations periods (§2244(d) and §2255), the certificate-of-appealability requirement (§2253), limits on relief for claims decided on the merits in state court and on federal hearings (§2254), circuit authorization for successive petitions (§2244(b)), and chapter 154's special capital procedures, which §107(c) expressly applies to pending cases. The admission decided no case, transition question, or whether any State qualifies for chapter 154.
- Calderon v. Moore
  > Held (per curiam, 9-0): A State's steps toward a new trial under an unstayed conditional habeas writ do not moot its appeal while reversal could still lift the duty to retry. The Ninth Circuit's dismissal as moot was reversed and remanded.
- Gray v. Netherland
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 5-4): Gray's claim that last-minute evidence denied him a real opportunity to answer it at capital sentencing rested on law dictated before his 1987 finality, so Teague did not bar it, and the proven denial and prejudice required restoring conditional sentencing relief. His separately dismissed Brady claim stayed dismissed, 9-0.
- Felker v. Turpin
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 9-0): AEDPA's §2244(b)(3)(E) bars certiorari review of a circuit's ruling on permission to file a successive petition, but it leaves this Court's original habeas jurisdiction intact and, as applied, does not suspend the writ. The certiorari petition was dismissed and the original petition and stay application denied.

OT1996
- California v. Roy
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 6-3): Omitting the defendant's personal intent from an instruction is trial error subject to harmlessness review, and on habeas Chapman governs, with the State proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict. The Ninth Circuit's judgment was vacated and remanded for that review.
- Lynce v. Mathis (threshold)
  > Held (Thomas for 8, 9-0 judgment): Before granting habeas relief on Lynce's ex post facto claim, the lower courts must decide whether he exhausted state remedies and what the State's qualified futility concession means. The certificate denial was vacated and the case remanded for exhaustion-first proceedings.
- Lambrix v. Singletary
  > Held (Stevens for the Court, 6-3): Procedural default and Teague are distinct nonjurisdictional limits, and a court may decide either first; Lambrix's Espinosa claim invoked a rule already dictated at his 1986 finality (five votes). The judgment was vacated and remanded to resolve the State's preserved default defense.
- Bracy v. Gramley
  > Held (Kennedy for the Court, 9-0): Under Habeas Rule 6, good cause for discovery exists when specific allegations give reason to believe that fully developed facts may show entitlement to relief. Bracy's allegations linking the corrupt trial judge to his own trial justified focused discovery on judicial bias.
- O'Dell v. Netherland
  > Held (Stevens for the Court, 6-3): Teague did not bar O'Dell's claim to tell the jury he was legally ineligible for parole, on two independent five-vote grounds: Gardner and Skipper already dictated the rule, and alternatively Simmons is a watershed rule (the only ground Stone joined). The adverse ruling was vacated and remanded.
- Lindh v. Murphy
  > Held (Ginsburg for the Court, 6-3): AEDPA's chapter 153 amendments, including the new §2254(d), do not govern a noncapital habeas application filed before April 24, 1996. The judgment was reversed and remanded for decision under prior law.
- Kolster v. INS
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 9-0): AEDPA §440(a) ended direct appellate review of Kolster's deportation order, so dismissal of his petition was affirmed. Separately, with six votes, the general habeas statute (§2241) still permits review of substantial legal and constitutional challenges to deportation.

OT1997
- Trest v. Cain
  > Held (Stevens for the Court, 9-0): A court of appeals is not obliged to raise a procedural default the State has not asserted, because default does not deprive a federal court of jurisdiction. Whether a court may raise it on its own was left open; the judgment was vacated and remanded.
- Buchanan v. Angelone
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 6-3): Virginia's charge gave the jury an effective way to consider Buchanan's mitigation, so no additional mitigation instructions were constitutionally required. The denial of habeas relief was affirmed, and a separate due process claim was rejected 9-0.
- Carriger v. Stewart
  > Held (Stevens for the Court, 5-4): Pre-AEDPA law, including former §2254(d)(8), governs a capital application already pending when AEDPA took effect, and the innocence gateway requires Schlup's whole-record probability inquiry. The new-trial order was set aside and the case remanded for that inquiry and, if the gateway opens, the Brady claim.
- Calderon v. Thompson
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 5-4): A court of appeals may recall its mandate to reopen the merits of a completed habeas case only on a miscarriage-of-justice showing under Schlup (for guilt) or Sawyer (for death eligibility). Thompson met neither, so the recall was reversed and the mandate denying relief reinstated.
- Bousley v. United States
  > Held (Ginsburg for the Court, 7-2): Bailey's reading of the firearm statute is substantive law, so Teague does not bar it, but Bousley's plea claim, defaulted on direct review, may proceed only on cause and prejudice or factual innocence. The Government may rebut innocence with evidence outside the plea record; the judgment was reversed and remanded.
- Stewart v. Martinez-Villareal
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 7-2): The Court may review a circuit's ruling that authorization was unnecessary (9-0), and a Ford competency claim dismissed as premature in a first petition is not second or successive when renewed. The claim returns to the district court without circuit authorization.
- Calderon v. Ashmus
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 9-0): A prisoner class's suit for a declaration that chapter 154 does not apply would settle only one issue in future habeas cases and presents no Article III controversy, so it must be dismissed. Chapter 154's applicability is to be decided in a concrete habeas proceeding.
- Hohn v. United States
  > Held (Ginsburg for the Court, 6-3): A court of appeals' denial of a certificate of appealability is reviewable on certiorari under §1254(1), and House v. Mayo is overruled to that extent. The denial was vacated and remanded for reconsideration.
- Lucas v. Johnson
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court; 9-0 to 5-4 by component): Commutation to life mooted the death-only claims, and former §2254(d) governs this pending pre-AEDPA application; most conviction claims were denied. Denial of the Brady claim was vacated and remanded, 5-4, for the full suppression and materiality inquiry.

OT1998
- Calderon v. Coleman
  > Held (per curiam, 7-2): On habeas, Chapman governs a misleading commutation instruction affecting the capital jury's choice between death and life without parole (five votes). The appellate sentence ruling was vacated for a genuine whole-record inquiry into the error's effect, leaving the conviction undisturbed.
- Stewart v. LaGrand
  > Held (per curiam, 7-2): LaGrand waived his challenge to lethal gas by choosing it twice when lethal injection was available; independently, with five votes, a later favorable ruling did not supply cause for omitting a gas claim that was reasonably available earlier. The gas-specific injunction was vacated.
- Reno v. Goncalves
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 6-3): AEDPA and IIRIRA did not clearly withdraw habeas jurisdiction under §2241 over a substantial legal challenge to the denial of eligibility for discretionary relief (9-0). AEDPA §440(d) does not apply to a section 212(c) application pending when AEDPA took effect.
- O'Sullivan v. Boerckel
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 6-3): Under pre-AEDPA law, a prisoner must present each federal claim through one complete round of the State's ordinary appellate process, including a discretionary petition to its highest court when that is an ordinary step. Claims omitted once that opportunity expired are procedurally defaulted, subject to the existing cause and innocence gateways.
- Strickler v. Greene
  > Held (Stevens for the Court; 9-0 on the conviction, 6-3 on the sentence): Suppression of documents together with counsel's reasonable reliance on the prosecution's open-file practice established cause for the omitted Brady claim (8-1). But the suppressed documents were not material to guilt or to the death sentence, so relief was denied.

OT1999
- Fiore v. White (certification)
  > Held (Stevens for the Court, 9-0): The Court certified to the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania whether its later Scarpone construction stated the statute's meaning when Fiore's conviction became final or changed the law. It retained jurisdiction and reserved the federal judgment.
- Smith v. Robbins
  > Held (Stevens for the Court, 5-4): California's Wende procedure is inadequate because appointed counsel need not identify any arguable issue, and Teague does not bar relief because Anders and its successors already dictated that protection. The conditional writ restoring adequate appellate review was affirmed.
- Weeks v. Angelone
  > Held (Breyer for the Court, 5-4): The claim was fairly presented and not defaulted, and for this post-AEDPA application relief requires meeting §2254(d)(1) (both 9-0). The response to the jury's question left a reasonable likelihood that jurors could not give mitigation effect, so the case was remanded to decide §2254(d)(1) eligibility and the error's effect.
- Portuondo v. Agard
  > Held (Kennedy lead opinion, 6-3): Pre-AEDPA law governs Agard's 1995 application and the Court declined to consider the State's belated Teague argument (eight votes). On the merits, the prosecutor's summation remark on Agard's presence at trial supplied no constitutional ground for the writ, though no rationale for that conclusion drew a majority.
- Williams v. Taylor (Terry Williams)
  > Held (Breyer for the Court, 7-2): The two clauses of §2254(d)(1) do independent work, and clearly established law includes general rules such as Strickland (eight votes, without Stone). The Virginia court unreasonably applied Strickland by truncating the mitigation evidence (seven votes, with Stone), so Williams receives a conditional writ for resentencing.
- Williams v. Taylor (Michael Wayne Williams)
  > Held (Ginsburg for the Court; 9-0 and 8-1): Section 2254(e)(2) bars a hearing only when the applicant or counsel failed to use diligence in developing the facts in state court; the claim based on a known psychiatric report was barred, 8-1. The concealed juror-relationship and prosecutor-nondisclosure claims were not barred, and cause was shown for their default, with prejudice left to the lower courts.
- Edwards v. Carpenter
  > Held (Souter for 7, 9-0 judgment): An ineffective-assistance claim offered as cause for another default must itself be preserved, or its own default excused by cause and prejudice or a miscarriage-of-justice showing. The Sixth Circuit's judgment was reversed and remanded.
- Slack v. McDaniel
  > Held (Ginsburg for the Court, 7-2): An appeal begun after April 24, 1996 needs a certificate of appealability, which for a procedural dismissal requires that reasonable jurists could debate both the constitutional claim and the procedural ruling. A petition refiled after a dismissal for failure to exhaust is not second or successive.
- Ramdass v. Angelone
  > Held (Stevens for the Court, 5-4): Amended §2254(d)(1) applies, measured by this Court's holdings as of November 4, 1994, but the lower court could not reject the parole-information claim categorically under §2254(d)(1) and Teague without examining it. The case was remanded with no present sentencing relief.

### 2.4 Procedural default, innocence and timeliness

## 3. The doctrine as it stands

### 3.1 Federal habeas in plain terms

After a state conviction becomes final, a prisoner may ask a federal district court for a writ of habeas corpus on the ground that his custody violates federal law (§2254); a federal prisoner files a motion under §2255. Habeas is not a second appeal. Before a federal court reaches the merits, the prisoner must pass a series of gates: he must be in custody; the claim must be one the court may hear; he must have exhausted state remedies; he must not have defaulted the claim in state court without excuse; the petition must be timely and not an improper repeat; the rule he relies on must have existed when his conviction became final (Teague); and, for applications filed after April 24, 1996, the state court's decision must fail the deference test of §2254(d). Even after a violation is shown, relief usually depends on harmless error. The usual remedy is a conditional writ: release unless the State retries or resentences him within a set time. Most of what this Court has built concerns which gate applies, who bears the burden at each, and how strict each gate is.

### 3.2 Which law applies

AEDPA's Title I took effect on April 24, 1996. Applications filed before that date are governed by the earlier chapter 153 law, whether noncapital (Lindh, Ginsburg, 6-3) or capital outside chapter 154's special procedures (Carriger, Stevens, 5-4). An appeal begun after enactment needs a certificate of appealability (Slack). Felker applied the new bar on review of circuit authorization rulings to proceedings begun after enactment without deciding the reach of every provision. Whether any State qualifies for chapter 154's expedited capital procedures is undecided; Calderon v. Ashmus dismissed a prisoner class's declaratory suit on that question for want of an Article III controversy (9-0). The one-year limitations period in §2244(d) is enacted but has never been construed.

### 3.3 Custody, cognizable claims and exhaustion

Doctrine now: a prisoner serving consecutive sentences is in custody under each conviction in the series while invalidating it would advance his release (Garlotte). Fourth Amendment claims stay barred when the State gave a full and fair opportunity to litigate them (Stone v. Powell), but Miranda claims may be heard (Withrow, Souter, 6-3). A statutory, nonconstitutional claim must show a fundamental defect (Reed v. Farley, written by Stone). A federal sentencing court under the Armed Career Criminal Act need not hear attacks on the prior state convictions other than a complete denial of appointed counsel; the defendant may seek whatever review of those convictions remains and return if he succeeds (Custis). The prisoner must give the state courts one complete round of review, including discretionary review that is part of the State's ordinary appellate process (O'Sullivan, 6-3), and must fairly present the federal claim (Weeks). A federal court must actually decide an exhaustion predicate before granting relief (Lynce). Mixed petitions are dismissed (Rose v. Lundy), and a petition refiled after that dismissal is not "successive" (Slack, 7-2).

### 3.4 Procedural default and the innocence gateways

Doctrine now: a claim the state courts rejected on an adequate and independent state procedural ground is barred unless the prisoner shows cause and prejudice or a miscarriage of justice, and errors of postconviction counsel are not cause (Coleman v. Thompson, 1991). Default is not jurisdictional; a court need not raise it when the State has not, and whether it may is reserved (Trest). A court may take up default and Teague in either order (Lambrix). Cause exists when the State suppressed evidence and the defense reasonably relied on an open-file policy (Strickler, 8-1) or concealed the facts (Michael Williams). An ineffective-assistance claim offered as cause must itself be preserved, or its own default excused (Edwards v. Carpenter).

Two innocence gateways open the door to a barred claim. For guilt, the prisoner must bring reliable new evidence showing it more likely than not that no reasonable juror would convict (Schlup, six votes). The gateway also reaches defaulted guilty-plea claims; the Government may rebut with evidence outside the plea record, and the showing must cover any more serious charge actually forgone in the bargain (Bousley). For a death sentence, the prisoner must show by clear and convincing evidence that no reasonable juror would find him eligible for death; mitigation does not count (Sawyer, Kennedy for five). Recalling a completed habeas mandate requires the same showings (Calderon v. Thompson, 5-4). Whether innocence alone is a freestanding constitutional claim is unresolved: Herrera produced only a record-specific holding, and Delo v. Blair divided 4-4 on a similar showing, leaving a stay in place without deciding anything.

### 3.5 Fact development (Keeney and §2254(e)(2))

Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes in plain terms. Tamayo-Reyes, a Cuban immigrant, pleaded no contest to manslaughter through an interpreter; he later claimed the intent element was never properly translated, but his state postconviction lawyer did not develop that evidence. The real Court (White, 5-4) held that a prisoner who failed to develop facts in state court gets a federal hearing only by showing cause and prejudice. Here the Court (O'Connor, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy) kept the older rule: only a deliberate bypass, a knowing choice to skip state process, forfeits the hearing. Counsel's carelessness does not. That rule still governs pre-AEDPA applications.

For later applications, §2254(e)(2) applies as Michael Williams construed it (Part I, unanimous). A prisoner "failed to develop" the facts only if he or his counsel lacked diligence or did worse; an incomplete record alone is not enough, and diligence is measured against the leads and procedures actually available. Once triggered, the bar yields only if the claim rests on a new retroactive rule or on facts that could not have been discovered earlier with diligence, and clear and convincing evidence shows that no reasonable factfinder would have found guilt but for the error. Because this Court's Keeney never adopted cause and prejudice, the historical explanation that (e)(2) continued Keeney's rule does not hold here: it is a new restriction. Whether a diligent applicant is entitled to a hearing under the older standards is undecided.

### 3.6 Review of state-court decisions

Before AEDPA, federal courts decide legal and mixed questions independently, including whether a suspect was "in custody" for Miranda (Thompson v. Keohane), and give state findings of historical fact the former statute's presumption of correctness, subject to its eight exceptions (Parke v. Raley). Whether federal courts owe general deference to state legal rulings under the old law was left open (Wright v. West, which reversed the writ on sufficiency of the evidence).

After AEDPA, §2254(d)(1) allows relief on a claim the state court decided on the merits only if the decision was "contrary to" or "an unreasonable application of" clearly established federal law as determined by this Court.

Williams v. Taylor (Terry Williams) in plain terms. Williams's lawyers failed to find records of a horrific childhood and other mitigation before his Virginia capital sentencing. Historically, Justice O'Connor's opinion for the Court set the framework for §2254(d)(1) that every later case uses, and Williams won 6-3. Here Williams also won, 7-2 (Breyer), but on different reasoning. What controls:
- The two clauses do independent work. A decision is "contrary to" this Court's law if it applies a rule that conflicts with the governing rule (identical facts are not needed) or reaches the opposite result on materially indistinguishable facts. Unreasonableness is objective, not a count of judges who agree. "Clearly established" law means this Court's holdings at the time of the state decision, and a general rule such as Strickland qualifies (Part I, eight Justices; Stone did not join).
- A state court unreasonably applies Strickland when it truncates the mitigation evidence or weighs it in an objectively indefensible way; mere disagreement among reasonable jurists is not enough (Part II, seven Justices including Stone).
- Lockhart v. Fretwell adds no separate "unfairness" requirement to Strickland prejudice (Part I, eight Justices), and a state court that demands one rules contrary to clearly established law (Part III, six Justices).

What does not control: the general division of work between the two clauses split 4-4. Souter, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer would let federal courts apply a governing rule independently under "contrary to"; O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas hold the historical view. Stone joined neither group, so O'Connor's historical Part II is not law here.

Lockhart v. Fretwell itself came out as in history (relief reversed), but on a narrower rule: losing the benefit of a rule later rejected is not cognizable prejudice, and the decision does not require an added showing of unfairness in the ordinary case (O'Connor for six).

### 3.7 Harmless error on habeas

Brecht v. Abrahamson in plain terms. The prosecutor impeached Brecht with his silence after Miranda warnings, a due-process violation under Doyle. The question was which harmless-error test a federal habeas court uses. On direct appeal, Chapman requires the State to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict. The real Court (Rehnquist, 5-4) adopted the gentler Kotteakos test for habeas: relief only if the error had a "substantial and injurious effect" on the verdict, which became the habeas standard for every constitutional trial error. Here the Court (White, joined by Stone, Blackmun, O'Connor and Souter; Stone the fifth vote) held that Chapman governs this preserved Doyle error on habeas, with the State keeping the burden, and vacated and remanded.

Doctrine now: Chapman governs on habeas for four kinds of preserved trial error properly before the court:
- Doyle impeachment with post-warning silence (Brecht).
- Instructions and prosecutorial argument that let the jury convict without finding the defendant's own required intent (O'Neal, O'Connor, five votes for Chapman). Stone, O'Connor and Souter would have applied Chapman to all preserved trial errors (Part III), but that drew only three votes.
- An instruction that omits the defendant's personal intent (Roy, Part II unanimous). Part III, with five votes and without Stone, sets the method: the court looks at the verdict actually returned; the State may rely on supported inferences from the whole record, but not on a hypothetical properly instructed jury or the mere sufficiency of the evidence.
- A misleading capital commutation instruction affecting the jury's choice between death and life without parole (Calderon v. Coleman, per curiam, five votes with Stone).

No majority has chosen a standard for any other constitutional error; Gray, Tuggle, Weeks and Ramdass each declined to. Structural errors require automatic reversal, and a defective reasonable-doubt instruction is structural (Sullivan v. Louisiana, 9-0, its collateral effect reserved). A Brady violation shown to be material gets no second harmlessness test (Kyles). Historically O'Neal, Roy and Calderon v. Coleman applied Brecht; here they reached the same dispositions on Chapman.

### 3.8 New rules (Teague)

Doctrine now: Teague is retained with both exceptions. A prisoner on collateral review may not rely on a rule announced after his conviction became final unless it places conduct beyond the State's power to punish or is a watershed rule of criminal procedure. Teague is a nonjurisdictional defense the State must raise, and a court may decline it when raised late (Schiro, Portuondo). A ruling on what a criminal statute prohibits is substantive law, not a barred procedure (Bousley).

This Court classifies claims as old rules more readily than history did: Graham v. Collins, Gray, Lambrix and O'Dell found no Teague bar where the real Court found one, and Weeks and Ramdass also rejected a Teague bar on the way to results that differ from history. Stringer (6-3) matches history. Gilmore (7-2) and Caspari (8-1) found new rules, as in history.

O'Dell v. Netherland in plain terms. O'Dell argued that his capital jury should have been told he would never be eligible for parole, the rule of Simmons v. South Carolina (1994), which came after his conviction became final. The real Court (Thomas, 5-4) held Simmons a new rule that fit neither exception. Here the Court (Stevens, 6-3) held the claim not barred on two independent grounds: Simmons applied an old rule (Part I, five votes), and alternatively Simmons is a watershed rule (Part II, five votes with Stone, who joined only that ground and would have reversed outright). History never recognized a single watershed rule and in 2021 declared the exception dead; this Court has recognized one.

### 3.9 Successive petitions, appeals, counsel and stays

Before AEDPA, abuse-of-the-writ doctrine and former §2244(b) govern repeat petitions (McCleskey v. Zant), and a court may not dismiss a first petition on free-floating equitable grounds (Lonchar). After AEDPA, a circuit's ruling granting or denying authorization for a successive petition cannot be reviewed on certiorari, but this Court's original habeas jurisdiction survives and the scheme did not suspend the writ as applied (Felker); whether §2244(b)(1)-(2) bind this Court directly is reserved. This Court may review a circuit's ruling that authorization was unnecessary, and a competency-to-be-executed claim dismissed as premature is not successive when renewed (Martinez-Villareal, 7-2). A certificate of appealability issues when reasonable jurists could debate the claim, and for a procedural dismissal also the procedural ruling (Slack); a denial is reviewable on certiorari, overruling House v. Mayo in part (Hohn, 6-3). A capital prisoner may have counsel appointed before filing, with a stay meanwhile (McFarland). Bracy (9-0) ordered focused discovery under Rule 6 on a specific claim of judicial bias. In stay practice, Vasquez v. Harris vacated the specified stays and, as in history, barred any further federal stay of that execution without this Court's order (6-3, Stone dissenting); Bowersox v. Williams vacated a stay 5-4 with Stone dissenting.

### 3.10 Where this Court departed from history

Results that differ: Keeney (deliberate bypass kept); Brecht (Chapman on habeas); Graham v. Collins; Gray v. Netherland; Lambrix; O'Dell (a watershed rule recognized); Weeks; Ramdass; Smith v. Robbins (California's Wende procedure inadequate, Stevens for five); Delo v. Blair (stay left in place, 4-4, where history vacated it); Johnson v. Texas (a direct-review capital case that, with Graham, changes the predicate for Penry).

Same results, different reasoning: O'Neal, Roy and Calderon v. Coleman (Chapman, not Brecht); Herrera (no opinion of the Court); Terry Williams (O'Connor's framework is not law); Lockhart v. Fretwell (a narrow rule with no general unfairness requirement); Michael Williams (§2254(e)(2) is a new restriction, not Keeney codified).

Same results by wider margins: Withrow, Lindh and Hohn (6-3 where history was 5-4).

No historical counterpart: Carriger (certiorari was denied in history), Allridge, Lucas v. Johnson.

Stone's distinctive positions, none controlling: Herrera (recognize a freestanding innocence claim proved by clear and convincing evidence); Sawyer (a broader "more likely than not" test for innocence of the death penalty); O'Neal Part III (Chapman for all preserved trial errors); the Coleman stay (would have granted it); Lambrix (decide default before Teague); O'Dell and Weeks (would grant relief outright); Michael Williams (dissent on the psychiatric-report claim); Strickler (dissent on materiality to the death sentence); Terry Williams (joined neither group on the division between the clauses); Vasquez v. Harris (would not have barred further stays).

## Footnote: why Almendarez-Torres is a sleeper

In history, Almendarez-Torres v. United States (1998, 5-4) held that the Constitution lets a judge, not a jury, find a prior conviction that raises the maximum sentence. Two years later Apprendi made every other such fact an element for the jury, "other than the fact of a prior conviction." That exception became a load-bearing joint: Ring (capital aggravators), Blakely (state guidelines) and Booker (federal guidelines) all carried it forward, and a later line rests in part on it (Shepard, Descamps and Mathis limit which records a judge may consult to decide what a prior conviction was; Erlinger in 2024 sent one prior-conviction question to the jury).

Here Almendarez-Torres came out the other way on a statutory ground: the prior conviction in §1326(b)(2) is an element to be charged and proved (Scalia's Part I, with Stone, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg). Only Stone would have rested that on the Constitution, and no majority decides whether a constitutional prior-conviction exception exists. Apprendi (Stone for six) contains no such exception and reserves state charging practice, mandatory minimums, capital eligibility and guidelines systems. So the constitutional question history answered in 1998 is open here, and every later jury-sentencing landmark arrives built on an answer this Court never gave.

That is why it is a sleeper. It looks like a narrow statutory case about an immigration reentry statute, and it changed no constitutional rule, so nothing flags it. But prior convictions are the most common sentencing enhancement there is (career-offender and armed-career-criminal statutes, three-strikes laws, guidelines criminal-history scores), and it reaches across fields: due process, where Almendarez-Torres and Apprendi sit; federal habeas, through Summerlin and a watershed exception that is still alive after O'Dell; and capital sentencing, through Ring. Vehicles are already on the OT2000 list: Buford v. United States (March 20, 2001; a guidelines ruling on how judges classify prior convictions), and Daniels and Lackawanna (sentences enhanced by prior convictions).
