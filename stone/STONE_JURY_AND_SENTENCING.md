# Stone tracker: jury trial and sentencing

Scope: the Sixth Amendment jury right and due process as they decide which facts are elements the jury must find beyond a reasonable doubt and which a judge may find at sentencing: the Apprendi line; prior convictions (Almendarez-Torres) and the categorical approach that depends on them; mandatory minimums; capital aggravators; sentencing guidelines and the remedy if they violate the jury right; relevant and acquitted conduct; fines, forfeiture and restitution; supervised-release revocation; jury unanimity; harmless and plain error for omitted elements; and materiality and similar element questions for the jury. Federal habeas, including whether jury-finding rules apply retroactively (Schriro v. Summerlin), and due process generally, including excessive fines and retroactive laws, appear here only where they touch these questions. Capital sentencing appears here only as to who finds the facts that make a defendant eligible for death and how the jury's role is protected.

Organization:
1. Completed cases.
2. Reserved landmark cases, with their historical questions and blank fields for Stone's disposition and reasoning.
3. A short primer, the doctrine as it stands, and where this Court departed from history.
4. Questions to answer before the landmarks (questions only).

## 1. Completed cases

OT1991
- Griffin v. United States
  > Held (Scalia for the Court, 8-0): A general guilty verdict on a multiple-object federal conspiracy stands when the evidence fails on one legally valid object but suffices on another. The Court left open legally invalid objects, element-diluting instructions and an independently raised unanimity defect.
- Williams v. United States
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 7-2): A Guidelines departure resting on a factor the Commission forbids, such as a bare arrest record, misapplies the Guidelines. The sentence must be remanded unless the reviewing court finds from the whole record that the judge would have imposed it anyway, and the departure's size must separately be reasonable.
- United States v. R. L. C.
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 7-2): The ceiling on a juvenile's detention, measured by what a comparable adult could receive, includes the adult Guidelines range maximum, absent a lawful departure. The Guidelines supply only that ceiling, not a minimum.
- Wade v. United States
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 9-0): Substantial assistance alone does not let a court go below a statutory minimum or the Guidelines without the Government's motion, but a refusal to move for an unconstitutional reason, such as race or religion, can be remedied. Wade's bare claim of useful assistance justified no hearing or reduction.
- Sochor v. Florida
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 6-3): A Florida judge's express weighing of an aggravating factor that the state supreme court found unsupported is constitutional error, curable only by lawful reweighing or a showing beyond a reasonable doubt that it did not contribute to the sentence. Florida's review did neither, so the judgment sustaining the death sentence was vacated.
- Espinosa v. Florida
  > Held (per curiam, 7-2): Because Florida's judge must give great weight to the jury's death recommendation, an inadequately narrowed aggravator in the jury's instructions infects the sentence even if the judge finds only valid aggravators. The sentence required lawful reweighing, Chapman harmlessness review or a new sentencing.

OT1992
- Parke v. Raley
  > Held (O'Connor for 8, 9-0 judgment): When a State uses an old guilty-plea conviction to enhance a sentence, due process lets it presume the judgment regular and require the defendant to produce evidence of invalidity; it need not prove validity by clear and convincing evidence. Raley's 1981 plea was valid.
- Richmond v. Lewis
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 8-1): When a death sentence in a weighing State rests on an unconstitutionally vague aggravator, the remaining valid aggravators do not cure it; an authorized sentencer must actually reweigh, or the State must prove harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. Arizona's divided appellate affirmance did neither, so conditional relief was ordered.
- United States v. Nachtigal
  > Held (per curiam, 9-0): An offense punishable by no more than six months' imprisonment is presumed petty, with no jury right, unless its other authorized penalties are so severe that they clearly show the legislature considers it serious. A $5,000 fine and up to five years' probation for drunk driving in a national park did not overcome that presumption.
- United States v. Dunnigan
  > Held (Kennedy for the Court, 9-0): A Guidelines increase for perjury at trial does not violate the right to testify, but on objection the judge must independently find willful false testimony on a material matter. One finding covering each element suffices.
- United States v. Olano
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 6-3): An appellate court may correct an error the defendant did not raise only if it is plain and affected substantial rights, ordinarily by affecting the outcome. Alternate jurors' mere presence during deliberations, without proof of participation or chilling, did not meet that test.
- Stinson v. United States
  > Held (Kennedy for 8, 9-0 judgment): Guidelines commentary interpreting a guideline is binding unless it violates the Constitution or a statute or is inconsistent with, or a plainly erroneous reading of, the guideline. The judgment applying a contrary reading was vacated.
- Deal v. United States
  > Held (Scalia for the Court, 5-4): In the firearm statute, "conviction" means the finding of guilt, so a second firearm count in the same prosecution triggers the enhanced twenty-year consecutive term. Stone dissented, reading the phrase to require a conviction before the later offense.
- Sullivan v. Louisiana
  > Held (Scalia for the Court, 9-0): A reasonable-doubt instruction that vitiates the standard for all of the jury's findings leaves no valid guilty verdict. The error is structural and requires reversal without harmless-error review.

OT1993
- Schiro v. Farley
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 7-2): An Indiana judge's consideration of an intentional-killing aggravator at sentencing was not a second prosecution, and the jury's advisory recommendation against death was not an acquittal of that aggravator. The felony-murder verdict did not necessarily decide that Schiro lacked intent.
- Caspari v. Bohlen
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 8-1): On habeas, a rule barring renewed proof of persistent-offender status at a noncapital sentencing was a new rule when Bohlen's conviction became final in 1986, and neither Teague exception applied. The double jeopardy merits were left open.
- United States v. Granderson
  > Held (Ginsburg for 6, 8-1 judgment): When drug possession triggered mandatory probation revocation under the former statute, the replacement prison term had to fall between one-third of the original Guidelines range's six-month maximum and that maximum, here two to six months. Sixty months of probation did not become sixty months of prison.
- Victor v. Nebraska and Sandoval v. California
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 9-0 on the standard): A reasonable-doubt instruction violates due process if, read as a whole, there is a reasonable likelihood the jury understood it to allow conviction on less than proof beyond a reasonable doubt; no set wording is required. Both charges passed that test (Victor 6-3, Stone dissenting; Sandoval 9-0).
- Custis v. United States
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 6-3): At sentencing under the Armed Career Criminal Act, a defendant may attack a prior state conviction only for a complete denial of appointed counsel, not for ineffective assistance or an involuntary plea. He may seek review of those convictions through other available procedures and, if he succeeds, seek relief from the enhancement.
- Nichols v. United States
  > Held (O'Connor plurality, 5-4): An uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, valid because no jail was imposed, may supply a Guidelines criminal-history point when the defendant can contest its reliability and seek a departure. No majority overruled Baldasar generally; Stone dissented.
- Tuilaepa v. California and Proctor v. California
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 8-1): Once eligibility for death is established, the sentencer may consider understandable, relevant subjects such as the circumstances of the crime, prior violent conduct and age, without making each a yes-or-no factual finding or giving it a fixed weight. California's challenged selection factors were upheld.
- International Union, United Mine Workers of America v. Bagwell
  > Held (Kennedy for the Court, 9-0): Large fixed fines for completed out-of-court violations of a court decree are criminal punishment, and serious criminal contempt fines of this size (about $52 million) require a jury. Proof beyond a reasonable doubt before a judge is no substitute.

OT1994
- O'Neal v. McAninch
  > Held (O'Connor for 5, 7-2 judgment): On habeas, Chapman governs a preserved error in instructions and argument that let the jury convict without finding the defendant's own required intent, so the State must prove harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. The Sixth Circuit's judgment was vacated for review under that standard.
- Harris v. Alabama
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 7-2): Where a State gives the final capital sentencing decision to the judge, the Eighth Amendment does not require it to give the jury's advisory recommendation any set weight or to adopt Florida's standard for overriding a life recommendation. Harris's death sentence after an override was affirmed, with Stone dissenting.
- Witte v. United States
  > Held (O'Connor for 6, 8-1 judgment): Using uncharged relevant conduct to set a sentence within the range authorized for the offense of conviction is not prosecution or punishment for that conduct, so it does not bar a later prosecution for it. The Court adopted no rule for acquitted conduct or for facts that raise the statutory maximum.
- United States v. Gaudin
  > Held (Scalia for the Court, 9-0): When materiality is an element of a criminal false-statement offense, the Fifth and Sixth Amendments require the jury, not the judge, to decide it beyond a reasonable doubt.

OT1995
- Libretti v. United States
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 8-1): Under the federal drug forfeiture statutes reviewed, the extent of forfeiture is part of the sentence, not an element, so the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to decide it (five votes; Stone reserved that question). Libretti validly waived the statutory forfeiture jury.
- Neal v. United States
  > Held (Kennedy for the Court, 9-0): The statutory LSD mandatory minimum counts the weight of the carrier paper, while the Guidelines may use a different per-dose measure; when the statutory minimum exceeds the Guidelines range, the minimum becomes the Guidelines sentence.
- Koon v. United States; Powell v. United States
  > Held (Kennedy for the Court, 9-0 on the standard of review): Appellate courts review Guidelines departures for abuse of discretion, correcting legal error and unsupported grounds while deferring to the judge's case-specific judgments about what makes a case unusual. Part of the departure was sustained and the cases were remanded for resentencing.
- Melendez v. United States
  > Held (Kennedy for the Court, 7-2): A court may go below a statutory mandatory minimum for substantial assistance only on the Government's request or consent to that relief; a motion seeking only a Guidelines departure does not supply it. Melendez's ten-year minimum stood.
- Lewis v. United States
  > Held (no opinion of the Court, 7-2): Lewis's convictions on two petty offenses tried without a jury were affirmed without a majority rationale. Five Justices, including Stone, agreed that after a timely jury demand, a nonjury trial of joined petty offenses cannot produce total imprisonment over six months.

OT1996
- California v. Roy
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 6-3): Omitting the defendant's personal intent from an instruction is trial error subject to harmlessness review, and on habeas Chapman governs, with the State proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict. The Ninth Circuit's judgment was vacated and remanded for that review.
- United States v. Watts / United States v. Putra
  > Held (per curiam, 7-2): An acquittal does not bar a judge from considering the same conduct, proved by a preponderance, in setting a Guidelines sentence within the punishment the conviction authorizes. That permission does not reach punishment beyond that authority or removal of an element from the jury.
- Old Chief v. United States
  > Held (Ginsburg for the Court, 6-3): When the defendant offers an adequate, unconditional admission of the prior-conviction status the charge requires, and the conviction's name and nature add only propensity risk, Rule 403 requires excluding them. The case was remanded for harmless-error review, over Stone's view that a new trial was required.
- United States v. Wells
  > Held (Thomas for the Court, 8-1): The federal statute punishing false statements made to influence banks does not make materiality an element. The Government must still prove a knowingly false statement made for the purpose of influencing a covered institution.
- Young v. Harper
  > Held (Breyer for the Court, 9-0): A prisoner released to a community program that works like parole has conditional liberty that cannot be revoked without the hearings Morrissey v. Brewer requires. The program's label does not change that.
- Johnson v. United States (perjury materiality)
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 9-0): Letting the judge decide materiality in a perjury trial was constitutional error that Johnson forfeited by not objecting; when controlling law changes before appeal, an error is plain if it is clear at the time of appellate review. Correction was declined because materiality was clear and the error did not seriously affect the fairness or integrity of the proceedings.
- United States v. LaBonte
  > Held (Kennedy for the Court, 6-3): For career offenders, the "maximum term authorized" is the enhanced statutory maximum that applies because of the defendant's prior convictions, not the unenhanced one. The Sentencing Commission's amendment using the unenhanced maximum was invalid.

OT1997
- Almendarez-Torres v. United States
  > Held (Scalia for the Court, 5-4): The prior aggravated-felony conviction in 8 U.S.C. 1326(b)(2) is an element that must be charged and proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt, so this sentence may not exceed the two-year maximum of section 1326(a). The ruling is statutory; no majority adopts a general constitutional rule.
- Edwards v. United States
  > Held (Thomas for the Court, 9-0): Even if a general verdict is treated as a cocaine-only conviction, the judge may find that crack was part of the same course of conduct and sentence on that basis under the Guidelines, as long as the sentence stays within the cocaine-only statutory maximum.
- Caron v. United States
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 6-3): When a State restores civil rights but still forbids carrying handguns outside the home or business, the prior conviction keeps its federal firearms effect, even for the rifles and shotguns the State allows. The federal offense and enhancement requirements must still be met.
- Monge v. California
  > Held (Thomas for the Court, 5-4): The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar a second attempt, in a noncapital sentencing, to prove that a prior conviction qualifies as a serious felony after the first proof failed. The Court relied on no constitutional exception for prior convictions and left jury and charging claims open.

OT1998
- Jones v. United States (carjacking)
  > Held (Scalia for the Court, 6-3): Under the federal carjacking statute, serious bodily injury and death are elements of aggravated offenses that must be charged and proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Jones's sentence may not exceed the basic fifteen-year maximum.
- Mitchell v. United States
  > Held (Breyer for the Court, 6-3): A guilty plea does not waive the privilege against self-incrimination at sentencing, and a judge may not draw an adverse inference from the defendant's silence in finding the details of the crime, such as drug quantity. Mitchell's sentence was vacated.
- Richardson v. United States
  > Held (Stevens for the Court, 6-3): In a continuing-criminal-enterprise prosecution, each violation making up the required series is an element, so the jury must agree unanimously on which violations occurred. Jurors need not agree on the means of committing an agreed violation.
- Neder v. United States
  > Held (Thomas for 7): Withholding an element such as materiality from the jury is trial error, so the Government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that it did not contribute to the actual verdict; strong evidence alone is not enough. The tax convictions passed that test, 6-3 over Stone's dissent, and the fraud counts were remanded.
- Jones v. United States (federal death penalty)
  > Held (Breyer for the Court, 5-4): Under the Federal Death Penalty Act, a deadlocked sentencing jury requires the judge to impose a lawful nondeath sentence, here life without release. Because the charge misleadingly suggested a sentence below life and a request to correct it was refused, the death sentence was vacated for a new penalty hearing.

OT1999
- United States v. Johnson
  > Held (Kennedy for the Court, 9-0): Supervised release begins only on actual release from prison, so excess prison time does not automatically shorten it, though the court may weigh it in deciding whether to modify or end supervision.
- Johnson v. United States (supervised-release revocation)
  > Held (Souter for the Court, 7-2): Penalties on revocation of supervised release are punishment for the original offense, so the law in force when that offense was committed governs, not a later amendment. That former law let the court impose prison followed by more supervision, which Stone, dissenting on that point, would not have allowed.
- Castillo v. United States
  > Held (Scalia for the Court, 9-0): Under the pre-1998 firearm statute, the type of weapon, such as a machinegun, defines an aggravated offense that must be charged and proved to the jury beyond a reasonable doubt; a judge's finding cannot authorize the higher sentence.
- Carter v. United States
  > Held (O'Connor for the Court, 5-4): Bank larceny is not a lesser included offense of the bank robbery charged, so no instruction on it was required. Unanimously, value over $1,000 is an element of the higher larceny grade, not a sentencing factor.
- Apprendi v. New Jersey
  > Held (Stone for the Court, 6-3): Under the Sixth Amendment jury right and the Due Process Clause, any fact about the present offense needed to raise punishment above the maximum the verdict or plea supports is an element. It must be admitted or proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt.

## 2. Reserved landmark cases

Questions are quoted from the Supreme Court's Questions Presented documents (supremecourt.gov/qp). For the OT2000 cases and for Harris, Cotton and Ring in OT2001, whose question documents are not online, the question comes from the Court's opinion: "as stated" follows the opinion's own statement, "as described" is a close paraphrase. Long preambles are omitted and marked. Where the Court limited its grant, the question as granted is quoted, with the Court's capitals set in ordinary case and formatting codes removed.

Status says what this Court's law already does to each case: Open (nothing decided bears directly), Framed (decided law sets the terms but leaves the question), Partly answered, Reshaped (the question arrives in a different form because a predicate differs from history), or Collision (deciding it as history did would require overruling this Court's own holding). None of these landmarks is a Collision. The most common reshaping is the missing prior-conviction exception: many historical questions say "other than a prior conviction" because history's Almendarez-Torres created that exception, and here it does not exist.

### 2.1 On the OT2000 list

### OT2000: Glover v. United States, 531 U.S. 198 (No. 99-8576)

Area: Guidelines errors and ineffective assistance.
Status: Partly answered. Here Lockhart v. Fretwell (O'Connor for six, without Stone) denies Strickland prejudice only for the loss of a benefit the law does not recognize and adds no unfairness requirement in the ordinary case, and Terry Williams (Part I, eight Justices, without Stone) confirms that Fretwell adds no separate unfairness test to ordinary reasonable-probability prejudice. Because the federal Guidelines bind the judge (Williams v. United States, Koon, R.L.C.), a Guidelines error changes the lawful sentence. What remains is whether any added prison time is prejudice, whatever its size. On the OT2000 list (January 9, 2001).
Question, as described in the opinion: whether an increase of at least 6 and perhaps 21 months in a federal prison sentence, assumed to result from a Sentencing Guidelines error that counsel failed to press, would be "prejudice" under Strickland v. Washington.

### OT2000: Buford v. United States, 532 U.S. 59 (No. 99-9073)

### 2.2 Facts that set the range: maximums, minimums and the indictment

### OT2001: United States v. Cotton, 535 U.S. 625 (No. 01-687)

Area: Indictment omissions; plain error.
Status: Reshaped. History's Cotton began from the premise that Apprendi and Jones require a fact raising the maximum to be charged in a federal indictment. Here the federal charging duty rests on statutory decisions (Almendarez-Torres, Jones and Castillo: an aggravating element must be in the indictment, and admitting the fact does not cure its omission), while Apprendi, a state case, decided no charging question. No decision classifies drug quantity under 21 U.S.C. §841(b). The forfeited-error framework is set by Olano and by Johnson (1997, Stone for the Court), which judges plainness at the time of appeal when the law changed after trial and declined correction where the omitted element was clearly met.
Question, as stated in the opinion: In this case, we address whether the omission from a federal indictment of a fact that enhances the statutory maximum sentence justifies a court of appeals' vacating the enhanced sentence, even though the defendant did not object in the trial court.

### OT2001: Harris v. United States, 536 U.S. 545 (No. 00-10666)

Area: Mandatory minimums; McMillan.
Status: Framed. McMillan v. Pennsylvania (1986) stands: Almendarez-Torres, Jones, Castillo and Apprendi each distinguished it because its minimum left the maximum unchanged, and Apprendi expressly reserved minimums. Noncontrolling positions: Thomas (concurring in Apprendi) and Stevens with Scalia (concurring in Jones, Part I) would treat a fact raising the minimum as an element; Souter and Ginsburg limited their constitutional view to the maximum; Stone has reserved the question; O'Connor, Kennedy and Breyer dissented in Apprendi. The statutory question concerns the 1998 version of §924(c), which Castillo expressly left undecided.
Questions, as stated in the opinion: "The principal question before us is whether McMillan stands after Apprendi." And: "We must first answer a threshold question of statutory construction: Did Congress make brandishing an element or a sentencing factor in § 924(c)(1)(A)?"

### 2.3 Capital sentencing

### OT2001: Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (No. 01-488)

Area: Capital aggravators; who finds them.
Status: Framed. Walton v. Arizona (1990) lets a judge sitting without a jury find the aggravating circumstance that makes an Arizona defendant eligible for death, and Apprendi left Walton undisturbed while reserving capital eligibility and selection. Only Stevens (concurring in Jones, Part II) has urged reconsidering Walton; Thomas reserved the capital question in his Apprendi concurrence; O'Connor's Apprendi dissent called the tension with Walton substantial. Tuilaepa (eight Justices) separates eligibility from selection, and Schiro holds that an advisory recommendation against death is not an acquittal of an aggravator. Richmond v. Lewis reviewed Arizona's judge-found aggravators and appellate reweighing without any jury question.
Question, as stated in the opinion: The question presented is whether that aggravating factor may be found by the judge, as Arizona law specifies, or whether the Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee, made applicable to the States by the Fourteenth Amendment, requires that the aggravating factor determination be entrusted to the jury.

### OT2015: Hurst v. Florida, 577 U.S. 92 (No. 14-7505)

Area: Hybrid capital sentencing.
Status: Framed, and it depends on Ring (OT2001). Florida's jury recommends by majority without naming aggravators, and the judge finds them. Espinosa (seven Justices) holds that because Florida's judge must give the recommendation great weight, a defect in the jury's instructions infects the sentence; Sochor Part III-A (five Justices, Stone reserving) holds that a general recommendation naming no aggravators does not by itself show reliance on any one of them; Harris v. Alabama (seven Justices, Stone dissenting) lets a judge override an advisory life recommendation without giving it a set weight. Spaziano v. Florida (1984), which lets a State give the final decision to a judge, is still good law.
Question as granted:
> Whether Florida’s death sentencing scheme violates the Sixth Amendment or the Eight Amendment in light of this Court’s decision in Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (2002)

The petition also asked whether the jury must find intellectual disability; the grant was limited to the question above.

### 2.4 Guidelines systems, the remedy and review

### OT2003: Blakely v. Washington, 542 U.S. 296 (No. 02-1632)

Area: State guidelines; the relevant maximum.
Status: Framed and reshaped. Apprendi reserved "materially different sentencing systems, including general Guidelines classifications." R.L.C. (seven Justices, with Stone) treats the federal Guidelines ceiling as the maximum lawfully authorized for a juvenile, so this Court has already treated a guidelines limit as a legal ceiling in one setting. Witte, Watts and Edwards allow judges to find facts by a preponderance only within the punishment legally authorized by the conviction. The question's parenthetical "(other than a prior conviction)" reflects history's exception, which does not exist here.
> Whether a fact (other than a prior conviction) necessary for an upward departure from a statutory standard sentencing range must be proved according to the procedures mandated by Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466 (2000).

### OT2004: United States v. Booker, 543 U.S. 220 (Nos. 04-104 and 04-105)

Area: Federal Guidelines; remedy.
Status: Framed and reshaped, and it depends on Blakely (OT2003). Here the federal Guidelines are binding law: a judge must apply them and may depart only on grounds the Commission did not adequately consider (Williams v. United States, Koon), interpretive commentary binds (Stinson), and the Commission may not displace an applicable enhanced statutory maximum (LaBonte). No decision addresses severing the provisions that make the Guidelines mandatory. Like Blakely's, the question excepts prior convictions on history's premise.
> 1. Whether the Sixth Amendment is violated by the imposition of an enhanced sentence under the United States Sentencing Guidelines based on the sentencing judge's determination of a fact (other than a prior conviction) that was not found by the jury or admitted by the defendant.
> 2. If the answer to the first question is "yes," the following question is presented: whether, in a case in which the Guidelines would require the court to find a sentence-enhancing fact, the Sentencing Guidelines as a whole would be inapplicable, as a matter of severability analysis, such that the sentencing court must exercise its discretion to sentence the defendant within the maximum and minimum set by statute for the offense of conviction.

United States v. Fanfan (No. 04-105), consolidated, presented the same questions.

### OT2005: Washington v. Recuenco, 548 U.S. 212 (No. 05-83)

Area: Harmless error for sentencing-fact errors.
Status: Framed and reshaped. Here an omitted or withdrawn element is trial error under Chapman, but the method is narrower than history's: the Government must show that the error did not contribute to the actual verdict, and strong evidence alone fails when the omission breaks the connection to that verdict (Neder Part III, Thomas for seven; Roy Part III). ## 3. The doctrine as it stands

### 3.1 A short primer

What an element is. A crime is defined by a list of facts the prosecution must prove: for example, that the defendant, while armed, took a car from another person by force. Each fact on that list is an element. Elements carry three protections. In federal court a grand jury's indictment must charge each one (Fifth Amendment). A jury, not the judge, decides each one (Sixth Amendment, applied to the States through Duncan v. Louisiana). And each must be proved beyond a reasonable doubt (due process, In re Winship). A defendant who pleads guilty must admit the elements.

What a sentencing factor is. After a conviction the judge chooses a sentence within the range the law allows for that crime. In choosing, the judge considers many facts: the harm done, the defendant's role, his record, his conduct at trial. These are sentencing factors. The judge finds them, usually by a preponderance of the evidence (more likely than not), and the indictment need not mention them.

Why it matters who finds a fact. The label decides the protections. If a legislature could call any fact a sentencing factor, it could move the most important facts in a case away from the jury and lower the standard of proof. This whole line of cases asks when the Constitution, or a statute read in its light, forbids that move.

What "the maximum the verdict authorizes" means. Every conviction carries a legal ceiling. Apprendi is the example. He pleaded guilty to a firearm offense punishable by five to ten years. A separate New Jersey law raised the range to ten to twenty years if the judge found that the crime was motivated by racial bias. The judge found that fact by a preponderance and imposed twelve years. Twelve years was more than the plea alone allowed, so the bias finding, not the plea, was what authorized the extra two years. A fact like that, one needed to go above the ceiling that the verdict or plea sets, is what this Court now treats as an element for the jury. Facts that only guide the choice of a sentence below the ceiling remain for the judge.

Three complications run through the rest of this document, and this Court has reserved all three: a prior conviction (a fact about the defendant's past, already proved in an earlier case); a mandatory minimum (a fact that raises the floor without raising the ceiling); and binding sentencing guidelines (rules that limit the judge inside the statutory range, so that facts the judge finds can raise the practical ceiling).

### 3.2 Elements and the jury

Doctrine now: when a fact is an element, the jury decides it beyond a reasonable doubt, including a mixed question such as materiality; the judge states the legal standard but may not apply it for the jury (Gaudin, 9-0; Neder Part II, 9-0). Taking an element from the jury is constitutional error, which a defendant can forfeit by not objecting (Johnson v. United States, 1997, Stone for the Court). Whether a statute makes a fact an element is a question of construction: materiality is an element of the mail, wire and bank fraud statutes (Neder Part I) but not of the statute punishing false statements to banks (Wells, 8-1), and value over $1,000 is an element of the higher grade of bank larceny (Carter, unanimous on that point). A reasonable-doubt instruction is tested as a whole, for a reasonable likelihood that the jury understood it to allow conviction on less proof (Victor); one that vitiates the standard for every finding leaves no valid verdict and is structural error (Sullivan, 9-0). The jury right attaches to serious offenses. An offense punishable by six months or less is presumed petty unless its other authorized penalties clearly show the legislature considers it serious (Nachtigal, 9-0); serious criminal contempt fines require a jury (Bagwell, 9-0); and in a nonjury trial of joined petty offenses after a timely jury demand, total imprisonment may not exceed six months (Lewis, five Justices, with no majority rationale for the judgment).

Lewis v. United States in plain terms. Lewis was tried by a magistrate judge, without a jury, on two counts of obstructing the mail, each punishable by up to six months. Before trial the magistrate judge announced that imprisonment would not exceed six months in all; Lewis received probation. The real Court (O'Connor) held that the jury right is measured offense by offense, so combining two petty offenses never creates a right to a jury. Here the judgment was the same (affirmed, 7-2), but no rationale has a majority. Five Justices (Stone, Kennedy, Breyer, Stevens and Ginsburg) agreed only that after a timely jury demand, a nonjury trial of joined petty offenses cannot lead to more than six months of total imprisonment. Stone's own view counts the combined exposure first and then asks whether a binding limit announced in advance removes serious punishment from the case; here the magistrate's announcement did.

### 3.3 Facts that raise the maximum

Doctrine now: the law has two layers. In the statutory layer, the Court has read federal statutes to make maximum-raising facts elements of aggravated offenses: the prior aggravated-felony conviction in the reentry statute (Almendarez-Torres, 5-4), serious bodily injury and death in the original carjacking statute (Jones, 6-3), weapon type in the pre-1998 firearm statute (Castillo, 9-0) and value in bank larceny (Carter). Each such element must be alleged in the federal indictment and, unless validly admitted as an element, proved to the jury beyond a reasonable doubt; admitting the fact does not cure an indictment that omits it. The constitutional layer is Apprendi (Stone for six): under the Sixth Amendment jury right and due process, a fact about the commission or consequences of the present offense that is legally necessary to authorize punishment above the maximum supported by the verdict or plea is an element, whatever the legislature calls it, and must be admitted as an element or proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Judges may still find facts that select punishment within the authority the conviction already gives (Witte, Watts, Edwards).

Apprendi expressly left open: facts of prior conviction (it creates no prior-conviction exception and decides nothing about them); whether and how a State must charge a covered fact (the Fifth Amendment grand-jury clause is not applied to the States); mandatory minimums that leave the maximum unchanged (McMillan is not overruled); general consecutive-sentence rules; capital eligibility and selection (Walton is not overruled); materially different sentencing systems, including general Guidelines classifications; and the harmless-error treatment of a missing finding (no universal structural-error rule, and Neder is not automatically extended). Each is a question in section 4.

Noncontrolling positions on those open questions. Prior convictions: Stone, concurring in Almendarez-Torres, would hold as a constitutional matter that a prior conviction raising the maximum from two to twenty years is an element; Thomas, concurring in Apprendi, and Stevens with Scalia, concurring in Jones (Part I), reject any categorical exclusion for prior convictions; Souter and Ginsburg, concurring in Jones, reserved bare prior-conviction status; O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer dissented in Almendarez-Torres, though Thomas has since said in Apprendi that he no longer treats prior convictions as categorically different. Mandatory minimums: Thomas and Stevens with Scalia would include facts that raise the minimum; Souter and Ginsburg confined their view to the maximum; Stone has reserved the point. Capital eligibility: Stevens alone (Jones, Part II) would include death-eligibility facts and reconsider Walton; Thomas reserved the capital question. O'Connor, Kennedy and Breyer dissented in Apprendi and would allow a bounded legislative choice to treat such a fact as a sentencing factor.

Almendarez-Torres v. United States in plain terms. Almendarez-Torres had been deported after aggravated-felony convictions and returned illegally. The illegal-reentry statute set a two-year maximum in subsection (a) and a twenty-year maximum in subsection (b)(2) for a person deported after an aggravated felony. His indictment did not mention the earlier convictions; he admitted them at his plea but objected that the indictment authorized no more than two years, and he received 85 months. The real Court (Breyer, 5-4) held that (b)(2) is only a penalty provision and that the Constitution lets a judge find a prior conviction even when it raises the maximum; that holding became Apprendi's "other than the fact of a prior conviction." Here the Court reversed, 5-4. Scalia's Part I, joined by Stone, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, read (b)(2) as defining a separate aggravated offense whose prior-conviction element must be charged and proved, so his sentence could not exceed two years. Four of the five reached that reading to avoid a serious constitutional doubt; Stone alone would have held it constitutionally required. O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer dissented. The result: there is no constitutional prior-conviction exception here, and the constitutional question history answered in 1998 is open.

Jones v. United States (carjacking) in plain terms. Jones was convicted under the original federal carjacking statute, which set fifteen years for the basic crime, twenty-five if serious bodily injury resulted and life if death resulted. Neither the indictment nor the jury's verdict established injury; the judge found it and imposed the aggravated sentence. The real Court (Souter, 5-4) read the statute as creating three offenses in order to avoid constitutional doubt. Here the result is the same, but the margin and grounds differ: Scalia wrote for six (with Stone, Stevens, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg) on the statutory reading alone, and Souter, joined by Stevens, Scalia and Ginsburg, added a constitutional ground that is not the Court's holding. Stone joined the statutory ground and noted his broader view without making it a basis of decision. Kennedy, O'Connor and Breyer dissented.

Apprendi v. New Jersey in plain terms. The facts are in the primer. The real Court (Stevens, 5-4, with Rehnquist among the dissenters) held: "Other than the fact of a prior conviction, any fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury, and proved beyond a reasonable doubt." Here Stone wrote for six (Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg); O'Connor, Kennedy and Breyer dissented. The result is the same, but the rule differs in four ways. It has no prior-conviction exception, because none exists here; it simply does not decide prior convictions. It is stated for facts about the present offense. It decides no charging question. And it lists its reservations expressly: mandatory minimums, consecutive sentences, capital sentencing, guidelines systems and the harmless-error treatment of a missing finding. Thomas joined in full and wrote separately for a broader rule that would cover prior convictions and minimums.

### 3.4 Prior convictions

Doctrine now: there is no constitutional rule, either way, on whether a jury must find a prior conviction that raises the maximum. Under the reentry statute it is an element (Almendarez-Torres). For double jeopardy, a State whose proof that a prior conviction qualifies as a "serious" felony fails may try again at a noncapital sentencing (Monge, 5-4, Stone joining); that holding relies on no constitutional recidivism exception and reserves jury, charging and due-process claims. At federal sentencing under the Armed Career Criminal Act, a defendant may attack a prior conviction only for a complete denial of appointed counsel (Custis, 6-3, Stone joining). A State may presume an old conviction regular and make the defendant produce evidence of invalidity (Parke). A valid fine-only uncounseled misdemeanor may add a Guidelines criminal-history point where the defendant can contest it (Nichols, five Justices, no majority rationale; Baldasar is not overruled generally). On habeas, a rule barring renewed proof of persistent-offender status was new under Teague (Caspari). When prior-conviction status is an element and the defendant offers an adequate, unconditional admission, Rule 403 requires excluding the name and nature of the prior offense if they add only propensity risk (Old Chief, 6-3, Stone joining). On statutory meaning: a career offender's "maximum term authorized" is the recidivist-enhanced maximum (LaBonte, 6-3); a restoration of civil rights that keeps a firearm restriction leaves the conviction's federal effect intact (Caron, Stone for six); and a second firearm count in the same prosecution is a "second or subsequent conviction" (Deal, 5-4, Stone dissenting). No decision of this Court has applied or refined the categorical approach of Taylor v. United States (1990), under which a sentencing court asks what offense a prior conviction necessarily established, looking to the statute and, in a narrow class of cases, to the charging papers and jury instructions, rather than to what the defendant actually did.

Monge v. California in plain terms. Under California's three-strikes law, a prior "serious felony" doubled Monge's sentence for drug offenses. His prior assault counted as serious only if he had personally used a weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury. At a hearing before a judge (Monge waived a jury), the State's prison record proved the assault conviction but not that personal conduct, and the State conceded the proof failed. The question was whether the State could try again. The real Court (O'Connor, 5-4) said yes, holding that double jeopardy protections do not extend to noncapital sentencing and relying in part on Almendarez-Torres's treatment of recidivism as a sentencing matter. Here the answer and coalition are the same (Thomas for five, with Stone in the Chief Justice's seat), but the Court relied on no constitutional exception for prior convictions; it classified this particular qualification as recidivist sentencing for double jeopardy purposes and reserved any jury, charging or due-process claim. Scalia, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg dissented, treating the qualifying fact as an element. Note for Stone: he joined a holding that a maximum-raising fact about a prior offense is sentencing for double jeopardy, three months after concurring in Almendarez-Torres that a maximum-raising prior conviction is constitutionally an element; question 3 asks how the two fit.

Nichols v. United States in plain terms. Nichols's earlier Georgia drunk-driving conviction, entered on a no-contest plea without a lawyer, brought only a $250 fine, but it added a point to his federal criminal history, moving his Guidelines range from 168 to 210 months up to 188 to 235 months; he received 235. The real Court (Rehnquist, 6-3) overruled Baldasar v. Illinois and held that any uncounseled misdemeanor conviction valid under Scott v. Illinois may enhance a later sentence. Here the sentence was affirmed 5-4, but no rationale has five votes: O'Connor's four would adopt the broad rule, and Souter supplied a fifth vote only for this Guidelines use, where the defendant can challenge the point's reliability and seek a departure. Stone dissented and would have remanded to decide whether Nichols validly waived counsel.

### 3.5 Mandatory minimums

Doctrine now: McMillan v. Pennsylvania (1986) still holds that a fact raising a mandatory minimum, without raising the maximum, may be found by the judge by a preponderance; Almendarez-Torres, Jones, Castillo and Apprendi each distinguished and preserved it. Statutory minimums bind. A court may go below one for substantial assistance only on the Government's request or consent to that relief (Melendez, 7-2), and only the Government's motion opens that door (Wade, 9-0). A statutory minimum above the Guidelines range becomes the Guidelines sentence, and the statute and the Guidelines may measure drugs differently (Neal, 9-0). The enhanced twenty-year term in §924(c) applies to a second count in the same prosecution (Deal, 5-4, Stone dissenting). The recorded positions on whether minimum-raising facts are elements are listed in 3.3.

### 3.6 Sentencing within the range: guidelines, relevant conduct and acquitted conduct

Doctrine now: the federal Guidelines are binding law. A judge must apply them and may depart only on a ground the Commission did not adequately consider; a departure resting on a forbidden factor misapplies them (Williams v. United States, 7-2), and departure decisions are reviewed for abuse of discretion, with legal errors corrected (Koon, unanimous on that core; Stone did not join its detailed method for classifying factors). Commentary interpreting a guideline binds unless it conflicts with the Constitution, a statute or the guideline (Stinson, eight Justices, without Stone). The Guidelines ceiling is part of the lawful maximum for a juvenile (R.L.C., 7-2), and the Commission may not replace an applicable enhanced statutory maximum with a lower one (LaBonte). Within the punishment the conviction authorizes, a judge may find facts by a preponderance: uncharged relevant conduct, which is not prosecution or punishment for that conduct (Witte, 8-1); conduct underlying an acquittal (Watts, 7-2); drug types in the same course of conduct as a general verdict (Edwards, 9-0); and perjury at trial, on independent findings (Dunnigan, 9-0). A judge may not draw an adverse inference from the defendant's silence about the facts of the crime (Mitchell, 6-3). Reliance on an invalid factor requires resentencing unless the record shows the judge would have imposed the same sentence anyway (Williams; Koon, Part IV). Every one of these decisions limits itself to punishment legally authorized by the conviction, and Watts reserves extraordinary magnification and a special factual acquittal. Apprendi reserved general Guidelines classifications. The open question is therefore whether a binding Guidelines range is itself part of what the conviction authorizes, which is what Blakely and Booker ask.

### 3.7 Capital sentencing: who finds the facts

Doctrine now: Walton v. Arizona (1990) lets a judge sitting without a jury find the aggravating circumstances that make a defendant eligible for death, and Apprendi left it undisturbed. A State may give the final sentencing decision to a judge (Spaziano, 1984) and need not give a jury's advisory recommendation any set weight or adopt Florida's override standard (Harris v. Alabama, 7-2, Stone dissenting). Where a State gives the recommendation legal force, as Florida does by requiring great weight, a vague aggravator in the jury's instructions infects the sentence (Espinosa, 7-2). A judge's weighing of an invalid or unsupported aggravator requires a genuine cure, either lawful reweighing or proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute; counting the remaining aggravators is not enough (Sochor; Richmond v. Lewis). A general jury recommendation that names no aggravators does not by itself show that the jury relied on an unsupported one (Sochor Part III-A, five Justices; Stone reserved). Selection factors need not be yes-or-no factual findings (Tuilaepa, 8-1), which marks the line between eligibility and selection. An advisory recommendation against death is not an acquittal that bars a judge from finding an aggravator (Schiro, 7-2). Under the Federal Death Penalty Act, the jury's death recommendation must be unanimous, a deadlock requires a nondeath sentence, and the jury must not be misled about the lawful alternatives (Jones v. United States, 1999). Bullington and Rumsey still protect acquittal-like capital determinations, as Monge preserved.

Jones v. United States (federal death penalty) in plain terms. Jones was convicted of a federal kidnapping in which the victim died, an offense punishable only by death or life without release. The jury recommended death. The charge told jurors that if they could not agree unanimously, they could choose a form that seemed to allow a sentence below life, and the judge refused Jones's request for an accurate account of what would follow a deadlock. The real Court (Thomas, 5-4) affirmed: no instruction about deadlock is required, the charge was not misleading, and any error in the aggravators was harmless. Here the death sentence was vacated for a new penalty hearing, 5-4. Breyer wrote; eight Justices agreed that a deadlock requires a nondeath sentence (Stone agreeing in the result), all nine rejected a required deadlock script, and five (Stone, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer) held that this misleading charge had to be corrected once Jones asked. Stone's vote, joining the four historical dissenters, decided the case.

### 3.8 Fines, forfeiture and restitution

Doctrine now: the authorized fines count in deciding whether an offense is serious enough to require a jury (Nachtigal; Lewis), and serious criminal contempt fines, here about $52 million, require a jury (Bagwell, 9-0). Under the federal drug statutes, the extent of criminal forfeiture is part of the sentence, and the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to decide it (Libretti Part II-A, five Justices; Stone, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg reserved the constitutional source of the forfeiture jury); a defendant may knowingly waive the statutory forfeiture jury, but consent cannot enlarge what the statute allows (Libretti Part I-B, unanimous). Excessive fines are reviewed under Bajakajian. No decision addresses restitution or whether Apprendi reaches fines.

Libretti v. United States in plain terms. After several days of trial, Libretti pleaded guilty to running a continuing criminal enterprise and agreed to forfeit property. He later argued that the court needed an independent factual basis for the forfeiture and that he had a right to a jury on it. The real Court (O'Connor, 8-1) held that forfeiture is part of the sentence, not an element, so neither the plea rule's factual-basis requirement nor the Sixth Amendment jury right applies to it. Here the result is the same, 8-1, but the Sixth Amendment holding has only five votes (O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer). Stone concurred on the ground that Libretti knowingly gave up the forfeiture jury, and said that a sentencing label alone cannot answer the constitutional question.

### 3.9 Revocation of release

Doctrine now: revoking parole-like conditional liberty requires the hearings Morrissey v. Brewer describes, not a jury (Young v. Harper, 9-0). Supervised release begins on actual release from prison (United States v. Johnson, 9-0). Penalties imposed on revocation are punishment for the original offense, so the law in force when that offense was committed governs (Johnson v. United States, 2000, Part I-A unanimous); former §3583(e)(3) allowed a court to impose prison followed by further supervision (seven Justices; Stone and Scalia dissenting). Stone wrote that a later statute cannot add punishment to the original offense and that former law allowed no renewed supervision. Under the former probation statute, the mandatory revocation term on Granderson's record ran from one-third of the original Guidelines range's six-month maximum to that maximum (Granderson, 8-1). No decision addresses whether a revocation that triggers a mandatory prison term on a judge's finding of a new crime requires a jury.

### 3.10 Unanimity and verdicts

Doctrine now: a federal jury must agree unanimously on each element, and in a continuing-criminal-enterprise prosecution each violation in the required series is an element (Richardson, 6-3); jurors need not agree on the means of committing an agreed element (Schad v. Arizona, 1991). A general verdict on a multiple-object conspiracy stands if one valid object is supported, with an independently raised unanimity defect reserved (Griffin, 8-0). The Federal Death Penalty Act requires a unanimous death recommendation (Jones, 1999). In state courts, Apodaca v. Oregon (1972) allows verdicts that are not unanimous, and no decision of this Court revisits it.

### 3.11 Harmless error and plain error

Doctrine now: a reasonable-doubt instruction that vitiates the standard for all findings is structural error and requires reversal (Sullivan). Omitting or withdrawing an element is trial error reviewed under Chapman: the Government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the actual verdict. It may rely on supported inferences from the whole record, but only after examining what the instructions required and what the jury actually considered; a hypothetical properly instructed verdict, or strong evidence alone, will not do (Neder Part III, seven Justices; Roy Part III, five). On habeas, Chapman also governs instructions that omit, or let the jury skip, the defendant's own intent (O'Neal, Roy). An unpreserved error may be corrected only if it is plain and affected substantial rights (Olano), and then only if it seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of the proceedings; when the law changes after trial, plainness is judged at the time of appeal (Johnson v. United States, 1997). A Guidelines error is harmless only if the record shows the judge would have imposed the same sentence (Williams). Apprendi adopted no structural-error rule for a missing sentencing finding and did not automatically extend Neder to it.

Neder v. United States in plain terms. Neder was convicted of filing false tax returns and of mail, wire and bank fraud; the trial judge decided materiality himself instead of giving it to the jury. All nine Justices agreed that materiality is an element of the fraud statutes and that the jury had to decide it. The question was the remedy. The real Court (Rehnquist) held the omission harmless when the omitted element was uncontested and supported by overwhelming evidence, so that the verdict would have been the same. Here Thomas, for seven, adopted a narrower test: the Government must show the error did not contribute to the verdict the jury actually returned, and strong evidence alone fails if the omission keeps the court from connecting the evidence to that verdict. Applying it, the tax convictions stood, 6-3, because the jury's actual findings on the specific false income figures supplied that connection; Stone, Scalia and Ginsburg dissented. The fraud counts were sent back for the first proper harmlessness review, and Stone would instead have ordered new trials on all affected counts. The narrower test builds on Part III of Roy.

### 3.12 Where this Court departed from history

Results that differ: Almendarez-Torres (the prior conviction is a statutory element and the 85-month sentence was vacated, 5-4; history affirmed and allowed judges to find recidivism that raises the maximum); Jones v. United States (federal death penalty; the death sentence was vacated for a new penalty hearing, 5-4, with Stone joining the four historical dissenters).

Same results, different reasoning: Apprendi (6-3 with Stone writing; no prior-conviction exception; a present-offense rule with express reservations; no charging holding); Jones v. United States (carjacking; six votes for the statutory ground and only four for a constitutional one); Castillo (9-0 without any recidivism exception to rely on); Monge (no constitutional recidivism exception; jury and charging claims reserved); Neder (a qualified actual-verdict test instead of history's overwhelming-evidence test); O'Neal and Roy (Chapman on habeas instead of history's Kotteakos and Brecht standards); Lewis (no majority rationale, and a five-Justice limit on total nonjury imprisonment instead of an offense-by-offense rule); Nichols (no majority rationale; Baldasar not generally overruled); Libretti (the no-jury forfeiture holding has five votes, with Stone reserving); Witte (explained without overruling Grady v. Corbin, which this Court's Dixon retained).

Same results, different margins: Jones (carjacking), Apprendi, Old Chief and Mitchell are 6-3 where history was 5-4; Nichols and Deal are 5-4 where history was 6-3, with Stone dissenting in both; Harris v. Alabama is 7-2 where history was 8-1, with Stone dissenting.

Stone's distinctive positions, none controlling: Almendarez-Torres (the element status is constitutionally required); Jones (carjacking; maintained a broader view that a fact increasing authorized punishment gets the jury protection owed an element, without making it a ground of decision); Neder (dissented from affirming the tax counts and would have ordered new trials on all affected fraud counts rather than a harmlessness remand); Old Chief (would order a new trial rather than a harmlessness remand); Nichols (would remand to decide waiver of counsel and would extend Baldasar's concern to a Guidelines increase); Victor (would have reversed Victor's conviction for a defective reasonable-doubt charge); Harris v. Alabama (would have reversed the death sentence because the override of the jury's life recommendation was not adequately explained or reviewable); Lewis (counts combined exposure first and accepts only an effective advance cap); Libretti (reserved the constitutional source of the forfeiture jury); Deal (would require the earlier conviction to precede the later offense); Johnson v. United States, 2000 (a later statute cannot add punishment to the original offense, and former law allowed no renewed supervision); Koon (did not join the factor-classification method and opposed two departure grounds); Sochor (reserved whether a general jury recommendation shows reliance on an unsupported aggravator); Jones (federal death penalty; joined Souter's constitutional and plain-error grounds for the new penalty hearing). Stone also wrote for the Court in Apprendi, in Johnson (1997) and in Caron.

## 4. Questions to answer before the landmarks

Questions only. Under each, the landmarks it should be answered before.

### 4.1 Prior convictions and the categorical approach

1. Does the Apprendi rule reach a prior conviction that raises the maximum? Almendarez-Torres decided only the reentry statute, Stone alone would have held the element status constitutionally required, and Apprendi left prior-conviction facts undecided. Typical vehicles: Armed Career Criminal Act sentences under 18 U.S.C. §924(e), aggravated reentry under 8 U.S.C. §1326(b), recidivist drug enhancements under 21 U.S.C. §841(b) and §851, and state habitual-offender laws in which the judge finds the prior convictions.
    > Answer before: OT2003, Blakely v. Washington; OT2004, United States v. Booker; OT2004, Shepard v. United States; OT2012, Descamps v. United States; OT2023, Erlinger v. United States.
2. If prior convictions are covered, does the rule distinguish the bare fact of a conviction, proved by the judgment, from facts about the earlier offense, such as what the defendant did, whether he used a weapon, or whether two offenses were committed on different occasions? Monge classified one such personal-conduct qualification as recidivist sentencing for double jeopardy, with Stone in the majority, while reserving jury and charging claims.
    > Answer before: OT2004, Shepard v. United States; OT2023, Erlinger v. United States.
3. Does Monge's double jeopardy classification survive if the qualifying fact is an element for jury purposes, and how does Stone's Monge join fit with his Almendarez-Torres concurrence? Typical vehicle: a California three-strikes or serious-felony finding retried after insufficient proof.
    > Answer before: no listed landmark.
4. When a federal court decides what a prior conviction was (for example, whether a burglary conviction was generic burglary under Taylor), which records may it consult when the defendant pleaded guilty: the plea colloquy, the plea agreement, police reports, complaint applications? No decision of this Court has applied Taylor.
    > Answer before: OT2004, Shepard v. United States.
5. Does the modified categorical approach apply only to statutes that list alternative elements, or also to indivisible statutes and to statutes that list alternative means? How does a court tell elements, on which a jury must agree (Richardson), from means, on which it need not (Schad)?
    > Answer before: OT2012, Descamps v. United States; OT2015, Mathis v. United States.
6. How deferentially do appellate courts review a Guidelines classification of prior convictions, such as whether they were related because consolidated for sentencing? Koon chose abuse-of-discretion review for departures.
    > Answer before: OT2000, Buford v. United States (on the OT2000 list).
7. After sentencing, may a prisoner attack an expired prior conviction that enhanced his sentence, through §2255 or §2254, and with what exceptions? Custis reserved it.
    > Answer before: OT2000, Daniels v. United States (on the OT2000 list); OT2000, Lackawanna County District Attorney v. Coss (on the OT2000 list).
8. Does Nichols's limited permission (a valid fine-only uncounseled misdemeanor may add a Guidelines point where the defendant can contest it and seek a departure) reach recidivist laws that add prison time automatically? Stone's Nichols dissent would extend Baldasar's concern to any Guidelines increase.
    > Answer before: no listed landmark.

### 4.2 Facts that set the range

9. Is a fact that raises a mandatory minimum, without raising the maximum, an element, and should McMillan be overruled? Apprendi reserved this. Thomas (Apprendi) and Stevens with Scalia (Jones, Part I) would treat such facts as elements; Souter and Ginsburg confined their view to the maximum; Stone has reserved it. Typical vehicles: judge-found brandishing or discharge minimums under §924(c), drug-quantity minimums under 21 U.S.C. §841(b), and state firearm minimums like the one in McMillan.
    > Answer before: OT2001, Harris v. United States; OT2012, Alleyne v. United States.
10. As a matter of statutory construction, are brandishing, discharge and weapon type under the restructured 1998 §924(c) elements or sentencing factors? Castillo decided the pre-1998 statute and reserved the 1998 version.
    > Answer before: OT2001, Harris v. United States; OT2009, United States v. O'Brien.
11. In a federal case, must the indictment allege a fact that the Apprendi rule makes an element, such as drug quantity under 21 U.S.C. §841(b)? Apprendi, a state case, decided no charging question; the federal charging duty so far rests on the statutory decisions (Almendarez-Torres, Jones, Castillo).
    > Answer before: OT2001, United States v. Cotton.
12. When a federal indictment omits such a fact and the defendant did not object, is the defect jurisdictional, structural, or reviewable for plain error under Olano and Johnson (1997)? Does an indictment omission differ from a jury omission?
    > Answer before: OT2001, United States v. Cotton.
13. What is the maximum "supported by the verdict or plea" in a system with a presumptive or standard range that the judge may exceed only after finding added facts: the top of the statutory range, or the most the judge could impose without any added finding? R.L.C. treated the Guidelines ceiling as the maximum authorized for a juvenile. Typical vehicles: state presumptive-range and determinate systems, such as those of Washington, California, Oregon and Kansas.
    > Answer before: OT2003, Blakely v. Washington; OT2006, Cunningham v. California.
14. Apprendi covers facts about "the commission or consequences of the present offense." Does it also cover facts about the offender that are neither prior convictions nor part of the offense, such as being on probation or release at the time, or a pattern of conduct?
    > Answer before: OT2003, Blakely v. Washington; OT2006, Cunningham v. California.
15. What does a guilty plea admit for these purposes, and what makes a waiver of the jury on sentencing facts valid? Apprendi said only that a lawful waiver keeps its own requirements.
    > Answer before: OT2003, Blakely v. Washington.
16. Do facts required before a judge may impose consecutive rather than concurrent sentences count? Apprendi reserved general consecutive-sentence rules while holding that possible punishment on other counts cannot authorize a higher sentence on one count.
    > Answer before: OT2008, Oregon v. Ice.
17. Must a State charge a fact covered by the Apprendi rule in its indictment or information, and what notice does due process require? Apprendi did not apply the Fifth Amendment grand-jury clause to the States. Typical vehicle: a state prosecution by information that omits a maximum-raising fact.
    > Answer before: no listed landmark.

### 4.3 Sentencing guidelines, relevant conduct and acquitted conduct

18. Does a binding guidelines system make the top of the guideline range the relevant maximum, so that facts the judge finds to raise the range must go to a jury? Apprendi reserved general Guidelines classifications. Here the federal Guidelines are binding law (Williams, Stinson, Koon, R.L.C., LaBonte). Typical vehicles: federal enhancements found by a preponderance, such as drug quantity, loss amount, role in the offense or obstruction.
    > Answer before: OT2003, Blakely v. Washington; OT2004, United States v. Booker.
19. If the federal Guidelines violate the jury right, what is the remedy: jury findings for Guidelines facts, advisory Guidelines (severing the provisions that make them mandatory), or something else?
    > Answer before: OT2004, United States v. Booker.
20. What becomes of Witte, Watts and Edwards, which allow preponderance findings of uncharged, acquitted and same-course conduct within the punishment legally authorized by the conviction, if the Guidelines range is that authorization? Watts reserved extraordinary magnification and a special factual acquittal. Is there any limit, under the Sixth Amendment or due process, on using acquitted conduct to raise a sentence? Typical vehicles: federal sentences raised by drug or firearm conduct of which the jury acquitted.
    > Answer before: OT2004, United States v. Booker; OT2006, Rita v. United States.
21. Under whatever review follows a remedy, may appellate courts presume a within-Guidelines sentence reasonable, and may they demand extraordinary circumstances for a sentence far outside the range? Koon set abuse-of-discretion review for departures; Williams requires remand unless the judge would have imposed the same sentence without an invalid factor.
    > Answer before: OT2006, Rita v. United States; OT2007, Gall v. United States.
22. May a sentencing judge reject a guideline on policy grounds, such as the 100-to-1 crack-to-powder ratio? Neal treats the statutory and Guidelines drug measures as separate; Stinson makes commentary binding.
    > Answer before: OT2007, Kimbrough v. United States.
23. Does a Guidelines error that adds prison time count as prejudice under Strickland, whatever its size? Here Lockhart v. Fretwell denies prejudice only for the loss of a benefit the law does not recognize, and Terry Williams (eight Justices, without Stone) adds no general unfairness requirement.
    > Answer before: OT2000, Glover v. United States (on the OT2000 list).
24. If the Guidelines become advisory, does applying a harsher amended guideline to earlier conduct violate the Ex Post Facto Clause?
    > Answer before: no listed landmark; OT2012, Peugh v. United States is the historical vehicle.

### 4.4 Capital sentencing

25. Are the aggravating circumstances that make a defendant eligible for death elements the jury must find, and should Walton be overruled? Apprendi reserved capital eligibility; only Stevens (Jones, Part II) has urged reconsidering Walton. Typical vehicles: the States in which a judge alone finds the aggravators, such as Arizona, Colorado, Idaho, Montana and Nebraska.
    > Answer before: OT2001, Ring v. Arizona.
26. Which capital findings count: eligibility only, or also the finding that aggravation outweighs mitigation, and the final choice? Tuilaepa separates eligibility from selection; Spaziano lets a judge make the final choice.
    > Answer before: OT2001, Ring v. Arizona; OT2015, Hurst v. Florida.
27. In a hybrid system where a jury recommends by majority vote without stating findings and a judge finds the aggravators, does the recommendation satisfy the jury right? Must the jury's findings of aggravators be unanimous? Espinosa (seven Justices), Sochor Part III-A (five, Stone reserving) and Harris v. Alabama (Stone dissenting) frame it. Typical vehicles: Florida, Alabama, Delaware and Indiana.
    > Answer before: OT2015, Hurst v. Florida.
28. May a state appellate court cure a capital sentencing error by reweighing aggravation and mitigation itself (Clemons, Richmond, Sochor), or does a jury-finding rule require a new jury? Which law applies to a reweighing done years after the original sentence?
    > Answer before: OT2019, McKinney v. Arizona.
29. If aggravators are elements, does a jury's failure to agree on death, or a default life sentence, act as an acquittal that bars a second capital proceeding? Schiro held an advisory recommendation not an acquittal; Monge preserved Bullington for capital acquittals; Jones (1999) holds that a deadlocked federal jury requires a nondeath sentence.
    > Answer before: no listed landmark; OT2002, Sattazahn v. Pennsylvania is the historical vehicle.
30. Is a rule moving capital-eligibility findings from judge to jury substantive or procedural under Teague, and could it be a watershed rule?
    > Answer before: OT2003, Schriro v. Summerlin.

### 4.5 Fines, forfeiture and restitution

31. Does the Apprendi rule apply to criminal fines whose maximum depends on a fact, such as the number of days of violation or the amount of gain or loss? Bagwell requires a jury for serious criminal contempt fines, and Apprendi speaks of punishment generally. Typical vehicles: per-day environmental or regulatory fines, and fines measured by gain or loss under 18 U.S.C. §3571(d).
    > Answer before: OT2011, Southern Union Co. v. United States.
32. Should Libretti Part II-A (no Sixth Amendment jury for the extent of criminal forfeiture), which has five votes and from which Stone reserved, be reconsidered in light of Apprendi?
    > Answer before: no listed landmark.
33. Is federal criminal restitution punishment for jury purposes, and can a rule built on maximums apply to restitution statutes that set no maximum? Typical vehicle: a restitution order under the Mandatory Victims Restitution Act of 1996.
    > Answer before: no listed landmark.

### 4.6 Revocation of release

34. When a statute requires a mandatory prison term on revocation of supervised release because the judge finds, by a preponderance, that the defendant committed a new crime, must a jury make that finding? Johnson v. United States (2000) treats revocation penalties as punishment for the original offense; Young v. Harper requires only Morrissey hearings to revoke conditional liberty; Granderson construed a mandatory revocation term. Typical vehicles: mandatory revocation under 18 U.S.C. §3583(g) and (k).
    > Answer before: OT2018, United States v. Haymond.
35. May total punishment (the original prison term plus revocation prison) exceed the maximum the original verdict authorized? Stone's Johnson (2000) dissent would bar a later statute from adding punishment to the original offense.
    > Answer before: OT2018, United States v. Haymond.

### 4.7 Unanimity and verdicts

36. Does the Sixth Amendment's unanimity requirement apply to the States? Apodaca says no; Richardson requires federal unanimity on each element. Typical vehicles: Louisiana and Oregon convictions returned by 10-2 or 11-1 verdicts.
    > Answer before: OT2019, Ramos v. Louisiana.
37. When a statute lists alternative ways to commit one crime, when must jurors agree on which alternative? Richardson (constituent violations are elements), Schad (alternative means) and Griffin (which reserved an independent unanimity defect) frame it.
    > Answer before: OT2015, Mathis v. United States.
38. When does the jury right attach in a prosecution that joins petty offenses, and may a judge avoid a jury by announcing an advance cap on total imprisonment? Lewis produced only a five-Justice agreement that total imprisonment above six months cannot follow a nonjury trial after a timely demand.
    > Answer before: no listed landmark.

### 4.8 Harmless error and plain error

39. Is the failure to submit a sentencing fact to the jury structural error or trial error under Chapman? If Chapman, does Neder's actual-verdict test apply when the jury was never asked about the fact at all? Apprendi expressly did not extend Neder to sentencing aggravators, and Stone joined Neder's test but would have ordered new trials.
    > Answer before: OT2005, Washington v. Recuenco.
40. When the error is forfeited, how do the third and fourth plain-error conditions apply to an unsubmitted sentencing fact supported by strong evidence? Johnson (1997), written by Stone, declined correction because materiality was clear.
    > Answer before: OT2001, United States v. Cotton; OT2005, Washington v. Recuenco.
