# Supreme Court Doctrine: Master Taxonomy of Reasoning, Tests, Rules, Exceptions, and Precedent

## How to understand the entire system

There is no official Supreme Court master list called “the tests.” Courts use many different kinds of legal machinery.

Your original notes correctly identified several major families, including categorical rules, multi-factor tests, totality analysis, balancing, scrutiny, history-and-tradition analysis, boundary devices, and methods of changing precedent.
The cleaner system is:

| Layer                                      | Question                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1. Interpretive reasoning**              | How is the Court determining what the Constitution means?       |
| **2. Doctrinal form**                      | What shape does the governing rule take?                        |
| **3. Trigger / threshold**                 | What must happen before the doctrine applies?                   |
| **4. Burden / presumption**                | Who must establish what?                                        |
| **5. Rule boundary**                       | What is included or excluded?                                   |
| **6. Justiciability / gatekeeping**        | May the federal court decide the dispute at all?                |
| **7. Challenge / interpretation / remedy** | How broadly is the law challenged, interpreted, or invalidated? |
| **8. Precedent treatment**                 | What is the Court doing to an earlier decision?                 |
| **9. Subject-specific doctrine**           | What actual test controls this constitutional issue?            |

A single case can involve several of these simultaneously.

For example:

> Historical reasoning + a threshold inquiry + burden shifting + historical analogy + an as-applied challenge + distinguishing an earlier precedent.

That is why trying to put every Supreme Court “test” into one flat list creates confusion.

# I. CONSTITUTIONAL REASONING AND INTERPRETATION

Congress's Constitution Annotated identifies eight commonly used modes of constitutional interpretation. It expressly notes that the list is not necessarily exhaustive.

## 1. Textualism

### Core question

> What does the constitutional text mean?

The analysis begins with the words of the Constitution, their linguistic meaning, grammar, context, and relationship to other constitutional language.

### Mental shortcut

**TEXT**

### Typical materials

* constitutional wording
* grammar
* definitions
* textual context
* nearby provisions
* relationships among clauses

Textualism overlaps with originalism, but the two are not identical. Constitution Annotated distinguishes a textual focus from originalist consideration of the text's historical meaning.

---

# 2. Original Meaning / Originalism

### Core question

> What did the constitutional language mean when it was adopted?

Modern originalist reasoning commonly looks for the Constitution's original public meaning.

### Mental shortcut

**TEXT + MEANING AT ADOPTION**

### Common materials

* founding-era dictionaries
* historical legal usage
* ratification debates
* contemporaneous writings
* early legal practices
* historical context

The important distinction is:

**Textualism:** What does the text mean?

**Originalism:** What did the text mean at the relevant historical moment?

Constitution Annotated recognizes original meaning as a separate interpretive mode.

---

# 3. Precedential / Doctrinal Reasoning

### Core question

> What have prior Supreme Court decisions established?

The Court reasons from:

* holdings
* doctrinal rules
* prior tests
* analogies
* distinctions
* lines of precedent
* stare decisis

### Mental shortcut

**CASES → DOCTRINE → CURRENT CASE**

This mode explains why lawyers spend so much time asking whether a precedent should be:

* followed
* distinguished
* extended
* limited
* clarified
* overruled

Judicial precedent is recognized as one of the common modes of constitutional interpretation.

---

# 4. Structural Reasoning / Structuralism

### Core question

> What follows from the Constitution's overall design and allocation of governmental authority?

Structural reasoning draws conclusions from relationships among constitutional institutions rather than necessarily from one isolated sentence.

### Mental shortcut

**CONSTITUTIONAL ARCHITECTURE**

Common subjects:

* separation of powers
* federalism
* checks and balances
* relations among Congress, President, and courts
* state versus federal authority

Constitution Annotated recognizes structuralism as a distinct mode of constitutional reasoning.

## Formalism and functionalism belong here

This corrects the organization of the earlier notes.

### A. Formalist structural reasoning

Core question:

> Which constitutional institution possesses this power, and were the constitutionally prescribed boundaries and procedures respected?

Think:

**category → branch → boundary → procedure**

Formalist reasoning tends to emphasize relatively defined institutional boundaries.

### B. Functionalist structural reasoning

Core question:

> What does the institutional arrangement actually do, and does it materially interfere with another branch's constitutional function?

Think:

**operation → practical effect → institutional interference**

Congress's Constitution Annotated specifically discusses formalist and functionalist approaches within separation-of-powers and structural analysis.

### Therefore

Do not organize them like this:

> Formalist
> Functionalist
> Textualist
> Originalist

A more accurate hierarchy is:

> **Structural reasoning**
>
> * Formalist
> * Functionalist

---

# 5. Historical-Practice Reasoning

### Core question

> What does longstanding governmental practice reveal about constitutional meaning?

This can use conduct occurring after ratification as evidence of how constitutional authority has historically been understood.

### Mental shortcut

**WHAT HAS GOVERNMENT HISTORICALLY DONE?**

It is different from originalism.

**Original meaning:** meaning at adoption.

**Historical practice:** subsequent governmental practice may illuminate constitutional meaning.

The Court has particularly used historical practice in structural and separation-of-powers disputes.

---

# 6. Pragmatic / Consequential Reasoning

### Core question

> What practical consequences follow from adopting one interpretation instead of another?

The Court may consider:

* administrability
* institutional competence
* government functionality
* real-world consequences
* reliance
* workable constitutional rules

### Mental shortcut

**CONSEQUENCES**

Pragmatism is identified as another recognized mode of constitutional interpretation.

Do not equate pragmatism automatically with functionalism.

Functionalism is especially associated with governmental structure.

Pragmatism is a broader concern with practical consequences.

---

# 7. Moral Reasoning

### Core question

> What principles of liberty, equality, justice, or political morality inform the constitutional guarantee?

This method is especially relevant where the Constitution uses broad concepts such as:

* liberty
* equality
* punishment
* due process

### Mental shortcut

**CONSTITUTIONAL PRINCIPLE**

Moral reasoning is another mode expressly identified in Constitution Annotated's taxonomy.

---

# 8. National Ethos / Constitutional Identity

### Core question

> What interpretation reflects distinctive American constitutional traditions, commitments, and institutions?

This resembles moral reasoning but focuses specifically on principles understood to characterize the American constitutional system.

### Mental shortcut

**AMERICAN CONSTITUTIONAL IDENTITY**

Constitution Annotated identifies national identity or national ethos as a separate interpretive mode.

---

# Important rule about interpretive methods

Supreme Court opinions frequently combine methods.

An opinion might use:

**Text + original meaning + historical practice + precedent + structure**

rather than selecting exactly one method.

These approaches are therefore **sources and modes of reasoning**, not mutually exclusive tests.

---

# II. STATUTORY INTERPRETATION IS A RELATED BUT SEPARATE SYSTEM

Supreme Court cases also interpret statutes. Do not automatically mix statutory interpretation with constitutional interpretation.

A major divide in statutory interpretation has been between textual and purposive approaches. Courts also use ordinary meaning, statutory context, canons of construction, structure, and other interpretive evidence.

## Textual approach

Focus:

> What is the best reading of the enacted statutory text?

Typical tools:

* ordinary meaning
* grammar
* statutory definitions
* surrounding provisions
* whole-statute context
* structural relationships

## Purposive reasoning

Focus:

> What statutory purpose or problem was Congress addressing?

Purpose may help explain statutory structure and language, although the degree to which judges rely on purpose varies.

## Canons of construction

Canons are recurring interpretive principles rather than standalone constitutional tests.

Major varieties include:

### Linguistic canons

Rules about how language ordinarily functions.

### Substantive canons

Rules influenced by broader legal or constitutional values.

Important substantive examples include:

* constitutional avoidance
* federalism clear-statement rules
* major questions doctrine

The Court has developed clear-statement principles protecting federalism and other structural values.

---

# III. FORMS OF DOCTRINAL DECISION RULES

These describe the **shape** of the Court's legal framework.

Your original notes correctly recognized that this is different from constitutional interpretation.

## 1. Categorical rule

Structure:

**If X belongs to category A → legal consequence B**

Little individualized weighing occurs after classification.

### Shortcut

**CLASSIFY → RESULT**

Example type:

> Certain categories of speech receive no ordinary First Amendment protection.

The Court recognizes narrowly defined categories including obscenity, incitement, true threats, defamation, and speech integral to criminal conduct.

---

# 2. Bright-line rule

A bright-line rule establishes a comparatively definite boundary.

Structure:

**Cross line X → consequence Y**

The purpose is often predictability and administrability.

“Bright-line” and “categorical” overlap considerably, although not every categorical doctrine is perfectly bright.

---

# 3. Per se rule

A per se rule treats a particular condition as sufficient to establish a legal consequence without ordinary case-by-case balancing.

### Shortcut

**THIS FACT ITSELF CONTROLS**

Takings doctrine, for example, recognizes certain per se categories alongside the more flexible *Penn Central* framework.

---

# 4. Elements / requirements test

Structure:

**A + B + C required**

Each specified requirement ordinarily must be established.

This differs from a factor test.

### Elements

Each requirement must generally be satisfied.

### Factors

The considerations are weighed together and one missing factor may not necessarily defeat the claim.

---

# 5. Prong test

A “prong” is essentially a separately stated part of a larger test.

Examples:

* two-prong test
* three-prong test
* four-prong test

The important question is whether each prong is mandatory or whether the framework operates more flexibly.

---

# 6. Multi-factor test

Structure:

**F1 + F2 + F3 + F4 → overall judgment**

The Court identifies particular considerations but may refuse to make one automatically controlling.

### Shortcut

**CONSIDER THESE SPECIFIC THINGS**

Examples include:

* *Barker v. Wingo* speedy-trial analysis
* *Penn Central* regulatory-takings analysis
* *Baker v. Carr* political-question considerations

The Supreme Court uses multi-factor frameworks in each of those areas.

---

# 7. Totality of the circumstances

Structure:

**All legally relevant circumstances → conclusion**

### Shortcut

**LOOK AT THE WHOLE SITUATION**

This differs from a defined factor test.

**Multi-factor:** the Court specifically identifies considerations.

**Totality:** no closed list necessarily controls.

A major example is *Illinois v. Gates*, which replaced the *Aguilar-Spinelli* probable-cause framework with a totality-of-the-circumstances approach.

---

# 8. Balancing test

Structure:

**Interest A ↔ Interest B**

The Court weighs competing interests.

Possible considerations:

* constitutional burden
* governmental objective
* practical consequences
* institutional interests

### Shortcut

**WEIGH BOTH SIDES**

Examples include *Pickering* public-employee speech and *Barker* speedy-trial doctrine.

---

# 9. Sliding-scale framework

The strength of the required justification changes with the severity of the constitutional burden.

Structure:

**Greater burden → stronger justification required**

The Anderson-Burdick election-law framework is a prominent example. Courts weigh the character and magnitude of the burden against the state's interests and the necessity of the restriction.

---

# 10. Reasonableness standard

Many constitutional doctrines ask whether conduct or regulation is “reasonable.”

That word does not identify one universal test.

Its content depends entirely on the doctrine.

Examples occur throughout:

* Fourth Amendment
* public-forum doctrine
* election administration
* procedural due process

Always identify **which reasonableness doctrine** is being used.

---

# 11. Historical analogy

Structure:

**Modern regulation ↔ historical analogue**

The Court asks whether contemporary government action is sufficiently consistent with historically accepted regulation.

This became particularly important in modern Second Amendment doctrine under *Bruen* and *Rahimi*.

---

# IV. LEVELS AND TYPES OF SCRUTINY

Scrutiny asks how demanding judicial review will be.

Do not assume that every use of words such as “strict,” “intermediate,” or “exacting” operates identically across constitutional doctrines.

## 1. Rational-basis review

Typical Equal Protection formulation:

**legitimate governmental interest + rational relationship**

This is generally deferential.

The Court continues to apply rational-basis review to classifications that do not invoke a suspect or quasi-suspect classification or another basis for heightened review.

---

# 2. Intermediate scrutiny

Classic Equal Protection formulation for sex classifications:

**important governmental objective + substantial relationship**

---

# 3. Strict scrutiny

Classic Equal Protection formulation:

**compelling governmental interest + narrow tailoring**

Race classifications are the paradigmatic Equal Protection example.

---

# 4. Exacting scrutiny

The Court uses “exacting scrutiny” in some First Amendment contexts, including compelled disclosure.

For compelled disclosure, the Court has required a substantial relationship between the disclosure requirement and a sufficiently important governmental interest, with modern cases also emphasizing tailoring.

Do not assume exacting scrutiny is simply another universal “fourth tier.”

It is context-specific.

---

# 5. Intermediate First Amendment review

Content-neutral speech restrictions often receive a form of intermediate scrutiny rather than Equal Protection's intermediate-scrutiny formula.

For example, a content-neutral time, place, or manner restriction generally must be narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest and leave open ample alternative channels of communication.

This illustrates why scrutiny formulas must be tied to the doctrine in which they appear.

---

# V. BURDENS, PRESUMPTIONS, AND BURDEN SHIFTING

These answer:

> Who must establish what, and when?

Your original notes correctly separated burden shifting from ordinary factor tests.

## 1. Burden of production

A party must produce enough evidence to put an issue into dispute.

## 2. Burden of persuasion

A party bears the ultimate responsibility for persuading the decision-maker.

## 3. Rebuttable presumption

Structure:

**Assume X → unless sufficient contrary showing**

## 4. Conclusive / irrebuttable rule

Specified facts establish the legal consequence without ordinary rebuttal.

## 5. Burden-shifting framework

Structure:

**Party A shows X
→ burden moves to Party B
→ Party B must show Y**

A major constitutional example is *Batson*, which uses a staged burden-shifting framework to evaluate discriminatory peremptory challenges.

---

# VI. GATEKEEPING: MAY THE COURT REACH THE MERITS?

This was missing from the first organization.

These doctrines are fundamentally different from merits tests such as strict scrutiny.

## A. Article III justiciability doctrines

### 1. Advisory-opinion prohibition

Federal courts generally decide concrete cases and controversies rather than issue abstract advisory opinions.

The prohibition on advisory opinions is a foundational component of Article III doctrine.

---

### 2. Standing

The familiar Article III standing requirements are:

1. injury in fact
2. causation
3. redressability

### Shortcut

**INJURY → CAUSED BY DEFENDANT → COURT CAN REDRESS**

---

### 3. Ripeness

Ripeness asks whether the dispute has developed enough to permit judicial resolution.

The traditional *Abbott Laboratories* formulation considers:

1. fitness of the issues for judicial decision
2. hardship from withholding review

---

### 4. Mootness

A live controversy generally must continue throughout litigation.

If events eliminate the live dispute, the case may become moot.

Major exceptions or related doctrines include:

#### Voluntary cessation

A defendant ordinarily cannot automatically moot litigation simply by stopping the challenged conduct.

#### Capable of repetition, yet evading review

Applies in limited circumstances where the controversy is too short-lived to be fully litigated and there is a sufficient expectation of recurrence involving the complaining party.

---

### 5. Political-question doctrine

Some constitutional questions are treated as inappropriate for judicial resolution.

*Baker v. Carr* identified six considerations associated with political questions.

Do not confuse:

**Political question** with **politically controversial question**.

The doctrine is about justiciability, not merely political importance.

---

# B. Merits thresholds that operate like gates

These are not Article III justiciability doctrines, but they often determine whether a constitutional claim gets off the ground.

## 1. State-action requirement

Many constitutional protections constrain government rather than purely private conduct.

Under modern First Amendment state-action doctrine, a private entity may qualify as a state actor in limited circumstances, including where it performs a traditional exclusive public function, is compelled by government, or acts jointly with government.

### Shortcut

**IS THIS CONDUCT ATTRIBUTABLE TO GOVERNMENT?**

---

## 2. Selective incorporation

When determining whether a Bill of Rights guarantee applies to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, the Court has asked whether the right is:

* fundamental to the scheme of ordered liberty, and
* deeply rooted in the Nation's history and tradition.

Once incorporated, the right generally imposes the same substantive limitation on state and federal governments.

---

# VII. RULE-BOUNDARY DEVICES

These determine where a doctrine begins, ends, or does not apply.

The original notes correctly identified many of these as separate from “tests.”

| Device                    | Function                                                                   |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Trigger**               | Event or condition activating the doctrine                                 |
| **Threshold**             | Minimum requirement before substantive analysis                            |
| **Predicate**             | Foundational fact necessary for the doctrine                               |
| **Prerequisite**          | Requirement that must first be satisfied                                   |
| **Exception**             | Removes something otherwise governed by the rule                           |
| **Carve-out**             | Defined subset excluded from the general rule                              |
| **Exemption**             | Conduct or party relieved from a generally applicable requirement          |
| **Categorical exclusion** | Category lies outside the doctrine altogether                              |
| **Safe harbor**           | Defines circumstances guaranteed to avoid a specified legal consequence    |
| **Limiting principle**    | Establishes the outer boundary of a rule or argument                       |
| **Presumption**           | Establishes a starting legal position                                      |
| **Rebuttal**              | Showing sufficient to defeat a presumption                                 |
| **Fallback rule**         | Secondary rule governing when the primary route does not resolve the issue |

---

# Safe harbor versus exception

These are not synonyms.

## Exception

Structure:

**General rule applies except X.**

X is removed from the rule.

## Safe harbor

Structure:

**If conditions A + B + C are satisfied → protected zone**

The important point from the original notes remains correct:

**Inside safe harbor → guaranteed protection under the specified rule.**

But ordinarily:

**Outside safe harbor ≠ automatically unlawful.**

---

# Limiting principle

A limiting principle answers:

> If we accept this legal proposition, where does its logic stop?

It is usually not itself the merits test.

It prevents a proposed rule from expanding without a defensible boundary.

---

# VIII. INTERPRETIVE AND AVOIDANCE DEVICES

## 1. Narrowing construction

A court interprets potentially broad statutory language more narrowly.

Structure:

**Broad possible reading → narrower permissible reading**

The narrower interpretation must still be supported by the statutory text.

---

# 2. Constitutional avoidance

“Constitutional avoidance” is actually a family of judicial principles.

The *Ashwander* tradition includes ideas such as deciding no more constitutional law than necessary, preferring nonconstitutional grounds where available, and construing statutes to avoid constitutional problems when a plausible interpretation permits that result.

---

# 3. Constitutional-doubt canon

This is the specific statutory interpretation principle most people mean when they casually say “constitutional avoidance.”

Structure:

**Interpretation A → serious constitutional problem**

**Interpretation B → plausible and constitutionally safer**

**Choose B**

But only if B is actually a plausible interpretation of the statutory language.

The Court has repeatedly emphasized that avoidance does not authorize judicial rewriting of a statute.

---

# 4. Saving construction

A useful label for a permissible interpretation that allows a statute to survive a constitutional objection.

Relationship:

**Narrowing interpretation + constitutional compatibility → law survives**

“Saving construction” is therefore better understood as an interpretive result than as a universal test.

---

# 5. Clear-statement rule

A clear-statement rule requires Congress to speak with unusual clarity before courts will read legislation to produce certain constitutionally sensitive results.

Federalism provides important examples.

### Shortcut

**NO MAJOR INTRUSION WITHOUT CLEAR CONGRESSIONAL LANGUAGE**

---

# 6. Major Questions Doctrine

In administrative-law cases involving agency assertions of unusually consequential regulatory authority, the Court has required clear congressional authorization for decisions of major economic and political significance.

This is best understood as a special statutory-interpretation doctrine concerning the scope of delegated authority, not as ordinary constitutional scrutiny.

---

# 7. Judicial minimalism

General idea:

> Decide no more than necessary to resolve the case.

The constitutional-avoidance tradition expressly includes a judicial-minimalism principle.

---

# 8. Last-resort principle

Where a case can be decided on an adequate nonconstitutional ground, the Court generally avoids unnecessarily deciding the constitutional issue.

This is another component of constitutional-avoidance doctrine.

---

# IX. CHALLENGE TYPE, INVALIDATION, AND REMEDY

## 1. As-applied challenge

Argument:

> The law cannot constitutionally be applied to this plaintiff in these circumstances.

The claimant need not necessarily establish that every application of the law is invalid.

---

# 2. Facial challenge

Argument:

> The law itself is unconstitutional across the relevant range of applications.

Facial challenges are generally more demanding than ordinary as-applied challenges, although the exact standard varies with the constitutional doctrine involved.

---

# 3. First Amendment overbreadth

Overbreadth is a special facial doctrine.

The Court asks whether a law reaches a substantial amount of constitutionally protected speech relative to its plainly legitimate sweep.

### Shortcut

**THE LAW REACHES TOO MUCH PROTECTED SPEECH**

---

# 4. Vagueness

Vagueness is different from overbreadth.

A law may be unconstitutionally vague when it fails to give adequate notice of what is prohibited or creates insufficient standards to prevent arbitrary or discriminatory enforcement.

### Shortcut

**THE LAW IS TOO UNCLEAR**

Therefore:

**Overbreadth:** reaches too much.

**Vagueness:** says too little clearly enough.

**Facial / as-applied:** describes scope of challenge.

These are three different concepts.

---

# 5. Severability

Suppose:

**A + B + C + D**

C is unconstitutional.

The question becomes:

**Can A + B + D remain operative?**

That is severability.

It differs from narrowing construction because the court is determining whether an invalid provision can be separated from the rest of the statute rather than giving the invalid provision a narrower meaning.

Your original notes correctly separated these two concepts.

---

# 6. Partial invalidation

A court may invalidate only the unconstitutional portion or application rather than eliminating an entire statutory scheme.

Severability is one mechanism for doing that.

---

# 7. Unconstitutional-conditions doctrine

General principle:

> Government ordinarily cannot condition a public benefit on surrendering a constitutional right in circumstances where directly imposing that burden would be unconstitutional.

The doctrine appears in several constitutional areas, including speech and property.

This is another example of doctrine that does not fit neatly into the traditional scrutiny categories.

---

# X. WHAT THE COURT DOES TO PRECEDENT

Your original document identified this category correctly.

## 1. Apply / follow

Use the earlier precedent as controlling authority.

---

## 2. Reaffirm

Expressly confirm that a precedent remains authoritative.

---

## 3. Clarify

Explain more precisely what an earlier holding or rule means.

**Rule stays basically the same, meaning becomes clearer.**

---

## 4. Refine

Develop details, factors, terminology, or operation of an existing doctrine.

Usually a smaller doctrinal move than modification.

---

## 5. Reconcile / harmonize

Interpret apparently conflicting precedents so both can continue operating.

---

## 6. Distinguish

The earlier precedent remains good law, but a legally material difference prevents it from controlling the new case.

Structure:

**Earlier case: A + B + C → X**

**Current case: A + B + D → different result possible**

### Key point

Distinguishing ordinarily does **not** overrule the earlier precedent.

Your original notes correctly emphasized this distinction.

---

## 7. Extend

Apply an existing principle to a new, sufficiently analogous situation.

---

## 8. Expand

Broaden the doctrine's overall reach.

---

## 9. Narrow

Reduce the range of circumstances covered by the precedent.

---

## 10. Limit / cabin

Establish an outer boundary on how far the precedent may be taken.

“Cabin” is common legal vocabulary for restricting a precedent's reach without overruling it.

---

## 11. Modify

Substantively change part of an existing rule while leaving the precedent or doctrine substantially intact.

---

## 12. Recharacterize

Describe what an earlier decision stands for in a different way, which can materially alter its later effect.

---

## 13. Disapprove

Express disagreement with earlier reasoning or language without necessarily formally overruling the entire case.

---

## 14. Depart from

Move away from an earlier approach.

Whether this constitutes overruling depends on what was actually held and what the later decision does.

---

## 15. Overrule

The Court determines that an earlier precedent will no longer control as law.

Stare decisis favors continuity and predictability but is not an absolute command against overruling precedent.

---

## 16. Abrogate / supersede

Later legal authority displaces an earlier rule.

Depending on context, this may occur through:

* later Supreme Court authority
* congressional legislation where Congress possesses authority to change the relevant statutory rule
* constitutional amendment

Be careful with this term because a statute cannot simply “abrogate” a Supreme Court interpretation of the Constitution.

---

# Useful precedent spectrum

**Apply → reaffirm → clarify → refine → extend/narrow → modify → overrule**

Keep **distinguish** somewhat separate because distinguishing usually leaves the prior rule itself unchanged.

---

# XI. MAJOR NAMED SUPREME COURT FRAMEWORKS

The following is not literally every test the Supreme Court has ever announced. That would run into hundreds of highly specialized doctrines. This section captures many of the major recurring frameworks worth recognizing.

---

# FIRST AMENDMENT: SPEECH

## 1. Content-based speech restriction

General framework:

**Content based → presumptively unconstitutional → strict scrutiny**

The government ordinarily must satisfy strict scrutiny unless a recognized doctrinal exception applies.

---

## 2. Content-neutral time, place, and manner test

Generally asks whether the restriction is:

1. content neutral
2. narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest
3. leaves open ample alternative channels of communication

---

## 3. Brandenburg incitement test

Advocacy can fall outside ordinary First Amendment protection where it is:

1. directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action, and
2. likely to incite or produce that action.

### Type

**Two-part categorical threshold**

---

## 4. Miller obscenity test

*Miller v. California* established the modern three-part obscenity framework.

It considers whether:

1. the work appeals to the prurient interest under applicable community standards
2. it depicts defined sexual conduct in a patently offensive way
3. taken as a whole, it lacks serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value

### Type

**Three-prong categorical test**

---

## 5. Central Hudson commercial-speech test

The Court uses the *Central Hudson* framework for many restrictions on commercial speech.

The framework addresses:

1. whether the speech concerns lawful activity and is nonmisleading
2. whether the governmental interest is substantial
3. whether the regulation directly advances that interest
4. whether the restriction is appropriately fitted to the interest

The Court continues to describe *Central Hudson* as a four-part framework.

### Type

**Four-part intermediate framework**

---

## 6. O'Brien expressive-conduct test

For certain content-neutral regulations incidentally burdening expressive conduct, *United States v. O'Brien* asks whether the regulation is within governmental authority, furthers an important or substantial governmental interest, the interest is unrelated to suppression of expression, and the incidental speech restriction is no greater than essential to advancing that interest.

### Type

**Four-part intermediate framework**

---

## 7. Pickering-Connick-Garcetti public-employee framework

Simplified sequence:

1. Was the employee speaking as a citizen rather than pursuant to official job duties?
2. Did the speech involve a matter of public concern?
3. If so, balance the employee's speech interest against the government's interests as employer.

### Type

**Thresholds + balancing**

---

## 8. Public-forum doctrine

The applicable test depends heavily on the type of government forum.

### Traditional / designated public forum

Content-based restrictions generally receive strict scrutiny.

### Nonpublic / limited forums

Restrictions may receive reasonableness and viewpoint-neutrality review depending on the forum and restriction.

### Type

**Classification → different standard of review**

---

## 9. True-threat doctrine

After *Counterman v. Colorado*, the First Amendment requires a constitutionally sufficient subjective mental state before punishment for a true threat.

Recklessness concerning whether the communication would be perceived as threatening is sufficient.

### Type

**Categorical doctrine + mens rea threshold**

---

## 10. New York Times actual-malice standard

Public officials and public figures generally must satisfy the constitutional actual-malice requirement in qualifying defamation actions.

Actual malice means knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for truth, not ordinary ill will.

### Type

**Heightened fault requirement**

---

# FIRST AMENDMENT: RELIGION

## 11. Smith neutral-and-generally-applicable rule

Under *Employment Division v. Smith*, a law burdening religious exercise generally does not violate the Free Exercise Clause merely because of that burden if the law is neutral and generally applicable.

Nonneutral or non-generally-applicable laws may receive heightened scrutiny.

### Type

**Threshold classification → scrutiny consequence**

---

## 12. Establishment Clause history-and-practice approach

### Current status

The Court has abandoned the *Lemon* test and its endorsement-test offshoot.

Modern Establishment Clause analysis instead emphasizes historical practices and understandings.

### Type

**Historical inquiry**

---

# SECOND AMENDMENT

## 13. Bruen-Rahimi text-and-history framework

Simplified structure:

1. determine whether the Second Amendment's text covers the conduct
2. if protected conduct is implicated, evaluate whether the restriction is consistent with the Nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation
3. historical regulations need not be identical twins
4. courts reason through relevant historical analogues and underlying regulatory principles

### Type

**Textual threshold + historical analogy**

This is not ordinary means-end scrutiny.

---

# FOURTH AMENDMENT

## 14. Katz reasonable-expectation-of-privacy framework

*Katz* produced the familiar reasonable-expectation-of-privacy approach for identifying certain Fourth Amendment searches.

Modern Fourth Amendment doctrine also recognizes a property-based approach, so *Katz* should not be treated as the exclusive definition of a search.

### Type

**Threshold test**

---

## 15. Gates probable-cause totality test

*Illinois v. Gates* replaced the rigid *Aguilar-Spinelli* informant framework with totality-of-the-circumstances analysis for probable cause.

### Type

**Totality of circumstances**

---

## 16. Terry reasonable-suspicion framework

*Terry* permits certain brief investigative stops based on reasonable suspicion rather than probable cause.

The doctrine requires objective, articulable grounds rather than a purely subjective hunch.

### Type

**Reasonableness / totality threshold**

---

## 17. Warrant-exception doctrines

Fourth Amendment doctrine contains numerous recognized warrant exceptions or special frameworks, including:

* consent
* exigent circumstances
* search incident to arrest
* vehicle searches
* plain view
* special-needs searches
* border searches
* certain school searches
* searches involving probationers or parolees

Constitution Annotated organizes Fourth Amendment doctrine around these separate exceptions and specialized frameworks.

---

## 18. Exclusionary-rule exceptions

Important doctrines limiting suppression include:

* good-faith exception
* attenuation
* independent-source principles
* inevitable discovery in qualifying circumstances

The Supreme Court's exclusionary-rule jurisprudence recognizes several circumstances where evidence need not be suppressed despite an earlier constitutional violation.

### Type

**Remedial exceptions**

---

# FIFTH AND FOURTEENTH AMENDMENTS: DUE PROCESS

## 19. Mathews procedural due-process test

*Mathews v. Eldridge* balances:

1. the private interest affected
2. the risk of erroneous deprivation under existing procedures and probable value of additional safeguards
3. the government's interest, including administrative and fiscal burdens

### Type

**Three-factor balancing test**

---

## 20. Substantive due-process fundamental-rights analysis

A major modern strand of substantive due-process doctrine asks whether an asserted unenumerated right is deeply rooted in the Nation's history and tradition.

The exact formulation and degree of specificity have varied across cases, so this should not be reduced to one universal mechanical test.

### Type

**Historical / fundamental-rights inquiry**

---

## 21. Void-for-vagueness doctrine

The law must provide sufficient definiteness to give fair notice and guard against arbitrary or discriminatory enforcement.

### Type

**Due-process clarity requirement**

---

# FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT: EQUAL PROTECTION

## 22. Rational basis

**Legitimate interest + rational relationship**

---

## 23. Intermediate scrutiny

For sex classifications:

**Important governmental objective + substantial relationship**

---

## 24. Strict scrutiny

For racial classifications:

**Compelling governmental interest + narrow tailoring**

---

## 25. Washington v. Davis discriminatory-intent requirement

A racially disparate impact from a facially neutral law ordinarily does not by itself establish an Equal Protection violation.

The claimant generally must prove discriminatory intent.

---

## 26. Arlington Heights framework

Where discriminatory purpose is disputed, *Arlington Heights* supplies a framework for evaluating circumstantial evidence of discriminatory intent.

Relevant evidence can include impact, historical background, sequence of events, departures from ordinary procedure, and legislative or administrative history.

The broader *Davis-Arlington Heights* line remains central to intentional-discrimination claims involving facially neutral government action.

### Type

**Intent-focused multi-factor framework**

---

# ELECTION LAW

## 27. Anderson-Burdick framework

Courts weigh:

* character and magnitude of the burden on constitutional rights
* governmental interests offered in justification
* the extent to which those interests make the burden necessary

### Type

**Sliding-scale balancing**

---

# SEPARATION OF POWERS

## 28. Formalist analysis

Focus:

**constitutional category + institutional boundary**

---

## 29. Functionalist analysis

Focus:

**practical operation + interference with constitutional functions**

Both are recognized approaches in separation-of-powers analysis.

---

## 30. Nondelegation intelligible-principle test

The Court's traditional primary nondelegation test asks whether Congress has provided an intelligible principle guiding the exercise of delegated discretion.

### Type

**Structural threshold test**

# SPENDING POWER

## 31. Spending Clause conditions

The Court's Spending Clause doctrine includes requirements concerning:

* pursuit of the general welfare
* clear notice of funding conditions
* relationship between conditions and the federal interest
* independent constitutional limitations
* prohibition against unconstitutional coercion

### Type

**Multi-requirement structural framework**

# FEDERALISM

## 32. Anti-commandeering rule

Congress generally may not directly command state legislatures or executive officials to administer a federal regulatory program.

The doctrine protects structural federalism and political accountability.

### Type

**Structural categorical rule**

---

## 33. Federalism clear-statement rule

Courts may require Congress to state its intention clearly before construing a statute to significantly alter the traditional federal-state balance.

### Type

**Interpretive presumption / clear-statement rule**

---

# DORMANT COMMERCE CLAUSE

## 34. Discriminatory-law branch

State laws discriminating against interstate commerce face highly demanding review.

---

## 35. Pike balancing

For certain evenhanded laws imposing incidental burdens on interstate commerce, courts balance the burden on interstate commerce against the local benefits or interests.

### Type

**Balancing test**

# TAKINGS CLAUSE

## 36. Per se takings

Certain government actions fall within categorical or per se takings rules.

---

## 37. Penn Central framework

For many regulatory takings claims, courts consider:

1. economic impact of the regulation
2. interference with reasonable or distinct investment-backed expectations
3. character of the governmental action

### Type

**Three-factor test**

---

## 38. Lucas total-deprivation rule

A regulation eliminating all economically beneficial use of property may fall within a categorical takings rule, subject to doctrinal qualifications.

The Court distinguishes this category from the flexible *Penn Central* framework.

### Type

**Categorical / per se rule**

---

## 39. Nollan-Dolan exactions test

Government permit exactions are examined for:

1. essential nexus
2. rough proportionality

### Type

**Two-stage unconstitutional-conditions test**

# FIFTH AMENDMENT: SELF-INCRIMINATION

## 40. Miranda framework

Miranda protections are triggered by:

**custody + interrogation**

Before custodial interrogation, police generally must provide the required warnings or their equivalent.

### Type

**Trigger + procedural safeguard**

Miranda doctrine then contains additional rules concerning:

* waiver
* invocation
* reinitiation of questioning
* public-safety and other exceptions
* use of statements in particular proceedings

# FIFTH AMENDMENT: DOUBLE JEOPARDY

## 41. Blockburger same-elements test

When deciding whether two statutory offenses count as the same offense for double-jeopardy purposes, the *Blockburger* framework asks whether each offense requires proof of an element that the other does not.

### Type

**Elements comparison**

---

# SIXTH AMENDMENT: COUNSEL

## 42. Strickland ineffective-assistance test

A defendant generally must establish:

1. constitutionally deficient attorney performance
2. resulting prejudice

Prejudice generally requires a reasonable probability that the result would have been different absent counsel's deficient performance.

### Type

**Two-prong test**

---

# SIXTH AMENDMENT: SPEEDY TRIAL

## 43. Barker v. Wingo test

The Court balances:

1. length of delay
2. reason for delay
3. defendant's assertion of the speedy-trial right
4. prejudice

### Type

**Four-factor balancing test**

---

# JURY SELECTION / EQUAL PROTECTION

## 44. Batson framework

*Batson* uses a three-stage burden-shifting process for claims of discriminatory peremptory challenges.

General structure:

1. circumstances support an inference of prohibited discrimination
2. the party exercising the strike provides a neutral explanation
3. the court determines whether purposeful discrimination has been proved

### Type

**Burden-shifting framework**

---

# SIXTH AMENDMENT: CONFRONTATION

## 45. Crawford testimonial-statement framework

The key threshold is whether an out-of-court statement is **testimonial**.

For testimonial statements, the Confrontation Clause generally requires:

* the declarant to be available for cross-examination, or
* if unavailable, the defendant previously had an opportunity for cross-examination, subject to recognized doctrinal exceptions.

### Type

**Categorical threshold + procedural requirement**

---

## 46. Primary-purpose test

In determining whether statements to authorities are testimonial, the Court examines the primary purpose of the exchange.

Statements whose primary purpose is responding to an ongoing emergency may be treated differently from statements primarily designed to establish past events for prosecution.

### Type

**Purpose-based contextual test**

---

# EIGHTH AMENDMENT

## 47. Proportionality analysis

The Court recognizes proportionality principles under the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause, but the framework differs depending on whether the challenge concerns:

* term-of-years sentences
* capital punishment
* juvenile punishment
* categorical punishment rules

### Type

**Proportionality / categorical analysis**

---

## 48. Conditions-of-confinement deliberate-indifference framework

Certain Eighth Amendment prison-condition claims involve both objective and subjective components, including whether officials acted with deliberate indifference to a sufficiently serious risk.

### Type

**Objective + subjective requirements**

# XII. RELATED DOCTRINES OFTEN MISTAKEN FOR CONSTITUTIONAL “TESTS”

## 1. Qualified immunity

Qualified immunity concerns civil liability of government officials, not whether the Constitution itself was violated.

A major question is whether the relevant constitutional rule was sufficiently clearly established.

Keep separate:

**Constitutional merits:** Was there a constitutional violation?

**Qualified immunity:** Can this official be held personally liable for damages?

---

## 2. Exclusionary rule

The exclusionary rule is principally a **remedial doctrine** governing suppression of evidence.

It is not the definition of what constitutes an unconstitutional search.

---

## 3. Stare decisis

Stare decisis regulates treatment of precedent.

It is not itself the substantive constitutional test governing the underlying dispute.

---

## 4. Standards of proof

Concepts such as:

* preponderance of the evidence
* clear and convincing evidence
* beyond a reasonable doubt

describe evidentiary burdens, not constitutional interpretive approaches.

Specific constitutional doctrines may nevertheless require particular proof standards. For example, First Amendment defamation doctrine imposes a clear-and-convincing requirement for actual malice in relevant cases.

---

# XIII. IMPORTANT ABANDONED OR REPLACED TESTS

Do not memorize obsolete frameworks as though they were current law.

## 1. Lemon test

### Historical

The Establishment Clause once prominently used the *Lemon v. Kurtzman* framework.

### Current status

**Abandoned.**

The Court has stated that Establishment Clause analysis should instead look to historical practices and understandings.

---

## 2. Chevron deference

### Historical

*Chevron* instructed courts in qualifying circumstances to defer to reasonable agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes.

### Current status

**Overruled in 2024 by Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo.**

Courts must exercise independent judicial judgment in statutory interpretation rather than defer simply because a statute is ambiguous.

---

## 3. Aguilar-Spinelli probable-cause test

### Historical

A more rigid two-part framework governed certain informant-based probable-cause inquiries.

### Current status

The Supreme Court expressly abandoned that framework in *Illinois v. Gates* in favor of totality of the circumstances.

---

## 4. Older incitement formulations

Earlier First Amendment doctrine employed formulations including the “clear and present danger” approach.

Modern incitement doctrine is governed by *Brandenburg*'s imminent-lawless-action standard.

---

# XIV. RULES VERSUS STANDARDS

This is a useful conceptual spectrum, not an official Supreme Court taxonomy.

Your original notes correctly identified the general idea.

### More rule-like

**Per se / categorical
→ bright line
→ elements / prongs
→ structured factors
→ balancing / sliding scale
→ totality of circumstances**

### More standard-like

Moving left usually gives judges less case-specific discretion.

Moving right generally permits more contextual judgment.

But doctrines can combine both.

Example:

> Categorical trigger → multi-factor analysis → balancing → exception.

# The single most important organizational rule

Never ask only:

> “What test is this?”

Instead ask:

> **What job is this doctrine performing?**

Is it:

**interpreting the Constitution?**

**deciding the merits?**

**triggering a doctrine?**

**assigning a burden?**

**creating an exception?**

**limiting a rule?**

**deciding whether the court can hear the case?**

**determining how broadly a law is unconstitutional?**

**preserving or severing a statute?**

**or determining what to do with precedent?**

Once the function is identified, the terminology stops looking like hundreds of unrelated Supreme Court “tests” and starts operating as a coherent system.
